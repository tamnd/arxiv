<!-- defn-1: body_md -->
\label{zircon}
A poset $P$ is a \emph{zircon} if, for every non-minimal element $p \in P$, the principal order ideal $P_{\leq p}$ is finite and admits a special matching.
<!-- end defn-1 -->
<!-- defn-2: body_md -->
\label{spm}
Let $P$ be a finite poset with $\hat{1}$ and covering relation $\vartriangleleft$.
A \emph{special partial matching}, or \emph{SPM}, on $P$ is a function $M \colon P \to P$ such that
\begin{itemize}
  \item $M^2=\mathrm{id}$,
  \item $M(\hat{1}) \vartriangleleft \hat{1}$,
  \item for all $x \in P$, we have $M(x) \vartriangleleft x$, $x \vartriangleleft M(x)$, or $M(x)=x$, and
  \item if $x \vartriangleleft y$ and $M(x) \neq y$, then $M(x)<M(y)$.
\end{itemize}
<!-- end defn-2 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lifting}
Suppose that $P$ is a finite poset with $\hat{1}$ and an SPM $M$. If $x,y \in P$ with $x<y$ and $M(y) \leq y$, then
\begin{itemize}
  \item[(i)]   $M(x) \leq y$,
  \item[(ii)]  $M(x) \leq x \Rightarrow M(x)<M(y)$, and
  \item[(iii)] $M(x) \geq x \Rightarrow x \leq M(y)$.
 \end{itemize}

<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:autom}
Let $P$ be a finite poset with a maximum and a special partial matching $M$.
Let also $\theta$ be any automorphism of $P$.
Then the subposet $P^\theta$ induced by the fixed elements of $\theta$ has a special partial matching.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Since $P$ is finite, $\theta$ has finite order $K$.
Observe that each automorphism $\theta^i$, $i \in [K]$, transforms the SPM $M$ into an SPM $M_i$, i.e., $\theta^i \circ M=M_i \circ \theta^i$.
In particular, $M_K=M$.

For a given $p \in P$, define
\[
C(p)=\{a \in P \mid \text{$a=M_{i_t} \circ M_{i_{t-1}} \circ \cdots \circ M_{i_1}(p)$ for some $i_1,\ldots,i_t \in [K]$}\}.
\]
Thus, $C(p)$ consists of the elements that are connected to $p$ by the SPMs $M_i$.
By abuse of notation, we also let $C(p)$ denote the subposet of $P$ induced by the set $C(p)$.
For $a \in C(p)$ given by $a=M_{i_t} \circ M_{i_{t-1}} \circ \cdots \circ M_{i_1}(p)$,
define $b \in C(p)$ as $b=M_{i_t}' \circ M_{i_{t-1}}' \circ \cdots \circ M_{i_1}'(p)$, where the $M_{i_j}'$ are recursively given by
\begin{equation} \label{prim}
M_{i_j}'=\begin{cases}M_{i_j} & \text{if $M_{i_j} \circ M_{i_{j-1}}' \circ \cdots \circ M_{i_1}'(p)<M_{i_{j-1}}' \circ \cdots \circ M_{i_1}'(p)$,} \\
\mathrm{id} & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\end{equation}

For $a_j,b_j \in C(p)$, where $a_j=M_{i_j} \circ \cdots \circ M_{i_1}(p)$ and $b_j=M_{i_j}' \circ \cdots \circ M_{i_1}'(p)$, $1 \leq j \leq t$, we have $b_j \leq a_j$.
To see it, we use induction by assuming that $b_{j-1} \leq b_{a-1}$.
We have the following three cases.
\begin{itemize}
  \item[(i)]   If $a_j \geq a_{j-1}$, there is nothing to prove.
  \item[(ii)]  If $a_j<a_{j-1}$ and $M_{i_j}'=M_{i_j}$, we have $b_j= M_{i_j}(b_{j-1})<b_{j-1}$ and $M_{i_j}(a_{j-1})=a_j<a_{j-1}$.
Hence, we may apply the lifting property to get $b_j \leq a_j$ (with equality if and only if $a_{j-1}=b_{j-1}$).
  \item[(iii)] Finally, if $a_j<a_{j-1}$ and $M_{i_j}'=\mathrm{id}$, we have $M_{i_j}(b_{j-1}) \geq b_{j-1}$ and $M_{i_j}(a_{j-1})=a_j<a_{j-1}$.
We can then apply the lifting property to get $b_j=b_{j-1} \leq a_j$.
\end{itemize}

Thus $b_j \leq a_j$, proving the claim. In particular, $b \leq a$.
By construction, $b \leq p$, so if $p$ and $a$ are both minimal in $C(p)$, we have $a=b=p$.
Hence $C(p)$ contains a unique minimal element.
Analogously, if we reverse the inequality in \eqref{prim}, $C(p)$ also contains a unique maximal element. 
\footnote{Observe that $P$ need not have a minimum.
However, Lemma~\ref{lifting} will still hold, with the same proof as in \cite{A-H-H_pircons}, even if $P$ does not have a maximum, as long as $M$ satisfies the other three conditions in Definition~\ref{spm}.}

If $p$ is not minimal in $C(p)$, then $M_i(p) \vartriangleleft p$ for some $i$.
To see this, note that if $a<p$ in the above construction, then $b \neq p$ and so we may choose $i=i_j$ for the minimal $j$ which satisfies $M_{i_j}'=M_{i_j}$.
Reversing the inequality in \eqref{prim}, a similar reasoning shows that if $p$ is not maximal in $C(p)$, then there is at least one $i$ such that $p \vartriangleleft M_i(p)$.

If $p \in P^\theta$ and $p \leq M_i(p)$ for at least one $i$, then this will hold for all $i$ and hence $p=\min C(p)$.
Similarly, if $p \in P^\theta$ and $M_i(p) \leq p$, then $p=\max C(p)$.
Therefore, for any $p \in P^\theta$ we have $p=\min C(p) $ or $p=\max C(p)$, both if $C(p)=\{p\}$.

Since $\theta$ permutes the SPMs $M_i$, we have $\theta(C(p))=C(\theta(p))$ for all $p \in P$. Thus the claim below follows.

\begin{ClaimA} \label{claim:c1}
If $p \in P^\theta$, then both $\min C(p)$ and $\max C(p)$ belong to $P^\theta$.
\end{ClaimA}

Moreover we have:

\begin{ClaimA} \label{claim:c2}
For any $x,y \in P$, if $\min C(x) \leq \max C(y)$, then $\min C(x) \leq \min C(y)$ and $\max C(x) \leq \max C(y)$.
\end{ClaimA}

\begin{proof}
Let $p \in P$ be arbitrary. If $s=\max C(s)$ and $C(s) \neq C(p)$, it follows from the lifting property that $s>p \Rightarrow s>M_i(p)$ for all $i$.
This shows that $s>p \Rightarrow s>\max C(p)$ (by repeated application of the lifting property).
A similar argument shows that if $s=\min C(s)$ and $C(s) \neq C(p)$, then $s<p \Rightarrow s<\min C(p)$.
Hence, by using the above argument, if we take $s=\max C(y)$ and $p=\min C(x)$ where $s>p$, we get $\max C(y) \geq \max C(x)$.
Similarly, if $s=\min C(x)$ and $p=\max C(y)$ where $s<p$, then $\min C(x) \leq \min C(y)$.
\end{proof}

Let $\vartriangleleft_\theta$ denote the covering relation in $P^\theta$.

\begin{ClaimA} \label{claim:c3}
If $\alpha=\min C(p)$ and $\beta=\max C(p)$ belong to $P^\theta$, then $\alpha \vartriangleleft_\theta \beta$ or $\alpha=\beta$.
\end{ClaimA}

\begin{proof}
Assume that $x\in P^\theta$. If $x=\min C(x)$ and $x<\beta$, then by Claim~\ref{claim:c2}, $x \leq \alpha$.
Similarly, if $x=\max C(x)$ and $\alpha<x$, then by Claim~\ref{claim:c2}, $\beta \leq x$.
In either case, $x$ does not satisfy $\alpha<x<\beta$.
\end{proof}

Define the function $\mathrm{M}^\theta \colon P^\theta\to P^\theta$ by
\[
\mathrm{M}^\theta(p)=\begin{cases}\max C(p) & \text{if $p=\min C(p)$,} \\ \min C(p) & \text{if $p=\max C(p)$.}\end{cases}
\]
By Claim~\ref{claim:c1}, $\mathrm{M}^\theta$ is well defined.
We show that $\mathrm{M}^\theta$ is an SPM on $P^\theta$ by verifying the conditions in Definition~\ref{spm}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item For every $x \in P^\theta$, $(\mathrm{M}^\theta)^2(x)=x$. That is, $(\mathrm{M}^\theta)^2=\mathrm{id}$.

  \item Since $\theta$ is an automorphism of $P$, $\theta(\hat{1})=\hat{1}$, so $\hat{1} \in P^\theta$.
Because $M_i$ is an SPM, $M_i(\hat{1}) \neq \hat{1}$, so $\hat{1} \neq \min C(\hat{1})$.
By Claim~\ref{claim:c3}, $\mathrm{M}^\theta(\hat{1})=\min C(\hat{1}) \vartriangleleft_\theta \hat{1}$.

  \item For any $x \in P^\theta$, Claim~\ref{claim:c3} shows that $\mathrm{M}^\theta(x) \vartriangleleft_\theta x$, $x \vartriangleleft_\theta \mathrm{M}^\theta(x)$, or $\mathrm{M}^\theta(x)=x$.

  \item Let $x \vartriangleleft_\theta y$ and $\mathrm{M}^\theta(x) \neq y$. We must show that $\mathrm{M}^\theta(x)<\mathrm{M}^\theta(y)$.
By Claim~\ref{claim:c2}, $\min C(x) \leq \min C(y)$ and $\max C(x) \leq \max C(y)$.
Moreover, both inequalities are strict since $\mathrm{M}^\theta(x) \neq y$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item[(i)]   If $x \neq \min C(x)$, then $\mathrm{M}^\theta(x)=\min C(x)<\min C(y) \leq \mathrm{M}^\theta(y)$.
  \item[(ii)]  If $y \neq \max C(y)$, we have $\mathrm{M}^\theta(x) \leq \max C(x)<\max C(y)=\mathrm{M}^\theta(y)$.
  \item[(iii)] If $x=\min C(x)$ and $y=\max C(y)$, we have $x=\min C(x)<\min C(y) \leq y$ and $x \leq \max C(x)<\max C(y)=y$.
Because $x \vartriangleleft_\theta y$, we conclude that $\min C(x)=\max C(x)$ and $\min C(y)=\max C(y)$.
Hence $\mathrm{M}^\theta(x)=x \vartriangleleft_\theta y=\mathrm{M}^\theta(y)$. \qedhere
\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\label{incr}
Let $\sigma<\tau$ in $I_n^B$. Then there is exactly one increasing $\sigma$-$\tau$-chain, and it is lex-minimal.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{decr}
Let $\sigma<\tau$ in $I_n^B$. Then there is exactly one decreasing $\sigma$-$\tau$-chain.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Since $\operatorname{Bct}_{(i,j)}(\sigma)(i)>\operatorname{Bct}_{(i,j)}(\sigma)(j)$, there is also exactly one $\sigma$-$\tau$-chain with weakly decreasing labels.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- thm-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:EL}
The poset $F_n^B$ is EL-shellable.
<!-- end thm-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $\sigma<\tau$ in $F_n^B$, let $C_D=(\sigma \vartriangleleft \tau_k \vartriangleleft \cdots \vartriangleleft \tau_1 \vartriangleleft \tau)$ be the decreasing $\sigma$-$\tau$-chain in $I_n^B$, where $k \geq 1$,
and let $(i_\tau,j_\tau)$ be the label on $\tau_1 \vartriangleleft \tau$.
Observe that if we can prove that $\tau_1 \in F_n^B$, it follows that $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_k \in F_n^B$, because the decreasing $\sigma$-$\tau_1$-chain in $I_n^B$ is $\sigma \vartriangleleft \tau_k \vartriangleleft \cdots \vartriangleleft \tau_2 \vartriangleleft \tau_1$.

Let $h=\operatorname{di}(\sigma,\tau)$.
Since $\sigma$ has no fixed points, $h$ is an exceedance of $\sigma$ (i.e., $\sigma(h)>h$).
Indeed, if $\sigma(i)<i$, then $\sigma(i)$ is an exceedance of $\sigma$, and $\sigma(\sigma(i))=\tau(\sigma(i)) \Leftrightarrow \sigma(i)=\tau(i)$.
Consider any cover $\pi \vartriangleleft \pi'$ in $[\sigma,\tau]$.
Two applications of Definition~\ref{order} yield $\operatorname{di}(\pi,\pi') \geq \operatorname{di}(\pi,\tau) \geq h$.
Since the label $(i,j)$ on $\pi \vartriangleleft \pi'$ satisfies $i=\operatorname{di}(\pi,\pi')$, it follows that $h$ is in some label on $C_D$, and because $C_D$ is decreasing, $i_\tau=h$.

Suppose $\tau_1 \notin F_n^B$ so that $\tau_1 \vartriangleleft \tau$ is a case where the number of fixed points decreases.
The only such cases are E1.1, N1.1, N4.2, and H2.1, and in all of them, $h$ is a fixed point of $\tau_1$.
Observe that $\tau_1$ has fixed points also in E2.6a, N1.2a, N1.2b, N1.3a, N1.3b, N2.2, H1.2, H2.2a, H2.2b, and H2.3, and $h$ is not always one of them, but the number of fixed points does not decrease in those cases.
Therefore, $\sigma[h,h+1]>\tau_1[h,h+1]$.
Since $\sigma \leq \tau_1$, it follows from Definition~\ref{order} that $\tau_1 \in F_n^B$.

We have proved that the decreasing $\sigma$-$\tau$-chain in $I_n^B$ is contained in $F_n^B$.
The next step is to show that this chain is lex-maximal.
This is done in the same way as in the proof of \cite[Theorem~1.4]{Hansson_graded_A}, where Incitti's type~$A$ version of Lemma~\ref{incr} is used.
We include the argument here in order to make the proof complete.

To obtain a contradiction, consider the lex-maximal $\sigma$-$\tau$-chain in $I_n^B$, and suppose it is not decreasing;
call it $C=(\pi_1 \vartriangleleft \cdots \vartriangleleft \pi_{k+2})$ and say that $\lambda(\pi_1,\pi_2) \leq \lambda(\pi_2,\pi_3)$.
By Lemma~\ref{incr}, $\pi_1 \vartriangleleft \pi_2 \vartriangleleft \pi_3$ is lex-minimal among the $\pi_1$-$\pi_3$-chains in $I_n^B$.
Thus, $\pi_1 \vartriangleleft \pi_2' \vartriangleleft \pi_3 \vartriangleleft \cdots \vartriangleleft \pi_{k+2}$, where $\pi_1 \vartriangleleft \pi_2' \vartriangleleft \pi_3$ is the decreasing $\pi_1$-$\pi_3$-chain, is lex-larger than $C$, a contradiction.

So, the decreasing $\sigma$-$\tau$-chain in $I_n^B$ is contained in $F_n^B$ and it is lex-maximal.
Now, by reversing the lexicographic order on the set $\{(i,j) \in [\pm n]^2 \mid i<j\}$, we obtain an edge-labelling of $F_n^B$ such that in each interval $[\sigma,\tau]$, there is an increasing $\sigma$-$\tau$-chain which is lex-minimal.
By Lemma~\ref{decr} and the remark following it, this is an EL-labelling of $F_n^B$.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
