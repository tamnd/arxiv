<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{De1}
  Let $q\colon X \times X \to [0,\infty)$ be a mapping. Then $\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ is a {\bf Cauchy sequence} 
in $\mbox{$(X,q)$}$, if for  each $\epsilon > 0$ there exists an $\mbox{$n_{0}$} \in \mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}$ such that each 
$m,n \in \mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}$, $\mbox{$n_{0}$} < m < n$ yield $\mbox{$q(x_{n},x_{m})$} < \epsilon$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{De2}
  Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a uniform space. A mapping $q\colon X \times X \to [0,\infty)$ is a 
$\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}${\bf-istance} in $X$ if the following conditions are satisfied:
\begin{subequations}
\label{con1}
\begin{align}
 & q(z,x) \leq q(z,y) + q(y,x), \quad \mbox{$x,y,z \in X$}, \label{con1a}\\
 & q(\cdot,x) \mbox{ is lower semicontinuous,} \quad x \in X, \label{con1b}\\
 & \mbox{each Cauchy sequence in } \mbox{$(X,q)$} \mbox{ is a Cauchy sequence in } \mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}. \label{con1c}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{Pro3}
Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a uniform Hausdorff space. Then any $\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$-istance $q$ in $X$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{con2}
 q(x,y)=0 \mbox{ and } q(y,x)=0 \mbox{ yield } x=y,  \quad \mbox{$x,y \in X$}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- head-1: body_md -->
Assume $q(x,y)=0$ and $q(y,x)=0$. Let us adopt $x_{2k-1} = x$ and $x_{2k} = y$, 
$k \in \mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}$. Clearly, $\mbox{$q(x_{n},x_{m})$} = 0$ holds for each $m \neq n$, $m, n \in \mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}$, and 
$\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ (see (\ref{con1c})), i.e. $x = y$. $\quad \square
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{Le4}
Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a complete uniform Hausdorff space, $q$ a $\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$-istance in $X$, and let $\mbox{$\psi\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$}$ 
be a lower semicontinuous mapping bounded below. Assume that $C \subset X$ is a nonempty 
maximal chain (for $\mbox{$\preccurlyeq$}$) such that $q(x,y)=0$ or $q(y,x)=0$, for each $x,y \in C$,
$x \neq y$. Then $C$ has a unique smallest element.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- head-2: body_md -->
Assume that $C$ contains at least two elements. Let us adopt $\mbox{$C_{y}$}=\{z \in C\colon q(z,y)=0 \}$ and suppose 
that there exists a $U \in \mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$ such that $\mbox{$C_{y}$} \not \subset U(y)$, for each $y \in C$. Then 
there exists a sequence $\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ in $C$ such that $q(\mbox{$x_{n+1}$},\mbox{$x_{n}$})=0$ and $\mbox{$x_{n+1}$} \not \in U(\mbox{$x_{n}$})$. 
In view of (\ref{con1a}), $\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $\mbox{$(X,q)$}$ and consequently, $\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ is a Cauchy sequence 
in $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$. This contradicts the way $\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ was defined. Thus, for each $U \in \mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$ there exists a 
$y \in C$ such that $\mbox{$C_{y}$} \subset U(y)$. The family $\mbox{$\mathcal{C}$} = \{\mbox{$\overline{C_{y}}$}\colon y \in C \}$ of closed 
sets has the finite intersection property and $\mbox{$\mathcal{C}$}$ contains small sets. Therefore 
\cite[Theorem 23, p. 193]{Ke}, there exists a unique $x \in \bigcap \mbox{$\mathcal{C}$}$. From the fact that 
$x \in \mbox{$\overline{C_{y}}$}$ and the lower semicontinuity of $q(\cdot,y)$ it follows that 
$q(x,y)=0$, $\mbox{$y \in C \setminus \{x\}$}$. On the other hand, $x \in  \{z \in X\colon \psi(z) \leq \psi(y)\}$, for each 
$\mbox{$y \in C \setminus \{x\}$}$, and consequently, $x \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} y$, $\mbox{$y \in C \setminus \{x\}$}$, i.e. $x$ is the unique smallest element of $C$ (see 
(\ref{con2})). $\quad \square
<!-- end head-2 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{Th5}
Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a uniform space, $q$ a $\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$-istance in $X$, while $q$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\label{con5}
 q(x,y) = 0 \mbox{ yields } x = y,  \quad \mbox{$x,y \in X$}
\end{equation}
or $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ is Hausdorff. Assume that $\mbox{$\psi\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$}$ is a lower semicontinuous mapping bounded below satisfying:
\begin{equation}
\label{con6}
 \mbox{$\psi_{\alpha}$}=\{z \in X\colon \psi(z) \leq \alpha\} \mbox{ is complete for each } \alpha \in \mbox{$\mathbb{R}$},
\end{equation}
and that the following holds (see (\ref{con3}), (\ref{con4})):
\begin{eqnarray}
\label{con7}
 \mbox{for each } x \in X \setminus B  \mbox{ there exists a } \mbox{$y \in X \setminus \{x\}$} \mbox{ such that } y \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} x .
\end{eqnarray}
Then for any $\mbox{$x_{0}$} \in X \setminus B$, each maximal chain $A \subset X$ containing $\mbox{$x_{0}$}$ 
(such a chain exists) has a unique smallest element $x$ which in addition satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item $\psi(x) = \inf \{\psi(z)\colon z \in X\}$ (i.e. $x \in B$),
\item $\psi(x) + q(x,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$}) = \inf \{\psi(z) + q(z,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$})\colon z \in A \} \leq 0$,
\item $0 < \psi(y) + q(y,x) - \psi(x)$, $\quad \mbox{$y \in X \setminus \{x\}$}$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- head-3: body_md -->
The relation $\mbox{$\preccurlyeq$}$ is transitive and in view of Kuratowski's lemma \cite[p. 33]{Ke} for any 
$\mbox{$x_{0}$} \in X \setminus B$ (see (\ref{con7})) there exists a maximal chain $A$ containing $\mbox{$x_{0}$}$. Let us adopt 
$\alpha = \inf \{ \psi(z)\colon z \in A \}$ and suppose $\alpha < \psi(x)$, for each $x \in A$. 
Then there exists a sequence $\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ in $A$ such that $\mbox{$(\psi(x_{n}))_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ decreases to $\alpha$. Condition 
$\mbox{$x_{m}$} \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} \mbox{$x_{n}$}$ for $m < n$ would mean that

\[
 0 \leq q(\mbox{$x_{m}$},\mbox{$x_{n}$}) \leq \psi(\mbox{$x_{n}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{m}$}) < 0,
\]
which is impossible. Therefore, $\mbox{$x_{n}$} \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} \mbox{$x_{m}$}$ must be true ($\mbox{$x_{n}$}, \mbox{$x_{m}$} \in A$) and we obtain
\[
 q(\mbox{$x_{n}$},\mbox{$x_{m}$}) \leq \psi(\mbox{$x_{m}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{n}$}), \quad m,n \in \mbox{$\mathbb{N}$} \mbox{, } m < n,
\]
i.e. $\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $\mbox{$(X,q)$}$, as $\psi$ is bounded below. Consequently, (see (\ref{con1c})) 
$\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$. On the other hand, all $\mbox{$x_{n}$}$ for large 
$n$ are contained in $\{z \in X\colon \psi(z) \leq \psi(\mbox{$x_{m}$}) \}$ which is complete
(see (\ref{con6})). Therefore, $\mbox{$(x_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$}$ is convergent; let us say that $v \in \mbox{$\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty}$} \mbox{$x_{n}$}$. From the lower 
semicontinuity of $\psi$, $\mbox{$q(\cdot,x_{m})$}$ it follows that $\psi(v) \leq \alpha$ and 
\[
 \psi(v) + q(v,\mbox{$x_{m}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{m}$}) \leq 0, \quad m \in \mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}.
\]
On the other hand, for any $y \in A$ there exists an $\mbox{$x_{n}$}$ such that $\psi(\mbox{$x_{n}$}) < \psi(y)$ and
consequently, ($\mbox{$x_{n}$}, y \in A$)
\[
 \psi(\mbox{$x_{n}$}) + q(\mbox{$x_{n}$},y) - \psi(y) \leq 0
\]
must hold, as
\[
 0 \leq q(y,\mbox{$x_{n}$}) \leq \psi(\mbox{$x_{n}$}) - \psi(y) < 0,
\]
is false. Now, we obtain (see (\ref{con1a}))
\[
 \psi(v) + q(v,y) - \psi(y) \leq \psi(v) + q(v,\mbox{$x_{n}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{n}$}) + \psi(\mbox{$x_{n}$}) + q(\mbox{$x_{n}$},y) - \psi(y) \leq 0. 
\]
This reasoning proves that there exists a $v \in A$ such that $\psi(v) = \alpha$. If $v$ is not a 
(unique) smallest element of $A$, then from $y \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} v$ for a $y \in A \setminus \{v\}$ it 
follows that
\[
 0 \leq q(y,v) \leq \psi(v) - \psi(y) \mbox{,}
\]
and we obtain $\psi(y) = \alpha $, $q(y,v) = 0$. Consequently, if  (\ref{con5}) is satisfied, then 
$x = v$ is the unique smallest element of $A$. Assume that $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ is Hausdorff. Let us consider 
$C = \{z \in A\colon   q(z,v) = 0\}$. It is a maximal chain satisfying the assumptions of Lemma \ref{Le4} 
for $\mbox{$\psi_{\alpha}$}$ in place of $X$ (see (\ref{con6})). The smallest element $x$ of $C$ is the unique smallest 
element of the maximal chain $A$. Conditions (ii), (iii) follow. Now, (\ref{con7}) yields $x \in B$ 
(otherwise $x$ would have another predecessor), i.e. (i) is satisfied. $\quad \square

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{Th6}
Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a uniform space, $q$ a $\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$-istance in $X$, while $q$ satisfies (\ref{con5}) or $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ is 
Hausdorff. Assume that a lower semicontinuous mapping $\mbox{$\psi\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$}$ is bounded 
below and  condition (\ref{con6}) holds. If a maximal chain $A \subset X$ (for $\mbox{$\preccurlyeq$}$ as in 
condition (\ref{con3})) contains a point $\mbox{$x_{0}$}$, then $A$ has a unique smallest element $x$ which in addition 
satisfies: 
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item $\psi(x) = \inf \{\psi(z)\colon z \in A\}$,
\item $\psi(x) + q(x,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$}) = \inf \{\psi(z) + q(z,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$})\colon z \in A \} \leq 0$,
\item $0 < \psi(y) + q(y,x) - \psi(x)$, $\quad \mbox{$y \in X \setminus \{x\}$}$,
\item $q(x,x)=0$, if $\ x \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} x$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- head-4: body_md -->
For any maximal chain $A$ containing $\mbox{$x_{0}$}$ we follow the proof of Theorem \ref{Th5}. If $x$ is the smallest 
element of $A$ and precedes itself, then the following holds
\[
 0 \leq q(x,x) = \psi(x) + q(x,x) - \psi(x) \leq 0.
\]
The last sentence of the proof of Theorem \ref{Th5} cannot be added, and we do not know if $x \in B$. $\quad \square

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{Th7}
Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a uniform space and $q\colon X \times X \to [0,\infty)$ a mapping satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item $q(z,x) \leq q(z,y) + q(y,x)$, $\quad \mbox{$x,y,z \in X$}$,
\item $q(x,y) = 0$ yields $x = y$, $\quad \mbox{$x,y \in X$}$ (Br\o ndsted assumes  equivalence)
\item for each $U \in \mathcal{U}$ there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that 
 $q^{-1}([0,\delta)) \subset U$,
\item $q(\cdot,x)$ is lower semicontinuous, $\quad x \in X$.
\end{enumerate}
Assume that a lower semicontinuous mapping $\mbox{$\psi\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$}$ is bounded below and  
$\{z \in X\colon \psi(z) \leq \alpha\}$ is complete for each $\alpha \in \mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}$. Then for any 
$\mbox{$x_{0}$} \in X$ there exists an $x \in X$ such that 
$\psi(x) + q(x,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$}) \leq q(\mbox{$x_{0}$},\mbox{$x_{0}$})$, and $0 < \psi(y) + q(y,x) - \psi(x)$, $\mbox{$y \in X \setminus \{x\}$}$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{Th8}
Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a uniform space, $q$ a $\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$-istance in $X$, with $q$ satisfying (\ref{con5}) or $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ being 
Hausdorff. Assume that a lower semicontinuous mapping $\mbox{$\psi\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$}$ is bounded 
below, condition (\ref{con6}) holds, $X \subset Y$ and $\mbox{$F\colon X \to 2^{Y}$}$ is a mapping satisfying 
(see (\ref{con3})):
\begin{eqnarray}
\label{con8}
 \mbox{for each } x \in X \mbox{ there exists a } y \in F(x) \mbox{ such that } y \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} x.
\end{eqnarray}
Then for any $\mbox{$x_{0}$} \in X$, each maximal chain $A \subset X$ containing $\mbox{$x_{0}$}$ (such a chain exists) 
has a unique smallest element $x$ which in addition satisfies conditions (i),(ii),(iii) of Theorem \ref{Th6}, 
$q(x,x) = 0$ and $x \in F(x)$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- head-5: body_md -->
In view of (\ref{con8}), Theorem \ref{Th6} here applies to nonempty chains. Now, condition (\ref{con8}) 
and (iii) mean $x \in F(x)$, and $q(x,x) = 0$. $\quad \square

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{Th9}
Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a uniform space, $q$ a $\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$-istance in $X$, with $q$ satisfying (\ref{con5}) or $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ being 
Hausdorff. Assume that a lower semicontinuous mapping $\mbox{$\psi\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$}$ is bounded 
below, condition (\ref{con6}) holds, $X \subset Y$, and $\mbox{$F\colon X \to 2^{Y}$}$ is a mapping satisfying 
(see (\ref{con3})):
\begin{eqnarray}
\label{con9}
 \mbox{for each } \mbox{$x \in X \setminus F(x)$} \mbox{ there exists a } \mbox{$y \in X \setminus \{x\}$}  \mbox{ such that } y \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} x.
\end{eqnarray}
Then $F$ has a fixed point.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- head-6: body_md -->
Suppose $F$ has no fixed point. Then Theorem \ref{Th6} applies ($A$ is nonempty) and (iii) contradicts 
(\ref{con9}). $\quad \square

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{Th10}
Let $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ be a uniform space, $q$ a $\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$-istance in $X$, with $q$ satisfying (\ref{con5}) or $\mbox{$(X,\mathcal{U})$}$ being 
Hausdorff. Assume that a lower semicontinuous mapping $\mbox{$\psi\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$}$ is bounded 
below and condition (\ref{con6}) holds. Then the following are satisfied:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item for each $\mbox{$x_{0}$} \in X$ there exists an $x \in X$ such that  
$\psi(x) \leq \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$})$ and $\psi(x) - q(y,x) < \psi(y)$, $\quad \mbox{$y \in X \setminus \{x\}$}$, 
\item for any $\epsilon > 0$ and each $\mbox{$x_{0}$} \in X$ with $q(\mbox{$x_{0}$},\mbox{$x_{0}$}) = 0$ and 
$\psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$}) \leq \epsilon + \inf\{\psi(z)\colon z \in X \}$ there exists an $x \in X$ such that 
$\psi(x) \leq \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$})$, $q(x,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) \leq 1$ and  
$\psi(x) - \epsilon q(y,x) < \psi(y)$, $\quad \mbox{$y \in X \setminus \{x\}$}$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- head-7: body_md -->
The reasoning based on our Theorem \ref{Th6} is similar to the one presented in the proof of 
\cite[Theorem 3]{Ka}. The set $Y = \{z \in X\colon \psi(z) \leq \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$})\}$ is complete. Suppose that 
for each $x \in Y$ there exists a $y \in Y \setminus \{x\}$ such that 
$\psi(y) + q(y,x) - \psi(x) \leq 0$. Then by Theorem \ref{Th6} (iii) there exists an $x \in Y$ 
such that $0 < \psi(y) + q(y,x) - \psi(x)$ - a contradiction, i.e. (i) is proved for $Y$ in place of 
$X$. Condition (i) is trivial for $y \in X \setminus Y$.
\par Now, let us consider $\epsilon q$ in place of $q$. From $\mbox{$x_{0}$} \mbox{$\preccurlyeq$} \mbox{$x_{0}$}$ and Theorem \ref{Th6} 
it follows that there exists an $x \in X$ such that 
$\psi(x) + \epsilon q(x,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$}) \leq 0$ and 
$0 < \psi(y) + \epsilon q(y,x) - \psi(x)$, $\mbox{$y \in X \setminus \{x\}$}$. From 
$\epsilon q(x,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) \leq \psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \psi(x)$ and the second assumption of (ii) we obtain
\[
 q(x,\mbox{$x_{0}$}) \leq [\psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \psi(x)]/\epsilon \leq [\psi(\mbox{$x_{0}$}) - \inf \{\psi(z)\colon z \in X\}]/\epsilon \leq 1.
 \quad \square
