<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
The set $X_{d,k}$ is a closed subvariety of $\operatorname{Gr}(V, d)$.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $\mathcal B$ be an ordered basis of $V$, and let $J$ be a $d$-tuple of distinct indices from $\{ 1, \dots, \dim V \}$. Let $M_J$ be the set of $d \times \dim V$ matrices whose columns indexed by $J$ form the identity matrix. The row spans of matrices in $M_J$ form an open affine subset $\mathcal O_J \subseteq \operatorname{Gr}(V, d)$. These open subsets give a chart for $\operatorname{Gr}(V, d)$ as $J$ varies. Let
\[
  Y_{d,k} = \{ (U, g_1, \dots, g_k) \in \operatorname{Gr}(V, d) \times G^k \mid \textstyle \sum_{1 \leq i \leq k} \rho(g_i) \cdot U = V \}.
\]
The set $Y_{d,k} \cap (\mathcal O_J \times G^k)$ corresponds to matrices $M_J$ with rows $m_1, \dots, m_d$ (where $m_i \in V$) for which the matrix with rows $\rho(g_i) \cdot m_j$ for $1 \leq i \leq k$ and $1 \leq j \leq d$ has full rank. This is an open condition, so $Y_{d,k}$ is an open subset of $\operatorname{Gr}(V, d) \times G^k$. Consider the projection $\operatorname{Gr}(V,d) \times G^k \to \operatorname{Gr}(V, d)$ onto the first component. This is an open map, so its image, consisting of subspaces $U \in \operatorname{Gr}(V, d)$ that cover $V$ with at most $k$ conjugates, is open in $\operatorname{Gr}(V, d)$. Its complement $X_{d,k}$ is then closed.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:optimal_SL_2}
Every irreducible representation of $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{C})$ exhibits optimal group-additive diameters.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $V = \mathbf{C}[X,Y]_k$ for some $k \geq 1$. For simplicity, let us further identify $\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_k$ with polynomials of degree at most $k$, denoted as $\mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k}$, under $X = x, Y = 1$. By \Cref{diameter bound reduction to Borel fixed subspaces}, it suffices to prove the claim for every upper closed subspace $U = \langle e_j, e_{j+1}, \dots, e_k \rangle$, which is, by the above identification, equal to $\langle x^j,x^{j+1},\dots,x^k\rangle$. For any $0 \leq i < d$ and $a \in \mathbf{C}$, we have
  \[
    \rho_k \begin{pmatrix}
      1 & 0 \\
      a & 1
    \end{pmatrix}
    \cdot x^{k - i} =(x + a)^{k - i}.
  \]
Let $d = \dim U = k - j + 1$. We can assume $d < k + 1$. Let $n= \lceil (k+1)/d \rceil$. We claim that for distinct $a_1, \dots, a_n \in \mathbf{C}$, we have
\[
\sum_{0 \leq \ell < n}
\left(
\rho_k\begin{pmatrix}
  1 & 0 \\
  a_\ell & 1
\end{pmatrix} \cdot U 
\right)
= V.
\]
This is equivalent to saying that the polynomials $(x+a_\ell)^{k-i}$ for $0 \leq \ell < n$ and $0 \leq i < d$ span $\mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k}$, which is in turn the same as
\[
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \left\langle \partial_x^i ( (x+a_\ell)^k ) \mid 0\leq \ell < n, \ 0\leq i < d \right\rangle
= \mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k},
\]  
where $\partial_x$ is the derivative operator. The latter holds by the following lemma.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:poly_basis}
Let $p \in \mathbf{C}[x]$ be of degree $k$. Let $d<k+1$ and $n=\lceil\frac{k+1}{d}\rceil$. Then
\[
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \left\langle \partial_x^i ( p(x+a_\ell) ) \mid 0\leq \ell < n, \ 0\leq i < d \right\rangle
= \mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k}
\] 
for any distinct $a_1, \dots, a_n \in \mathbf{C}$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Using the Taylor series, we can expand the polynomial $p(x+a)$ as
\[
p(x+a)=\sum_{0 \leq j \leq k} \frac{1}{j!} a^j \partial_x^j ( p(x) ).
\]
Hence
\[
\partial_x^i ( p(x+a) )   
= \partial_a^i ( p(x+a) ) 
= \sum_{0 \leq j \leq k} \partial_a^i ( a^j ) \cdot \frac{1}{j!} \partial_x^j ( p(x) ).
\]
We can therefore express $\partial_x^i( p(x+a) )$ in the basis $\partial_x^j( p(x) )/j!$ of $\mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k}$ with the matrix of coefficients
\[
  V_a = \left[ \partial_a^i (a^j) \right]_{0 \leq j < k, 0 \leq i < d} \in \operatorname{M}_{(k+1) \times d}(\mathbf{C}).
\]
Now, for $a_1, \dots, a_n \in \mathbf{C}$, the block matrix
\[
V = \left[V_{a_1}, \dots, V_{a_n}\right] \in \operatorname{M}_{(k+1) \times nd}(\mathbf{C})
\]
is exactly the confluent Vandermonde matrix corresponding to the Hermite interpolation problem (see \cite{kalman1984generalized}). Taking $a_\ell$ to be distinct, the first $k+1$ columns of $V$ form an invertible matrix.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{upper right block from diagonal entry}
  Let $U \in \operatorname{Gr}(\operatorname{M}_n(\mathbf{C}), d)$ be upper right block closed. Let $\delta = d - 3n^2/4 - n/2 + 1/4 > 0$. For every $k$ with $\abs{k - (n+1)/2} < \sqrt{\delta}$, we have $B_{ij} \leq U$ for some $i \neq j$ with $i > k > j$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
There are $(k-1)(n-k)$ elementary matrices $E_{ij}$ with $i \neq j$ and $i > k > j$. The condition $\abs{k - (n+1)/2} < \sqrt{\delta}$ is equivalent to $(k-1)(n-k) > n^2 - d$. Hence the space $U$ contains a matrix $u$ with $u_{ij} \neq 0$ for some $i \neq j$ with $i > k > j$. As $U$ is upper right block closed, we thus have $B_{ij} \leq U$.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{critical upper right closed subspaces}
Let $U \in \operatorname{Gr}(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), d)$ be upper right block closed with $d = (n-1)^2 + 1$. Then one of the following holds (see \Cref{figure: critical_upper_right_closed_subspaces}):
\[
  \mathfrak{b}_{\widehat{11}} \leq U; \qquad
  \mathfrak{b}_{\widehat{nn}} \leq U; \qquad
  U = B_{21} + B_{n3}; \qquad
  U = B_{n,n-1} + B_{n-2,1}.
\]
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $\delta = d - 3n^2/4 - n/2 + 1/4 = n^2/4 - 5n/2 + 9/4$. If $n > 10$, we have $\sqrt{\delta} > (n+1)/2 - 4$ and so it follows from the previous lemma that for any $k$ with $4 \leq k \leq n - 3$, we have $B_{ij} \leq U$ for some $i \neq j$ with $i > k > j$. In particular, $U$ contains $E_{ii} - E_{jj}$ for all $3 \leq i < j \leq n - 2$. It can be verified by hand or with a computer that the same conclusion holds for all $6 \leq n \leq 10$. Let us now inspect the boundary indices of matrices in $U$.

\begin{description}
    \item[$E_{31}, E_{n,n-3} \in U$] In this case, $U$ contains the whole diagonal in $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ and $\mathfrak{b} \leq U$.
    
    \item[$E_{31} \notin U$] In this case, every matrix $u \in U$ satisfies $u_{i1} = 0$ for $i \geq 3$. Let us inspect the second column.
    \begin{description}
        \item[$E_{32} \notin U$] Here, all matrices in $U$ have zeroes in entries $(i,j)$ for $i \geq 3$ and $j \leq 2$. There are a total of $2n-4$ of these entries. Since the codimension of $U$ in $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ is $2n - 3$, we can either miss exactly one more $E_{ij}$ ($i\neq j$) or the dimension of the intersection of $U$ with the traceless diagonal is $n-2$. In the first case, since $U$ is upper right block closed, we cannot miss any $E_{ij}$ with $i<j$, thus $\mathfrak{b} \leq U$. In the second case, we have $E_{21}, E_{n,3} \in U$, and so $E_{ii}-E_{jj} \in U$ for all $3\leq i<j\leq n$ as well as $E_{11}-E_{22} \in U$. Thus, $U=B_{21}+B_{n3}$.

        \item[$E_{32} \in U$] Inspect the last row.
        \begin{description}
            \item[$E_{n,n-1} \notin U$] If $E_{21} \in U$, then $U$ contains $B_{21}$, and so $\mathfrak{b}_{\widehat{nn}} \leq U$. On the other hand, if $E_{21} \notin U$, then, for codimension reasons, $\mathfrak{b} \leq U$.
            
            \item[$E_{n,n-1} \in U$] In this case, we must have, for codimension reasons, that $E_{n-1,n-2} \in U$. Hence $E_{ii} - E_{jj} \in U$ for all $2 \leq i < j \leq n$, and so $\mathfrak{b}_{\widehat{11}} \leq U$.
        \end{description}
    \end{description}

    \item[$E_{n,n-3} \notin U$] This case is symmetric to the previous one after flipping all elements of $U$ along the antidiagonal of the matrix. \qedhere
\end{description}

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:counterexample}
Let $U = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \langle E_{11}-E_{nn} \rangle + B_{n-1,2} \leq \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ (see \Cref{figure: counterexample diameter 3}). Then 
\[
\dim U = (n-1)^2 \quad \text{and} \quad 
\operatorname{diam}_+^{\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), U) = 3.
\]
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Suppose there is some $g \in \operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})$ with $U + g^{-1}U g = \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$. 
Note that $U$ is stable under the action of the Borel subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})$. By the Bruhat decomposition \cite{borel2012linear}, we can write $g\in G$ as $bwb'$ where $b,b'$ belong to the Borel subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})$ and $w$ is an element of the corresponding Weyl group, which we may identify here with the symmetric group $S_n$, or  permutation matrices. 
Then $U+(bwb')^{-1}Ubwb'=b'^{-1}(U+w^{-1}Uw)b'=\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ (using that $U$ is invariant for $b,b'$) and hence $U+w^{-1}Uw=\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$. 
Thus, we can view $g$ as a permutation $w \in S_n$.

Suppose first that $w(1) \neq n$. Then there exist matrices $A, B \in U$ such that
\[
    A + w^{-1} B w = E_{w^{-1}(n)1}.
\]
Hence we must have $w^{-1} E_{ij} w = E_{w^{-1}(n)1}$ for some $E_{ij} \in U$. This forces $j = w(1)$ and $w^{-1}(i) = w^{-1}(n)$, and so $i = n$, which is impossible since $E_{ij} \in U$.

Therefore, we must have $w(1) = n$. Now we can find, for any $2 \leq k \leq n$, matrices $A_k, B_k \in U$ such that
\[
    A_k + w^{-1}B_k\, w = E_{k1}.
\]
Repeating the argument from above, we must have $w^{-1} E_{i_k j_k} w = E_{k1}$ for some $E_{i_k j_k} \in U$, which forces $j_k = w(1) = n$ and $w^{-1}(i_k) = k$. Consequently, the set of indices $\{i_2, \dots, i_{n-1}\}$ must be equal to $\{ w(2), \dots, w(n-1)\}$. Note that as $E_{i_k j_k} \in U$, we must have $i_k \leq n-1$, and since $w(1) = n$, it follows that $\{i_2, \dots, i_{n-1}\} = \{ 2,\dots,n-1\}$. Therefore $w(n) = 1$. This means that the permutation $w$ swaps the first and the $n$-th positions, and so $w^{-1} U w$ is contained in the hyperplane $H = \{ X \in \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C}) \mid X_{11}-X_{nn}=0 \}$. As $U \leq H$, we must have $U + w^{-1} U w \leq H$, a contradiction.

Having shown no two conjugates suffice to cover $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$, we now exhibit three conjugates that do. Flip $U$ through the center of the matrix to obtain $U + FUF = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \langle E_{ij} \mid (i,j) \neq (1,1), (n,n) \rangle + \operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \langle E_{11} - E_{nn} \rangle$. Let $w$ be the permutation matrix corresponding to the long cycle $(1 \ 2 \dots n)$. Then $w^{-1} U w$ contains $w^{-1}(E_{11} - E_{nn})w = E_{nn} - E_{n-1,n-1}$. Hence $U + FUF + w^{-1}Uw = \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$.

<!-- theorem-11: body_md -->
\label{diam_+ <= 2 diam_+^G}
Let $G$ be a complex linear algebraic group. Let $f \colon W \to V$ be a $G$-equivariant algebraic morphism. Then, for any $w \in W$,
\[
  \operatorname{diam}_+(V, \operatorname{im} f) \leq 2 \cdot \operatorname{diam}^G_+(V, \operatorname{im} (D_w f)).
\]
<!-- end theorem-11 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Let $\lambda, \rho$ be the representations of $G$ on $W, V$. Let $g_1, \dots, g_k \in G$, and set $p = (\lambda(g_1) \cdot w, \dots, \lambda(g_k) \cdot w) \in W^k$. We have

      D_{p} f^{[k]} =
      D_{\lambda(g_1) \cdot w} f \oplus \dots \oplus D_{\lambda(g_k) \cdot w} f,
  \]
  and so its image $\operatorname{im}(D_{p} f^{[k]})$ is equal to
\[
    \operatorname{im}(D_{\lambda(g_1) \cdot w} f) + \dots + \operatorname{im}(D_{\lambda(g_k) \cdot w} f)

\]
by \Cref{lemma on images of derivatives}.
Taking $k = \operatorname{diam}^G_+(W, \operatorname{im} (D_w f))$, we can thus find $g_1, \dots, g_k \in G$ so that $\operatorname{im}(D_{p} f^{[k]}) = W$.

<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
Let $Z_1, \dots, Z_\ell$ be all the proper subrepresentations of $V$. \label{remark on Terracini lemma}
  This can be compared with Terracini's lemma \cite[Proposition 10.10]{eisenbud20163264} from classical algebraic geometry in the case when $\operatorname{im}(f)$ is a homogeneous variety. If a projective variety $X \subset \mathbf{P}(V)$ has the property that its $d$-th secant variety is the whole $\mathbf{P}(V)$, then the sum of the tangent spaces at $d$ general points of $X$ equals $\mathbf{P}(V)$. Our result can be seen as an equivariant analogue: if a homogeneous variety $X = \operatorname{im}(f)$ has finite additive diameter in $V$, then the sum of \emph{some} number of tangent spaces at suitable \emph{$G$-translates of a single point} also spans $V$.

<!-- example-9: body_md -->
\label{example sl2 reps twisted cubic}
  Let $G = \operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbf{C})$. Let $W = \mathbf{C}^2$ be the standard representation and let $V = \mathbf{C}[X,Y]_k$ be the irreducible representation of $G$ of degree $k + 1$. Let $f \colon W \to V$ be the corresponding $G$-equivariant morphism. Note that for any nonzero $w \in W$, the derivative $D_w f$ is of rank $2$, so $\operatorname{im}(D_w f)$ is $2$-dimensional
  and then $\operatorname{diam}_+^G(V,\operatorname{im}(D_wf))=\lceil (k+1)/2 \rceil$ by Theorem \ref{thm:optimal_SL_2}. Hence $\operatorname{diam}_+(V,\operatorname{im} f)\leq 2\lceil (k+1)/2 \rceil$ by the previous theorem. In particular, the case $k = 3$ gives that every element of $\mathbf{C}^4$ is a sum of $4$ elements from the twisted cubic.

Suppose that $\operatorname{SL}_n(\mathbf{C})$ acts on  $V=\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ by conjugation. Let $f \colon W \to \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ be an $\operatorname{SL}_n(\mathbf{C})$-equivariant morphism (for example a trace polynomial). Suppose that for some $w \in W$, we have
\[
\dim C_{\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})}(f(w)) < (1 - \epsilon) n^2 - 1
\]
for some $0 < \epsilon < 1/3$. By \Cref{images of derivative gathered results}, we know $\operatorname{im} (D_w f) \geq \operatorname{im} (D_I \rho) \cdot f(w) = \operatorname{im} (\operatorname{ad}_{f(w)})$. It follows from the assumption on the dimension of the centralizer that $\dim \operatorname{im} (\operatorname{ad}_{f(w)}) > \epsilon n^2$. It now follows from \Cref{epsilon n^2 proposition}, as long as $n \geq \max (9, 2/\epsilon)$ that
\[
  \operatorname{diam}_+^{\operatorname{SL}_n(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), \operatorname{im} (D_w f)) \leq \frac{3456}{\epsilon} \left\lceil \log_2 \frac{4}{\epsilon} \right\rceil,
\]
and so \Cref{diam_+ <= 2 diam_+^G} implies that 
\[
  \operatorname{diam}_+(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), \operatorname{im} f) \leq \frac{6912}{\epsilon} \left\lceil \log_2 \frac{4}{\epsilon} \right\rceil.
\]
In the extreme case, we could have $w$ with the property that $f(w)$ is regular semisimple (and so without loss of generality diagonal due to equivariance). In this situation, we have $\operatorname{im} (D_w f) \geq \operatorname{M}_n^0(\mathbf{C})$, and so, by \Cref{diam of sl wrt zero diagonal},
\[
  \operatorname{diam}_+(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), \operatorname{im} f) \leq 2 \cdot \operatorname{diam}_+^{\operatorname{SL}_n(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), \operatorname{M}_n^0(\mathbf{C})) \leq 4.
\]

Let $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})$ act on $\operatorname{M}_n(\mathbf{C})$ by conjugation. Let $f$ be a nonconstant noncommutative polynomial for which $\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \langle \operatorname{im} (f - f(0)) \rangle = \operatorname{M}_n(\mathbf{C})$.\footnote{This means that $\operatorname{im}(f)$ is not contained in an additive coset of the scalars or of $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$.} Assuming $n > (2\deg f)^2$, there is, by \cite[Corollary 2.10]{brevsar2024matrix}, an element $f(w)$ that is diagonal with distinct eigenvalues. As in the previous example, we obtain $\operatorname{im} (D_w f) \geq \operatorname{M}_n^0(\mathbf{C})$. It follows from \Cref{im f not in subrep implies finite diameter wrt derivative} that there is also an element $x \in \operatorname{im} (D_w f)$ with $x \notin \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$. Hence
\[
  \operatorname{diam}_+(\operatorname{M}_n(\mathbf{C}), \operatorname{im} f) \leq 2 \cdot \operatorname{diam}_+^{\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})}(\operatorname{M}_n(\mathbf{C}), \operatorname{M}_n^0(\mathbf{C}) + \operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \langle x \rangle) \leq 4.
\]
This bound should be compared with the results in \cite{brevsar2023waringISRAEL,brevsar2023waring,brevsar2024matrix}. In \cite{brevsar2023waring}, it was shown that for a noncommutative polynomial $f \colon \operatorname{M}_n(\mathbf{C})^d \to \operatorname{M}_n(\mathbf{C})$ of degree $\deg f \leq n + 1$, there exist scalars $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ such that $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}) \subseteq \lambda_1 \operatorname{im} f + \lambda_2 \operatorname{im} f + \lambda_3 \operatorname{im} f$. In other words,
\[
\operatorname{diam}^{\mathbf{C}^*}_+(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), \operatorname{im} f) \leq 3.
\]
Shortly thereafter, in \cite{brevsar2024matrix}, it was further established that this diameter can in fact be bounded by $2$. Moreover, the authors prove the stronger statement that $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}) \subseteq \operatorname{im} f - \operatorname{im} f$. We note that the methods in these papers differ considerably from those presented here and do not yield results on the finiteness of the diameter with respect to $\operatorname{im} f$ alone (that is, without incorporating scalars or taking differences).

<!-- example-12: body_md -->
Let $f \colon \mathbf{C}^2 \to \mathbf{C}^4$ be the polynomial map given by $f(x,y) = (x^3, x^2 y, x y^2, y^3)$. Its image $\operatorname{im}(f)$ is a subvariety of $\mathbf{C}^4$, whose projectivization in $\mathbf{P}^3$ is the twisted cubic $\mathcal C$. Its secant variety (the Zariski closure of the union of all lines between two points in $\mathcal C$) is the whole $\mathbf{P}^3$ \cite[Proposition 10.11]{eisenbud20163264}. Since $\operatorname{im}(f)$ is a homogeneous variety, every point on the line between two points on it is a sum of two elements in $\operatorname{im}(f)$. It follows that the Zariski closure of the sumset $\operatorname{im}(f) + \operatorname{im}(f)$ is $\mathbf{C}^4$, and so $\operatorname{diam}_+(\mathbf{C}^4, \operatorname{im}(f)) \leq 4$ by Borel's trick \cite[Chapter I, 1.3]{borel2012linear}. Alternatively, the same conclusion follows from \Cref{example sl2 reps twisted cubic}. The diameter is certainly not equal to $2$, since the point $(0,1,0,0) \in \mathbf{C}^4$ is not a sum of two elements in $\operatorname{im}(f)$. In order to deduce that the diameter is in fact equal to $3$, a bit more work is needed (see \cite[Example 3.10]{carlini2014four}).

Let $G = \operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{C})$ act by conjugation on $V = \operatorname{M}_2(\mathbf{C})$, and let $U = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \langle I, E_{12} \rangle$. Every conjugate of $U$ contains $I$, so the diameter is at least $3$ for dimension reasons. On the other hand, let $F$ be the permutation matrix that swaps the two standard basis vectors. Then $F U F^{-1} = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \langle I, E_{21} \rangle$, and let $E = I + E_{21}$. We have $E U E^{-1} = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}} \langle I, -E_{11} + E_{12} - E_{21} + E_{22} \rangle$. This shows that $U + F U F^{-1} + E U E^{-1} = \operatorname{M}_2(\mathbf{C})$. Therefore the $\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbf{C})$-additive diameter of $\operatorname{M}_2(\mathbf{C})$ with respect to $U$ is $3$, which is not optimal.

Let $G = \operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})$ act by conjugation on $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$. This is an irreducible representation. Let $\operatorname{M}^0_n(\mathbf{C})$ be the set of matrices with zero diagonal. Let us show that
\[
    \operatorname{diam}^{\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})}_+(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), \operatorname{M}^0_n(\mathbf{C})) = 2.
\]
Let $\operatorname{D}_n(\mathbf{C})$ be the set of diagonal matrices in $\operatorname{M}_n(\mathbf{C})$.
It suffices to find an element $g \in \operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})$ such that the map 
\[
\operatorname{M}^0_n(\mathbf{C}) \to \operatorname{D}_n(\mathbf{C}) \cap \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), \quad z \mapsto \operatorname{diag}(g^{-1} z g)
\]
is surjective. We take $g = \prod_{i = 1}^{n-1} (I + E_{i,i+1})$. Note that $g = [\delta_{i \leq j}]_{i,j=1}^n$, and we have $g^{-1} = I + [- \delta_{j = i + 1}]_{i, j = 1}^n$. The $(i,i)$-term of $g^{-1} z g$ is equal to $e_i^T g^{-1} z g e_i$, which computes to
\[
(g^{-T} e_i)^T z (g e_i) =
(e_i - e_{i+1}) z (e_1 + e_2 + \cdots + e_i) =
\sum_{1 \leq k \leq i} (z_{i,k} - z_{i+1,k}).
\]
Let $v_\ell \in \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ be the matrix whose $\ell$-th row is equal to $[\delta_{j < \ell}]_{j = 1}^n$, and all other entries are zero. Then $v_\ell \in \operatorname{M}^0_n(\mathbf{C})$ and the $(i,i)$-term of $g^{-1} v_{\ell} g$ is $-(\ell-1)$ if $i = \ell - 1$, $\ell-1$ if $i = \ell$, and $0$ otherwise. Thus $\operatorname{diag}(g^{-1} v_\ell g) = (\ell-1) (- e_{\ell-1} + e_\ell)$. Since the $- e_{\ell-1} + e_\ell$ span $\operatorname{D}_n(\mathbf{C}) \cap \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ as $\ell$ varies, the claim follows.

Let $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})$ act by conjugation on $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$. Let $U \leq \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$ be the subspace of matrices with zero last column and row. We then have
\[
  \dim U = (n-1)^2 - 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \operatorname{diam}_+^{\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), U) = 3.
\]
The argument goes as follows. Let $g \in \operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})$. We first claim that $g \notin U + gUg^{-1}$. Indeed, if $g = u + gvg^{-1}$ for $u,v \in U$, then $I = g^{-1}u + v g^{-1}$. Applying this to $e_n$, we obtain 
\[
  \langle e_n, e_n \rangle 
  = \langle (g^{-1}u + v g^{-1})e_n, e_n \rangle
  = \langle v g^{-1} e_n, e_n \rangle
  = \langle g^{-1} e_n, v^\star e_n \rangle = 0
\]
since $v \in U$. This is a contradiction, and so $\operatorname{diam}_+^{\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C}), U) \geq 3$. 

Now let $g = P_{1n}$ be the permutation matrix that exchanges $e_1$ and $e_n$, and let $h = P_{2n}$ be the permutation matrix that exchanges $e_2$ and $e_n$. Then $U + gUg^{-1}$ consists precisely of traceless matrices whose $(1,n)$ and $(n,1)$ entries are zero, and similarly $U + hUh^{-1}$ consists of traceless matrices whose $(2,n)$ and $(n,2)$ entries are zero. The sum of these two spaces is $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbf{C})$. This completes the proof.
