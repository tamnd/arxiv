\label{pb:1}
Does $M(t)^2/t$ (resp.\ $M(t)^2/\log t$) converge as $t\to\infty$ when $N=1$ (resp.\ $N=2$)? If so, what are the limits? In particular, are they determined by the zeroth moment $P=\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}u_1(x)\,\,d x$ alone, or do they depend on further properties of the initial data, such as the quantity $I_{0,N}$?

\label{pb:2}

<!-- head-1: body_md -->
We normalize the Fourier transform by
$$
\hat f(\xi) = \mathcal F_{x\to\xi}(f)(\xi) := \int_{\mathbb{R}^N}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}f(x)\,\,d x,
$$
so that Plancherel's identity reads
$$
\left\Vert f\right\Vert_{L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)}^2 = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^N} \left\Vert \hat f\right\Vert_{L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)}^2.
$$
For $s\in\mathbb{R}$, we define
$$
\left\Vert f\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^N)}^2 := \frac{1}{(2\pi)^N} \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} \left\vert \xi\right\vert^{-2s}\left\vert \hat f(\xi)\right\vert^2\,\,d\xi = \left\Vert \left\vert \nabla\right\vert^{-s}f\right\Vert_{L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)}^2 \in[0,+\infty],
$$
and we write $f\in\dot H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ when this quantity is finite {(see \cite[Chapter~1]{BCD2011} for homogeneous Sobolev spaces)}. We also set
$$
\sigma_{N-1} := \left\vert \mathbb{S}^{N-1}\right\vert = \frac{2\pi^{N/2}}{\Gamma(N/2)},
$$
with the convention $\sigma_0=2$. Finally, $\gamma$ denotes the Euler--Mascheroni constant, and $C>0$ denotes a harmless constant whose value may change from line to line.
{Several results below only require $u_0\in L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$. For $[u_0,u_1]\in L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)\times L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$, the solution of \eqref{eq:wave}--\eqref{eq:data} is understood as the function defined by the Fourier representation \eqref{eq:fourier-solution} below. It belongs to $C([0,\infty);L^2(\mathbb{R}^N))$ and coincides with the weak solution described above whenever $[u_0,u_1]\in H^1(\mathbb{R}^N)\times L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$.}

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{th:dichotomy}
Let $N\geq1$, let $[u_0,u_1]\in H^1(\mathbb{R}^N)\times L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$, and let $u$ be the solution of \eqref{eq:wave}--\eqref{eq:data}. Then
$$
\sup_{t\geq0}\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert<\infty \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad u_1\in\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N).
$$
If $u_1\in\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:equipartition}
\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2 = \frac12 \left( \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-1}}^2+\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert^2 \right).
\end{equation}
If $u_1\notin\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$, then
$$
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert\longrightarrow+\infty \qquad\text{as }t\to\infty.
$$

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{th:profiles}
Let $N\ge1$ and $0\le s<N/2$, and let $u$ solve \eqref{eq:wave}--\eqref{eq:data} with
\[
u_1\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)\cap L^2(\mathbb{R}^N),\qquad u_0\in L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)\cap\dot H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^N).
\]
Put $P=\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}u_1\,d x$ and $\theta=2+2s-N$. Then the following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] {(Polynomial regime, $\theta>0$.)}
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\ t^{-\theta}\,\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2
=\frac{\kappa_{N,s}}{(2\pi)^N}\,P^2 .
\]
\item[(ii)] {(Logarithmic regime, $\theta=0$.)}
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\ (\log t)^{-1}\,\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2
=\frac{\sigma_{N-1}}{2\,(2\pi)^N}\,P^2 .
\]
\item[(iii)] {(Convergent regime, $\theta<0$.)} {The velocity $u_1$ automatically belongs to $\dot H^{-s-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$}, and
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2
=\frac12\Bigl(\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s-1}}^2+\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2\Bigr).
\]
\end{enumerate}

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:L2}
Let $N\geq1$, let $u_0\in L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$ and $u_1\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)\cap L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$, and set $P:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}u_1(x)\,\,d x$. Then the following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] If $N=1$, then
$$
\lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2}{t} = \frac{P^2}{2}.
$$
\item[(ii)] If $N=2$, then
$$
\lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2}{\log t} = \frac{P^2}{4\pi}.
$$
\item[(iii)] If $N\geq3$, then $u_1\in\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ automatically, and
$$
\lim_{t\to\infty} \left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2 = \frac12 \left( \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-1}}^2+\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert^2 \right).
$$
\end{enumerate}

\label{th:secondorder1}
Let $N=1$, $u_1\in {L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})}\cap L^2(\mathbb{R})$ and $u_0\in L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^2(\mathbb{R})$. Set $P=\int_\mathbb{R} u_1\,d x$ and $Q=\int_\mathbb{R} u_0\,d x$. Then, as $t\to\infty$,

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{th:secondorder2}
Let $N\ge2$, $s_c=(N-2)/2$, $u_1\in L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^N)\cap L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$ and $u_0\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)\cap L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$. Let

\qquad r>0,\qquad\text{so that}\quad g(0^+)=\sigma_{N-1}P^2 .
\end{equation}
Then, as $t\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:secondorder2}
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2_{\dot H^{-s_c}}

\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2_{L^2(\mathbb{R}^2)}

\end{equation}

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{th:LED}
Let $N=2$, $R>0$, and let $[u_0,u_1]\in H^1(\mathbb{R}^2)\times L^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$ satisfy {$u_1\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^2)$} and

Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LEDsharp}
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\ \frac{t\,E_R(t)}{\sqrt{\log t}}\ \le\
\frac{\left\vert P\right\vert}{2}\sqrt{\frac{E(0)}{2\pi}},
\end{equation}
and the right-hand side does not depend on $R$. If, moreover, $u_1\in\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ (for instance, if $u_1\in L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ and $P=0$), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LEDbounded}
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\ t\,E_R(t)\ \le\ K_0
+\frac{\sqrt{E(0)}}{2}\Bigl(\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-1}}^2+\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert^2\Bigr)^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $K_0$ is the constant defined in \eqref{eq:K0} below.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:RL}
Let $F\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$. Then
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F(\xi)e^{i\lambda\left\vert \xi\right\vert}\,\,d\xi \longrightarrow0 \qquad\text{as }\left\vert \lambda\right\vert\to\infty.
$$
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
By polar coordinates, we have
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F(\xi)e^{i\lambda\left\vert \xi\right\vert}\,\,d\xi = \int_0^\infty G(r)e^{i\lambda r}\,\,d r,
\qquad
G(r) := r^{N-1} \int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}F(r\omega)\,\,d\omega.
$$
Moreover, the triangle inequality and the polar-coordinate formula give
$$
\int_0^\infty\left\vert G(r)\right\vert\,\,d r \leq \int_0^\infty r^{N-1} \int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}} \left\vert F(r\omega)\right\vert\,\,d\omega\,\,d r = \left\Vert F\right\Vert_{L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)}.
$$
Hence $G\in L^1(0,\infty)$, and the conclusion follows from the classical Riemann--Lebesgue lemma on the half-line {(see, e.g., \cite[Chapter~I]{SteinWeiss1971})}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:dirichlet}
Let $h:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ be measurable and such that
\[
h(0^+):=\lim_{\xi\to0^+}h(\xi)\ \text{ exists},\qquad
\frac{h(\xi)-h(0^+)}{\xi}\in L^1(0,1),\qquad
\frac{h(\xi)}{\xi}\in L^1(1,\infty).
\]
Then
$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(\lambda\xi)}{\xi}h(\xi)\,d\xi
\longrightarrow\frac{\pi}{2}h(0^+)$ as $\lambda\to+\infty$.

We split the integral at $\xi=1$. Since $h(\xi)/\xi\in L^1(1,\infty)$, the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma gives
$$
\int_1^\infty \frac{\sin(\lambda\xi)}{\xi}h(\xi)\,\,d\xi \longrightarrow0.
$$
On $(0,1)$, we write
$$
\int_0^1 \frac{\sin(\lambda\xi)}{\xi}h(\xi)\,\,d\xi = h(0^+) \int_0^1\frac{\sin(\lambda\xi)}{\xi}\,\,d\xi+ \int_0^1 \sin(\lambda\xi) \frac{h(\xi)-h(0^+)}{\xi}\,\,d\xi.
$$
After the change of variables $s=\lambda\xi$, the first integral becomes
$$

$$
The second integral tends to zero by the {one-dimensional} Riemann--Lebesgue lemma, since $(h(\xi)-h(0^+))/\xi\in L^1(0,1)$. Combining the three contributions, we obtain the result.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:log}
Let $g:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be bounded {and measurable}, with $g(0^+):=\lim_{r\to0^+}g(r)$ existing and $\int_1^\infty g(r)r^{-1}\,d r<\infty$. Then
\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin^2(tr)}{r}\,g(r)\,d r
=\frac{g(0^+)}{2}\,\log t+o(\log t),\qquad t\to\infty.
\]
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The contribution of $(1,\infty)$ is bounded by $\int_1^\infty g(r)r^{-1}\,d r$, hence it is $O(1)$. On $(0,1)$, we substitute $s=tr$ and use $\sin^2 s=\tfrac12(1-\cos2s)$. For $t\ge1$, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sinc-log}
\int_0^1\frac{\sin^2(tr)}{r}\,d r=\int_0^t\frac{\sin^2 s}{s}\,d s
=\frac12\log t+O(1),
\end{equation}
because $\int_0^1\frac{1-\cos 2s}{2s}\,d s<\infty$ and $\int_1^t\frac{\cos 2s}{2s}\,d s$ converges as $t\to\infty$. Let $\varepsilon>0$, and choose $\delta\in(0,1)$ such that $\left\vert g(r)-g(0^+)\right\vert\le\varepsilon$ on $(0,\delta)$. Using \eqref{eq:sinc-log}, we obtain
\[
\Bigl\lvert\int_0^1\frac{\sin^2(tr)}{r}\bigl(g(r)-g(0^+)\bigr)\,d r\Bigr\rvert
\le\varepsilon\Bigl(\frac12\log t+C\Bigr)
+\bigl(\left\Vert g\right\Vert_\infty+\left\vert g(0^+)\right\vert\bigr)\log\frac1\delta .
\]
Dividing by $\log t$ and letting first $t\to\infty$ and then $\varepsilon\to0$, we obtain the claim.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:log2}
Let $g:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be bounded and measurable, with $g(0^+)$ existing, $\int_1^\infty g(r)r^{-1}\,d r<\infty$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dini}
\frac{g(r)-g(0^+)}{r}\in L^1(0,1).
\end{equation}
Then, as $t\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log2}
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin^2(tr)}{r}\,g(r)\,d r
=\frac{g(0^+)}{2}\log t+\frac{g(0^+)}{2}\log 2
+\frac12\int_0^\infty\frac{g(r)-g(0^+)e^{-r}}{r}\,d r+o(1),
\end{equation}
the last integral being absolutely convergent.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We write $\sin^2(tr)=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\cos(2tr)\bigr)$, so that
\[
2\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin^2(tr)}{r}g(r)\,d r
=\int_0^\infty\frac{1-\cos(2tr)}{r}\,g(r)\,d r
=:\mathcal I_1(t)+\mathcal I_2(t),
\]
where, with $a:=g(0^+)$,
\[
\mathcal I_1(t):=a\int_0^\infty\frac{1-\cos(2tr)}{r}e^{-r}\,d r,
\qquad
\mathcal I_2(t):=\int_0^\infty\frac{1-\cos(2tr)}{r}
\bigl(g(r)-ae^{-r}\bigr)\,d r .
\]
For $\mathcal I_1$, we use the classical formula, valid for every $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frullani}
\int_0^\infty\frac{1-\cos(\lambda r)}{r}e^{-r}\,d r
=\tfrac12\log\bigl(1+\lambda^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
It follows by taking real parts in the Frullani identity $\int_0^\infty r^{-1}\bigl(e^{-r}-e^{-(1-i\lambda)r}\bigr)\,d r=\log(1-i\lambda)$ {(alternatively, both sides of \eqref{eq:frullani} vanish at $\lambda=0$ and have the same derivative $\lambda/(1+\lambda^2)$)}. With $\lambda=2t$, this gives
\[
\mathcal I_1(t)=\frac a2\log\bigl(1+4t^2\bigr)
=a\log t+a\log2+o(1),\qquad t\to\infty .
\]
For $\mathcal I_2$, we set $\Psi(r):=r^{-1}\bigl(g(r)-ae^{-r}\bigr)$. Near the origin, $g(r)-ae^{-r}=\bigl(g(r)-a\bigr)+a\bigl(1-e^{-r}\bigr)$, so that \eqref{eq:dini} and $\left\vert 1-e^{-r}\right\vert\le r$ give $\Psi\in L^1(0,1)$. On $(1,\infty)$, we have $\left\vert \Psi(r)\right\vert\le g(r)r^{-1}+\left\vert a\right\vert e^{-r}r^{-1}$, which is integrable. Hence $\Psi\in L^1(0,\infty)$ and, by the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma,
\[
\mathcal I_2(t)=\int_0^\infty\Psi(r)\,d r
-\int_0^\infty\Psi(r)\cos(2tr)\,d r
=\int_0^\infty\Psi(r)\,d r+o(1).
\]
Adding the two contributions and dividing by $2$, we obtain \eqref{eq:log2}.

\rm[Normalization of the constant]\label{rem:normalization}
The reference profile $e^{-r}$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:log2} is not canonical. It may be replaced by any measurable function $\varphi$ satisfying
$$
\varphi(0^+)=1, \qquad \frac{\varphi(r)-1}{r}\in L^1(0,1), \qquad \frac{\varphi(r)}{r}\in L^1(1,\infty).
$$
The two constant terms in \eqref{eq:log2} then change separately, but their sum remains the same. We use $\varphi(r)=e^{-r}$ because the corresponding reference integral \eqref{eq:frullani} is explicit.

We use the Fourier representation \eqref{eq:fourier-solution} with $s=0$ and abbreviate $\mathcal A=\mathcal A_0$, $\mathcal B=\mathcal B_0$ and $\mathcal D=\mathcal D_0$.

\smallskip
\noindent{Step 1: Sufficiency and the limit \eqref{eq:equipartition}.} Assume that $u_1\in\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$, and set
$$
F_1(\xi):= \frac{\left\vert \hat u_1(\xi)\right\vert^2}{\left\vert \xi\right\vert^2}, \qquad F_0(\xi):= \left\vert \hat u_0(\xi)\right\vert^2.
$$
Then $F_1,F_0\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$. Moreover, the inequality $2ab\le a^2+b^2$ gives
$$
\frac{ \left\vert \hat u_1(\xi)\right\vert \left\vert \hat u_0(\xi)\right\vert }{ \left\vert \xi\right\vert } \leq \frac12\left(F_1(\xi)+F_0(\xi)\right),
$$
and hence $\hat u_1(\xi)\overline{\hat u_0(\xi)}/\left\vert \xi\right\vert\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$. Using $\sin^2(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert) = \frac12\left(1-\cos(2t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\right)$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:RL}, we obtain
$$
\mathcal A(t) = \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_1(\xi)\,\,d\xi - \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^N} F_1(\xi)\cos(2t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\,\,d\xi\longrightarrow \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_1(\xi)\,\,d\xi.
$$
Similarly, since $\cos^2(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert) = \frac12\left(1+\cos(2t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\right)$, we get
$$
\mathcal B(t) \longrightarrow \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_0(\xi)\,\,d\xi.
$$
Another application of Lemma~\ref{lem:RL} gives $\mathcal D(t)\to0$. Dividing \eqref{eq:expansion} by $(2\pi)^N$ and using Plancherel's identity, we conclude that
$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2 = \frac12\left( \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-1}}^2+\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert^2 \right),
$$
which is \eqref{eq:equipartition}. The uniform bound can also be read off directly from \eqref{eq:fourier-solution}. Indeed, since $\left\vert \sin(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\right\vert\leq1$ and $\left\vert \cos(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\right\vert\leq1$, we have
$$
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert \leq \left\Vert  \left\vert \nabla\right\vert^{-1}\sin(t\left\vert \nabla\right\vert)u_1 \right\Vert + \left\Vert  \cos(t\left\vert \nabla\right\vert)u_0 \right\Vert \leq \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-1}}+\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert,
$$
and therefore $\sup_{t\geq0}\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert<\infty$.

\smallskip
\noindent{Step 2: Necessity.} Assume that $u_1\notin\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$, so that
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_1(\xi)\,\,d\xi=+\infty, \qquad F_1(\xi):= \frac{\left\vert \hat u_1(\xi)\right\vert^2}{\left\vert \xi\right\vert^2}.
$$
We apply \eqref{eq:ab} pointwise to \eqref{eq:fourier-solution}, with
$$
a= \frac{\sin(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)}{\left\vert \xi\right\vert}\hat u_1(\xi), \qquad b= \cos(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\hat u_0(\xi),
$$
and we integrate over $\mathbb{R}^N$. We obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-crude}
(2\pi)^N\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2 \geq \frac12\mathcal A(t)-\left\Vert \hat u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2.
\end{equation}
For $m>0$, we define $F_1^{(m)}(\xi) := \min\{F_1(\xi),m\} \mathbf 1_{\{\left\vert \xi\right\vert\leq m\}}$. Then $F_1^{(m)}\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$, $0\leq F_1^{(m)}\leq F_1$, and $F_1^{(m)}\uparrow F_1$ almost everywhere as $m\to\infty$. The monotone convergence theorem therefore gives
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_1^{(m)}(\xi)\,\,d\xi \longrightarrow+\infty \qquad\text{as }m\to\infty.
$$
Since $F_1\geq F_1^{(m)}\geq0$, we have
$$
\mathcal A(t) \geq \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} \sin^2(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)F_1^{(m)}(\xi)\,\,d\xi.
$$
For each fixed $m>0$, the identity $\sin^2(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert) = \frac12\left(1-\cos(2t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\right)$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:RL} imply that
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb{R}^N} \sin^2(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)F_1^{(m)}(\xi)\,\,d\xi &= \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_1^{(m)}(\xi)\,\,d\xi- \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^N} F_1^{(m)}(\xi)\cos(2t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\,\,d\xi\\
&\longrightarrow \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_1^{(m)}(\xi)\,\,d\xi
\end{aligned}
$$
as $t\to\infty$. Consequently,
$$
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal A(t) \geq \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_1^{(m)}(\xi)\,\,d\xi
$$
for every $m>0$. Letting $m\to\infty$, we deduce that $\mathcal A(t)\to+\infty$ as $t\to\infty$. It now follows from \eqref{eq:lower-crude} that
$$
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert\longrightarrow+\infty \qquad\text{as }t\to\infty.
$$
This proves both the necessity of the condition $u_1\in\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ and the divergence in the complementary case.

\rm\label{rem:P-neq-0}
Suppose that $u_1\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)\cap L^2(\mathbb{R}^N)$. Then $\hat u_1$ is continuous and $\hat u_1(0)=P$. If $P\neq0$, continuity implies that $\left\vert \hat u_1(\xi)\right\vert$ is bounded away from zero in a neighborhood of the origin. When $N\leq2$, it follows that
$$
\int_{\left\vert \xi\right\vert\leq\rho} \frac{\left\vert \hat u_1(\xi)\right\vert^2}{\left\vert \xi\right\vert^2}\,\,d\xi = +\infty
$$
for every {$\rho>0$}. Thus $u_1\notin\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ and, by Theorem~\ref{th:dichotomy}, $M(t)\to+\infty$ as $t\to\infty$. For $N\geq3$, on the contrary, $L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)\cap L^2(\mathbb{R}^N) \subset\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$, and hence $M(t)$ is uniformly bounded. This recovers the qualitative dimensional dichotomy underlying \cite[Theorems~1.1--1.2]{Ikehata2023}, without the weighted first-moment condition $u_1\in L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\rm[No universal divergence rate]\label{rem:norate}
Theorem~\ref{th:dichotomy} asserts that $M(t)\to+\infty$ whenever $u_1\notin\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$, but it gives no quantitative rate. No universal rate is possible under the energy-space assumptions alone, because the divergence is determined by the precise behavior of $\hat u_1(\xi)$ near $\xi=0$. In dimension one, a profile $\left\vert \hat u_1(\xi)\right\vert\approx\left\vert \xi\right\vert^{\alpha}$ near the origin with $0\leq\alpha<1/2$ produces $M(t)\asymp t^{1/2-\alpha}$, while the critical order $\alpha=1/2$ may produce logarithmic growth. Slowly varying corrections allow divergence slower than any positive power; see Problem~\ref{pb:RV}. Thus membership in $\dot H^{-1}$ decides between boundedness and divergence, but not the rate. Under the additional hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{th:profiles}, the condition $P\neq0$ determines the exact first-order growth.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:kappa}
Let $N\ge1$ and $\tfrac{N-2}{2}<s<\tfrac N2$, and set $\mu:=3+2s-N\in(1,3)$. Then the integral
\[
\kappa_{N,s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}\frac{\sin^2\left\vert \eta\right\vert}{\left\vert \eta\right\vert^{2+2s}}\,d\eta
\]
in \eqref{eq:kappa} converges, and
\[
\kappa_{N,s}=\sigma_{N-1}\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin^2r}{r^{\mu}}\,d r
=-\frac{2^{\mu-3}\pi\,\sigma_{N-1}}{\Gamma(\mu)\cos(\pi\mu/2)}
=\frac{2^{\mu-3}\pi\,\sigma_{N-1}}{\Gamma(\mu)\left\vert \cos(\pi\mu/2)\right\vert} .
\]
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
By polar coordinates and the identity $N-3-2s=-\mu$, we have
$$
\kappa_{N,s} = \sigma_{N-1}\int_{0}^{\infty} \sin^{2}r\,r^{N-3-2s}\,\,d r = \sigma_{N-1}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin^{2}r}{r^{\mu}}\,\,d r.
$$
As $r\to0$, the estimate $\sin^{2}r=O(r^{2})$ gives $r^{-\mu}\sin^{2}r=O(r^{2-\mu})$. Since $\mu<3$, we have $2-\mu>-1$, and the integral converges near $r=0$. On the other hand, $0\leq r^{-\mu}\sin^{2}r\leq r^{-\mu}$, and since $\mu>1$, the integral also converges at infinity. Using $\sin^{2}r=\frac12(1-\cos(2r))$ and the change of variables $y=2r$, we obtain
$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^{2}r}{r^{\mu}}\,\,d r = \frac12\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{1-\cos(2r)}{r^{\mu}}\,\,d r = 2^{\mu-2}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{1-\cos y}{y^{\mu}}\,\,d y.
$$
For $-2<\nu<0$, the classical identity
$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{\nu-1}(\cos y-1)\,\,d y = \Gamma(\nu)\cos\left(\frac{\pi\nu}{2}\right)
$$
holds, the right-hand side being understood by continuous extension at $\nu=-1$ {(it follows from the formula $\int_0^\infty y^{\nu-1}\cos y\,\,d y=\Gamma(\nu)\cos(\pi\nu/2)$, $0<\nu<1$, by analytic continuation; see, e.g., \cite[Section~3.76]{GR2007})}. Taking $\nu=1-\mu\in(-2,0)$, we get
$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos y}{y^{\mu}}\,\,d y = -\Gamma(1-\mu) \cos\left(\frac{\pi(1-\mu)}{2}\right).
$$
Since $\cos\left(\frac{\pi(1-\mu)}{2}\right) =\sin\left(\frac{\pi\mu}{2}\right)$, the reflection formula $\Gamma(\mu)\Gamma(1-\mu) = \pi/\sin(\pi\mu)$ yields, for $\mu\neq2$,
$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos y}{y^{\mu}}\,\,d y = -\frac{\pi\sin(\pi\mu/2)} {\Gamma(\mu)\sin(\pi\mu)} = -\frac{\pi} {2\Gamma(\mu)\cos(\pi\mu/2)}.
$$
Both sides extend continuously to $\mu=2$, where $\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{-2}(1-\cos y)\,\,d y = \pi/2$. Thus the identity holds for every $\mu\in(1,3)$, and we deduce that
$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^{2}r}{r^{\mu}}\,\,d r = -\frac{2^{\mu-3}\pi} {\Gamma(\mu)\cos(\pi\mu/2)}.
$$
Multiplying by $\sigma_{N-1}$, we obtain the first closed form of $\kappa_{N,s}$. Finally, since $\mu\in(1,3)$, we have $\frac{\pi\mu}{2}\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2})$, and hence $\cos(\pi\mu/2)<0$. This gives the second closed form and completes the proof.

Since $u_1\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$, its Fourier transform $\widehat u_1$ is bounded and continuous. Moreover, $\left|\widehat u_1(\xi)\right| \leq \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}$ for every $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^N$, and $\widehat u_1(0) = P$.

\smallskip
\noindent{Step 1: The term $\mathcal B_s$ is bounded.} Since $0\leq s<\frac{N}{2}$ and $u_0\in\dot H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^N)$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:B-bounded}
0 \leq \mathcal B_s(t) \leq \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} |\xi|^{-2s} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi = (2\pi)^N \left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2 < \infty
\end{equation}
for every $t\geq0$. In particular, $\mathcal B_s(t)=O(1)$ as $t\to\infty$.

If one assumes in addition that $u_0\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$, then the condition $u_0\in\dot H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ is automatic. Indeed, we have
$$
\int_{|\xi|\leq1} |\xi|^{-2s} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi \leq \left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{L^1}^2 \int_{|\xi|\leq1}|\xi|^{-2s}\,\,d\xi < \infty,
$$
because $2s<N$, whereas
$$
\int_{|\xi|\geq1} |\xi|^{-2s} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi \leq \left\Vert \widehat u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 < \infty.
$$

\smallskip
\noindent{Step 2: The polynomial regime $\theta>0$.} The condition $\theta>0$ is equivalent to $\frac{N-2}{2}<s<\frac{N}{2}$. The change of variables $\xi=\eta/t$ in \eqref{eq:A} gives, for every $t>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scaling}
\mathcal A_s(t) = t^\theta \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} \frac{\sin^2|\eta|}{|\eta|^{2+2s}} \left| \widehat u_1\left(\frac{\eta}{t}\right) \right|^2 \,\,d\eta .
\end{equation}
Indeed, the scaling factor is $t^{2+2s-N}=t^\theta$. For every $\eta\in\mathbb{R}^N$ and $t>0$, we have $\left| \widehat u_1(\eta/t) \right|^2 \leq \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}^2$. {Therefore, the integrand in \eqref{eq:scaling} is dominated by $\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}^2\left\vert \eta\right\vert^{-2-2s}\sin^2\left\vert \eta\right\vert$, uniformly in $t>0$.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:kappa}, this dominating function belongs to $L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$ in the range $\frac{N-2}{2}<s<\frac{N}{2}$. For every fixed $\eta\in\mathbb{R}^N$, the continuity of $\widehat u_1$ at the origin gives $\widehat u_1(\eta/t) \to \widehat u_1(0) = P$ as $t\to\infty$, and consequently $\left| \widehat u_1(\eta/t) \right|^2 \to |P|^2$. The dominated convergence theorem therefore yields
$$
t^{-\theta}\mathcal A_s(t) \longrightarrow |P|^2 \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} \frac{\sin^2|\eta|}{|\eta|^{2+2s}} \,\,d\eta = \kappa_{N,s}|P|^2,
$$
that is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A-poly}
\mathcal A_s(t) = \kappa_{N,s}|P|^2t^\theta + o(t^\theta).
\end{equation}
By Step~1, $\mathcal B_s(t)=O(1)=o(t^\theta)$. Moreover, the Cauchy--Schwarz estimate \eqref{eq:cross-CS} gives
$$
|\mathcal D_s(t)| \leq {2}\,\mathcal A_s(t)^{1/2} \mathcal B_s(t)^{1/2}.
$$
Since $\mathcal A_s(t)=O(t^\theta)$ and $\mathcal B_s(t)=O(1)$, we obtain $\mathcal D_s(t) = O(t^{\theta/2}) = o(t^\theta)$. Substituting these estimates into \eqref{eq:expansion}, we conclude that
$$
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2 = \frac{\kappa_{N,s}}{(2\pi)^N} |P|^2t^\theta + o(t^\theta),
$$
which is equivalent to part~{\rm (i)}.

\smallskip
\noindent{Step 3: The logarithmic regime $\theta=0$.} In this case, $s=s_c=\frac{N-2}{2}$. Since $s\geq0$, this regime occurs only when $N\geq2$. Recall that
$$
g(r) = \int_{\mathbb S^{N-1}} |\widehat u_1(r\omega)|^2 \,\,d\omega.
$$
Passing to polar coordinates in \eqref{eq:A}, we obtain
$$
\mathcal A_{s_c}(t) = \int_0^\infty \sin^2(tr)\, r^{N-1-(2s_c+2)} g(r)\,\,d r.
$$
Since $N-1-(2s_c+2)=-1$, it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A-polar}
\mathcal A_{s_c}(t) = \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin^2(tr)}{r} g(r)\,\,d r.
\end{equation}
The function $g$ is measurable and bounded, since $0\leq g(r) \leq \sigma_{N-1}\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}^2$. Furthermore, $\widehat u_1(r\omega) \to P$ as $r\downarrow0$ for every $\omega\in\mathbb S^{N-1}$. By dominated convergence on $\mathbb S^{N-1}$, we get $g(r)\to\sigma_{N-1}|P|^2$, that is, $g(0^+)=\sigma_{N-1}|P|^2$. Since $N\geq2$, we have $r^{-1}\leq r^{N-1}$ for $r\geq1$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:g-tail}
\int_1^\infty\frac{g(r)}{r}\,\,d r \leq \int_1^\infty g(r)r^{N-1}\,\,d r \leq \int_0^\infty g(r)r^{N-1}\,\,d r = \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} |\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi = \left\Vert \widehat u_1\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 < \infty.
\end{equation}
All the assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:log} are therefore satisfied. Applying that lemma to \eqref{eq:A-polar}, we obtain
$$
\mathcal A_{s_c}(t) = \frac12g(0^+)\log t + o(\log t) = \frac{\sigma_{N-1}}{2} |P|^2\log t + o(\log t).
$$
By Step~1, $\mathcal B_{s_c}(t) = O(1) = o(\log t)$. Moreover, \eqref{eq:cross-CS} implies that
$$
|\mathcal D_{s_c}(t)| \leq {2}\,\mathcal A_{s_c}(t)^{1/2} \mathcal B_{s_c}(t)^{1/2} = O\bigl(\sqrt{\log t}\bigr) = o(\log t).
$$
Using \eqref{eq:expansion}, we conclude that
$$
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s_c}}^2 = \frac{\sigma_{N-1}}{2(2\pi)^N} |P|^2\log t + o(\log t),
$$
which proves part~{\rm (ii)}.

\smallskip
\noindent{Step 4: The convergent regime $\theta<0$.} The condition $\theta<0$ is equivalent to $2s+2<N$. Using the boundedness of $\widehat u_1$, we obtain
$$
\int_{|\xi|\leq1} \frac{|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2}{|\xi|^{2s+2}} \,\,d\xi \leq \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}^2 \int_{|\xi|\leq1} |\xi|^{-2s-2} \,\,d\xi < \infty,
$$
because $2s+2<N$. On the other hand, since $|\xi|^{-2s-2}\leq1$ for $|\xi|\geq1$, we have
$$
\int_{|\xi|\geq1} \frac{|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2}{|\xi|^{2s+2}} \,\,d\xi \leq \int_{|\xi|\geq1} |\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi \leq \left\Vert \widehat u_1\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 < \infty.
$$
It follows that $u_1\in\dot H^{-s-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$, while $u_0\in\dot H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ by assumption. Using $\sin^2(t|\xi|) = \frac12 \left( 1-\cos(2t|\xi|) \right)$, we obtain
$$
\mathcal A_s(t) = \frac12 \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} |\xi|^{-2s-2} |\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi - \frac12 \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} |\xi|^{-2s-2} |\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2 \cos(2t|\xi|) \,\,d\xi.
$$
Similarly, using $\cos^2(t|\xi|) = \frac12 \left( 1+\cos(2t|\xi|) \right)$, we get
$$
\mathcal B_s(t) = \frac12 \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} |\xi|^{-2s} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi + \frac12 \int_{\mathbb{R}^N} |\xi|^{-2s} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 \cos(2t|\xi|) \,\,d\xi.
$$
The functions $F_1(\xi) := |\xi|^{-2s-2} |\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2$ and $F_0(\xi) := |\xi|^{-2s} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2$ belong to $L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$. Therefore, Lemma~\ref{lem:RL} yields
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^N} F_1(\xi)\cos(2t|\xi|) \,\,d\xi \longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\int_{\mathbb{R}^N} F_0(\xi)\cos(2t|\xi|) \,\,d\xi \longrightarrow0.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\mathcal A_s(t) \longrightarrow \frac{(2\pi)^N}{2} \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s-1}}^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\mathcal B_s(t) \longrightarrow \frac{(2\pi)^N}{2} \left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2.
$$
For the mixed term, we define $F_{01}(\xi) := |\xi|^{-2s-1} \operatorname{Re} \left( \widehat u_1(\xi) \overline{\widehat u_0(\xi)} \right)$. By Young's inequality, we have
$$
|F_{01}(\xi)| \leq |\xi|^{-2s-1} |\widehat u_1(\xi)| |\widehat u_0(\xi)| \leq \frac12 |\xi|^{-2s-2} |\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2 + \frac12 |\xi|^{-2s} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 .
$$
The right-hand side belongs to $L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$, and hence $F_{01}\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^N)$. Since
$$
\mathcal D_s(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}F_{01}(\xi)\sin(2t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)\,\,d\xi,
$$
Lemma~\ref{lem:RL} gives $\mathcal D_s(t)\to0$. Finally, substituting the limits of $\mathcal A_s$, $\mathcal B_s$ and $\mathcal D_s$ into \eqref{eq:expansion}, we obtain
$$
\lim_{t\to\infty} \left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2 = \frac12 \left( \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s-1}}^2 + \left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2 \right).
$$
This proves part~{\rm (iii)} and completes the proof.

\rm[Higher dimensions]\label{rem:higher-d}
Let $N\geq3$ and $s=s_c=\frac{N-2}{2}$. Then Theorem~\ref{th:profiles}(ii) gives

<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
\rm[Interpolation is lossy]\label{rem:interp}
It is tempting to deduce the logarithmic regime by interpolating between the polynomial and the convergent ones. This cannot work. Interpolating the bounds $\left\Vert u(t)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2\lesssim t^{\theta(s)}$ for $s>s_c$ and $\left\Vert u(t)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s}}^2\lesssim 1$ for $s<s_c$ gives, at $s=s_c$, only $\left\Vert u(t)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s_c}}^2\lesssim_\varepsilon t^{\varepsilon}$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. The logarithm, and a fortiori its constant, is a genuine boundary effect, which requires the direct argument of Lemma~\ref{lem:log}.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:taylor}
Let $N=1$, let $u_1\in L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})$ be real-valued, and set
$$
P:=\int_\mathbb{R} u_1(x)\,\,d x,\qquad m_1:=\int_\mathbb{R}\left\vert x\right\vert\left\vert u_1(x)\right\vert\,\,d x .
$$
Then the function $F(\xi):=\xi^{-2}\bigl(|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2\bigr)$ belongs to $L^1(\mathbb{R})$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-L1}
\left\Vert F\right\Vert_{L^1(\mathbb{R})}\le 2\pi\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}m_1+2m_1^2+4\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}^2 .
\end{equation}
If, in addition, $u_1\in L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})$, then $\left\vert F(\xi)\right\vert\le \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}m_2+m_1^2$ for every $\xi\neq0$, where $m_2:=\int_\mathbb{R} x^2\left\vert u_1(x)\right\vert\,\,d x$.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Since $u_1$ is real-valued, we may write $\widehat u_1(\xi) = X(\xi)-iY(\xi)$, where
$$
X(\xi) := \int_\mathbb{R} \cos(x\xi)u_1(x)\,\,d x
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
Y(\xi) := \int_\mathbb{R} \sin(x\xi)u_1(x)\,\,d x
$$
are real. Hence $|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2 = \bigl(X(\xi)-P\bigr)\bigl(X(\xi)+P\bigr)+Y(\xi)^2$, with $\left\vert X(\xi)+P\right\vert\le2\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}$.

We first consider $\left\vert \xi\right\vert\le1$. Since
$$
\left\vert X(\xi)-P\right\vert=\left| \int_\mathbb{R} \bigl(\cos(x\xi)-1\bigr) u_1(x)\,\,d x \right| \le \int_\mathbb{R} \bigl(1-\cos(x\xi)\bigr)\left\vert u_1(x)\right\vert\,\,d x,
$$
Tonelli's theorem gives
$$
\int_{\left\vert \xi\right\vert\le1}\frac{\left\vert X(\xi)-P\right\vert}{\xi^2}\,\,d\xi
\le\int_\mathbb{R}\left\vert u_1(x)\right\vert\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1-\cos(x\xi)}{\xi^2}\,\,d\xi\right)\,d x .
$$
For $x\neq0$, the change of variables $y=\left\vert x\right\vert\xi$ shows that the inner integral equals
$$
2\left\vert x\right\vert\int_0^{\left\vert x\right\vert}\frac{1-\cos y}{y^2}\,\,d y\le2\left\vert x\right\vert\int_0^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos y}{y^2}\,\,d y=\pi\left\vert x\right\vert.
$$
We infer that $\int_{\left\vert \xi\right\vert\le1}\xi^{-2}\left\vert X(\xi)^2-P^2\right\vert\,\,d\xi\le2\pi\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}m_1$. Moreover, $\left\vert \sin\vartheta\right\vert\le\left\vert \vartheta\right\vert$ gives $\left\vert Y(\xi)\right\vert\le\left\vert \xi\right\vert m_1$, so that $\int_{\left\vert \xi\right\vert\le1}\xi^{-2}Y(\xi)^2\,\,d\xi\le2m_1^2$. For $\left\vert \xi\right\vert\ge1$, we use $\left\vert \widehat u_1(\xi)\right\vert\le\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}$ and $\left\vert P\right\vert\le\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}$, and we get $\left\vert F(\xi)\right\vert\le2\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}^2\xi^{-2}$, whose integral over $\{\left\vert \xi\right\vert\ge1\}$ equals $4\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}^2$. Adding these three bounds, we obtain \eqref{eq:F-L1}.

If $u_1\in L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})$, the inequality $1-\cos\vartheta\le\vartheta^2/2$ gives $\left\vert X(\xi)-P\right\vert\le\frac{\xi^2}{2}m_2$, and hence $\left\vert X(\xi)^2-P^2\right\vert\le\xi^2\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1}m_2$. Together with $Y(\xi)^2\le\xi^2m_1^2$, this yields the pointwise bound.

\rm[Sharpness of the moment condition]\label{rem:moment-sharp}
Since $u_1$ is real-valued, the linear term in the expansion of $|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2$ at $\xi=0$ cancels. More precisely, if $u_1\in L^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})$ and $M_1:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x u_1(x)\,\,d x$, then $\widehat u_1(\xi) = P-iM_1\xi+O(\xi^2)$, and therefore $|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2 = P^2+O(\xi^2)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:taylor} shows that the integrability of $\xi^{-2}\bigl(|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2\bigr)$, which is all that the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:secondorder1} requires, holds under the weaker assumption $u_1\in L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})$. This assumption cannot be weakened in general. Indeed, let $u_1\ge0$ be even, with $P>0$. Then $Y\equiv0$ and $0\le P-X(\xi)\to0$ as $\xi\to0$, so that $\left\vert F(\xi)\right\vert\ge P\,(P-X(\xi))\,\xi^{-2}$ for $\left\vert \xi\right\vert\le\delta$ and some $\delta\in(0,1]$. The computation in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:taylor} then gives
$$
\int_{\left\vert \xi\right\vert\le\delta}\left\vert F(\xi)\right\vert\,\,d\xi\ \ge\ 2P\int_\mathbb{R} \left\vert x\right\vert\,u_1(x)\left(\int_0^{\delta\left\vert x\right\vert}\frac{1-\cos y}{y^2}\,\,d y\right)\,d x
\ \ge\ 2P\,c_\delta\int_{\left\vert x\right\vert\ge1}\left\vert x\right\vert\,u_1(x)\,\,d x,
$$
with $c_\delta:=\int_0^{\delta}y^{-2}(1-\cos y)\,\,d y>0$. Hence, for such data, $F\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ if and only if $u_1\in L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})$. Example~\ref{ex:cauchy}, for which $u_1\in L^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})$ for every $\alpha<1$ but $u_1\notin L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})$, shows that the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{th:secondorder1} itself fails in this borderline case: the second-order term is then $-\pi\log t$ instead of a constant.

We use \eqref{eq:expansion} with $N=1$ and $s=0$.

\smallskip
\noindent{The term $\mathcal A_0$.} The change of variables $\eta=t\xi$ and the classical identity $\int_\mathbb{R}\eta^{-2}\sin^2\eta\,\,d\eta=\pi$ give
$$
\int_\mathbb{R}\frac{\sin^2(t\xi)}{\xi^2}\,\,d\xi = t\int_\mathbb{R}\frac{\sin^2\eta}{\eta^2}\,\,d\eta = \pi t.
$$
Since $\sin^2(t|\xi|)=\sin^2(t\xi)$, we get
$$
\mathcal A_0(t) = \int_\mathbb{R} \frac{\sin^2(t\xi)}{\xi^2} |\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi = \pi P^2t + \int_\mathbb{R} \frac{\sin^2(t\xi)}{\xi^2} \left( |\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2 \right) \,\,d\xi.
$$
By Lemma~\ref{lem:taylor}, the function $F(\xi) := \xi^{-2}\bigl(|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2\bigr)$ belongs to $L^1(\mathbb{R})$. Since $\sin^2(t\xi) = \frac12 \bigl( 1-\cos(2t\xi) \bigr)$, we obtain
$$
\int_\mathbb{R} F(\xi)\sin^2(t\xi) \,\,d\xi = \frac12\int_\mathbb{R} F(\xi)\,\,d\xi - \frac12\int_\mathbb{R} F(\xi)\cos(2t\xi)\,\,d\xi.
$$
The Riemann--Lebesgue lemma gives $\int_\mathbb{R} F(\xi)\cos(2t\xi)\,\,d\xi \to0$. Consequently,
$$
\mathcal A_0(t) = \pi P^2t + \frac12 \int_\mathbb{R} \frac{|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2}{\xi^2} \,\,d\xi + o(1).
$$

\smallskip
\noindent{The term $\mathcal B_0$.} Since $u_0\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$, Plancherel's theorem gives $|\widehat u_0|^2\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$. Using $\cos^2(t|\xi|) = \frac12 \bigl( 1+\cos(2t|\xi|) \bigr)$ and the fact that $\cos(2t\left\vert \xi\right\vert)=\cos(2t\xi)$, we have
$$
\mathcal B_0(t) = \int_\mathbb{R} \cos^2(t|\xi|) |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 \,\,d\xi = \frac12 \left\Vert \widehat u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 + \frac12 \int_\mathbb{R} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 \cos(2t\xi) \,\,d\xi.
$$
The Riemann--Lebesgue lemma yields $\int_\mathbb{R} |\widehat u_0(\xi)|^2 \cos(2t\xi) \,\,d\xi \to0$, and therefore $\mathcal B_0(t) = \frac12\left\Vert \widehat u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 + o(1)$. With our normalization, Plancherel's identity reads $\left\Vert \widehat u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 = 2\pi\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2$, and we conclude that
$$
\mathcal B_0(t) = \pi\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 + o(1).
$$

\smallskip
\noindent{The term $\mathcal D_0$.} We define $h(\xi) := \operatorname{Re} \bigl( \widehat u_1(\xi) \overline{\widehat u_0(\xi)} \bigr)$. Since $u_0$ and $u_1$ are real-valued, $\widehat u_j(-\xi) = \overline{\widehat u_j(\xi)}$, and hence $h$ is real-valued and even. Moreover, $h(0) = \widehat u_1(0)\widehat u_0(0) = PQ$. Using $2\sin(t|\xi|)\cos(t|\xi|) = \sin(2t|\xi|)$, we obtain
$$
\mathcal D_0(t) = \int_\mathbb{R} \frac{\sin(2t|\xi|)}{|\xi|} h(\xi) \,\,d\xi = 2\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(2t\xi)}{\xi} h(\xi) \,\,d\xi.
$$
Since {$u_1,u_0\in L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})$}, the Fourier transforms $\widehat u_1$ and $\widehat u_0$ are continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives. It follows that $h\in C^1(\mathbb{R})$, so that $(h(\xi)-h(0))/\xi$ is bounded on $(0,1)$, hence integrable there. Furthermore, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_1^\infty\frac{|h(\xi)|}{\xi}\,\,d\xi &\leq \int_1^\infty \frac{ |\widehat u_1(\xi)| |\widehat u_0(\xi)| }{\xi} \,\,d\xi \leq \left\Vert \widehat u_1\right\Vert_{L^2(1,\infty)} \left\Vert \xi^{-1}\widehat u_0\right\Vert_{L^2(1,\infty)}\\
&\leq \left\Vert \widehat u_1\right\Vert_{L^2(\mathbb{R})} \left\Vert \widehat u_0\right\Vert_{L^2(\mathbb{R})} < \infty.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:dirichlet} are satisfied, and that lemma gives
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(2t\xi)}{\xi} h(\xi) \,\,d\xi \longrightarrow \frac{\pi}{2}h(0).
$$
We deduce that $\mathcal D_0(t) \to \pi PQ$.

\smallskip
\noindent{Conclusion.} By \eqref{eq:expansion}, $\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \left( \mathcal A_0(t) + \mathcal B_0(t) + \mathcal D_0(t) \right)$. Substituting the preceding asymptotic formulas, we obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \left[ \pi P^2t + \frac12 \int_\mathbb{R} \frac{|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2}{\xi^2} \,\,d\xi + \pi\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 + \pi PQ \right] + o(1) \\
&= \frac{P^2}{2}\,t + \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_\mathbb{R} \frac{|\widehat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2}{\xi^2} \,\,d\xi + \frac12\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{L^2}^2 + \frac{PQ}{2} + o(1).
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves \eqref{eq:secondorder1}.

We split the last integral into the regions $|\xi|\leq1$ and $|\xi|\geq1$. On $\{|\xi|\leq1\}$, the boundedness of the Fourier transforms gives
$$
\int_{|\xi|\leq1} \frac{ |\widehat u_1(\xi)| |\widehat u_0(\xi)| }{ |\xi|^{N-1} } \,\,d\xi \leq \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1} \left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{L^1} \int_{|\xi|\leq1} |\xi|^{-(N-1)} \,\,d\xi = \sigma_{N-1} \left\Vert u_1\right\Vert_{L^1} \left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{L^1} .
$$
On $\{|\xi|\geq1\}$, we have $|\xi|^{-(N-1)}\leq1$, and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
$$
\int_{|\xi|\geq1} \frac{ |\widehat u_1(\xi)| |\widehat u_0(\xi)| }{ |\xi|^{N-1} } \,\,d\xi \leq \left\Vert \widehat u_1\right\Vert_{L^2} \left\Vert \widehat u_0\right\Vert_{L^2} < \infty.
$$
Therefore $H\in L^1(0,\infty)$, and the one-dimensional Riemann--Lebesgue lemma gives $\mathcal D_{s_c}(t)\to0$.

\smallskip
\noindent{Conclusion.} By \eqref{eq:expansion}, we have
$$
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s_c}}^2 = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^N} \left( \mathcal A_{s_c}(t) + \mathcal B_{s_c}(t) + \mathcal D_{s_c}(t) \right).
$$
Combining the preceding asymptotic formulas, we obtain
$$
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s_c}}^2 = \frac{\sigma_{N-1}P^2}{2(2\pi)^N} \log t + \mathcal C_2 + o(1),
$$
where
$$
\mathcal C_2 = \frac{1}{2(2\pi)^N} \left[ \sigma_{N-1}P^2\log2 + \int_0^\infty \frac{ g(r)-\sigma_{N-1}P^2e^{-r} }{r} \,\,d r \right] + \frac12 \left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s_c}}^2 .
$$
This proves \eqref{eq:secondorder2} and \eqref{eq:C2}.

Finally, when $N=2$, we have $s_c=0$, $\sigma_1=2\pi$ and $(2\pi)^N=4\pi^2$. Hence $\frac{\sigma_1P^2}{2(2\pi)^2} = \frac{P^2}{4\pi}$ and $\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^2} = \frac{1}{8\pi^2}$. Substituting these values into \eqref{eq:secondorder2}, we obtain \eqref{eq:secondorder2d}.

\rm\label{rem:cross-vanishes}
It is worth contrasting the two second-order results. In dimension one, the cross term $\mathcal D_0$ contributes the nonzero amount $\pi PQ$, so that the initial position $u_0$ enters $\mathcal C_1$ both through $\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert^2$ and through its total mass $Q$. At critical regularity in dimension $N\ge2$, by contrast, the cross term vanishes in the limit, and $u_0$ enters $\mathcal C_2$ only through $\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{\dot H^{-s_c}}^2$. The reason is purely dimensional. The weight $\left\vert \xi\right\vert^{-2s_c-1}=\left\vert \xi\right\vert^{1-N}$ is exactly compensated by the volume element $\left\vert \xi\right\vert^{N-1}$, so that $\mathcal D_{s_c}$ becomes a plain Fourier sine transform of an $L^1$ function, with no boundary contribution at the origin.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:midway}
Let $N=2$, and let $[u_0,u_1]\in H^1(\mathbb{R}^2)\times L^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$ satisfy
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} |x| \left( |u_1(x)|^2+|\nabla u_0(x)|^2 \right) \,\,d x < \infty.
$$
Then, for every $R>0$ and every $t>R$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:midway}
(t-R)E_R(t) \leq K_0+\frac12|F(t)|,
\end{equation}
where $E_R$ is defined in \eqref{eq:localenergy} and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K0}
K_0 := \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} u_1(x) \bigl(x\cdot\nabla u_0(x)\bigr) \,\,d x + \frac12 \int_{\mathbb{R}^2}u_1(x)u_0(x)\,\,d x + \frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\left\vert x\right\vert\left(\left\vert u_1(x)\right\vert^2+\left\vert \nabla u_0(x)\right\vert^2\right)\,d x.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
All the terms in \eqref{eq:midway} depend continuously on the data for the norm $\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert_{H^1}+\left\Vert u_1\right\Vert+\bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\left\vert x\right\vert(\left\vert u_1\right\vert^2+\left\vert \nabla u_0\right\vert^2)\,d x\bigr)^{1/2}$, for each fixed $t$. By a standard density argument, we may therefore assume that $u_0,u_1\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^2)$. Then $u$ is smooth and, by finite speed of propagation, $u(t,\cdot)$ has compact support for every $t\ge0$. We set $e(t,x):=\frac12\bigl(u_t(t,x)^2+\left\vert \nabla u(t,x)\right\vert^2\bigr)$.

Since $E(t)=E(0)$, identity \eqref{eq:morawetz} with $N=2$ can be written as
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\Bigl(t\,e(t,x)+u_t(t,x)\,\bigl(x\cdot\nabla u(t,x)\bigr)\Bigr)\,d x=J_0-\frac12F(t),
$$
where
$$
J_0:=\frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}u_1u_0\,d x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}u_1(x\cdot\nabla u_0)\,d x .
$$
The pointwise bound $\left\vert u_t\,(x\cdot\nabla u)\right\vert\le\left\vert x\right\vert\left\vert u_t\right\vert\left\vert \nabla u\right\vert\le\left\vert x\right\vert\,e$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-step}
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\bigl(t-\left\vert x\right\vert\bigr)e(t,x)\,\,d x\le J_0+\frac12\left\vert F(t)\right\vert .
\end{equation}
We split the integral on the left-hand side into the regions $\{\left\vert x\right\vert\le R\}$, $\{R<\left\vert x\right\vert<t\}$ and $\{\left\vert x\right\vert\ge t\}$. The integrand is at least $(t-R)e$ on the first region and is nonnegative on the second one. We deduce that the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:weighted-step} is bounded from below by $(t-R)E_R(t)-\Phi(t)$, where
$$
\Phi(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\bigl(\left\vert x\right\vert-t\bigr)_+\,e(t,x)\,\,d x .
$$
We claim that $\Phi$ is nonincreasing. Indeed, $\partial_te=\operatorname{div}(u_t\nabla u)$, and the function $\psi(t,x):=(\left\vert x\right\vert-t)_+$ is Lipschitz, with $\partial_t\psi=-\mathbf 1_{\{\left\vert x\right\vert>t\}}$ and $\nabla\psi=\frac{x}{\left\vert x\right\vert}\mathbf 1_{\{\left\vert x\right\vert>t\}}$ almost everywhere. Hence, for almost every $t$,
$$
\Phi'(t)=-\int_{\{\left\vert x\right\vert>t\}}e\,\,d x-\int_{\{\left\vert x\right\vert>t\}}u_t\,\frac{x}{\left\vert x\right\vert}\cdot\nabla u\,\,d x\le-\int_{\{\left\vert x\right\vert>t\}}\bigl(e-\left\vert u_t\right\vert\left\vert \nabla u\right\vert\bigr)\,d x\le0 .
$$
Consequently, $\Phi(t)\le\Phi(0)=\frac12\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\left\vert x\right\vert\bigl(\left\vert u_1\right\vert^2+\left\vert \nabla u_0\right\vert^2\bigr)\,d x$. Inserting this bound into \eqref{eq:weighted-step}, we obtain $(t-R)E_R(t)\le J_0+\Phi(0)+\frac12\left\vert F(t)\right\vert=K_0+\frac12\left\vert F(t)\right\vert$, which is \eqref{eq:midway}.

\rm\label{rem:midway-ikehata}
Lemma~\ref{lem:midway} is a variant of the midway estimate \cite[Proposition~4.1]{Ikehata2023}, which relies on \cite[(2.13)]{IkeharaNishihara2005} and \cite[(3.5)]{CharaoIkehata2020}. In \cite{Ikehata2023}, the local energy is defined without the factor $\frac12$, and the constant of the midway estimate is
$$

$$
so that the total energy $E(0)$ appears in place of the weighted energy in \eqref{eq:K0}. The weighted energy cannot be dispensed with. \widehat u_1(\xi)=e^{-\left\vert \xi\right\vert}-e^{-a\left\vert \xi\right\vert},
$$
belongs to $\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^2)$, satisfies $\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\left\vert x\right\vert\left\vert u_1\right\vert^2\,d x<\infty$, and has $P=0$. However, $u_1(x)\sim\frac{1-a}{2\pi}\left\vert x\right\vert^{-3}$ as $\left\vert x\right\vert\to\infty$, so that $u_1\notin L^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^2)$.}
The present argument does not decide whether $t^{-1}\sqrt{\log t}$ is the optimal decay rate of the local energy $E_R(t)$ itself. It only proves that this is the rate produced by the multiplier estimate combined with the sharp asymptotics of $\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert$. The optimality of the rate for $E_R(t)$ remains open{, and, as R.~Ikehata pointed out to us, it seems to be a difficult question}; see Problem~\ref{pb:LEDsharp}.

<!-- problem-4: body_md -->
\label{pb:nomoments}
Example~\ref{ex:cauchy} shows that, when $u_1\in L^1\setminus L^{1,1}$, the second-order term in dimension one need not be constant. For the Poisson kernel, it equals $-\pi\log t$. Describe the whole family of possible second-order terms. Concretely, suppose {(say with $u_0\equiv0$)} that $|\hat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2\sim-c|\xi|^\beta$ as $\xi\to0$, where $0<\beta<2$ and $c>0$. For $0<\beta<1$, the scaling identity \eqref{eq:scaling} suggests that $\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2=\frac{P^2}{2}t-c_\beta t^{1-\beta} +o(t^{1-\beta})$, with $c_\beta=\frac{c}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sin^2|\eta|\,|\eta|^{\beta-2}\,d\eta$. At the borderline exponent $\beta=1$, one expects a logarithmic correction, which is exactly what Example~\ref{ex:cauchy} exhibits. For $1<\beta<2$, the function $\xi^{-2}(|\hat u_1(\xi)|^2-P^2)$ is locally integrable, so that the second-order term is constant, while a correction of order $t^{1-\beta}$ may appear at the next order. Making this trichotomy precise under minimal assumptions on the remainder, and computing all the corresponding constants, seems within reach of the present methods.

<!-- problem-5: body_md -->
\label{pb:RV}
Theorem~\ref{th:profiles} describes the case $\hat u_1(0)=P\ne0$. Suppose instead that $P=0$ but $u_1\notin\dot H^{-1}$, with a spherical average behaving regularly at the origin, say
\[
g(r)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\left\vert \hat u_1(r\omega)\right\vert^2\,d\omega
= r^{2\beta}\,\ell(1/r)\,\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),\qquad r\to0^+,
\]
for some $\beta\ge0$ and some function $\ell$ slowly varying in the sense of Karamata {\cite{BGT1987}}. The scaling identity \eqref{eq:scaling} suggests that
\[
\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert^2\ \sim\ c_{N,\beta}\ t^{\,2-N-2\beta}\,\ell(t),
\qquad 0\le\beta<\tfrac{2-N}{2},
\]
with an explicit constant $c_{N,\beta}$, and a logarithmic correction at the critical value $\beta=(2-N)/2$. {This range of $\beta$ is nonempty only for $N=1$; for $N=2$, only the critical value $\beta=0$ occurs.} In dimension one, this would produce the full continuum of rates $t^{\alpha}$, $0\le\alpha\le\tfrac12$, {up to slowly varying factors}, for $\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert$. These rates interpolate between Theorem~\ref{th:dichotomy} and Corollary~\ref{cor:L2}, and they would show that Remark~\ref{rem:norate} is sharp. Making this rigorous, and characterizing the velocities $u_1$ that produce a prescribed rate, would give a complete Karamata-type classification of infinite-time $L^2$-blowup for the free wave equation. The main technical point is that $g$ is a spherical average, so that regular variation must be imposed on $g$ rather than on $\hat u_1$ itself. The one-dimensional case, where the sphere consists of two points, should be settled first.

<!-- problem-6: body_md -->
\label{pb:LEDsharp}
Is the rate $t^{-1}\sqrt{\log t}$ in \eqref{eq:LEDsharp} attained by some datum with $P\ne0$? Or is the genuine decay of $E_R$ faster, the factor $\sqrt{\log t}$ being an artifact of the multiplier method? A matching lower bound for $E_R$ in dimension two seems to be unknown. Note that \eqref{eq:midway} loses information twice, once in the Cauchy--Schwarz step \eqref{eq:F-bound} and once by discarding the oscillation of $F$. Since Theorem~\ref{th:profiles} now identifies $\left\Vert u(t,\cdot)\right\Vert$ exactly at leading order, the remaining gap lies entirely in the correlation between $u$ and $u_t$, which is itself a natural object of study.
{The question concerns data that are not compactly supported. Indeed, let $u_0,u_1\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^2)$ vanish outside $\{\left\vert x\right\vert\le a\}$, and let $\left\vert x\right\vert\le R$ and $t>R+a$. Poisson's formula then reads $u=\partial_t(W*u_0)+W*u_1$, with
$$
W*f(t,x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\frac{f(z)}{\sqrt{t^2-\left\vert x-z\right\vert^2}}\,\,d z,
$$
and the expansion $(t^2-\left\vert x-z\right\vert^2)^{-1/2}=t^{-1}+O(t^{-3})$, which may be differentiated term by term, gives $u_t(t,x)=-\frac{P}{2\pi}t^{-2}+O(t^{-3})$ and $\nabla u(t,x)=O(t^{-3})$, uniformly in $\left\vert x\right\vert\le R$. Hence $E_R(t)=\frac{P^2R^2}{8\pi}\,t^{-4}+O(t^{-5})$, which is much smaller than $t^{-1}\sqrt{\log t}$. The relevant class is therefore that of data decaying slowly near infinity, as in \cite{IkeharaNishihara2005,IkehataSobukawa2007}, and determining the optimal rate in the class of Theorem~\ref{th:LED} seems to be difficult.}

\label{pb:models}
The proofs above use only the explicit multiplier in \eqref{eq:fourier-solution} and the continuity of $\hat u_1$ at $\xi=0$. They should therefore adapt to other dispersive models. For the Klein--Gordon equation $u_{tt}-\Delta u+m^2u=0$, the multiplier
$$
\frac{\sin\bigl(t\sqrt{\left\vert \xi\right\vert^2+m^2}\bigr)}{\sqrt{\left\vert \xi\right\vert^2+m^2}}
$$
is bounded. Boundedness of $M$ is then expected in all dimensions, with limit
$$
\frac12\Bigl(\left\Vert (m^2-\Delta)^{-1/2}u_1\right\Vert^2+\left\Vert u_0\right\Vert^2\Bigr).
$$ For the plate (beam) equation $u_{tt}+\Delta^2u=0$, the multiplier is $\sin(t\left\vert \xi\right\vert^2)/\left\vert \xi\right\vert^2$, so that the critical index becomes $s_c=(N-4)/2$ and the exceptional dimensions are $N\le4$. For the fractional wave equation $u_{tt}+(-\Delta)^\sigma u=0$, the critical index is $s_c=(N-2\sigma)/2$. Strongly damped wave equations, for which analogous questions were the original motivation in \cite{Ikehata2014,IkehataOnodera2017}, are also natural candidates. {Optimal growth rates of the $L^2$-norm, with unspecified constants, are already known for the plate equation \cite{Ikehata2024plate} and, in dimension one, for the fractional wave equation \cite{IkedaZhao2024}; they agree with the critical indices above.} Determining the exact constants in each of these models, in particular the analogue of $\kappa_{N,s}$, is a natural continuation of the present {paper}.

<!-- problem-8: body_md -->
\label{pb:exterior}
Does Theorem~\ref{th:dichotomy} admit an analogue in an exterior domain with Dirichlet boundary conditions? {The $L^2$-behaviour of solutions in exterior domains was studied in \cite{IkehataMatsuyama2002}.} The difficulty is that the Fourier transform is no longer available, and the quantity $P$ is no longer conserved in the sense of \eqref{eq:massconservation}. A resolvent-based low-frequency expansion should replace \eqref{eq:fourier-solution}. One may expect the role of $\dot H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^N)$ to be played by the domain of the inverse square root of the Dirichlet Laplacian, or by an appropriate low-frequency range space. Determining the correct formulation is part of the problem. This is also the setting in which the local energy decay question of \cite{Morawetz1961} was originally posed.
