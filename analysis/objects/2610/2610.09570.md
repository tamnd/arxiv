<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Under assumptions \textnormal{(i)--(iii)}, the series in
\eqref{eq:V} defining $F_t(f)$ and in \eqref{eq:Z} are absolutely
convergent almost surely for every finite $t$. For each deterministic
$t\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 \sqrt n\bigl(V_t^n(f)-F_t(f)\bigr)\xrightarrow{\mathcal L\text{-}s} Z_t(f).
\end{equation}
The convergence also holds jointly at any finite collection of
deterministic times. No independence between $\theta$ and $X$ is required,
and common jumps of $X$, $\sigma$, and $\theta$ are allowed.

The statement concerns random variables at fixed deterministic times.
It does not assert convergence in the Skorokhod space with the centering
$F_t(f)$. The distinction between actual-time and discretized centering
already occurs on a regular grid \cite[Remark 5.1.3]{JP}.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:left-scale}
Let $N$ be a rate-one Poisson process, let $T$ be its first jump time,
and let $W$ be a Brownian motion independent of $N$. On their usual
joint filtration, set
\[
 J_s=\mathbf 1_{\{T\le s\}},\qquad X_s=W_s+J_s,\qquad
 \theta_s=1+J_s,\qquad f(x)=x^4.
\]
Then $X$, $\theta$, and $f$ satisfy assumptions \textnormal{(i)--(iii)}.
For every deterministic $t>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:left-example}
 \sqrt n\bigl(V_t^n(f)-J_t\bigr)
 \xrightarrow{\mathcal L}4Z\mathbf 1_{\{T\le t\}},
\end{equation}
where $Z\sim N(0,1)$ is independent of $T$. Replacing
$\theta_{T-}$ by $\theta_T$ in \eqref{eq:R} gives a different law.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The representation
\[
 J_s=\int_0^s(1-J_{u-})\,dN_u
     =\int_0^s(1-J_{u-})\,du
       +\int_0^s(1-J_{u-})\,d(N_u-u)
\]
verifies (H), with bounded coefficients and Brownian coefficient
$\sigma\equiv1$. Moreover, $1\le\theta\le2$, $\theta$ is adapted and
c\`adl\`ag, and $f''(x)=12x^2=o(|x|)$.

Almost surely, $nT$ is not an integer for any $n$. Until the first grid
point after $T$, all steps have length $1/n$. Thus the interval
containing $T$ has endpoints
\[
 L_n=\frac{\lfloor nT\rfloor}{n},\qquad
 R_n=\frac{\lfloor nT\rfloor+1}{n},\qquad R_n-L_n=\frac1n.
\]
Subsequent steps have length $1/(2n)$. Conditional on $T$, the whole
grid is deterministic and $W$ remains Brownian. Write
\[
 G_n=W_{R_n}-W_{L_n},\qquad
 \xi_i^n=W_{\tau_i^n}-W_{\tau_{i-1}^n},\qquad
 h_i^n=\tau_i^n-\tau_{i-1}^n.
\]
Expanding the fourth power on the interval containing $T$ gives
\begin{align}\label{eq:left-expansion}
 \sqrt n\bigl(V_t^n(f)-J_{\eta_n(t)}\bigr)
 &=\sqrt n
   \sum_{\substack{1\le i\le N_n(t)\\
                    T\notin(\tau_{i-1}^n,\tau_i^n]}}
        (\xi_i^n)^4
   +4\sqrt n\,G_n\mathbf 1_{\{R_n\le t\}}\notag\\
 &\quad+\sqrt n\bigl(6G_n^2+4G_n^3+G_n^4\bigr)
                         \mathbf 1_{\{R_n\le t\}}.
\end{align}
The first term is nonnegative, and its conditional expectation is at
most
\[
 3\sqrt n\sum_{i=1}^{N_n(t)}(h_i^n)^2
 \le 3\sqrt n\,\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{N_n(t)}h_i^n
 \le\frac{3t}{\sqrt n}.
\]
Also $G_n\mid T\sim N(0,1/n)$, so the last term of
\eqref{eq:left-expansion} tends to zero in $L^1$.
Since $0<R_n-T\le1/n$ and the density of $T$ is bounded by one,
\[
 \mathbb P(T\le t<R_n)\le
 \mathbb P\bigl((t-1/n)^+<T\le t\bigr)\le\frac1n.
\]
Consequently both the change from $J_{\eta_n(t)}$ to $J_t$ and the
change from $\mathbf 1_{\{R_n\le t\}}$ to $\mathbf 1_{\{T\le t\}}$ produce
terms tending to zero in probability, even after multiplication by
their respective factors in \eqref{eq:left-expansion}. Hence
\[
 \sqrt n\bigl(V_t^n(f)-J_t\bigr)
 =4\sqrt n\,G_n\mathbf 1_{\{T\le t\}}+o_{\mathbb P}(1).
\]
The conditional law of $\sqrt n\,G_n$ given $T$ is $N(0,1)$ for every
$n$, which proves \eqref{eq:left-example}.

Here $\theta_{T-}=1$, $\theta_T=2$, and $f'(\Delta X_T)=4$.
The corrected mark therefore has the law of
\[
 4\left(\sqrt U\,\Psi^-+\sqrt{1-U}\,\Psi^+\right)\sim N(0,16).
\]
Using $\theta_T$ instead would multiply this mark by $1/\sqrt2$,
giving $N(0,8)$. Since $\mathbb P(T\le t)=1-e^{-t}>0$, these yield
different mixture laws at time $t$. Thus the post-jump sampling value
fails even for convergence in law, and hence also for stable
convergence.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:unbounded-reciprocal}
Let $X=W$, let $f(x)=x^4$, and let the deterministic sampling intensity
be
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degenerate-theta}
 \theta_s=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&s=0,\\
  s^{3/4},&0<s\le1,\\
  1,&s>1.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Then $\theta$ is positive, adapted, and bounded, and its grid is
locally finite with mesh tending to zero. Nevertheless, for every
deterministic $t>0$ and every $\varepsilon>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degenerate-failure}
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P\bigl(\sqrt n\,V_t^n(f)>\varepsilon\bigr)
 \ge\mathbb P(Z^4>\varepsilon)>0,\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).
\end{equation}
In particular, the proposed convergence to the jump limit
$Z_t(f)=0$ fails.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The first two grid intervals satisfy
\[
 \tau_1^n=\frac1n,\qquad
 \tau_2^n-\tau_1^n
 =\frac1{n(1/n)^{3/4}}=n^{-1/4}.
\]
Since $\theta_s\le1$, every step is at least $1/n$, and
$\tau_i^n\ge i/n$, proving local finiteness. For $i\ge1$, the
inequality $\tau_i^n\ge1/n$ implies
$\theta_{\tau_i^n}\ge n^{-3/4}$. Thus
\[
 \sup_{i\ge0}(\tau_{i+1}^n-\tau_i^n)\le n^{-1/4}\longrightarrow0.
\]
For fixed $t>0$, the second interval is completed before $t$ for all
sufficiently large $n$. Put
\[
 Q_n=\sqrt n\left(W_{\tau_2^n}-W_{\tau_1^n}\right)^4.
\]
The Brownian increment has variance $n^{-1/4}$, and
$\sqrt n\,(n^{-1/4})^2=1$, so $Q_n$ has the law of $Z^4$ for every
$n$. All summands of $V_t^n(f)$ are nonnegative; hence
$\sqrt n\,V_t^n(f)\ge Q_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. This proves
\eqref{eq:degenerate-failure}. Brownian motion has no jumps, so both
$F_t(f)$ and the proposed limit \eqref{eq:Z} vanish. Convergence in
law to zero would imply convergence in probability to zero, contrary
to \eqref{eq:degenerate-failure}.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:cadlag-reciprocal}
Let $X=W$, let $f(x)=x^4$, and define the deterministic process
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cadlag-theta}
 \theta_s=
 \begin{cases}
  (1-s)^3,&0\le s<1,\\
  1,&s\ge1.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
All the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} hold except for the
local boundedness of $1/\theta$. The grid is locally finite, and the
partition of $[0,1]$ formed by its completed intervals and the added
terminal point $1$ has mesh at most $n^{-1/4}$. Nevertheless, for
every $\varepsilon>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cadlag-failure}
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}
 \mathbb P\bigl(\sqrt n\,V_1^n(f)>\varepsilon\bigr)
 \ge\mathbb P(Z^4>64\varepsilon)>0,\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).
\end{equation}
Thus the fixed-time convergence to the jump limit $Z_1(f)=0$ fails.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The process $\theta$ is adapted, satisfies $0<\theta_s\le1$ for
every $s$, and has c\`adl\`ag paths. At the only discontinuity,
$\theta_{1-}=0$ and $\theta_1=1$. Hence
$\sup_{0\le s\le1}1/\theta_s=\infty$. The assumptions on $X$ and
$f$ hold as in Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded-reciprocal}.
Every step is at least $1/n$, so $\tau_i^n\ge i/n$ and the grid
has no finite accumulation point.

Put $r_n=n^{-1/4}$. For $n>1$, let
\[
 k_n=\min\{i:\tau_i^n\ge1-r_n\},\qquad
 a_n=\tau_{k_n-1}^n,\qquad b_n=\tau_{k_n}^n,
 \qquad h_n=b_n-a_n.
\]
The index is finite, and $a_n<1-r_n\le b_n$. With $d_n=1-a_n$,
the sampling recursion gives
\[
 d_n>r_n,\qquad
 h_n=\frac1{n d_n^3}<\frac1{n r_n^3}=r_n.
\]
It follows that $b_n=a_n+h_n<1$, so this interval is already
completed at the time under consideration. Since $b_n\ge1-r_n$,
we also have $d_n-h_n\le r_n$ and therefore $d_n<2r_n$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cadlag-long-interval}
 \frac{r_n}{8}<h_n<r_n,\qquad b_n<1,\qquad
 \sqrt n\,h_n^2>\frac1{64}.
\end{equation}
All grid times are deterministic, so
$W_{b_n}-W_{a_n}$ has the law of $\sqrt{h_n}\,Z$. Nonnegativity of
the summands yields
\[
 \sqrt n\,V_1^n(f)\ge\sqrt n\,(W_{b_n}-W_{a_n})^4.
\]
The term on the right has the law of $\sqrt n\,h_n^2Z^4$;
\eqref{eq:cadlag-long-interval} proves \eqref{eq:cadlag-failure}.
Since $X$ is continuous, $F_1(f)=Z_1(f)=0$. The probability bound
rules out convergence in probability to zero, and hence convergence
in law or stable convergence to that constant.

For completeness, consider any completed interval with left endpoint
$a<1$, length $h$, and $d=1-a$. Then
$h=1/(nd^3)\le d$, implying $d\ge r_n$ and $h\le r_n$.
If $\eta_n(1)<1$, its next grid point is larger than $1$. With
$d=1-\eta_n(1)$, this gives $1/(nd^3)>d$, so $d<r_n$.
If $\eta_n(1)=1$, the terminal remainder is zero. Thus
\[
 \max\left\{
   \max_{1\le i\le N_n(1)}(\tau_i^n-\tau_{i-1}^n),\,
   1-\eta_n(1)\right\}\le n^{-1/4},
\]
which proves the asserted mesh bound.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:reciprocal-scope}
Proposition~\ref{prop:left-scale} determines the left-limit scale
in \eqref{eq:R}. The other two examples concern the disappearance of
the continuous contribution: a Brownian increment on an interval of
length $h$ contributes a fourth power of order $h^2$, which need not
vanish after multiplication by $\sqrt n$. The local reciprocal bound
in \eqref{eq:theta} excludes the exhibited intervals of length
comparable to $n^{-1/4}$ by giving steps of order $1/n$ after
localization. Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded-reciprocal} also lacks
right continuity, whereas Proposition~\ref{prop:cadlag-reciprocal}
retains every other hypothesis of the theorem. The latter proves
that the reciprocal bound cannot be deleted without a replacement
assumption; it does not assert that every model with unbounded
$1/\theta$ fails to converge. Only bounds on each finite horizon
are required, not a uniform bound over all $s\ge0$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:marks}
Let $A\subset E$ be deterministic with $0<\lambda(A)<\infty$, and let
$S_1<S_2<\cdots$ be the successive atoms of $p(\cdot,A)$. Assume
\eqref{eq:bounded} for $\theta$. Define
\begin{align*}
 U_n(S)&=\frac{S-\ell_n(S)}{r_n(S)-\ell_n(S)},\\
 B_n^-(S)&=\sqrt n\,(W_S-W_{\ell_n(S)}),\qquad
 B_n^+(S)=\sqrt n\,(W_{r_n(S)}-W_S).
\end{align*}
For every finite number of atoms, jointly and stably relative to $\mathcal F$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marklimit}
 \bigl(U_n(S_j),B_n^-(S_j),B_n^+(S_j)\bigr)_j
 \xrightarrow{\mathcal L\text{-}s}
 \left(U_j,\sqrt{\frac{U_j}{\theta_{S_j-}}}\Psi_j^-,
            \sqrt{\frac{1-U_j}{\theta_{S_j-}}}\Psi_j^+\right)_j.
\end{equation}
The auxiliary triples are independent across $j$ and independent of $\mathcal F$.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Write $\lambda_A=\lambda(A)$ and $N_t=p((0,t]\times A)$.
Fix a horizon $H$ and a bounded continuous function
$\varphi$ on $[0,1]\times\mathbb R^{d'}\times\mathbb R^{d'}$. For $v>0$ put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
 K_\varphi(v)=\int_0^1
 \mathbb E\!\left[\varphi\bigl(u,\sqrt{vu}\,G^-,
                           \sqrt{v(1-u)}\,G^+\bigr)\right]du,
\end{equation}
where $G^-,G^+$ are independent standard Gaussian vectors.
This is a bounded continuous function of $v$.

\emph{Step 1: a centered interval identity.}
Suppress the superscript $n$ on interval endpoints. Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta}
 \zeta_i^n=\sum_{S\in(a_i,b_i]:\Delta N_S=1}
 \left\{\varphi\bigl(U_n(S),B_n^-(S),B_n^+(S)\bigr)
             -K_\varphi(nh_i)\right\}.
\end{equation}
Conditionally on $\mathcal F_{a_i}$, $h_i$ is known and the future Brownian
motion and Poisson process have their standard independent laws.
Integration over a Poisson atom in $(a_i,a_i+h_i]$, followed by the
change of variable $s=a_i+u h_i$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:center}
 \mathbb E[\zeta_i^n\mid\mathcal F_{a_i}]=0.
\end{equation}
This identity counts all atoms in the interval; it does not require that
there be exactly one atom.

\emph{Step 2: testing against the original sigma-field.}
We establish the following weighted identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted}
 \mathbb E\!\left[B\sum_{i:b_i\le H}H_i^n\zeta_i^n\right]\longrightarrow0
\end{equation}
for every bounded $\mathcal F$-measurable $B$ and every array $H_i^n$ which is
$\mathcal F_{a_i}$-measurable and bounded in absolute value by one.
Let $M_s=\mathbb E[B\mid\mathcal F_s]$ be its bounded c\`adl\`ag martingale version.
Using \eqref{eq:center}, the left side of \eqref{eq:weighted} is
\[
 \mathbb E\sum_{i:b_i\le H}H_i^n\zeta_i^n(M_{b_i}-M_{a_i}).
\]
There are finitely many atoms of $N$ on $[0,H]$. For each such atom $S$,
the c\`adl\`ag property and \eqref{eq:steps} give
\[
 M_{r_n(S)}-M_{\ell_n(S)}-\Delta M_S\longrightarrow0
 \quad\text{almost surely}.
\]
The absolute error in replacing the martingale increment by $\Delta M_S$
in the preceding expectation is bounded by a constant times $N_H$.
It therefore tends to zero in $L^1$, uniformly in the choices of $H_i^n$.

For $a_i<s\le b_i$ define
\begin{align}\label{eq:q}
 q_n(s)={}&\mathbb E_G\!\left[
 \varphi\left(\frac{s-a_i}{h_i},\sqrt n(W_s-W_{a_i}),
                                  \sqrt{n(b_i-s)}\,G\right)\right]
 -K_\varphi(nh_i).
\end{align}
At a Poisson time $S$, conditioning on $\mathcal F_S$ integrates out the
post-$S$ Brownian increment and replaces the braces in \eqref{eq:zeta}
by $q_n(S)$. The right endpoint $r_n(S)$ is already $\mathcal F_S$-measurable,
which justifies this conditional Gaussian calculation.
The process $q_n$, defined on left-open, right-closed grid intervals,
is predictable: $W$ is continuous and the endpoint data on each interval
are known at its left endpoint. It is uniformly bounded.

There exists a predictable process $a_s$, with
$|a_s|\le2\lambda_A\lVert B\rVert_\infty$, such that for every bounded
predictable $v$ supported in $[0,H]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projection}
 \mathbb E\sum_{S\le H:\Delta N_S=1}v_S\Delta M_S
       =\mathbb E\int_0^H v_s a_s\,ds.
\end{equation}
Indeed, on the predictable sigma-field the signed measure on the left
is dominated in absolute value by
$2\lVert B\rVert_\infty\lambda_A\,d\mathbb P\,ds$; the Radon--Nikodym theorem
gives \eqref{eq:projection} and its bound.
Consequently the expectation in \eqref{eq:weighted}, up to an $o(1)$
term, equals
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projected}
 \mathbb E\sum_{i:b_i\le H} H_i^n\int_{a_i}^{b_i}q_n(s)a_s\,ds.
\end{equation}

Approximate $a$ in $L^1(d\mathbb P\,ds)$ on $[0,H]$ by bounded elementary
predictable processes, constant on finitely many deterministic intervals
$(t_j,t_{j+1}]$, with each value $\mathcal F_{t_j}$-measurable. The error in
\eqref{eq:projected} is bounded by
$2\lVert \varphi\rVert_\infty$ times this $L^1$ error, uniformly in $n$ and
$H_i^n$. For an interval $(a_i,b_i]$ wholly inside one such deterministic
interval, its elementary value is $\mathcal F_{a_i}$-measurable and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:timeaverage}
 \mathbb E\!\left[\int_{a_i}^{b_i}q_n(s)\,ds\,\middle|\,\mathcal F_{a_i}\right]=0.
\end{equation}
This follows directly from \eqref{eq:kernel}: integrate the two independent
Brownian pieces and then integrate the relative position over $[0,1]$.
The grid intervals crossing any of the finitely many $t_j$ have total
length at most a constant times $1/n$. Their contribution vanishes.
First let $n\to\infty$, then remove the elementary approximation.
This proves \eqref{eq:weighted}.

\emph{Step 3: joint convergence and independence of the marks.}
The only interval crossing $H$ can contain an atom only in
$(H-1/(nc),H]$, an event of probability at most $\lambda_A/(nc)$.
On each path, all atoms on $[0,H]$ are in distinct grid intervals for
all sufficiently large $n$. Furthermore, for each atom $S$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernelreplace}
 K_\varphi\bigl(n(r_n(S)-\ell_n(S))\bigr)
       \longrightarrow K_\varphi(\theta_{S-}^{-1}).
\end{equation}

To prove joint convergence, consider product test functions. On $\{N_H=m\}$ choose bounded
continuous functions $\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_m$, scaled to have
absolute value at most one. Write
$\varphi_j^n=\varphi_j(U_n(S_j),B_n^-(S_j),B_n^+(S_j))$
and $K_j=K_{\varphi_j}(\theta_{S_j-}^{-1})$. In the telescoping identity
\[
 \prod_{j=1}^m\varphi_j^n-\prod_{j=1}^m K_j
 =\sum_{j=1}^m
   \left(\prod_{q<j}\varphi_q^n\right)
   (\varphi_j^n-K_j)\left(\prod_{q>j}K_q\right),
\]
use \eqref{eq:weighted} with original multiplier
$B\mathbf 1_{\{N_H=m\}}\prod_{q>j}K_q$.
In the array $H_i^n$, select $N_{a_i}=j-1$ and multiply the marks of
the preceding $j-1$ atoms, all of whose observation intervals have
ended by $a_i$. This is $\mathcal F_{a_i}$-measurable. Once the atoms occupy
distinct intervals it equals exactly the required preceding product.
Use \eqref{eq:kernelreplace} to replace the interval kernel by $K_j$.
The exceptional multiple-atom intervals have vanishing contribution,
by domination by a constant times $N_H$ and eventual pathwise separation.
It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product}
 \mathbb E\!\left[B\mathbf 1_{\{N_H=m\}}\prod_{j=1}^m\varphi_j^n\right]
 \longrightarrow
 \mathbb E\!\left[B\mathbf 1_{\{N_H=m\}}\prod_{j=1}^m K_j\right].
\end{equation}
The right side is the conditional product kernel of the marks displayed
in \eqref{eq:marklimit}.

For completeness, these tests determine joint stable convergence.
The $U_n$ are bounded, and a conditional Brownian/Poisson calculation gives
\[
 \sup_n\mathbb E\sum_{S\le H:\Delta N_S=1}
     \bigl(\lVert B_n^-(S)\rVert^2+\lVert B_n^+(S)\rVert^2\bigr)<\infty.
\]
One may include the crossing interval in this bound and use
$h_i\le1/(nc)$ and at most $nCH+1$ intervals. Thus the finite mark
vectors are tight. Finite products of continuous functions determine
their joint laws; applying \eqref{eq:product} also to nonnegative
bounded multipliers and normalizing the resulting finite measures gives
stable convergence. The Poisson tail removes the restriction to bounded
$m$. Finally $H\to\infty$ covers any fixed finite number of atoms.

\label{lem:localX}
Assume \eqref{eq:bounded}. Let $A_m=\{z:\Gamma(z)>1/m\}$, and let

Set
\begin{align*}
 J^{(m)}&=(\delta\mathbf 1_{A_m})\star p,\qquad Y^{(m)}=X-J^{(m)},\\
 b_s^{(m)}&=b_s-\int_{A_m}\delta(s,z)

For $m\ge1$,
\[
 Y_t^{(m)}=X_0+\int_0^t b_s^{(m)}\,ds
       +\int_0^t\sigma_s\,dW_s
       +(\delta\mathbf 1_{A_m^c})\star(p-q)_t.
\]
The drift $b^{(m)}$ is bounded for fixed $m$, since
$\int_{A_m}\Gamma\,d\lambda\le m\int\Gamma^2\,d\lambda$.

Enlarge the filtration initially by the full restriction $p|_{A_m}$.
In the resulting filtration $(\mathcal G_s)$, $W$ is still Brownian and
$p|_{A_m^c}$ still has compensator $ds\,\lambda|_{A_m^c}(dz)$.
This follows from the joint independent increments of the drivers;
it is also the finite-intensity enlargement used in
\cite[Proposition 2.1.10]{JP}.
The time $S$ is now $\mathcal G_0$-measurable. Moreover, $\ell_n(S)$ is
a $\mathcal G$-stopping time: starting from $\tau_i^n$, the test
$\tau_{i+1}^n>S$ is $\mathcal G_{\tau_i^n}$-measurable because the next endpoint
is known there. The first index passing that test therefore selects a
stopping time. The same holds for $r_n(S)$.

Eventually the interval containing $S$ contains no other $A_m$-atom.
On this event its increment after removal of $\Delta X_S$ is the
increment of $Y^{(m)}$. Its drift, multiplied by $\sqrt n$, is bounded
by $C_m/\sqrt n$.

To bound its compensated-jump increment, put $u_n=n^{-1/4}$.
For marks with $\Gamma\le u_n$, the second moment after multiplication
by $\sqrt n$ is at most
\[
 C\int_{\{\Gamma\le u_n\}}\Gamma^2\,d\lambda\longrightarrow0.
\]
For the remaining marks in $A_m^c$, the probability of an atom in the
interval is at most
\[
 \frac Cn\lambda(\Gamma>u_n)
 \le Cn^{-1/2}\int\Gamma^2\,d\lambda\longrightarrow0.
\]
Their compensator, after multiplication by $\sqrt n$, has norm at most
\[
 \frac C{\sqrt n}\int_{\{\Gamma>u_n\}}\Gamma\,d\lambda
 \le Cn^{-1/4}\int\Gamma^2\,d\lambda\longrightarrow0.
\]
All three bounds apply to stopping intervals in the enlarged filtration
and use only their deterministic upper length bound $C/n$.

For the Brownian part before $S$, first freeze at the adapted value
$\sigma_{\ell_n(S)}$. The isometry bounds the squared error, multiplied
by $n$, by
\[
 C\mathbb E\!\left[
 \sup_{\ell_n(S)\le u<S}
 \lVert \sigma_u-\sigma_{\ell_n(S)}\rVert^2\right]\longrightarrow0.
\]
The assertion is restricted to $\{S\le H\}$ when necessary; this event
is $\mathcal G_0$-measurable. Boundedness and the left limit of $\sigma$ give
the convergence. Next $\sigma_{\ell_n(S)}\to\sigma_{S-}$, and
$B_n^-(S)$ is tight by Lemma~\ref{lem:marks}.
After $S$, freeze directly at $\sigma_S$; right continuity and the
same isometry give the corresponding vanishing error. This proves
\eqref{eq:localX}. Joint convergence follows for finitely many atoms.
Lemma~\ref{lem:marks} and stable multiplication by the original
coefficients then give the final assertion.

\label{lem:small}
Suppose \eqref{eq:bounded} holds. If $g\in C^2(\mathbb R^d;\mathbb R)$ satisfies
$g(0)=0$, $Dg(0)=0$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smallass}
 \lVert D^2g(x)\rVert\le a\lVert x\rVert\quad\text{for every }x,
\end{equation}
then, for each fixed $t$, uniformly in $n\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smallbound}
 \mathbb E\left|\sqrt n\left(
      V_t^n(g)-F_{\eta_n(t)}(g)\right)\right|\le C_t a.
\end{equation}
The same bound, with a possibly larger constant, holds with $F_t(g)$.

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Taylor's formula implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smallfirst}
 |g(x)|\le\frac a6\lVert x\rVert^3,\qquad
 \lVert Dg(x)\rVert\le\frac a2\lVert x\rVert^2.
\end{equation}
Define
\[
 K_g(x,y)=g(x+y)-g(x)-g(y),\qquad
 H_g(x,y)=K_g(x,y)-Dg(x)y.
\]
The following two bounds will be used:
\begin{align}\label{eq:kernelbounds}
 |K_g(x,y)|&\le C a\bigl(\lVert x\rVert^2\lVert y\rVert
                                +\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert^2\bigr),\notag\\
 |H_g(x,y)|&\le C a\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert^2.
\end{align}
For the first, integrate $x^\top D^2g(ux+vy)y$ over $(u,v)\in[0,1]^2$.
For the second, if $\lVert y\rVert\le\lVert x\rVert$, expand $g(x+y)$ about $x$
and bound $g(y)$ using \eqref{eq:smallfirst}. If
$\lVert x\rVert<\lVert y\rVert$, instead expand $g(y+x)$ about $y$ and bound
$g(x)$ and $Dg(x)y$. In this latter case all terms are bounded by a
constant times $a\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert^2$. This proves both inequalities
without assuming a third derivative of $g$.

Under \eqref{eq:bounded}, all jumps can be compensated by using the
bounded drift
\[
 \bar b_s=b_s+\int_{\{\lVert \delta(s,z)\rVert>1\}}\delta(s,z)\lambda(dz).
\]
On a grid interval $(a_i,b_i]$ let $Y_s^i=X_s-X_{a_i}$.
It\^o's formula, subtracting the sum of $g(\Delta X)$ on that interval,
decomposes the unscaled error into a finite-variation term and a
square-integrable martingale term, with integrands
\begin{align}\label{eq:ItoA}
 dA_s^i={}&\left(Dg(Y_{s-}^i)\bar b_s
       +\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\bigl(D^2g(Y_{s-}^i)c_s\bigr)
       +\int_E H_g(Y_{s-}^i,\delta(s,z))\lambda(dz)\right)ds,\\
 dM_s^i={}&Dg(Y_{s-}^i)\sigma_s\,dW_s
       +\int_E K_g(Y_{s-}^i,\delta(s,z))(p-q)(ds,dz),\label{eq:ItoM}
\end{align}
where $c_s=\sigma_s\sigma_s^\top$.
The stochastic Brownian integrals can equivalently use $\sigma_{s-}$;
the two versions agree in $ds\,d\mathbb P$ measure.

Restricting the sum to completed intervals preserves the
martingale property: $\mathbf 1_{\{b_i\le t\}}$ is $\mathcal F_{a_i}$-measurable,
so the integrands in \eqref{eq:ItoM}, multiplied by these indicators
and restricted to $(a_i,b_i]$, are predictable.

We record the moment bounds needed to sum these integrals. The
conditional maximal inequalities for the bounded drift, Brownian
integral, and compensated jump integral give, for $q=2,4$ and a
bounded $\mathcal F_{a_i}$-measurable duration $h_i$,
\begin{align*}
 \mathbb E\left[\sup_{a_i\le s\le b_i}\lVert Y_s^i\rVert^2
                    \,\middle|\,\mathcal F_{a_i}\right]&\le C h_i,\\
 \mathbb E\left[\sup_{a_i\le s\le b_i}\lVert Y_s^i\rVert^4
                    \,\middle|\,\mathcal F_{a_i}\right]&\le C(h_i+h_i^2+h_i^4)
                                                        \le C h_i.
\end{align*}
The last constant uses $h_i\le1/c$; $\int\Gamma^4d\lambda<\infty$
follows from boundedness of $\Gamma$. Consequently, with the sum always
restricted to $b_i\le t$,
\begin{align}\label{eq:moments}
 \mathbb E\sum_i\int_{a_i}^{b_i}
   \bigl(\lVert Y_{s-}^i\rVert^2+\lVert Y_{s-}^i\rVert^4\bigr)ds
   &\le C\mathbb E\sum_i h_i^2\le\frac{C_t}{n},\notag\\
 \mathbb E\sum_i\int_{a_i}^{b_i}\lVert Y_{s-}^i\rVert\,ds
   &\le C\mathbb E\sum_i h_i^{3/2}\le\frac{C_t}{\sqrt n}.
\end{align}
Multiplication of the conditional bounds by $\mathbf 1_{\{b_i\le t\}}$
is legitimate because that event is known at $a_i$.

Equations \eqref{eq:smallfirst}, \eqref{eq:kernelbounds}, and
\eqref{eq:moments} show that the expected total variation of the
sum in \eqref{eq:ItoA}, multiplied by $\sqrt n$, is at most $C_t a$.
The second moment of the scaled sum in \eqref{eq:ItoM} is at most
\begin{align*}
 C n a^2\mathbb E\sum_i\int_{a_i}^{b_i}
 \left[\lVert Y_{s-}^i\rVert^4
       +\lVert Y_{s-}^i\rVert^4\int_E\Gamma^2\,d\lambda
       +\lVert Y_{s-}^i\rVert^2\int_E\Gamma^4\,d\lambda\right]ds
 \le C_t a^2.
\end{align*}
We used the isometries and orthogonality of continuous and purely
discontinuous martingales. Cauchy--Schwarz proves \eqref{eq:smallbound}.

Finally, $\eta_n(t)\ge(t-1/(nc))\vee0$. The compensator formula and
\eqref{eq:smallfirst} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpointg}
 \mathbb E\left[\sqrt n\,
   |F_t(g)-F_{\eta_n(t)}(g)|\right]
 \le\frac{C a}{\sqrt n}\int_E\Gamma^3\,d\lambda.
\end{equation}
This proves the last assertion.

By the localization argument in Section~\ref{sec:localization}, it
suffices to work under \eqref{eq:bounded}. The proof follows the
standard truncation argument in \cite[Theorem 5.1.2]{JP}, using the
adaptive-grid lemmas established above, and proceeds in three steps.

\emph{Step 1: existence and tails of the limit.}
Integrating \eqref{eq:f} twice shows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:orders}
 f(x)=o(\lVert x\rVert^3),\qquad Df(x)=o(\lVert x\rVert^2).
\end{equation}
On every finite horizon the jumps of a semimartingale have a square
summable sequence of sizes, with only finitely many sizes exceeding
any positive threshold. Thus $F_t(f)$ is absolutely convergent.
Also, conditionally on $\mathcal F$, the expected norm of $R_p^{\mathrm{corr}}$
is bounded uniformly over $T_p\le t$. Equation \eqref{eq:orders} gives
\[
 \sum_{p:T_p\le t}\widetilde\mathbb E\left[
      |Df(\Delta X_{T_p})R_p^{\mathrm{corr}}|\,\middle|\,\mathcal F\right]
 <\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.
\]
Conditional Tonelli therefore proves absolute convergence of
\eqref{eq:Z}. The same reasoning works without localization, using the
pathwise bounds on $\sigma$, $\theta^{-1}$, and the jump sizes.

The summands are conditionally centered and independent. For any
function $g$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:small},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limittail}
 \widetilde\mathbb E|Z_t(g)|^2
 \le C a^2\mathbb E\sum_{s\le t}\lVert \Delta X_s\rVert^4
 \le C_t a^2\int_E\Gamma^4\,d\lambda.
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 2: functions vanishing near zero.}
Suppose first that $f(x)=0$ for $\lVert x\rVert\le\varepsilon$.
Choose a fixed $m$ with $2/m<\varepsilon$, and use $Y^{(m)}$ from
Lemma~\ref{lem:localX}. Its jumps have norm at most $1/m$.
The elementary oscillation property of a c\`adl\`ag path implies
\[
 \limsup_{h\downarrow0}\sup_{\substack{0\le u\le v\le t+1\\v-u\le h}}
       \lVert Y_v^{(m)}-Y_u^{(m)}\rVert\le\frac1m.
\]
Together with \eqref{eq:steps}, this shows that all its grid increments
have norm at most $2/m$ for all sufficiently large $n$, almost surely.
The finitely many $A_m$-atoms on this horizon are eventually in distinct
grid intervals.

On this event an interval without an $A_m$-atom contributes zero.
All jumps outside $A_m$ also have zero $f$-value. For an $A_m$-atom $S$,
put
\[
 Q_n(S)=\sqrt n\bigl(X_{r_n(S)}-X_{\ell_n(S)}-\Delta X_S\bigr).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:localX} gives joint tightness and stable convergence of
these variables to their corrected marks. Taylor's formula yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Taylor}
 \sqrt n\{f(\Delta X_S+n^{-1/2}Q_n(S))-f(\Delta X_S)\}
       =Df(\Delta X_S)Q_n(S)+o_{\mathbb P}(1).
\end{equation}
This holds jointly for any fixed number of atoms. Their random finite
number is handled by first restricting it to a deterministic bound and
then using the Poisson tail.
An atom before $t$ can be omitted by the completed-interval sum only if
it lies in $(t-1/(nc),t]$, an event of probability at most
$\lambda(A_m)/(nc)$. Hence \eqref{eq:Taylor}, Lemma~\ref{lem:marks}, and
stable multiplication by $Df(\Delta X_S)$ imply \eqref{eq:main} for
functions vanishing near zero. The limit is precisely \eqref{eq:Z},
because the other jumps have zero derivative as well.

\emph{Step 3: removal of the cutoff.}
Choose a smooth compactly supported $\chi:\mathbb R^d\to[0,1]$ equal to one
on the unit ball and zero outside the ball of radius two. Define
\[
 g_\varepsilon(x)=f(x)\chi(x/\varepsilon),\qquad
 f_\varepsilon=f-g_\varepsilon.
\]
The function $f_\varepsilon$ vanishes on the ball of radius
$\varepsilon$. If
\[
 \omega(r)=\sup_{0<\lVert x\rVert\le r}
          \frac{\lVert D^2f(x)\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert},
\]
then $\omega(r)\to0$. Differentiation of the cutoff, using
$\lVert Df(x)\rVert\le\omega(2\varepsilon)\lVert x\rVert^2/2$ and
$|f(x)|\le\omega(2\varepsilon)\lVert x\rVert^3/6$ on its support, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cutoffbound}
 \lVert D^2g_\varepsilon(x)\rVert\le a_\varepsilon\lVert x\rVert
 \quad\text{for every }x,\qquad
 a_\varepsilon=C_\chi\omega(2\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Write $Z_n(t;h)=\sqrt n(V_t^n(h)-F_t(h))$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:small} and \eqref{eq:limittail} imply
\begin{align}\label{eq:together}
 \sup_n\mathbb E|Z_n(t;f)-Z_n(t;f_\varepsilon)|
     &\le C_t a_\varepsilon\longrightarrow0,\notag\\
 \widetilde\mathbb E|Z_t(f)-Z_t(f_\varepsilon)|^2
     &\le C_t a_\varepsilon^2\longrightarrow0.
\end{align}
For fixed $\varepsilon$, Step 2 gives
$Z_n(t;f_\varepsilon)\xrightarrow{\mathcal L\text{-}s} Z_t(f_\varepsilon)$.
Testing against a bounded $\mathcal F$-measurable $B$ and a bounded Lipschitz
function, \eqref{eq:together} lets us first send $n\to\infty$ and then
$\varepsilon\downarrow0$. These tests, together with tightness inherited
from the same approximation, give stable convergence for all bounded
continuous tests. This proves \eqref{eq:main} under the bounded hypotheses.

For finitely many deterministic times $t_1,\ldots,t_k$, apply the same
argument to the vector of statistics. Step~2 uses joint convergence
of the marks on a common horizon, while the approximation errors in
\eqref{eq:together} are bounded by summing over the $k$ coordinates.
Testing against bounded Lipschitz functions on $\mathbb R^k$ therefore gives
the finite-dimensional stable convergence. Finally, the localization
argument of Section~\ref{sec:localization} removes the boundedness
restriction and completes the proof.
