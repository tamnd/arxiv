\label{lem:continuous-finite-positivity}
    Assume the coefficient and initial conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:intro-continuous} or Theorem~\ref{thm:degenerate-continuous}, in the corresponding regime. For every $N$ allowed in that theorem, the corresponding observable processes satisfy

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
In the strictly positive regime, \cite[Theorem~1]{Mal26} provides a weak solution up to its explosion time. Since $H>0$, integrability of the singular drift implies that every such solution is non-sticky. The argument proving \cite[Theorem~2]{Mal26} applies to every such solution and excludes positive-time triple collisions. The required compatibility of the diffusion coefficients on collision walls is automatic, since the same function $\sigma$ is used for every particle.
    
    In the degenerate regime, apply the non-collision result for an interior initial configuration from \cite[Proposition~6]{GraMal14} with
        \begin{equation*}
            \widetilde\sigma_i(x)=\frac{\sigma(x)}{\sqrt N}, \qquad \widetilde b_i(x)=b(x), \qquad \widetilde H_{ij}(x,y)=\frac{\beta}{N}H(x,y).
        \end{equation*}
    Indeed, Condition~\ref{ass:noncollision-structure}(ii) gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \widetilde\sigma_i^2(x)+\widetilde\sigma_j^2(y) =\frac{\sigma^2(x)+\sigma^2(y)}{N} \leq\frac{4H(x,y)}{N} \leq4\widetilde H_{ij}(x,y),
        \end{equation*}
    while Condition~\ref{ass:noncollision-structure}(iii) verifies the three-particle condition (A3) therein. The drift functions are identical and Lipschitz, so their ordering condition is automatic.

    Since $X_0^N\in W_N$, Proposition~6 of \cite{GraMal14} gives non-collision without the diffraction assumption (A4) therein. The existence construction in Section~5 of that paper applies with Proposition~6 in place of its Theorem~2; see also Remark~4 therein. Once non-collision is established, Condition~\ref{ass:strong-regularity} and Condition~\ref{ass:noncollision-structure}(i) give pathwise uniqueness by the argument of \cite[Theorem~4]{GraMal14}. The Yamada-Watanabe theorem therefore gives a unique strong solution up to explosion.

    In both regimes, non-explosion follows from \cite[Theorem~5]{Mal26}. Indeed, Conditions~\ref{ass:epsilon} and \ref{ass:bounds} imply
        \begin{equation*}
            \sum_{i=1}^N x_i b(x_i) +\frac{\varepsilon_N}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N\sigma^2(x_i) +\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{i<j}H(x_i,x_j) \leq K_\beta\left(N+\sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2\right).
        \end{equation*}
    For each fixed $N$, this verifies the radial growth condition \textup{(G1)} of that theorem. Consequently, the solutions constructed above are global.
    Recall that
        \begin{equation*}
            Z_\sigma:=\{x\in\mathbb R:x\sigma(x)=0\}, \qquad \#Z_\sigma\leq M_\sigma+1.
        \end{equation*}
    Since $C_N$ is an average of non-negative terms, $C_N(t)=0$ would require every particle to belong to $Z_\sigma$. In the strictly positive regime, at most two particles can occupy any fixed point at a positive time. Consequently, $C_N(t)=0$ for some $t>0$ would imply
        \begin{equation*}
            N\leq2\#Z_\sigma\leq2M_\sigma+2,
        \end{equation*}
    contradicting $N\geq2M_\sigma+3$. At time zero, positivity follows from Condition~\ref{ass:initial:non-zero}. In the degenerate regime, all particle locations are distinct for every $t\geq0$. Thus $C_N(t)=0$ would imply
        \begin{equation*}
            N\leq\#Z_\sigma\leq M_\sigma+1,
        \end{equation*}
    contradicting $N\geq M_\sigma+2$. Hence, in either regime, $C_N(t)>0$ throughout $[0,T]$ almost surely.

    Strict positivity of $H$ immediately gives $A_N(t)>0$ in the first regime. In the degenerate regime, Condition~\ref{ass:noncollision-structure}(ii) yields
        \begin{equation*}
            A_N(t) \geq \frac{1}{4N^2}\sum_{i<j} \left(\sigma^2(X_t^{i,N})+\sigma^2(X_t^{j,N})\right) = \frac14\left(1-\frac1N\right)\left\langle\mu_t^N,\sigma^2\right\rangle >0,
        \end{equation*}
    where the last inequality follows from $C_N(t)>0$.

    Finally, $A_N$ and $C_N$ are continuous. Their strict positivity on the compact interval $[0,T]$ therefore proves \eqref{eq:continuous-finite-positivity} and gives $0<J_N<\infty$. The weight $A_N/C_N$ is continuous and pathwise bounded on $[0,T]$, so the stochastic integral defining $\widehat\beta_N^{\,c}$ is well-defined.

\label{prop:continuous-martingale-criterion}
    Suppose that the particle systems satisfy \eqref{eq:intro-Y-one-dimensional} and that $C_N(t)>0$ on $[0,T]$ and $0<J_N<\infty$ almost surely. Define
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:continuous-score}
        M_N(t):=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{i=1}^N \int_0^t \frac{A_N(s)X_s^{i,N}\sigma(X_s^{i,N})}{C_N(s)} \,dB_s^i.
    \end{equation}

    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:continuous-score-error}
        \langle M_N\rangle_T=J_N, \qquad \widehat\beta_N^{\,c}-\beta =\delta_N\frac{M_N(T)}{J_N}.
    \end{equation}
    If $r_N^2J_N\to\infty$ in probability, then $\widehat\beta_N^{\,c}\to\beta$ in probability. If, moreover, $J_N\to J_\infty$ in probability for a deterministic $J_\infty\in(0,\infty)$, then
    \begin{equation*}
        r_N(\widehat\beta_N^{\,c}-\beta)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\mathcal L} \mathcal N(0,J_\infty^{-1}).

The processes $X^{i,N}$, $A_N$ and $C_N$ are continuous and adapted. Since $C_N(t)>0$ throughout $[0,T]$ almost surely, the integrands in \eqref{eq:continuous-score} are predictable and pathwise bounded on this interval. Consequently, $M_N$ is a well-defined continuous local martingale with $M_N(0)=0$. Independence of the driving Brownian motions gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \langle M_N\rangle_t = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\int_0^t\frac{A_N(s)^2(X_s^{i,N})^2\sigma^2(X_s^{i,N})}{C_N(s)^2}\,ds = \int_0^t\frac{A_N(s)^2}{C_N(s)}\,ds.
        \end{equation*}
    In particular, $\langle M_N\rangle_T=J_N<\infty$ almost surely. Substitution of the exact equation for $Y_N$ into the estimator gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \int_0^T\frac{A_N(t)}{C_N(t)}\bigl(dY_N(t)-B_N(t)\,dt\bigr) = 2\beta J_N+2\delta_N M_N(T),
        \end{equation*}
    which proves \eqref{eq:continuous-score-error}. To prove consistency, fix $\eta,L>0$. For any $\lambda\in\mathbb R$, It\^o's formula shows that
        \begin{equation*}
            \exp\left(\lambda M_N(t) -\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\langle M_N\rangle_t\right)
        \end{equation*}
    is a non-negative local martingale starting at one. It is therefore a supermartingale, and hence
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb E\left[\exp\left(\lambda M_N(T)-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}J_N\right)\right]\leq1.
        \end{equation*}
    Taking $\lambda=\eta r_N$ and $\lambda=-\eta r_N$, respectively, observe that on the corresponding events
        \begin{equation*}
            \{\pm M_N(T)>\eta r_NJ_N,\ r_N^2J_N\geq L\}
        \end{equation*}
    we have
        \begin{equation*}
            \pm\eta r_NM_N(T)-\frac{\eta^2r_N^2}{2}J_N > \frac{\eta^2r_N^2}{2}J_N\geq\frac{\eta^2L}{2}.
        \end{equation*}
    Markov's inequality therefore yields
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb P\bigl( \pm M_N(T)>\eta r_NJ_N,\ r_N^2J_N\geq L\bigr)\leq\exp(-\eta^2L/2).
        \end{equation*}
    Since $\delta_N=r_N^{-1}$ and $J_N>0$, the representation \eqref{eq:continuous-score-error} and the union bound give
        \begin{equation}
            \label{eq:continuous-self-normalized-bound}
            \mathbb P\bigl(|\widehat\beta_N^{\,c}-\beta|>\eta\bigr) \leq \mathbb P(r_N^2J_N<L)+2\exp(-\eta^2L/2).
        \end{equation}
    The assumption $r_N^2J_N\to\infty$ in probability makes the first term tend to zero for every fixed $L$. Letting first $N\to\infty$ and then $L\to\infty$ proves consistency.

    Suppose now that $J_N\to J_\infty$ in probability for a deterministic constant $J_\infty\in(0,\infty)$. Extend $M_N$ constantly after $T$. This gives a continuous local martingale on $[0,\infty)$ with total quadratic variation $J_N<\infty$ almost surely. By the Dambis-Dubins-Schwarz theorem in its finite-quadratic-variation form \cite[Chapter~V, Theorem~1.7]{RevuzYor1999}, on a possibly enlarged probability space there exists a standard Brownian motion $\widetilde W^{\,N}$ such that
        \begin{equation*}
            M_N(t) = \widetilde W^{\,N}(\langle M_N\rangle_t), \qquad 0\leq t\leq T.
        \end{equation*}
    In particular, $M_N(T)=\widetilde W^{\,N}(J_N)$. To justify replacing $J_N$ by $J_\infty$, fix $\eta>0$ and $0<h<J_\infty$. Then
        \begin{equation*}
              \mathbb P\left(\left|\widetilde W^{\,N}(J_N)-\widetilde W^{\,N}(J_\infty) \right|>\eta \right)\leq
            \mathbb P(|J_N-J_\infty|>h) + \mathbb P\left(\sup_{|s-J_\infty|\leq h}\left|\widetilde W^{\,N}(s)-\widetilde W^{\,N}(J_\infty)\right|>\eta\right).
        \end{equation*}
    For fixed $h$, the first probability tends to zero as $N\to\infty$. Since all the processes $\widetilde W^{\,N}$ have the standard Brownian law, the second probability has the same value for every $N$ and tends to zero as $h\downarrow0$ by continuity of Brownian paths. Consequently,
        \begin{equation*}
            \widetilde W^{\,N}(J_N)
            -\widetilde W^{\,N}(J_\infty)
            \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0.
        \end{equation*}
    This argument does not require independence between $\widetilde W^{\,N}$ and $J_N$.

    Because $J_\infty$ is deterministic, $\widetilde W^{\,N}(J_\infty)$ has distribution $\mathcal N(0,J_\infty)$ for every $N$. It follows that
        \begin{equation*}
            M_N(T)\Longrightarrow\mathcal N(0,J_\infty).
        \end{equation*}
    Finally, \eqref{eq:continuous-score-error} gives
        \begin{equation*}
            r_N(\widehat\beta_N^{\,c}-\beta) = \frac{M_N(T)}{J_N}.
        \end{equation*}
    Since $J_N\to J_\infty>0$ in probability, Slutsky's theorem yields
        \begin{equation*}
            r_N(\widehat\beta_N^{\,c}-\beta) \Longrightarrow \mathcal N(0,J_\infty^{-1}),
        \end{equation*}
    as claimed.

Fix $p\in\{2,4,8\}$ and write
        \begin{equation*}
            m_{p,N}(t) :=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N|X_t^{i,N}|^p =\int_{\mathbb R}|x|^p\,\mu_t^N(dx).
        \end{equation*}
    Throughout the proof, constants may depend on $p$, $\beta$ and the coefficient bounds, and also on $T$ when indicated, but are independent of $N$ and of the localization levels. Since $p$ is even, $|x|^p=x^p$. Applying It\^o's formula to each particle coordinate gives
        \begin{align*}
            d(X_t^{i,N})^p &= p\sqrt{\varepsilon_N}(X_t^{i,N})^{p-1} \sigma(X_t^{i,N})\,dB_t^i + p(X_t^{i,N})^{p-1}b(X_t^{i,N})\,dt\\
            &\quad + \frac{p(p-1)\varepsilon_N}{2} (X_t^{i,N})^{p-2}\sigma^2(X_t^{i,N})\,dt + \frac{p\beta}{N}\sum_{j\ne i} \frac{(X_t^{i,N})^{p-1}H(X_t^{i,N},X_t^{j,N})}{X_t^{i,N}-X_t^{j,N}}\,dt.
        \end{align*}
    The singular drift is understood in the integrated sense. This application of It\^o's formula is justified by the assumed integrability of the singular drift terms. We now sum over the particles and divide by $N$. Symmetry of $H$ allows us to combine the terms indexed by $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$. For a configuration with distinct coordinates,
        \begin{equation*}
            \sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j\ne i} \frac{x_i^{p-1}H(x_i,x_j)}{x_i-x_j} = \sum_{i<j}H(x_i,x_j) \frac{x_i^{p-1}-x_j^{p-1}}{x_i-x_j} = \sum_{i<j}H(x_i,x_j)\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}x^{p-2-k}y^k,
        \end{equation*}
    which provides the continuous extension to the diagonal $\{x=y\}$ Consequently,
        \begin{equation*}
            m_{p,N}(t) = m_{p,N}(0)+\int_0^t d_{p,N}(s)\,ds+L_{p,N}(t),
        \end{equation*}
    where the drift is
        \begin{align*}
            d_{p,N}(s) &= \frac pN\sum_{i=1}^N (X_s^{i,N})^{p-1}b(X_s^{i,N})+ \frac{p(p-1)\varepsilon_N}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^N(X_s^{i,N})^{p-2}\sigma^2(X_s^{i,N})\\
            &\quad+ \frac{p\beta}{N^2}\sum_{i<j} H(X_s^{i,N},X_s^{j,N}) \frac{(X_s^{i,N})^{p-1}-(X_s^{j,N})^{p-1}}{X_s^{i,N}-X_s^{j,N}},
        \end{align*}
    with the preceding continuous interpretation on the diagonal, and the continuous local martingale is
        \begin{equation*}
            L_{p,N}(t) := \frac{p\sqrt{\varepsilon_N}}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N\int_0^t(X_s^{i,N})^{p-1}\sigma(X_s^{i,N})\,dB_s^i.
        \end{equation*}

    \medskip

    We first estimate the drift. The growth assumptions imply
        \begin{equation*}
            |x^{p-1}b(x)|+|x|^{p-2}\sigma^2(x)\leq K_p(1+|x|^p).
        \end{equation*}
    For the interaction term, the polynomial representation and the bound on $H$ give
        \begin{align*}
            H(x,y) \left|\frac{x^{p-1}-y^{p-1}}{x-y}\right| \leq K_p(1+|x|)(1+|y|)(1+|x|+|y|)^{p-2}\leq K_p(1+|x|^p+|y|^p).
        \end{align*}
    The same bound holds on the diagonal by continuity. Each term $|X_s^{i,N}|^p$ occurs in $N-1$ unordered pairs, so
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac1{N^2}\sum_{i<j} \bigl(1+|X_s^{i,N}|^p+|X_s^{j,N}|^p\bigr) \leq 1+ \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N|X_s^{i,N}|^p = 1+m_{p,N}(s).
        \end{equation*}
    Using also $\varepsilon_N\leq1$, we obtain
        \begin{equation*}
            |d_{p,N}(s)| \leq K_p(1+m_{p,N}(s)).
        \end{equation*}
    Independence of the driving Brownian motions gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \langle L_{p,N}\rangle_t = \frac{p^2\varepsilon_N}{N^2} \sum_{i=1}^N\int_0^t |X_s^{i,N}|^{2p-2} \sigma^2(X_s^{i,N})\,ds.
        \end{equation*}
    Hence, by the growth bound on $\sigma$,
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{d}{dt}\langle L_{p,N}\rangle_t \leq \frac{K_p\varepsilon_N}{N^2} \sum_{i=1}^N(1+|X_t^{i,N}|^p)^2 \leq \frac{K_p}{N^2} \left(\sum_{i=1}^N (1+|X_t^{i,N}|^p)\right)^2 = K_p(1+m_{p,N}(t))^2.
        \end{equation*}
    To use these estimates without assuming integrability of higher moments, put
        \begin{equation*}
            V_N(t):=1+m_{p,N}(t),\qquad \tau_R:=\inf\{t\geq0:V_N(t)\geq R\}, \qquad R>1.
        \end{equation*}
    We suppress the dependence of $\tau_R$ on $p$ and $N$. Before $\tau_R$, the quadratic variation density of $L_{p,N}$ is bounded by $K_pR^2$. Thus $L_{p,N}(t\wedge\tau_R)$ is a square-integrable martingale on $[0,T]$ and has expectation zero. Notice also that $\tau_R=0$ when $V_N(0)\geq R$,  and continuity gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup_{t\leq T}V_N(t\wedge\tau_R) \leq \max\{R,V_N(0)\}.
        \end{equation*}
    In particular, this stopped supremum is integrable. Taking expectations in the stopped moment equation yields
        \begin{align*}
            \mathbb E V_N(t\wedge\tau_R) &\leq \mathbb E V_N(0) + K_p\int_0^t \mathbb E\bigl[\mathbf1_{\{s<\tau_R\}}V_N(s) \bigr]\,ds\\
                                         &\leq \mathbb E V_N(0) + K_p\int_0^t \mathbb E V_N(s\wedge\tau_R)\,ds.
        \end{align*}
    Initial Condition~\ref{ass:initial:moments} gives $\sup_N\mathbb E V_N(0)<\infty$, since $p\leq8$. Gronwall's lemma therefore implies
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup_N\sup_{R>1}\sup_{t\leq T} \mathbb E V_N(t\wedge\tau_R) \leq K_{p,T}.
        \end{equation*}
    To control the supremum in time, we apply the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality and then Young's inequality:
        \begin{align*}
            \mathbb E\sup_{t\leq T} |L_{p,N}(t\wedge\tau_R)| &\leq K_p\mathbb E \left(\int_0^{T\wedge\tau_R}V_N(s)^2\,ds \right)^{1/2}\\
                &\leq K_p\mathbb E\left[\left(\sup_{t\leq T}V_N(t\wedge\tau_R)\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_0^T V_N(s\wedge\tau_R)\,ds\right)^{1/2} \right]\\
                &\leq \frac12\mathbb E\sup_{t\leq T} V_N(t\wedge\tau_R) + K_p\int_0^T \mathbb E V_N(s\wedge\tau_R)\,ds.
        \end{align*}
    Combining this with the stopped moment equation and the drift estimate gives
        \begin{align*}
            \mathbb E\sup_{t\leq T}V_N(t\wedge\tau_R) &\leq \mathbb E V_N(0) + K_p\int_0^T \mathbb E V_N(s\wedge\tau_R)\,ds + \mathbb E\sup_{t\leq T} |L_{p,N}(t\wedge\tau_R)|\\
                &\leq \frac12\mathbb E\sup_{t\leq T} V_N(t\wedge\tau_R)+K_{p,T}.
        \end{align*}
    Absorbing the supremum term proves a bound independent of $N$ and $R$. Since the solutions are global and continuous, $\tau_R>T$ for all sufficiently large $R$ on each sample path. Fatou's lemma now gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup_N\mathbb E\left[ \sup_{t\leq T} \int_{\mathbb R}|x|^p\,\mu_t^N(dx) \right]<\infty.
        \end{equation*}
    This proves \eqref{eq:continuous-uniform-moments}. 

    \medskip

    Tightness of the empirical measure processes in $C([0,T],\mathcal P(\mathbb R))$ and the limiting equation stated in the introduction follow from \cite[Theorem~8 and the discussion following (2.25)]{Mal26}, applied with pair kernel $\beta H$ and noise coefficient $\sqrt{\varepsilon_N}\sigma$. The required coefficient, initial and solution assumptions are included in the hypotheses of the present lemma. We now establish the moment bound for a limiting path. For each fixed $t$, the map
        \begin{equation*}
            \nu\longmapsto \int_{\mathbb R}|x|^8\,\nu_t(dx)
        \end{equation*}
    is lower semicontinuous for weak convergence of measure-valued paths. Its supremum over $t\in[0,T]$ is therefore also lower semicontinuous. If $\mu^{N_k}\Rightarrow\mu$, the Portmanteau theorem and \eqref{eq:continuous-uniform-moments} give
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb E\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\int_{\mathbb R}|x|^8\,\mu_t(dx)\right] \leq \liminf_{k\to\infty} \mathbb E\left[\sup_{t\leq T}\int_{\mathbb R}|x|^8\,\mu_t^{N_k}(dx)\right] <\infty.
        \end{equation*}
    In particular, the supremum inside the expectation is finite almost surely, proving \eqref{eq:continuous-limit-moment}. It remains to prove convergence of the observables. Fix a subsequence with $\mu^{N_k}\Rightarrow\mu$. On a Skorokhod representation space, retaining the same notation, we may assume that
        \begin{equation*}
            \mu^{N_k}\longrightarrow\mu \quad\text{almost surely in } C([0,T],\mathcal P(\mathbb R)).
        \end{equation*}
    All the preceding moment bounds remain valid on this space because the laws are unchanged. For $R\geq1$, choose a continuous function $\chi_R$
    with values in $[0,1]$, equal to one on $[-R,R]$ and equal to zero outside $[-2R,2R]$. Define
        \begin{align*}
            \mathcal C_R(\nu) &:= \int_{\mathbb R} \chi_R(x)x^2\sigma^2(x)\,\nu(dx),\\ 
            \mathcal A_R(\nu) &:= \frac12\int_{\mathbb R^2} \chi_R(x)\chi_R(y)H(x,y)\,\nu(dx)\nu(dy).
        \end{align*}
    The truncated integrands are continuous and compactly supported. Weak convergence of the paths, together with continuity of the product-measure map, therefore gives, for each fixed $R$,
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup_{t\leq T} |\mathcal C_R(\mu_t^{N_k}) - \mathcal C_R(\mu_t)| \longrightarrow0, \qquad 
            \sup_{t\leq T} |\mathcal A_R(\mu_t^{N_k}) - \mathcal A_R(\mu_t)| \longrightarrow0
        \end{equation*}
    almost surely. In the next step, we control the omitted tails uniformly in time. The growth assumptions imply
        \begin{equation*}
            x^2\sigma^2(x)\leq K(1+|x|^4), \qquad H(x,y)\leq K(1+x^2+y^2).
        \end{equation*}
    Since $R\geq1$, it follows that
        \begin{align*}
            (1-\chi_R(x))x^2\sigma^2(x)  &\leq KR^{-4}|x|^8,\\
            (1-\chi_R(x)\chi_R(y))H(x,y) &\leq KR^{-6}(|x|^8+|y|^8).
        \end{align*}
    For the second inequality, the left-hand side vanishes unless $\max\{|x|,|y|\}>R$, and on that set
        \begin{equation*}
            1+x^2+y^2 \leq 3\max\{|x|,|y|\}^2 \leq 3R^{-6}(|x|^8+|y|^8).
        \end{equation*}
    Consequently, for every probability measure $\nu$ with finite eighth moment,
        \begin{align*}
            0\leq\mathcal C(\nu)-\mathcal C_R(\nu) &\leq KR^{-4} \int_{\mathbb R}|x|^8\,\nu(dx),\\
            0\leq\mathcal A(\nu)-\mathcal A_R(\nu) &\leq KR^{-6} \int_{\mathbb R}|x|^8\,\nu(dx).
        \end{align*}
    The moment bounds thus control the expected supremum of these errors by $K_TR^{-4}$ and $K_TR^{-6}$, respectively, both for $\mu^{N_k}$, uniformly in $k$, and for $\mu$. The triangle inequality gives
        \begin{align*}
            \sup_{t\leq T}
            |\mathcal C(\mu_t^{N_k})-\mathcal C(\mu_t)|
            & \leq
            \sup_{t\leq T}
            |\mathcal C_R(\mu_t^{N_k})
              -\mathcal C_R(\mu_t)|
            +
            \sup_{t\leq T}
            |\mathcal C(\mu_t^{N_k})
              -\mathcal C_R(\mu_t^{N_k})|\\
            &\quad +
            \sup_{t\leq T}
            |\mathcal C(\mu_t)-\mathcal C_R(\mu_t)|.
        \end{align*}
    The first term tends to zero almost surely for fixed $R$, while Markov's inequality controls the other two terms by their expected suprema. Letting first $k\to\infty$ and then $R\to\infty$, and arguing identically for $\mathcal A$, proves
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup_{t\leq T}|\mathcal C(\mu_t^{N_k})-\mathcal C(\mu_t)| \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0, \qquad 
            \sup_{t\leq T} |\mathcal A(\mu_t^{N_k})-\mathcal A(\mu_t)| \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0.
        \end{equation*}
    Moreover, for a fixed limiting path satisfying \eqref{eq:continuous-limit-moment}, the same tail estimates show that $\mathcal C_R(\mu_t)$ and $\mathcal A_R(\mu_t)$ converge uniformly in time as $R\to\infty$. Each truncated observable is continuous in time. Their limits $\mathcal C(\mu_t)$ and $\mathcal A(\mu_t)$ are therefore finite and continuous.

    Finally, symmetry of $H$ gives the exact identity
        \begin{equation*}
            A_N(t) = \mathcal A(\mu_t^N) - \frac1{2N}\int_{\mathbb R} H(x,x)\,\mu_t^N(dx).
        \end{equation*}
    Since $H(x,x)\leq K(1+x^2)$, we obtain
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb E\left[\sup_{t\leq T} |A_N(t)-\mathcal A(\mu_t^N)| \right] \leq \frac K N \left(1+\mathbb E\sup_{t\leq T}m_{2,N}(t)\right) \longrightarrow0.
        \end{equation*}
    Together with $C_N(t)=\mathcal C(\mu_t^N)$ and the preceding convergence on the representation space, this proves the joint convergence \eqref{eq:continuous-joint-observable-convergence}.

Fix $\mu\in\mathfrak L$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:continuous-observable-convergence}, the functions $t\mapsto\mathcal A(\mu_t)$ and $t\mapsto\mathcal C(\mu_t)$ are finite and continuous. Together with \eqref{eq:continuous-limit-positivity}, this gives
        \begin{equation*}
            0<\mathcal J(\mu) =\int_0^T \frac{\mathcal A(\mu_t)^2}{\mathcal C(\mu_t)}\,dt <\infty \qquad\text{almost surely}.
        \end{equation*}
    Consider a subsequence for which $\mu^{N_k}\Rightarrow\mu$. The maps
        \begin{equation*}
            (a,c)\longmapsto \int_0^T\frac{a(t)^2}{c(t)}\,dt, \qquad c\longmapsto \left(\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}c(t)\right)^{-1}
        \end{equation*}
    are continuous in the uniform topology at every pair of continuous functions satisfying $\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}c(t)>0$. Applying the continuous mapping theorem to \eqref{eq:continuous-joint-observable-convergence} therefore yields
        \begin{equation}
            \label{eq:continuous-information-subsequence}
            J_{N_k}\Longrightarrow\mathcal J(\mu).
        \end{equation}
    Since $\mathcal J(\mu)>0$ almost surely, this convergence holds jointly with
        \begin{equation*}
            \left( J_{N_k}^{-1}, \left(\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}C_{N_k}(t)\right)^{-1}\right) \Longrightarrow \left(\mathcal J(\mu)^{-1},\left(\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathcal C(\mu_t)\right)^{-1}\right).
        \end{equation*}
    All these limiting random variables are finite almost surely. By tightness of the empirical measure processes, every subsequence admits a further subsequence along which the preceding convergence applies. Consequently, the sequence of random vectors
        \begin{equation*}
            \left( J_N,\, J_N^{-1},\, \left(\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}C_N(t)\right)^{-1} \right)
        \end{equation*}
    is tight in $\mathbb R^3$. Thus, for every $\eta\in(0,1)$, there exists a deterministic $K_\eta\geq1$ such that, for all sufficiently large $N$,
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb P\left( J_N>K_\eta \ \text{or}\  J_N^{-1}>K_\eta \ \text{or}\  \left(\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}C_N(t)\right)^{-1} > K_\eta \right)\leq\eta.
        \end{equation*}
    Taking the complementary event gives exactly \eqref{eq:continuous-information-tightness}. We next prove \eqref{eq:continuous-information-divergence}. Fix $R>0$ and $\eta\in(0,1)$, and choose $K_\eta$ as above. Since $0<\varepsilon_N\leq1$,
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{N}{\varepsilon_NK_\eta}\longrightarrow\infty.
        \end{equation*}
    Hence, for all sufficiently large $N$, the event $\{J_N\geq K_\eta^{-1}\}$ implies $(N/\varepsilon_N)J_N>R$. Therefore,
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb P\left(\frac{N}{\varepsilon_N}J_N\leq R \right) \leq \mathbb P(J_N<K_\eta^{-1}) \leq\eta.
        \end{equation*}
    Since $\eta$ is arbitrary, this proves convergence to infinity in probability.

    Finally, suppose that every reachable limit satisfies $\mathcal J(\mu)=J_\infty$ almost surely for the same deterministic constant $J_\infty\in(0,\infty)$. Every subsequence then admits a further subsequence along which \eqref{eq:continuous-information-subsequence} gives convergence in distribution to $J_\infty$.         \end{equation}
    Write $\mathbb E_m[\cdot]=\mathbb E[\cdot\mid\mathcal F_{t_m}^N]$. We verify the following three properties of the array.

\medskip

    \noindent\textit{(i) Vanishing conditional expectations.}
    The prefactor is bounded and $\mathcal F_{t_m}^N$-measurable. Since $\sup_{N,t\leq T}\mathbb E C_N(t)<\infty$, the stochastic integral is square-integrable and
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb E_m[\xi_{m,N}] = 0.
        \end{equation*}

\medskip

    \noindent\textit{(ii) Convergence of the conditional variances to $J_\infty$.} Applying It\^o's isometry conditionally gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb E_m[\xi_{m,N}^2] =(w_{N,m}^{*})^2 \int_{t_m}^{t_{m+1}}\mathbb E_m[C_N(s)]\,ds.
        \end{equation*}
    Adding and subtracting $C_N(t_m)$, we decompose the sum as
        \begin{equation*}
            \sum_m\mathbb E_m[\xi_{m,N}^2] = \Delta_n\sum_m(w_{N,m}^{*})^2C_N(t_m)+D_{N,n}^{*},
        \end{equation*}
    where
        \begin{equation*}
            D_{N,n}^{*}:=\sum_m(w_{N,m}^{*})^2 \int_{t_m}^{t_{m+1}}\mathbb E_m[C_N(s)-C_N(t_m)]\,ds.
        \end{equation*}
    The first term equals $J_{N,n}^{\,d}$ whenever no weight is truncated, and hence converges in probability to $J_\infty$. For the remainder, conditional Jensen's inequality and \eqref{eq:discrete-integrated-increment-bound} give
        \begin{align*}
            \mathbb E|D_{N,n}^{*}| &\leq K_N^2\sum_m\int_{t_m}^{t_{m+1}} \mathbb E|C_N(s)-C_N(t_m)|\,ds\\
            &\leq K_TK_N^2(\Delta_n+\delta_N\sqrt{\Delta_n}) = K_T(\Delta_n^{3/4}+\delta_N\Delta_n^{1/4}) \longrightarrow0.
        \end{align*}
    Thus $D_{N,n}^{*}\to0$ in $L^1$, and
        \begin{equation*}
            \sum_m\mathbb E_m[\xi_{m,N}^2] \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}J_\infty.
        \end{equation*}

\medskip

    \noindent\textit{(iii) Lyapunov condition: vanishing fourth conditional moments.}
    The conditional Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz yield
        \begin{align*}
            \mathbb E_m[\xi_{m,N}^4] &\leq K(w_{N,m}^{*})^4 \mathbb E_m\left[\left(\int_{t_m}^{t_{m+1}}C_N(s)\,ds\right)^2\right]\\
            &\leq KK_N^4\Delta_n  \int_{t_m}^{t_{m+1}}\mathbb E_m[C_N(s)^2]\,ds.
        \end{align*}
    Taking the unconditional expectation of the sum and applying \eqref{eq:discrete-observable-moments}, we obtain
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbb E\left[\sum_m\mathbb E_m[\xi_{m,N}^4]\right] \leq K_TK_N^4\Delta_n = K_T\sqrt{\Delta_n}\longrightarrow0.
        \end{equation*}  
    In particular, for every $a>0$,
        \begin{equation*}
            \sum_m\mathbb E_m[\xi_{m,N}^2\mathbf1_{\{|\xi_{m,N}|>a\}}] \leq a^{-2}\sum_m\mathbb E_m[\xi_{m,N}^4] \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0.
        \end{equation*}
    This verifies the conditional Lindeberg condition.

    The array is conditionally centered, its conditional variance converges to $J_\infty$, and it satisfies the conditional Lindeberg condition. The deterministic-variance martingale difference array central limit theorem therefore gives
        \begin{equation*}
            \sum_m\xi_{m,N}\xrightarrow{\mathcal L} \mathcal N(0,J_\infty);
        \end{equation*}
    see \cite[Chapter~3]{HalHey80}. By \eqref{eq:discrete-array-localization}, the same limit holds for $L_{N,n}(T)$. Since $J_{N,n}^{\,d}\to J_\infty$ in probability, Slutsky's theorem yields
        \begin{equation*}
            r_NM_{N,n}=\frac{L_{N,n}(T)}{J_{N,n}^{\,d}} \xrightarrow{\mathcal L}\mathcal N(0,J_\infty^{-1}).
        \end{equation*}
    Finally, $r_NR_{N,n}\to0$ in probability, so a second application of Slutsky's theorem completes the proof:
        \begin{equation*}
            r_N(\widehat\beta_{N,n}^{\,d}-\beta) \xrightarrow{\mathcal L}\mathcal N(0,J_\infty^{-1}).
        \end{equation*}
    Here $r_N=\sqrt{N/\varepsilon_N}$ in the strictly positive regime, whereas $r_N=N$ in the degenerate regime. The respective mesh conditions are therefore \eqref{eq:intro-discrete-mesh-condition} and \eqref{eq:degenerate-discrete-mesh}.

Let $E$ be the invariant interval from Condition~\ref{ass:discrete-regularity}. We apply It\^o's formula to the $C^2$ extensions of the observable functions and use the same notation for these extensions. All coefficient evaluations and interpolating segments below lie in $E$ or $E^2$, so only the derivative bounds on that interval are used.

    We use the empirical moment bounds from Lemma~\ref{lem:continuous-observable-convergence}; no exchangeability of the ordered coordinates is required. In the notation $m_{p,N}(t):=\langle\mu_t^N,|x|^p\rangle$, these give
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup_N\mathbb E\sup_{t\leq T}m_{8,N}(t)<\infty.
        \end{equation*}

    \medskip
    
    \noindent\textit{Uniform moments of the observables.} The coefficient bounds imply
        \begin{equation*}
            |A_N(t)|+|B_N(t)|\leq K(1+m_{2,N}(t)), \qquad |C_N(t)|\leq K(1+m_{4,N}(t)).
        \end{equation*}
    Jensen's inequality gives $m_{2,N}^4\leq m_{8,N}$ and $m_{4,N}^2\leq m_{8,N}$. Taking suprema and expectations proves \eqref{eq:discrete-observable-moments}.

    \medskip
    
    \noindent\textit{The one-particle observables $B_N$ and $C_N$.} For $f=\phi_{B,N}$ or $f=\phi_C$, apply It\^o's formula to $N^{-1}\sum_i f(X_t^{i,N})$. Writing $x_i=X_t^{i,N}$ in the drift expression, symmetrization gives
        \begin{align*}
            D_N^f(t) &=\frac1N\sum_i f'(x_i)b(x_i) +\frac{\varepsilon_N}{2N}\sum_i f''(x_i)\sigma^2(x_i) + \frac{\beta}{2N^2}\sum_{i\neq j}H(x_i,x_j)\mathcal D_f(x_i,x_j), \\
            M_N^f(t) &=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_N}}{N}\sum_i \int_0^t f'(X_s^{i,N})\sigma(X_s^{i,N})\,dB_s^i.
        \end{align*}
    Here $\mathcal D_f$ is the divided difference defined in Section~\ref{s:continuous}. The derivative bounds in Condition~\ref{ass:discrete-regularity} imply
        \begin{equation*}
            |\mathcal D_{\phi_{B,N}}(x,y)|\leq K, \qquad |\mathcal D_{\phi_C}(x,y)|\leq K(1+x^2+y^2).
        \end{equation*}
    Combining these bounds with Condition~\ref{ass:bounds} and Young's inequality yields
        \begin{equation*}
            |D_N^B(t)|\leq K(1+m_{2,N}(t)), \qquad |D_N^C(t)|\leq K(1+m_{4,N}(t)).
        \end{equation*}
    Their squared expectations are uniformly bounded. Moreover,
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{d}{dt}\langle M_N^f\rangle_t = \frac{\varepsilon_N}{N^2}\sum_i \bigl(f'(x_i)\sigma(x_i)\bigr)^2.
        \end{equation*}
    The integrand has growth at most degree four for $f=\phi_{B,N}$ and degree eight for $f=\phi_C$. Therefore,
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{d}{dt}\langle M_N^B\rangle_t \leq K\delta_N^2(1+m_{4,N}(t)), \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\langle M_N^C\rangle_t \leq K\delta_N^2(1+m_{8,N}(t)).
        \end{equation*}

    \medskip
    
    \noindent\textit{The two-particle observable $A_N$.} Applying multidimensional It\^o's formula to $A_N=(2N^2)^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}H(X^{i,N},X^{j,N})$ gives $dA_N=D_N^A\,dt+dM_N^A$. Its regular drift is
        \begin{equation*}
            D_{N,\mathrm{reg}}^A(t) = \frac1{N^2}\sum_{i\neq j}\partial_1H(x_i,x_j)b(x_i) + \frac{\varepsilon_N}{2N^2}\sum_{i\neq j} \partial_{11}H(x_i,x_j)\sigma^2(x_i).
        \end{equation*}
    The symmetry of $H$ combines the two singular drift sums into
        \begin{equation*}
            D_{N,\mathrm{sing}}^A(t) = \frac{\beta}{N^3}\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{k\neq i} \partial_1H(x_i,x_j)\frac{H(x_i,x_k)}{x_i-x_k}.
        \end{equation*}
    Split the sum into $S_1$, corresponding to $j=k$, and $S_2$, corresponding to pairwise distinct indices. Swapping $i,k$ and averaging gives exactly
        \begin{align}
            S_1 &=\frac{\beta}{2N^3}\sum_{i\neq k}H(x_i,x_k) \frac{\partial_1H(x_i,x_k)-\partial_2H(x_i,x_k)}{x_i-x_k},\\
            S_2 &=\frac{\beta}{2N^3} \sum_{\substack{i,j,k\\\text{pairwise distinct}}}H(x_i,x_k) \frac{\partial_1H(x_i,x_j)-\partial_1H(x_k,x_j)}{x_i-x_k}.
        \end{align}
    Both singularities are removable. For the first quotient, symmetry gives $\partial_1H(y,y)=\partial_2H(y,y)$ and hence
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\partial_1H(x,y)-\partial_2H(x,y)}{x-y} = \int_0^1(\partial_{11}H-\partial_{12}H) (y+\theta(x-y),y)\,d\theta.
        \end{equation*}
    For the second quotient,
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\partial_1H(x,y)-\partial_1H(z,y)}{x-z} = \int_0^1\partial_{11}H(z+\theta(x-z),y)\,d\theta.
        \end{equation*}
    These formulas also define the continuous extensions to the diagonals. By Condition~\ref{ass:discrete-regularity}, their absolute values are bounded by $K(1+x^2+y^2)$ and $K(1+x^2+y^2+z^2)$, respectively. After multiplication by $H$ in $S_1,S_2$, every bounding monomial has total degree at most four. Young's inequality and the factors $N^{-2}$ and $N^{-3}$ in the empirical sums consequently give
        \begin{equation*}
            |D_N^A(t)|\leq K(1+m_{4,N}(t)).
        \end{equation*}
    Since $m_{4,N}^2\leq m_{8,N}$, this proves the required squared-drift bound for $A_N$.

    Grouping the stochastic integrals by their driving Brownian motions gives
        \begin{equation*}
            M_N^A(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_N}}{N^2}\sum_i \int_0^t\sigma(X_s^{i,N}) \left(\sum_{j\neq i}\partial_1H(X_s^{i,N},X_s^{j,N})\right)dB_s^i.
        \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
        \begin{align*}
            \frac{d}{dt}\langle M_N^A\rangle_t &=\frac{\varepsilon_N}{N^4}\sum_i\sigma^2(x_i)\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\partial_1H(x_i,x_j)\right)^2\\
                &\leq\frac{\varepsilon_N}{N^3}\sum_{i\neq j} \sigma^2(x_i)|\partial_1H(x_i,x_j)|^2 \leq K\delta_N^2(1+m_{6,N}(t)).
        \end{align*}
    This proves \eqref{eq:discrete-drift-bound} and \eqref{eq:discrete-macroscopic-bracket-bound} for all three observables. In particular their local martingale parts are square-integrable. Finally, for each $F\in\{A,B,C\}$, Cauchy-Schwarz and It\^o's isometry give
        \begin{align*}
            \mathbb E|F_N(t)-F_N(s)|^2 &\leq2(t-s)\int_s^t\mathbb E|D_N^F(u)|^2\,du +2\mathbb E\bigl[\langle M_N^F\rangle_t-\langle M_N^F\rangle_s \bigr]\\
            &\leq K_T\bigl((t-s)^2+\delta_N^2(t-s)\bigr).
        \end{align*}
    Summation over $F$ proves \eqref{eq:discrete-macroscopic-increments}. All It\^o identities are first justified by localization. In the non-sticky regime the symmetrizations hold for Lebesgue-a.e. time, which suffices for the integrated identities; their continuous divided-difference extensions justify all bounds at collision configurations as well.
