<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{2-trans}
Let $D$ be a transitive digraph. Then $D = H[S_1, S_2, \dots, S_r]$ where $H$ is 
an acyclic transitive digraph and $S_i$ is a complete digraph for each 
$i = 1, 2, \dots, r$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\cite{BH} \label{2-quasi-trans}
Let $D$ be a quasi-transitive digraph. 
\begin{itemize}
\item If $D$ is non-strong, then $D = H[S_1, S_2, \dots, S_r]$ where $r \geq 2$, $H$ is 
an acyclic transitive digraph, and $S_i$ is a strong quasi-transitive digraph for each
$i = 1, 2, \dots, r$.
\item If $D$ is strong, then $D = H[S_1, S_2, \dots, S_r]$ where $H$ is a strong
semicomplete digraph and $S_i$ is either a vertex or a non-strong quasi-transitive
digraph for each $i = 1, 2, \dots, r$.
\end{itemize}

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
A digraph is called {\em semi-acyclic} if it has no directed cycle of length other than 
$2$ and the $2$-cycles are pairwise vertex-disjoint. For convenience we use 
$\langle x,y\rangle$ to denote two cycle with endpoints $x, y$ (i.e., $(x,y), (y,x)$ 
are {\em symmetric} arcs).

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\cite{HC} \label{strong-3-trans}
Let $D$ be a 3-transitive digraph. If $D$ is strong then it is either complete or 
complete bipartite or a directed triangle with at most two symmetric arcs.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lemhash}
Let $D=(V,E)$ be a $3$-transitive digraph and let $D^\#$ be the skeleton of $D$.
Suppose that $M\in V^\#$ and $x \in V\setminus M$. Then
\begin{enumerate}
\item $x\rightarrow z$ in $D$ for some $z\in M$ $\Longrightarrow$ $x\longmapsto M$.
\item $z\rightarrow x$ in $D$ for some $z\in M$ $\Longrightarrow$ $M \longmapsto x$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $x\rightarrow z$ for some $z\in M$. The statement is trivial for $M\in\mathscr{S}_1$. Let $M\in \mathscr{S}_3$. Then $M$ is either complete with at least $3$ vertices or a directed triangle with at most two symmetric arcs. In the first case, for any $y\in M$, we have $x\rightarrow z\rightarrow y^\prime\rightarrow y$ for some $y^\prime\in M$ such that $y^\prime\neq\left\{y,z\right\}$. Since $|M|\geq 3$, such a $y^\prime$ always exists. Thus $x\rightarrow y$ in $D$. In the second case, let $(z,z_1,z_2,z)$ be the directed triangle. Then $x\rightarrow z\rightarrow z_1 \rightarrow z_2$ holds in $D$ which implies $x\rightarrow z_2$ in $D$. Also $x\rightarrow z_2\rightarrow z\rightarrow z_1$ in $D$ implies $x\rightarrow z_1$ in $D$. Thus $x\rightarrow y$ for all $y\in M$.

Next, let $M=X_S$ For some $S\in\mathscr{S}_2$. If $|X_S|=1$, we have nothing to prove. Let $|X_S|>1$ and $y\in X_S$. Since $Y_S\neq\emptyset$, let $z_1\in Y_S$. Then $x\rightarrow z\rightarrow z_1\rightarrow y$ in $D$ as $S$ is complete bipartite. This implies $x\rightarrow y$. The case is similar for $M=Y_S$.

Suppose $z\rightarrow x$ for some $z\in M$. As before, the result is trivial for $M\in\mathscr{S}_1$. Let $M\in\mathscr{S}_3$. If $M$ is complete with at least $3$ vertices, then for any $y,y_1\in M\smallsetminus\left\{z\right\}$, we have $y\rightarrow y_1\rightarrow z\rightarrow x$ in $D$ which implies $y\rightarrow x$ in $D$. Again let $(z,z_1,z_2,z)$ be the directed triangle in $M$. Then $z_1\rightarrow z_2\rightarrow z\rightarrow x$ in $D$ and so $z_1\rightarrow x$ in $D$. Thus $z_2\rightarrow z\rightarrow z_1\rightarrow x$ in $D$ and so $z_2\rightarrow x$ in $D$.

Finally, let $M=X_S$ and $|M|>1$. As $Y_S\neq\emptyset$, take $y^\prime\in Y_S$. Let $y\in M=X_S$. Then $y\rightarrow y^\prime\rightarrow z\rightarrow x$ in $D$ which implies $y\rightarrow x$ in $D$ as required. The proof for $M=Y_S$ is similar.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{sa1}
Let $D$ be a $3$-transitive digraph. Then $D^\#$ is a semi-acyclic $3$-transitive 
digraph.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $M,T\in V^\#$ such that $M\neq T$. If $M=X_S$ and $T=Y_S$ (or vice-versa) for some $S\in\mathscr{S}_2$, then $M\leftrightarrow T$. In all other cases, $M$ and $T$ correspond to different strong components. Thus $x\rightarrow y$ and $y\rightarrow x$ cannot happen simultaneously in $D$ for otherwise union of these components would be a strong subdigraph of $D$ which is a contradiction. The same thing happens if we assume a directed cycle in $D^\#$ of length more than $2$. Also since strong components are vertex-disjoint, we have $D^\#$ is semi-acyclic.

Let $X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow W$ in $D^\#$. Then there are $x\in X$, $y,y^\prime\in Y$, $z,z^\prime\in Z$ and $w\in W$ such that $x\rightarrow y$, $y^\prime\rightarrow z$, $z^\prime \rightarrow w$ in $D$. By Lemma \ref{lemhash}, we have $x\rightarrow y\rightarrow z\rightarrow w$ in $D$. Thus $x\rightarrow w$ in $D$ which implies $X\rightarrow W$ in $D^\#$. Therefore $D^\#$ is $3$-transitive.

<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
Let $D=(V,E)$ be an acyclic $3$-transitive digraph. An arc $uv\in E$ is called 
{\em flexible} if replacing the arc $uv$ by $vu$ the digraph remains to be acyclic and 
$3$-transitive.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{flprop}
Let $D=(V,E)$ be an acyclic $3$-transitive digraph. Then $uv\in E$ is flexible if and only if the following conditions hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(u,v)$ is the only directed path from $u$ to $v$.
\item $N^-(v)\longmapsto N^+(u)$.
\item $N^{--}(v)\longmapsto u$.
\item $v\longmapsto N^{++}(u)$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $D=(V,E)$ be an acyclic $3$-transitive digraph and $uv\in E$. Let $D^\prime=(V,E^\prime)$, where $E^\prime =(E\smallsetminus\left\{uv\right\})\cup\left\{vu\right\}$. Suppose $uv\in E$ is a flexible arc in $D$. Then $D^\prime$ is acyclic and $3$-transitive.

If there is a directed path $(u=x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n=v)$ ($n>1$) from $u$ to $v$ other than $(u,v)$, then after replacing $uv$ by $vu$, we have a directed cycle $(u=x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n=v,u)$ and so $D^\prime$ is not acyclic. Therefore $uv$ is the only directed path from $u$ to $v$.

Let $x\in N^-(v)$, $y\in N^+(u)$. Then $xv,vu,uy\in E^\prime$, then $xy\in E^\prime$ as $D^\prime$ is $3$-transitive. Then $xy\in E$ as $\left\{x,y\right\}\neq \left\{u,v\right\}$. So we have $N^-(v)\longmapsto N^+(u)$ in $D$. Next, if $x\in N^{--}(v)$, then $xw,wv,vu\in E^\prime$ for some $w\in V$. This implies $xu\in E^\prime$ and so $xu\in E$ as $x\neq v$. Thus $N^{--}(v)\longmapsto u$. Finally, if $x\in N^{++}(u)$, then $vu,uw,wx\in E^\prime$ for some $w\in V$, which implies $vx\in E^\prime$ and hence $vx\in E$ as $x\neq u$. Thus we have $v\longmapsto N^{++}(u)$.

Conversely, let $D=(V,E)$ be an acyclic $3$-transitive digraph and $uv\in E$ satisfying the given conditions. Let $D^\prime=(V,E^\prime)$, where $E^\prime =(E\smallsetminus\left\{uv\right\})\cup\left\{vu\right\}$. Now, since $D$ had no directed cycle, $D^\prime$ would also be free from directed cycle unless the arc $vu$ creates one. But then there is a directed path from $u$ to $v$ in $D$ other than $(u,v)$ which violets the first condition. Therefore $D^\prime$ is acyclic. 

All the three other conditions ensures that $D$ remains to be $3$-transitive after replacing the arc $uv$ by $vu$ to obtain $D^\prime$. In fact, let $x,y,z,w\in V$ such that $xy,yz,zw\in E^\prime$. If $vu\not\in\left\{xy,yz,zw\right\}$, then $xy,yz,zw\in E$. So $xw\in E$. Now $xw\neq uv$ for otherwise we would have a directed path $(u,y,z,v)$ which again violets the first condition. Thus $xw\in E^\prime$. Now, if $vu=xy$, then $w\in N^{++}(u)$ and since $v\longmapsto N^{++}(u)$, we have $xw=vw\in E$ and hence in $E^\prime$ also as $vw\neq uv$. Next, if $vu=yz$, then $x\in N^-(v)$ and $w\in N^+(u)$ and so $xw\in E$ as $N^-(v)\longmapsto N^+(u)$. Again $xw=uv$ contradicts the first condition. So we have $xw\in E^\prime$. Finally, if $vu=zw$, then $x\in N^{--}(v)$ and we have $xw=xu\in E$ as $N^{--}(v)\longmapsto u$. Since $xw=xu\neq uv$, we have $xw\in E^\prime$. Therefore $D^\prime$ is $3$-transitive as required.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
Let $D=(V,E)$ be a semi-acyclic $3$-transitive digraph. Let $\mathscr{S}$ be the collection of $2$-cycles in $D$. For each $C \in\mathscr{S}$, we replace $C = (u,v,u)$ by an arc $uv$, where $u$ and $v$ are chosen arbitrarily. Then the digraph $D^\prime=(V,E^\prime)$ obtained by this is an acyclic $3$-transitive digraph where the set $M$ of replaced arcs form a faithful matching in $D^\prime$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
We first note that $D^\prime$ is acyclic by construction as we removed all $2$-cycles and since $D$ has no other cycle, removing arcs cannot create further cycles in $D^\prime$. Suppose $x,y,z,w\in V$ such that $xy,yz,zw\in E^\prime$. Since $E^\prime\subseteq E$ and $D$ is $3$-transitive, $xw\in E$. If $(x,w,x)$ is a $2$-cycle in $D$, then $(x,y,z,w,x)$ is a cycle in $D$ which is a contradiction as $D$ is semi-acyclic and so cannot have a cycle of length more than $2$. Thus $(x,w,x)$ is not a $2$-cycle in $D$ and hence $xw$ is not deleted to form $D^\prime$, i.e., $xw\in E^\prime$. Therefore $D^\prime$ is $3$-transitive. Now since $2$-cycles in $D$ are vertex disjoint, $M$ is a matching in $D^\prime$. 

Let $(u,v,u)$ be a $2$-cycle in $D$ and we replaced $(u,v,u)$ by the arc $uv$, i.e., we removed the arc $vu$ in $D^\prime$. We show that $uv$ is a flexible arc in $D^\prime$. Since $E^\prime\subseteq E$, if there is another directed path from $u$ to $v$ in $D^\prime$, then there would be the same in $D$ and this path along with $vu$ would form a directed cycle of length more than $2$. But this is not possible as $D$ is semi-acyclic. Thus the first condition of Proposition \ref{flprop} is satisfied. The other $3$ conditions follow
from the definition of the 3-transitivity and the fact $uv,vu\in E$.

Now let $x,y,z,w\in V$ such that $yx,wz\in M$ and $yz\in E^\prime$. Then we have $xy,zw\in E$. Also as $E^\prime\subseteq E$, $yz\in E$. Since $D$ is $3$-transitive, we have $xw\in E$. Further, as $yx,wz\in M$, $xw$ or $wx$ cannot belong to $M$. Thus $xw\in E^\prime$. This implies $M$ is faithful.

Let $D=(V,E)$ be an acyclic $3$-trasitive digraph. Suppose $D$ has a faithful matching $M$. Let $E^\prime=E\cup\left\{vu\, :\, uv\in M\right\}$. Then $D^\prime =(V,E^\prime)$ is a semi-acyclic $3$-transitive digraph.

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $x,y,z,w\in V$ such that $xy,yz,zw\in E^\prime$. If $xy,yz,zw\in E$, then $xw\in E\subseteq E^\prime$. Suppose $yz\in E^\prime\smallsetminus E$. Then $zy\in M$. Since $M$ is a matching, $xy,zw\not\in M$ and thus $xy,zw\in E$. Now since $zy$ is flexible, by Proposition \ref{flprop}, $N^-(y)\longmapsto N^+(z)$. As $x\in N^-(y)$ and $w\in N^+(z)$, we have $xw\in E\subseteq E^\prime$. Next, let $xy\in E^\prime\smallsetminus E$, i.e., $yx\in M$ and $zw\in E$. Then $yz\in E$ as $xy$ and $yz$ have a common endpoint. So $w\in N^{++}(y)$ which implies $xw\in E$ as $x\longmapsto N^{++}(y)$ holds for the flexible arc $yx$. Similarly, if $xy,yz\in E$ and $wz\in M$, then $xw\in E$ as $N^{--}(z)\longmapsto w$ for the flexible arc $xw$. Finally, if $xy,zw\in E^\prime\smallsetminus E$, then $yx,wz\in M$ and $yz\in E$ and so by (\ref{extram}), we have $xw\in E$. Therefore $D^\prime$ is $3$-transitive.

Suppose $D^\prime$ has a directed cycle of length more than $2$. Since $D^\prime$ is $3$-transitive, we have a directed cycle $C$ in $D^\prime$ of length $3$ or $4$. Suppose $C$ is of length $4$. If $C$ contains only one such arc, then deleting the arc $vu$ from $C$ we obtain a directed path from $u$ to $v$ in $D$ which is a contradiction as $uv$ is a flexible arc in $D$. Again if $C$ contains arcs $vu, yx$ such that $uv,xy\in M$. Then $uy,xv\in E$ as $C=(v,u,y,x,v)$. On the other hand, we have $vx\in E$ by (\ref{extram}), which is a contradiction as $xv\in E$ and $D$ has no $2$-cycle. Therefore $D^\prime$ has only $2$-cycles and since $M$ is a matching, these $2$-cycles are vertex disjoint. Hence $D^\prime$ is semi-acyclic.

<!-- observation-1: body_md -->
\label{notrev}
Let $D=(V,E)$ be a $3$-quasi-transitive digraph. Let $S,T\in\mathscr{S}$ such that $x\rightarrow y$ for some $x\in S$ and $y\in T$, $S\neq T$. Then $y^\prime\not\rightarrow x^\prime$ for any $x^\prime\in S$ and $y^\prime\in T$.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{quasilem}
Let $D=(V,E)$ be a $3$-quasi-transitive digraph. Let $M\in V^\#$ and $x\in V\setminus$. 
Then
\begin{enumerate}
\item $x\rightarrow y$ for some $y\in M$ $\Longrightarrow x\longmapsto M$.
\item $y\rightarrow x$ for some $y\in M$ $\Longrightarrow M\longmapsto x$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We first note that, by Observation \ref{notrev}, the reverse direction is impossible throughout the proof. Hence whenever $3$-quasi-transitivity gives adjacency between $x$ and a vertex of $M$, the arc must be directed from $x$ to that vertex in case (1) and the opposite in case (2). 

{\bf (1):}\ If $M\in\mathscr{S}_1$, then we have nothing to prove. Let $M=X_S$ for some $S\in\mathscr{S}_2$. Let $x\in V\smallsetminus S$ such that $x\rightarrow y$ for some $y\in M$. Let $z\in M$. Since $S$ is a strong component of $D$ and $S=X_S\cup Y_S$ is bipartite, we have there is a directed path $y=y_0\rightarrow y_1\rightarrow y_2\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow y_{2k}=z$ for some $k\in\mathbb{N}$, where $y_i\in Y_S$ for odd $i$ and $y_i\in X_S$ for even $i$. Now we have $x\rightarrow y\rightarrow y_1\rightarrow y_2$. Then we have $x\rightarrow y_2$ by the comment made at the beginning. If $k=1$, we are done. Otherwise, we have $x\rightarrow y_2\rightarrow y_3\rightarrow y_4$. By the same argument, we have $x\rightarrow y_4$. We repeat the process until we reach $x\rightarrow y_{2k}=z$ as required. The case is similar for $M=Y_S$ for some $S\in \mathscr{S}_2$. 

Let $M\in \mathscr{S}_3$. Then $M$ is a strong semicomplete digraph or $M$ is isomorphic to $F_n$ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$. First suppose $M$ is strong and semicomplete. Let $x\in V\smallsetminus M$ such that $x\rightarrow y$ for some $y\in M$. Since $M$ is strong and semicomplete, we have $M$ is Hamiltonian. Let $y=y_0\rightarrow y_1\rightarrow y_2\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow y_k\rightarrow y$ be a Hamiltonian cycle in $M$ with $k\geq 2$ (as $|M|>2$). Now as before, we have $x\rightarrow y_i$ for all even $i$. Suppose $k>2$. Since $M$ is semicomplete, either $y_1\rightarrow y_3$ or $y_3\rightarrow y_1$. If $y_1\rightarrow y_3$, then $x\rightarrow y\rightarrow y_1\rightarrow y_3$ which implies $x\rightarrow y_3$ and hence by rotating the cycle, we have $x\rightarrow y_i$ for all odd $i$ also. Similar case happens if $y_3\rightarrow y_1$ as $x\rightarrow y_2\rightarrow y_3\rightarrow y_1$. So let $k=2$ and $M$ is a $3$-cycle $(y,y_1,y_2,y)$ with possibly some symmetric arcs. As before we have $x\rightarrow y_2$ and so we have $x\rightarrow y_2\rightarrow y\rightarrow y_1$ implying $x\rightarrow y_1$ as well. 

Finally, let $M\cong F_n$ for some $n\in \mathbb{N}$. Then every vertex of $M$ belongs to a $4$-cycle, say, $(a,b,c_i,d,a)$. Then as in the previous case, if we have $x\rightarrow a$, then $x\rightarrow a\rightarrow b\rightarrow c_i$ implies $x\rightarrow c_i$ and $x\rightarrow a\rightarrow b\rightarrow d$ implies $x\rightarrow d$. But then $x\rightarrow d\rightarrow a\rightarrow b$ implies $x\rightarrow b$. Since $c_i$ was taken arbitrarily, we have $x\rightarrow c_i$ for all $i\in [n]$. 

Similarly, $x\rightarrow b$ implies $x\rightarrow a$ as $b\rightarrow d\rightarrow a$ in $F_n$ and $x\rightarrow d$ implies $x\rightarrow b$ as $d\rightarrow a\rightarrow b$ in $F_n$. Lastly, if $x\rightarrow c_i$ for some $i\in [n]$, then we have $x\rightarrow a$ as $c_i\rightarrow d\rightarrow a$ in $F_n$. So in all cases we have $x\rightarrow a$ and hence we have $x\longmapsto M$ as shown in the above paragraph. 

{\bf (2):}\ It is interesting to note that, the converse of a 3-quasi-transitive digraph (obtained from the given digraph by reversing all the arc) is again 3-quasi-transitive and has the same strong components. Thus part (2) follows from part (1) applied to the converse digraph.

<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{sa2}
Let $D$ be a $3$-quasi-transitive graph. Then $D^\#$ is a semi-acyclic $3$-transitive 
digraph.
<!-- end theorem-8 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let $M,T\in V^\#=V(D^\#)$. Suppose $M=X_S$, $T=Y_S$ (or, $M=Y_S$, $T=X_S$) for some $S\in\mathscr{S}_2$. Now since $S$ is a strong component, there are $x_1,x_2\in M$ (may not be distinct), $y_1,y_2\in T$ (may not be distinct) such that $x_1\rightarrow y_1$ and $y_2\rightarrow x_2$. Thus we have $M\leftrightarrow T$ in $D^\#$. Otherwise $M$ and $T$ are different strong components or being part of different strong components. Thus if there is $x\rightarrow y$ for some $x\in M$ and $y\in T$, then there cannot be any $x^\prime\in M$, $y^\prime \in T$ such that $y^\prime \rightarrow x^\prime$ by Observation \ref{notrev}. Again, if there is a directed cycle in $D^\#$, then union of all of them would be a subdigraph of the same strong component. Thus the only possible cycle is a $2$-cycle. Also since strong components are vertex disjoint, these $2$-cycles (if they exist) are also vertex disjoint. Therefore $D^\#$ is semi-acyclic.

Let $X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow W$ in $D^\#$ with $X\neq W$. If the three skeleton arcs can be realized by a directed path $x\rightarrow y\rightarrow z\rightarrow w$ in D, then by $3$-quasi-transitivity of $D$, we have either $x\rightarrow w$ or $w\rightarrow x$. If $X$ and $W$ are parts of different strong components, then Observation \ref{notrev} gives $x\rightarrow w$ and consequently we get $X\rightarrow W$ in $D^\#$. If $X$ and $W$ are the two parts of the same split semicomplete bipartite component, then $X\leftrightarrow W$ holds.

The only obstruction to such a realization occurs when consecutive skeleton arcs pass back and forth between $X_S$ and $Y_S$ of the same semicomplete bipartite strong component $S$. Let us call a skeleton arc $U\rightarrow V$ in $D^\#$ as {\em special} if it is one of the two arcs between $X_S$ and $Y_S$ for some $S\in\mathscr{S}_2$ and we denote it by $U\dashrightarrow V$ and if the arc is not special, then by $U\longmapsto V$ to mean that we have $u\rightarrow v$ uniformly for all $u\in U$, $v\in V$. 

Now, if there are two consecutive special arcs, then we are just moving back and forth between $X_S$ and $Y_S$. In that case the desired conclusion $X\rightarrow W$ is immediate from one of the already existing arcs $X_S\leftrightarrow Y_S$, or from the fact that the repeated skeleton vertices force the conclusion to be one of the given arcs. For example, if we have, $X\dashrightarrow Y\dashrightarrow Z\longmapsto W$, then $X=Z=X_S$, $Y=Y_S$ (or, $X=Z=Y_S$, $Y=X_S$) for some $S\in\mathscr{S}_2$. Thus $X=Z\longmapsto W$ and so we have $X\rightarrow W$ in $D^\#$. The case $X\longmapsto Y\dashrightarrow Z\dashrightarrow W$ is similar. Let $X\dashrightarrow Y\dashrightarrow Z\dashrightarrow W$, then $X=Z=X_S$, $Y=W=Y_S$ or, $X=Z=Y_S$, $Y=W=X_S$ for some $S\in\mathscr{S}_2$. In any case, $\left\{X,W\right\}=\left\{X_S,Y_S\right\}$. Hence $X\leftrightarrow W$ in $D^\#$. Therefore $D^\#$ is $3$-transitive as required.

<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\label{def:h}
Let $D$ be a semi-acyclic digraph. For each $v\in V(D)$, we define the {\em height function} $h\, :\, V(D)\longrightarrow \mathbb{N}$ by
$$
h(v)=\max\left\{|P|\, :\, P\text{ is a path of }D\text{ ending at }v\right\},
$$
where $\mathbb{N}$ is the set of natural numbers. Thus $1\leq h(v)\leq\lambda(D)$.
<!-- end definition-4 -->
<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:ha2}
Let $D$ be a semi-acyclic digraph and let $h$ be defined as in Definition~\ref{def:h}. Then the following statements hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $uv\in E(D)$ and $vu\notin E(D)$, then $h(v)\geq h(u)+1$.
\item If $uv,vu\in E(D)$, then $|h(u)-h(v)|\leq 1$.
\item If $uv,vu\in E(D)$ and $h(u)=h(v)=t$, then every path of order $t$ ending at $u$ contains $v$, and in fact contains $v\rightarrow u$ as its final arc. Similarly, every path of order $t$ ending at $v$ contains $u$, and in fact contains $u\rightarrow v$ as its final arc.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
{\bf (1).}\ Let $P$ be a longest path ending at $u$. If $v\notin V(P)$, then appending the arc $u\rightarrow v$ gives a path ending at $v$ of order $h(u)+1$, and hence $h(v)\geq h(u)+1$. If $v\in V(P)$, then the segment of $P$ from $v$ to $u$, together with the arc $u\rightarrow v$, forms a cycle. Since $D$ is semi-acyclic and $vu\notin E(D)$, this is impossible. Thus necessarily $v\notin V(P)$, and the assertion follows.

\smallskip
{\bf (2).}\ Suppose $uv,vu\in E(D)$, i.e., $u\leftrightarrow v$. Let $P$ be a longest path ending at $u$. If $v\notin V(P)$, then appending $u\rightarrow v$ gives $h(v)\geq h(u)+1$ and in particular \(h(v)\ge h(u)-1\).

 If $v\in V(P)$, then the segment of $P$ from $v$ to $u$, together with $u\rightarrow v$, is a cycle. Since $D$ is semi-acyclic, this cycle must be the $2$-cycle $v\rightarrow u\rightarrow v$. Hence $v$ occurs immediately before $u$ in $P$, and the initial segment of $P$ ending at $v$ has order $h(u)-1$. Thus $h(v)\geq h(u)-1$. 

Therefore, in all cases, $h(v)\ge h(u)-1$, or equivalently, $h(u)-h(v)\le 1$.

Since the hypothesis \(uv,vu\in E(D)\) is symmetric in \(u\) and \(v\),
the same argument with the roles of \(u\) and \(v\) interchanged gives $h(v)-h(u)\le 1$. 
Hence we have
\[
        |h(u)-h(v)|\le 1.
\]

\smallskip
{\bf (3).}\ Assume $h(u)=h(v)=t$. Let $P$ be a path of order $t$ ending at $u$. If $v\notin V(P)$, then appending $u\rightarrow v$ gives a path of order $t+1$ ending at $v$, contradicting $h(v)=t$. Therefore $v\in V(P)$. As in (2), the segment of $P$ from $v$ to $u$, together with $u\rightarrow v$, must be the $2$-cycle itself. Hence $v\rightarrow u$ is the final arc of $P$. The proof for paths of order $t$ ending at $v$ is symmetric.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:unequal}
Let $D$ be a semi-acyclic digraph, and let $u\leftrightarrow v$ be a $2$-cycle in $D$ such that $h(u)\neq h(v)$. Let $P$ be a longest path in $D$ ending at $u$ with $v\notin V(P)$, and let $Q$ be a longest path in $D$ ending at $v$ with $u\notin V(Q)$. Then the following hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $h(v)=h(u)+1$ if and only if $|P|>|Q|$.
\item Suppose $h(v)=h(u)+1$. Let $R$ be a longest path in $D$ starting at $u$ with $v\notin V(R)$, and let $S$ be a longest path in $D$ starting at $v$ with $u\notin V(S)$. Then the path obtained from $P$ followed by $R$ is at least as long as the corresponding path obtained from $Q$ followed by the arc $v\rightarrow u$ and then by $R$. Moreover, the path obtained from $P$ followed by the arc $u\rightarrow v$ and then by $S$ is longer than the corresponding path obtained from $Q$ followed by $S$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
{\bf (1).}\ We first note that any path ending at $u$ contains $v$ if and only if it contains the arc $v\rightarrow u$ as its final arc; this follows from the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:ha2}(2). Similarly, any path ending at $v$ contains $u$ if and only if it contains the arc $u\rightarrow v$ as its final arc. Therefore, by Definition~\ref{def:h}, we have
$$
h(u)=\max\{|P|,|Q|+1\}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
h(v)=\max\{|Q|,|P|+1\}.
$$
If $h(v)=h(u)+1$, then the above equalities imply $h(u)=|P|$ and $h(v)=|P|+1$. Hence $|P|\geq |Q|+1$, and so $|P|>|Q|$. Conversely, if $|P|>|Q|$, then $|P|\geq |Q|+1$, whence
$$
h(u)=|P|\qquad\text{and}\qquad h(v)=|P|+1.
$$
Thus $h(v)=h(u)+1$.

\smallskip
{\bf (2).}\ We first observe that the concatenations used below are indeed paths. For example, suppose that $P$ and $R$ have a common vertex $w\neq u$. Then the segment of $P$ from $w$ to $u$, followed by the segment of $R$ from $u$ to $w$, forms a  cycle. Since $D$ is semi-acyclic, this cycle must be a $2$-cycle. But it contains $u$, which already belongs to the $2$-cycle $u\leftrightarrow v$, while both $P$ and $R$ avoid $v$; this contradicts the vertex-disjointness of the $2$-cycles. The same argument applies to the other concatenations below.

Since $h(v)=h(u)+1$, part (1) gives $|P|>|Q|$. The path obtained by concatenating $P$ and $R$ has order $|P|+|R|-1$, while the corresponding path obtained by concatenating $Q$, the arc $v\rightarrow u$, and $R$ has order $|Q|+|R|$. Since $|P|>|Q|$, we have
$$
|P|+|R|-1\geq |Q|+|R|.
$$
Similarly, the path obtained by concatenating $P$, the arc $u\rightarrow v$, and $S$ has order $|P|+|S|$, whereas the path obtained by concatenating $Q$ and $S$ has order $|Q|+|S|-1$. Since $|P|>|Q|$, we have
$$
|P|+|S|>|Q|+|S|-1.
$$
This proves the two asserted comparisons.

<!-- theorem-10: body_md -->
\label{thm:semi-acyclic-ppc}
Let \(D=(V,E)\) be a semi-acyclic digraph. Then \(D\) satisfies the
path partition conjecture.

<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Let
\[
        m=\lambda(D),
\]
and let \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) be positive integers such that
\[
        \lambda_1+\lambda_2=m.
\]
Put \(q=\lambda_1\), so that \(\lambda_2=m-q\). Let \(h\) be the height
function defined in Definition~\ref{def:h}.

We first define a preliminary partition. Let
\[
        A_0=\{x\in V: h(x)\le q\}
\]
and
\[
        B_0=V\setminus A_0=\{x\in V: h(x)\ge q+1\}.
\]

We now modify this partition only at \(2\)-cycles near the boundary.

\textbf{Rule I.}

Let \(\mathcal C_1\) be the set of all \(2\)-cycles \(x\leftrightarrow y\)
such that
\[
        h(x)=h(y)=q+1.
\]
For each \(2\)-cycle \(C=\{x,y\}\in\mathcal C_1\), choose one endpoint (arbitrarily),
denoted \(c_A\), to be placed in \(A\), and let the other endpoint,
denoted \(c_B\), remain in \(B\).

\textbf{Rule II.}

Let \(\mathcal C_2\) be the set of all \(2\)-cycles
\(x\leftrightarrow y\) such that
\[
        \{h(x),h(y)\}=\{q+1,q+2\}.
\]
For each such \(2\)-cycle, we put the endpoint of height \(q+2\) into
\(A\), and the endpoint of height \(q+1\) into \(B\).

Thus applying Rule I and Rule II, we define
\[
        A=A_0
          \cup \{c_A: C\in\mathcal C_1\}
          \cup \{z: z\in C\in\mathcal C_2,\ h(z)=q+2\},
\]
and put
\[
        B=V\setminus A.
\]
We shall prove that
\[
        \lambda(D[A])\le q
        \qquad\text{and}\qquad
        \lambda(D[B])\le m-q.
\]

First we prove that \(\lambda(D[A])\le q\). Suppose, to the contrary,
that \(D[A]\) contains a path \(P\) of order \(q+1\). Let \(u\) be the
terminal vertex of \(P\). Since \(P\) is also a path of \(D\), we have
\[
        h(u)\ge q+1.
\]
On the other hand, by the construction of \(A\), every vertex of \(A\)
has height at most \(q+2\), and the only vertices of \(A\) having height
larger than \(q\) arise from the two boundary cases Rule I and Rule II above.

If \(h(u)=q+1\), then \(u\) was placed in \(A\) as one endpoint of a
\(2\)-cycle \(u\leftrightarrow v\) with
\[
        h(u)=h(v)=q+1.
\]
Then, by Lemma~\ref{lem:ha2}(3), every path of order \(q+1\) ending at \(u\)
contains \(v\), and in fact contains the arc \(v\to u\) as its final
arc. But \(v\in B\), contradicting \(P\subseteq D[A]\).

It remains to consider the case \(h(u)=q+2\). Then \(u\) was placed in
\(A\) because it lies in a \(2\)-cycle \(u\leftrightarrow v\) with
\[
        h(v)=q+1,\qquad h(u)=q+2.
\]
Then, by Lemma~\ref{lem:unequal}(1), applied with the roles of \(u\) and
\(v\) interchanged, every path ending at \(u\) and avoiding \(v\) has
order strictly smaller than \(q+1\).


Since \(v\in B\), the
path \(P\subseteq D[A]\) avoids \(v\), which contradicts \(|P|=q+1\).
Therefore
\[
        \lambda(D[A])\le q.
\]

It remains to prove that \(\lambda(D[B])\le m-q\).

\begin{claim}
Let \(R=(r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_s)\) be a path in \(D[B]\). Then \(D\)
contains a path of order at least \(q+s\).
\end{claim}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the claim]
Since \(r_1\in B\), we have \(h(r_1)\ge q+1\). Let \(P\) be a path of
order \(q+1\) ending at \(r_1\).

If \(P\) and \(R\) meet only at \(r_1\), then their concatenation is a
path of order
\[
        (q+1)+s-1=q+s,
\]
as required.

If the arc by which \(P\) enters \(r_1\) is not the reverse of the first
arc of \(R\), then no such obstruction occurs, and the preceding
concatenation works. In particular, this covers the case where \(P\)
enters \(r_1\) through an ordinary one-directional arc crossing from
\(A_0\) to \(B_0\).

Hence we need only consider the
case where the first arc of \(R\) is part of a \(2\)-cycle.

Suppose that \(P\) meets \(R\) at a vertex other than \(r_1\). Taking
the first such common vertex along \(R\), the corresponding segment of
\(P\) together with the initial segment of \(R\) forms a cycle. Since
\(D\) is semi-acyclic, this cycle must be a \(2\)-cycle. Thus the
obstruction can occur only when
\[
        r_1\leftrightarrow r_2
\]
and \(P\) uses the arc \(r_2\to r_1\) as its final arc.


Write
\[
        u=r_1,\qquad v=r_2.
\]
Thus \(R\) begins with \(u\to v\), while \(P\) ends with \(v\to u\).

First suppose that \(h(u)=h(v)=q+1\). Then \(u\leftrightarrow v\)
belongs to \(\mathcal C_1\), so Rule I splits \(u\) and \(v\)
between \(A\) and \(B\). This contradicts \(u,v\in B\).

Next suppose that
\[
        \{h(u),h(v)\}=\{q+1,q+2\}.
\]
Then \(u\leftrightarrow v\) belongs to \(\mathcal C_2\), so Rule II puts the endpoint of height \(q+2\) into \(A\). Again this
contradicts \(u,v\in B\).

It remains to consider the case
\[
        h(u)>q+1,\qquad h(v)>q+1.
\]
In this case the obstruction can be bypassed.

We shall use, in each of the following cases, the same observation as in
the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:unequal}. The concatenations considered
below are indeed paths. For if the chosen initial path and the tail
\(r_3,r_4,\ldots,r_s\) had a common vertex, then the appropriate segment
of the initial path together with the corresponding segment of \(R\)
would form a cycle. Since \(D\) is semi-acyclic, this cycle would have to
be a \(2\)-cycle; but it would contain one of \(u\) or \(v\), already
belonging to the \(2\)-cycle \(u\leftrightarrow v\), a contradiction to
the vertex-disjointness of the \(2\)-cycles.

If \(h(u)=h(v)=t\), then by Lemma~\ref{lem:ha2}(3), there is a path
\(P_1\) of order \(t\) ending at \(v\), and \(P_1\) contains the arc
\(u\to v\) as its final arc. Since \(t>q+1\), we have \(t\ge q+2\).
Now concatenate \(P_1\) with the tail
\[
        r_3,r_4,\ldots,r_s
\]
of \(R\) using the arc $v\rightarrow r_3$ when $s\geq 3$. This gives a path of order
\[
        t+(s-2)\ge (q+2)+(s-2)=q+s.
\]

Now suppose \(h(v)>h(u)\). Since \(u\leftrightarrow v\), Lemma~\ref{lem:ha2}(2)
gives 
\(
        h(v)=h(u)+1.
\) 
By Lemma~\ref{lem:unequal}(1), there is a longest path \(P_1\) ending
at \(u\) and avoiding \(v\), with 
\(
        |P_1|=h(u)=h(v)-1.
\) 
We concatenate \(P_1\), the arc \(u\to v\), and the tail
\[
        r_3,r_4,\ldots,r_s.
\]
The resulting path has order
\[
        |P_1|+1+(s-2)=h(v)+s-2.
\]
Since \(h(u)>q+1\), we have \(h(v)>q+2\), and hence this order is at
least \(q+s\).

Finally suppose \(h(u)>h(v)\). Then 
\(
        h(u)=h(v)+1.
\) 
Again by Lemma~\ref{lem:unequal}(1), there is a longest path \(P_1\)
ending at \(v\) and avoiding \(u\), with 
\(
        |P_1|=h(v).
\) 
Concatenating \(P_1\) with the tail
\[
        r_3,r_4,\ldots,r_s
\]
using the arc $v\rightarrow r_3$ when $s\geq 3$ gives a path of order
\[
        h(v)+(s-2).
\]
Since \(h(v)>q+1\), this is at least
\[
        (q+2)+(s-2)=q+s.
\]

In each case, \(D\) contains a path of order at least \(q+s\). This
proves the claim.
\end{proof}

Now let \(R\) be a longest path in \(D[B]\). By the claim, \(D\) contains
a path of order at least
\[
        q+|R|.
\]
Since \(m=\lambda(D)\), we get 
\(
        q+|R|\le m.
\) 
Hence 
\(
        |R|\le m-q.
\) 
Therefore 
\(
        \lambda(D[B])\le m-q.
\) 

Combining the two inequalities, we have obtained a partition
\(V=A\cup B\) such that
\[
        \lambda(D[A])\le q=\lambda_1
\qquad 
and
\qquad 
        \lambda(D[B])\le m-q=\lambda_2.
\]
Thus \(D\) satisfies the path partition conjecture.

<!-- definition-5: body_md -->
\label{def:extn}
Let $D = H[\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}]$ be a digraph obtained from 
a semi-acyclic digraph $H$ by substituting bipartite digraphs $T_i$ with
bipartition $(X_i,Y_i)$, $i \in I$ for the 2-cycles $\langle x_i,y_i\rangle$ in $H$ and 
digraphs $Z_j, j \in J$ for the vertices $z_j$ not on 2-cycles.
We call each $Z_j$ an {\em ordinary block}, each $T_i$ a {\em bipartite block} and 
$X_i$ or $Y_i$ as a {\em port} of $T_i$.

For an ordinary block \(Z_j\), define
\[
        p_{z_j}^{in}
        =\max\{|P|: P\subseteq D-Z_j,
        \ \operatorname{ter}(P)\text{ has an arc into }Z_j\},
\]
where the maximum is taken to be \(0\) if no such path exists and $\operatorname{ter}(P)$ denotes the end or terminal vertex of a path $P$. Also define
\[
        p_{z_j}^{end}
        =\max\{|P|: P\text{ is a path in }D\text{ ending in }Z_j\}.
\]

For a bipartite block \(T_i=X_i\cup Y_i\), define
\[
        p_{X_i}^{in}
        =\max\{|P|: P\subseteq D-T_i,
        \ \operatorname{ter}(P)\text{ has an arc into }X_i\},
\]
where the maximum is taken to be \(0\) if no such path exists, and define
\(p_{Y_i}^{in}\) similarly. Also define
\[
        p_{X_i}^{end}
        =\max\{|P|: P\text{ is a path in }D\text{ ending in }X_i\},
\]
\[
        p_{Y_i}^{end}
        =\max\{|P|: P\text{ is a path in }D\text{ ending in }Y_i\}.
\]
Finally put
\[
        p_i^{in}=\max\{p_{X_i}^{in},p_{Y_i}^{in}\},
        \qquad
        p_i^{end}=\max\{p_{X_i}^{end},p_{Y_i}^{end}\}.
\]

\label{lem:block-intersection}
Let \(W\) be one of the substituted blocks of \(D\), that is, either
\(W=V(Z_j)\) for a vertex not on a 2-cycle, or \(W=V(T_i)\) for a
\(2\)-cycle block \(T_i\). If \(P\) is a path in \(D\), then the vertices
of \(P\) lying in \(W\) occur consecutively along \(P\). Consequently,
once \(P\) leaves \(W\), it cannot enter \(W\) again.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
Suppose, to the contrary, that \(P\) leaves \(W\) and later enters \(W\)
again. Project the corresponding portion of \(P\) to the skeleton \(H\):
vertices of ordinary blocks \(Z_j\) are projected to \(z_j\), and vertices
of a bipartite block \(T_i=X_i\cup Y_i\) are projected to the
corresponding vertex \(x_i\) or \(y_i\). The projected walk starts in the
vertex, or in one of the two vertices, corresponding to \(W\), leaves it,
and later returns to it. Hence this directed walk contains a directed
cycle in \(H\).

Since \(H\) is semi-acyclic, this cycle must be a directed \(2\)-cycle.
However, the walk has left \(W\) and returned to it through vertices
outside \(W\). Thus the resulting cycle either has length greater than
\(2\), or is a \(2\)-cycle. In the latter case, if \(W=V(Z_j)\), then
\(z_j\) lies in a \(2\)-cycle of \(H\), contrary to the choice of \(z_j\). If
\(W=V(T_i)\), then this \(2\)-cycle shares a vertex with the original
\(2\)-cycle \(x_i\leftrightarrow y_i\), contradicting the
vertex-disjointness of the \(2\)-cycles.

Therefore \(P\) cannot leave \(W\) and later enter \(W\) again. Hence the
vertices of \(P\) lying in \(W\) occur consecutively along \(P\).
<!-- end proof-15 -->
<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:pin-pend}
For every ordinary block \(Z_j\), 
\(
        0\le p_{z_j}^{in}<p_{z_j}^{end}.
\) 
For every bipartite block \(T_i=X_i\cup Y_i\), 
\(
        0\le p_{X_i}^{in}<p_{X_i}^{end},
        \ 
        0\le p_{Y_i}^{in}<p_{Y_i}^{end}.
\) 
Consequently,
\(
        0\le p_i^{in}<p_i^{end}.
\)
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
The non-negativity is clear from the definitions.

Let \(Z_j\) be an ordinary block. If \(p_{z_j}^{in}=0\), then
\(p_{z_j}^{end}\ge1\), since \(Z_j\ne\emptyset\). If \(p_{z_j}^{in}>0\), let
\(P\subseteq D-Z_j\) be a path of order \(p_{z_j}^{in}\) whose terminal vertex
has an arc into \(Z_j\). Since \(P\) is disjoint from \(Z_j\), we may
append one vertex of \(Z_j\) to \(P\). Thus 
\(
        p_{z_j}^{end}\ge p_{z_j}^{in}+1,
\) 
and hence \(p_{z_j}^{in}<p_{z_j}^{end}\).

The same argument applies to each port, namely, 
\(
        p_{X_i}^{in}<p_{X_i}^{end},
        \ 
        p_{Y_i}^{in}<p_{Y_i}^{end}.
\) 

Finally, if \(p_i^{in}=p_{X_i}^{in}\), then 
\[
        p_i^{end}
        =\max\{p_{X_i}^{end},p_{Y_i}^{end}\}
        \ge p_{X_i}^{end}
        >p_{X_i}^{in}
        =p_i^{in}.
\]
The case \(p_i^{in}=p_{Y_i}^{in}\) is symmetric. Hence
\(p_i^{in}<p_i^{end}\).

<!-- definition-6: body_md -->
\label{def:partition-substitution}
Let $D = H[\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}]$ be a digraph as defined in Definition \ref{def:extn}. Fix an integer \(q\) with
\[
        1\le q<\lambda(D).
\]
For each ordinary block \(Z_j\), exactly one of the following three cases
holds:
\[
        p_{z_j}^{end}\le q,
        \qquad
        p_{z_j}^{in}\ge q,
        \qquad
        p_{z_j}^{in}<q<p_{z_j}^{end}.
\]
Define
\[
        S_0=\{{z_j}:p_{z_j}^{end}\le q\},
        \qquad
        R_0=\{{z_j}:p_{z_j}^{in}\ge q\},
        \qquad
        J_0=\{{z_j}:p_{z_j}^{in}<q<p_{z_j}^{end}\}.
\]
If \({z_j}\in S_0\), put \(V(Z_j)\subseteq A\). If \({z_j}\in R_0\), put
\(V(Z_j)\subseteq B\). If \({z_j}\in J_0\), set
\[
        q_{z_j}=q-p_{z_j}^{in}.
\]
Then \(1\le q_{z_j}<\lambda(Z_j)\). Indeed, \(p_{z_j}^{in}<q\) gives
\(q_{z_j}\ge1\). Also, by Lemma~\ref{lem:block-intersection}, every path
ending in \(Z_j\) has a final segment inside \(Z_j\) and an initial
segment outside \(Z_j\) of order at most \(p_{z_j}^{in}\). Hence
\[
        p_{z_j}^{end}\le p_{z_j}^{in}+\lambda(Z_j),
\]
and since \(q<p_{z_j}^{end}\), we get \(q_{z_j}<\lambda(Z_j)\). Since \(Z_j\)
satisfies the path partition conjecture, choose a partition
\[
        V(Z_j)=A_{z_j}\cup B_{z_j}
\]
such that
\[
        \lambda(Z_j[A_{z_j}])\le q_{z_j},
        \qquad
        \lambda(Z_j[B_{z_j}])\le \lambda(Z_j)-q_{z_j}.
\]
Put \(A_{z_j}\subseteq A\) and \(B_{z_j}\subseteq B\).\\[0.5em]

Now let \(T_i=X_i\cup Y_i\) be a bipartite block. If 
\(
        p_i^{end}\le q,
\) 
put \(V(T_i)\subseteq A\).\\[1em]
If
\(
        p_i^{in}<q<p_i^{end},
\) 
choose a port \(U_i\in\{X_i,Y_i\}\) such that 
\(
        p_{U_i}^{end}=p_i^{end}>q.
\) 
Let \(W_i\) be the other port, and put 
\(
        W_i\subseteq A,
        \ 
        U_i\subseteq B.
\)\\[1em]
Finally suppose 
\(
        p_i^{in}\ge q.
\)  

If 
\(
        p_{X_i}^{in}<q\le p_{Y_i}^{in},
\) 
put 
\(
        X_i\subseteq A,
        \ 
        Y_i\subseteq B.
\) 
If 
\(
        p_{Y_i}^{in}<q\le p_{X_i}^{in},
\) 
put 
\(
        Y_i\subseteq A,
        \ 
        X_i\subseteq B.
\)\\[1em]
If 
\(
        p_{X_i}^{in}\ge q
        \ \text{and}\ 
        p_{Y_i}^{in}\ge q,
\) 
put \(V(T_i)\subseteq B\).  
This completes the definition of \(A\) and \(B\).

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:genuine-concat}
In the proofs below, whenever we concatenate paths through a substituted
block \(W\), the outside pieces are vertex-disjoint. Indeed, if an
outside vertex occurred in two pieces, then, by taking the first such
repetition, the projected walk in \(H\) would leave \(W\) and later return
to it. Lemma~\ref{lem:block-intersection} then gives a forbidden directed
cycle in \(H\). Thus the displayed concatenations are genuine paths.

<!-- theorem-11: body_md -->
\label{thm:semi-acyclic-substitution-ppc}
Let $D = H[\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}]$ be a digraph defined as in Definition \ref{def:extn}. 
Suppose that every ordinary block \(Z_j\) satisfies the path partition conjecture. 
Then \(D\) satisfies the path partition conjecture.
<!-- end theorem-11 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
Fix an integer \(q\) with 
\(
        1\le q<\lambda(D).
\)
Define the partition \(V(D)=A\cup B\) as in Definition \ref{def:partition-substitution}.\\[0.5em]

We first show that 
\[
        \lambda(D[A])\le q.
\]

Let \(P\) be a path in \(D[A]\), and let \(u\) be its terminal vertex. We
consider the substituted block containing \(u\). 
First suppose \(u\in V(Z_j)\) for some ordinary block \(Z_j\). If
\({z_j}\in S_0\), then by definition
\[
        |P|\le p_{z_j}^{end}\le q.
\]
If \({z_j}\in J_0\), then Lemma~\ref{lem:block-intersection} implies that the
vertices of \(P\) lying in \(Z_j\) form a final segment of \(P\). Thus 
\(
        P=P'P_{z_j},
\) 
where \(P_{z_j}\subseteq Z_j[A_{z_j}]\). Hence
\[
        |P'|\le p_{z_j}^{in},
        \qquad
        |P_{z_j}|\le \lambda(Z_j[A_{z_j}])\le q_{z_j}.
\]
Therefore
\[
        |P|\le p_{z_j}^{in}+q_{z_j}=q.
\]
The case \({z_j}\in R_0\) cannot occur, because \(V(Z_j)\subseteq B\).\\[0.5em]

Now suppose \(u\in V(T_i)\), where \(T_i=X_i\cup Y_i\). If
\(p_i^{end}\le q\), then \(V(T_i)\subseteq A\), and hence
\[
        |P|\le p_i^{end}\le q.
\]
Suppose next that 
\(
        p_i^{in}<q<p_i^{end}.
\) 
By construction, 
\(
        W_i\subseteq A,
        \ 
        U_i\subseteq B.
\) 

Thus \(u\in W_i\). Since \(T_i\) is bipartite, there are no arcs inside
the port \(W_i\). Hence the final segment of \(P\) inside \(T_i\) consists
only of the vertex \(u\). Therefore
\[
        P=P'u,
\]
where \(P'\subseteq D-T_i\), and the terminal vertex of \(P'\), if
\(P'\ne\emptyset\), has an arc into \(W_i\). Hence
\[
        |P'|\le p_{W_i}^{in}\le p_i^{in}<q.
\]
Since \(|P'|\) is an integer, \(|P'|\le q-1\), and so
\[
        |P|=|P'|+1\le q.
\]

Finally suppose \(p_i^{in}\ge q\). If the construction puts one port in
\(A\), say \(X_i\subseteq A\) and \(Y_i\subseteq B\), then necessarily
\(p_{X_i}^{in}<q\). The same argument as above shows that any path in
\(D[A]\) ending in \(X_i\) has order at most \(q\). The case
\(Y_i\subseteq A\) is symmetric. If both port-in values are at least
\(q\), then \(V(T_i)\subseteq B\), so \(u\notin V(T_i)\).

Therefore every path in \(D[A]\) has order at most \(q\), and the claim
follows.\\[0.5em]

Next we prove that 
\[
        \lambda(D[B])\le \lambda(D)-q.
\]

Let
\[
        R=(r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_s)
\]
be a longest path in \(D[B]\). It is enough to show that \(D\) contains a
path of order at least \(q+s\). Let \(W\) be the substituted block
containing \(r_1\).

First suppose \(W=V(Z_j)\) for some ordinary block \(Z_j\). The case
\({z_j}\in S_0\) cannot occur, since \(V(Z_j)\subseteq A\). If \({z_j}\in R_0\),
then \(p_{z_j}^{in}\ge q\). Choose a path \(P\subseteq D-Z_j\) of order
\(p_{z_j}^{in}\) whose terminal vertex has an arc into \(Z_j\). Since arcs
into \(Z_j\) are complete by the definition of the substitution, \(P\)
can be followed by \(R\). By Remark~\ref{rem:genuine-concat}, this
concatenation is a path. Hence
\[
        \lambda(D)\ge |P|+|R|\ge q+s.
\]

Now suppose \({z_j}\in J_0\). By Lemma~\ref{lem:block-intersection}, the
vertices of \(R\) lying in \(Z_j\) form an initial segment of \(R\). Write
\[
        R=R_{z_j}R',
\]
where \(R_{z_j}\subseteq Z_j[B_{z_j}]\), and \(R'\) is the remaining tail,
possibly empty. Then
\[
        |R_{z_j}|\le \lambda(Z_j[B_{z_j}])\le \lambda(Z_j)-q_{z_j}.
\]
Choose a path \(P\subseteq D-Z_j\) of order \(p_{z_j}^{in}\) whose terminal
vertex has an arc into \(Z_j\), and let \(L_{z_j}\) be a longest path in
\(Z_j\). By the substitution rule, \(P\), \(L_{z_j}\), and \(R'\) can be
concatenated to form a path in \(D\); if \(R'\) is empty, the last piece
is omitted. Thus
\[
        \lambda(D)\ge p_{z_j}^{in}+\lambda(Z_j)+|R'|.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
q+s
&=q+|R_{z_j}|+|R'|\\
&\le q+\lambda(Z_j)-q_{z_j}+|R'|\\
&=q+\lambda(Z_j)-(q-p_{z_j}^{in})+|R'|\\
&=p_{z_j}^{in}+\lambda(Z_j)+|R'|\\
&\le \lambda(D).
\end{aligned}
\]

Now suppose \(W=V(T_i)\), where \(T_i=X_i\cup Y_i\). If
\(p_i^{end}\le q\), then \(V(T_i)\subseteq A\), so this case cannot
occur. 
Suppose first that 
\[
        p_i^{in}<q<p_i^{end}.
\]
By construction,
\[
        W_i\subseteq A,
        \qquad
        U_i\subseteq B,
        \qquad
        p_{U_i}^{end}=p_i^{end}>q.
\]
Since \(R\subseteq D[B]\) starts in \(T_i\), we have \(r_1\in U_i\).
Moreover, \(U_i\) is an independent set, because it is one part of the
bipartition of \(T_i\). Hence the initial segment of \(R\) inside \(T_i\)
consists only of \(r_1\).

Let \(Q\) be a path in \(D\) ending in \(U_i\) of order
\[
        p_{U_i}^{end}=p_i^{end}\ge q+1.
\]
If \(s=1\), then \(|Q|\ge q+1=q+s\), and we are done. If \(s\ge2\), then
\(r_2\notin V(T_i)\). Since \(r_1\to r_2\), the substitution rule implies
that every vertex of \(U_i\) has an arc to \(r_2\). Thus the terminal
vertex of \(Q\) has an arc to \(r_2\), and by
Remark~\ref{rem:genuine-concat}, 
\(
        Qr_2r_3\cdots r_s
\) 
is a path in \(D\). Hence
\[
        \lambda(D)
        \ge |Q|+(s-1)
        \ge (q+1)+(s-1)=q+s.
\]

Finally suppose
\[
        p_i^{in}\ge q.
\]
If
\[
        p_{X_i}^{in}<q\le p_{Y_i}^{in},
\]
then by construction \(X_i\subseteq A\) and \(Y_i\subseteq B\). Thus
\(r_1\in Y_i\). Choose a path \(P\subseteq D-T_i\) of order at least \(q\)
whose terminal vertex has an arc into \(Y_i\). By the substitution rule,
the terminal vertex of \(P\) has an arc to \(r_1\), so \(P\) can be
followed by \(R\). Hence \(D\) contains a path of order at least
\(q+s\). The case
\[
        p_{Y_i}^{in}<q\le p_{X_i}^{in}
\]
is symmetric.

It remains to consider the case
\[
        p_{X_i}^{in}\ge q
        \qquad\text{and}\qquad
        p_{Y_i}^{in}\ge q.
\]
Then \(V(T_i)\subseteq B\). If \(r_1\in X_i\), choose a path
\(P\subseteq D-T_i\) of order at least \(q\) whose terminal vertex has an
arc into \(X_i\). If \(r_1\in Y_i\), choose such a path entering \(Y_i\).
In either case, \(P\) can be followed by \(R\), giving a path in \(D\) of
order at least \(q+s\).

In every case, \(D\) contains a path of order at least \(q+s\). Hence
\(q+s\le\lambda(D)\). Since \(s=|R|=\lambda(D[B])\), we get
\[
        \lambda(D[B])\le \lambda(D)-q.
\]

Thus, we have the partition
\(V(D)=A\cup B\) satisfying
\[
        \lambda(D[A])\le q,
        \qquad
        \lambda(D[B])\le \lambda(D)-q.
\]
Since \(q\) was arbitrary, \(D\) satisfies the path partition conjecture.
<!-- end proof-17 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:rules-recovered}
Let us check that the port assignment for a bipartite block really
contains the earlier Rules I and II as special cases. Suppose that
\(T_i=X_i\cup Y_i\) is a singleton \(2\)-cycle, say
\[
        X_i=\{x\},\qquad Y_i=\{y\},
\]
with both arcs \(x\to y\) and \(y\to x\). Then
\[
        p_{X_i}^{end}=h(x),\qquad p_{Y_i}^{end}=h(y).
\]
Moreover, \(p_{X_i}^{in}+1\) is the maximum order of a path ending at
\(x\) and avoiding \(y\), and \(p_{Y_i}^{in}+1\) is the maximum order of
a path ending at \(y\) and avoiding \(x\).

First suppose
\[
        h(x)=h(y)=q+1.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:ha2}(3), every path of order \(q+1\) ending at
\(x\) contains \(y\), in fact with final arc \(y\to x\). Hence every
path ending at \(x\) and avoiding \(y\) has order at most \(q\). On the
other hand, taking a path of order \(q+1\) ending at \(y\) and deleting
its final vertex \(y\), we obtain a path of order \(q\) ending at \(x\)
and avoiding \(y\). Therefore 
\(
        p_{X_i}^{in}=q-1.
\) 
Similarly, 
\(
        p_{Y_i}^{in}=q-1.
\) 
Thus 
\(
        p_i^{in}=q-1<q<p_i^{end}=q+1.
\)
So this situation lies in the middle case of the port assignment. Since
both ports have end-value \(q+1>q\), either port may be chosen as the
\(B\)-port. Consequently the construction separates \(X_i\) and \(Y_i\)
arbitrarily between \(A\) and \(B\), exactly as in Rule I.

Next suppose
\[
        h(x)=q+1,\qquad h(y)=q+2.
\]
Then 
\(
        p_{X_i}^{end}=q+1,\qquad p_{Y_i}^{end}=q+2.
\) 
By Lemma~\ref{lem:unequal}, the longest path ending at \(x\) and
avoiding \(y\) has order \(q+1\). Hence 
\(
        p_{X_i}^{in}=q.
\) 
On the other hand, every path ending at \(y\) and avoiding \(x\) has
order at most \(q\), and therefore 
\(
        p_{Y_i}^{in}\le q-1.
\) 
Thus 
\(
        p_i^{in}=q\ge q,
\) 
and this case lies in the final port-in part of the construction. Since 
\(
        p_{Y_i}^{in}<q\le p_{X_i}^{in},
\) 
the construction puts 
\(
        Y_i\subseteq A,\qquad X_i\subseteq B.
\) 
That is, the endpoint with larger height \(q+2\) is placed in \(A\), and
the endpoint with smaller height \(q+1\) is placed in \(B\), exactly as
in Rule II. The case 
\(
        h(y)=q+1,\ h(x)=q+2
\)
is symmetric.

Thus the earlier Rules I and II are not extra assumptions in the
substitution theorem. They appear naturally as the singleton \(2\)-cycle
cases of the present port assignment. Notice also that there is no
contradiction with Lemma~\ref{lem:pin-pend}: in the unequal case above,
for instance,
\[
        p_{X_i}^{in}=q<q+1=p_{X_i}^{end},
        \qquad
        p_{Y_i}^{in}\le q-1<q+2=p_{Y_i}^{end}.
\]
