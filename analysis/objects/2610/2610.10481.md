<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{t: fy}
    If $i_k \sim \operatorname{Unif}(\{k,k+1,\ldots,n\})$ are independent for $k = 1,\ldots,n$, then $(n \ i_n) \cdots$ $(2 \ i_2)(1 \ i_1) \sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm: subgroup alg}
Let $G$ be a compact group and $H$ a closed subgroup of $G$. Let
$x \sim \operatorname{Unif}(G/H)$ be uniform on the left cosets of $H$ in $G$, let
$y \sim \operatorname{Unif}(H\backslash G)$ be uniform on the right cosets of $H$ in
$G$, and let $z \sim \operatorname{Unif}(H)$ be independent of $x$ and $y$. Then
\(        xz,zy \sim \operatorname{Unif}(G).
   \)
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:unif_perm}
    If the first $n-1$ columns of $A \in \mathbb C^{n\times n}$ are independent and each column has independent and identically distributed (iid) entries from an absolutely continuous distribution, then $\pi(A) \sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:unif_perm}
    Let $U,V \sim \operatorname{Haar}(G_n)$ be iid for $G_n$ one of the (special) orthogonal or unitary groups of order $n$. If $A = U $ or $A = UBV^*$ where $B$ is deterministic nonsingular (and real when $U,V$ are real), then $\pi(A) \sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{l: fixed}
    Let $\pi \in S_n$ have form given by \eqref{eq:GEPP perm}. Then $i_1 = 1$ if and only if $1$ is a fixed point of $\pi$, i.e., $\pi(1) = 1$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We have $i_1 = 1$ if and only if $1$ is maximal in its corresponding cycle factor in the cycle decomposition of $\pi$ if and only if 1 is the only number in its $\pi$-orbit if and only if $\pi(1) = 1$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
This characterization of $i_1 = 1$ corresponding to 1 being a fixed point does not extend to any other case $i_k = k$: we only need to consider the $k$-cycle $\pi = (1 \ 2 \ \cdots k) = (k-1 \ k)\cdots (2 \ k)(1 \ k) \in S_n$ for $n \ge k$, as $i_k = k$ but $\pi(k) = 1$.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
Let $A \sim \operatorname{Bern}(p)^{n \times n}$ for $p \in [0,1]$ fixed  and $n\ge 2$. Then $p_n = \Theta(1)$ and $\pi(A) \not \sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Note first
    \[
    p_n = \mathbb{P}(i_1=1)
        =\mathbb{P}(A_{11}=1\ \mbox{or}\ A_{j1}=0\ \forall j)
        =p+(1-p)^n = \Theta(1).
    \]
    If $p = 0,1$, then $p_n = 1 > 1/n$ for all $n \ge 2$. Now assume $p \in (0,1)$. We note if $\pi(A) \sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$ then $\mathbb{P}(i_1 = 1) = \mathbb{P}(i_1 = 2) = 1/n$ so that $\mathbb{P}(i_1 = 1) - \mathbb{P}(i_1 = 2) = 0$. We now compute also
    \begin{align*}
        \mathbb{P}(i_1=2)
        &=\mathbb{P}(A_{11}=0,A_{21}=1)
        =(1-p)p = p - p^2,
    \end{align*}
    so that
    $$
        p_n-\mathbb{P}(i_1=2)
        =p^2+(1-p)^n \ge p^2>0.
    $$
    This establishes $\pi(A)$ can never be uniform on $S_n$.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{p: goe pn}
Let $p_n = \mathbb{P}(i_1 = 1)$ for $A \sim \operatorname{GOE}(n)$. Then, for $n\ge2$,
$$
    p_n > \frac1n, \qquad  \mathbb{P}(i_1=j)=\frac{1-p_n}{n-1},\qquad j=2,\ldots,n.
$$
In particular,  $\pi(A) \not \sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$ for any $n\ge2$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq: asym pn}
p_n \sim \frac{\pi^{1/4}/\sqrt 2}{\sqrt n (\log n)^{1/4}}
\approx \frac{0.941}{\sqrt n (\log n)^{1/4}}.
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Writing $A=(G+G^\top)/\sqrt2$ for a real Ginibre matrix
$G$ (with iid $N(0,1)$ entries) recovers the $\operatorname{GOE}(n)$
distribution. Thus,
$A_{11}\sim N(0,2)$ is independent of
$A_{j1}\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1)$ for $j\ge2$.
Writing $A_{11}^2=2X_1$ and $A_{j1}^2=X_j$ for $X_j\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\chi_1^2$, then
\[
    p_n
    =\mathbb{P}\left(2X_1\ge M\right), \qquad M = \max_{2\le j\le n}X_j.
\]
If $2X_1$ were replaced by $X_1$, exchangeability would give probability $1/n$. Since $2X_1>X_1$ almost surely, and
$$
\mathbb{P}\left(2X_1 \ge  M >  X_1\right)>0,
$$
we have
$$
    p_n
    =\mathbb{P}\left(2X_1\ge M\right) = \mathbb{P}(X_1 \ge M) + \mathbb{P}(2X_1 \ge M > X_1) > \mathbb{P}(X_1 \ge M) = \frac1n.
$$
This establishes $\pi(A) \not\sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$.

We now turn to the asymptotics for $p_n$. Conditioning on $X_1=x$ and using independence and $\mathbb{P}(\chi_1^2\le 2x)=\operatorname{erf}(\sqrt{x})$ gives
\[
    p_n
    =\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty
    x^{-1/2}e^{-x/2}
    \operatorname{erf}(\sqrt{x})^{n-1}\,dx.
\]
Substituting $s=\operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt{x})=1-\operatorname{erf}(\sqrt{x})$,
so that $ds = -e^{-x}/\sqrt{\pi x} \ dx$, converts this to
\[
    p_n
    =\frac1{\sqrt2}\int_0^1
    (1-s)^{n-1}e^{[\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(s)]^2/2}\,ds.
\]
Since $\operatorname{erfc}(x)\sim e^{-x^2}/(x\sqrt\pi)$
as $x\to\infty$, inverting gives $[\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(s)]^2
    =\log(1/s)-\frac12\log\log(1/s)
    -\frac12\log\pi+o(1)$ as $s\downarrow0$, and hence $e^{[\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(s)]^2/2}
    \sim \pi^{-1/4}s^{-1/2}[\log(1/s)]^{-1/4}$. The factor $(1-s)^{n-1}$ localizes the integral near $s=1/n$,
where $[\log(1/s)]^{-1/4}\sim(\log n)^{-1/4}$ varies slowly
enough to pull out of the integral, leaving the Beta integral
\[
    \int_0^1(1-s)^{n-1}s^{-1/2}\,ds
    =B\left(\frac12,n\right)
    \sim\sqrt\pi\,n^{-1/2}.
\]
Combining constants yields \eqref{eq: asym pn}.

\label{p:ns factor}
Let $B = B(\theta,A_1,A_2) = (R_\theta \otimes {\mathbf I}_m)(A_1 \oplus A_2) \in \mathbb R^{2m \times 2m}$, where nonsingular $A_1,A_2 \in \mathbb R^{m \times m}$ and $|\tan \theta| \ne 1$. \begin{align}
P &= (P_1 \oplus P_2)(P_\theta \otimes {\mathbf I}_m) \label{eq: P ns butterfly}\\
L &= (P_1 \oplus P_2)(L_{\hat \theta} \otimes {\mathbf I}_m)(P_1^\top \oplus P_2^\top)(L_1 \oplus L_2) \label{eq: L 1st}\\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
L_1 & {\mathbf 0} \\ -\tan \hat \theta P_2 P_1^\top L_1 & L_2
\end{bmatrix} \label{eq: L 2nd}\\
U &= (L_1^{-1} \oplus L_2^{-1})(P_1\oplus P_2)(U_{\hat \theta}D_\theta \otimes {\mathbf I}_m)(A_1 \oplus A_2) \label{eq: U 1st} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}
(-1)^{e(\theta)}\cos \hat \theta U_1 & \sin \hat \theta L_1^{-1}P_1 A_2 \\ {\mathbf 0} & \sec \hat \theta U_2
\end{bmatrix}. \label{eq: U 2nd}
\end{align}

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The condition $|\tan \theta| \ne 1$ then eliminates the cases when there are pivot search ties  when using GEPP on $R_\theta$, and hence eliminates such ties between separate blocks on $B$. A straightforward computation verifies $PB = LU$ when using the forms given on \eqref{eq: L 1st} and \eqref{eq: U 1st}:
\begin{align*}
LU &= (P_1 \oplus P_2)(L_{\hat \theta} \otimes {\mathbf I}_m)(U_{\hat \theta}D_\theta \otimes {\mathbf I}_m)(A_1 \oplus A_2)\\
&= (P_1 \oplus P_2)(R_{\hat \theta}D_\theta \otimes {\mathbf I}_m)(A_1 \oplus A_2)\\
&= (P_1 \oplus P_2)(P_\theta R_\theta \otimes  {\mathbf I}_m)(A_1 \oplus A_2)\\
&=(P_1 \oplus P_2)(P_\theta \otimes {\mathbf I}_m)(R_\theta \otimes {\mathbf I}_m)(A_1 \oplus A_2)\\
&= PB,
\end{align*}
using the mixed-product property and \eqref{eq: 2x2 gepp}. Additional  straightforward checks  verify equality between forms \eqref{eq: L 1st} and \eqref{eq: L 2nd} as well as \eqref{eq: U 1st} and \eqref{eq: U 2nd}. <!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm: unif ns}
Let $B \sim \operatorname{B}(N,\Sigma_S)$. Then $\pi(B) \sim \operatorname{Unif}(B_n)$.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
We will use induction on $n = \log_2 N$. For $n = 1$, the result holds from \Cref{t: simple b} since $\operatorname{B}(2,\Sigma_S) = \operatorname{B}_s(2,\Sigma_S) = \operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{SO}(2))$ and $S_2 = B_1$. Assume the result holds for $n$. Let $B \sim \operatorname{B}(2^{n+1},\Sigma_S)$ and write $B = B(\theta,A_1,A_2) = (R_\theta \otimes {\mathbf I}_{2^n})(A_1 \oplus A_2)$ for independent $\theta,A_1,A_2$ where $\theta \sim \operatorname{Unif}([0,2\pi))$ and $A_i \sim \operatorname{B}(2^n,\Sigma_S)$. By the recursive construction, all rotation angles are continuously distributed, so almost surely no angle is at a pole or tie value of the corresponding rotation matrix factorization. So all GEPP factorizations arising in the recursion are uniquely determined almost surely, independently of the tie strategy. Since $\mathbb{P}(|\tan \theta| \ne 1) = 1$, by \Cref{p:ns factor} we have
$$
    \pi(B) = (\pi_1 \oplus \pi_2)(\pi_\theta \otimes 1_{2^n}) \in B_{n+1}
$$
almost surely using GEPP with any tie strategy, where $\pi_i \in B_n$ and $\pi_\theta$ comprise the permutations from the corresponding GEPP permutation matrix factors, respectively, for $A_i$ and $R_\theta$. By the inductive hypothesis, $\pi_i \sim \operatorname{Unif}(B_n) = \operatorname{Haar}(B_n)$, with the corresponding GEPP factorizations well-defined almost surely, and so $\pi_1 \oplus \pi_2 \sim \operatorname{Unif}(B_n \oplus B_n)$. Moreover,
$$
    \pi_\theta\otimes 1
    \sim \operatorname{Haar}\bigl(\langle (1\ 2)\otimes 1_{2^n}\rangle\bigr)
    \sim \operatorname{Unif}\bigl((B_n\oplus B_n)\backslash B_{n+1}\bigr),
$$
that is, it is a uniform representative of the right cosets of the subgroup $B_n\oplus B_n$ in $B_{n+1}$ using \eqref{eq: Bn form}. This is independent of $\pi_1\oplus\pi_2$. It follows from the Subgroup algorithm (\Cref{thm: subgroup alg}) that
$$
    \pi(B)\sim\operatorname{Haar}(B_{n+1})=\operatorname{Unif}(B_{n+1}),
$$
as desired.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{p: beta pivot}
    Let $A \sim H_\beta$ be the $n \times n$ tridiagonal $\beta$-Hermite ensemble and $p_n(\beta) = \mathbb{P}(i_1 = 1)$. Then for every $\beta > 0$ and $n \ge 2$,
    \[
    p_n(\beta) = I_{2/3}\left(\frac{\beta(n-1)}2,\frac12\right) \sim \sqrt{\frac6{\pi\beta(n-1)}}\left(\frac23\right)^{\beta(n-1)/2},
    \]
    where $I_z(a,b)$ is the regularized incomplete beta function. In particular, $p_n(\beta)$ decays exponentially in $n$, faster than the order $1/n$ decay for a uniform permutation.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
The first pivot is $i_1 = 1$ exactly when $|A_{11}| \ge |A_{21}|$. Since $A_{11}^2 \sim \chi_1^2$ and $A_{21}^2 \sim \chi^2_{\beta(n-1)}/2$ are independent, this can be evaluated exactly using the $F$ distribution, where $F_{\mu,\nu} \sim (\chi^2_\mu/\mu)/(\chi^2_\nu/\nu)$ for independent numerator and denominator, with cumulative distribution function
    \[
    \mathbb{P}(F_{\mu,\nu} \le x) = I_{\mu x/(\mu x+\nu)}\left(\frac\mu2,\frac\nu2\right).
    \]
    Taking $\mu = \beta(n-1)$, $\nu = 1$, and $x = 2/\mu$ gives $\mu x = 2$ and $\mu x + \nu = 3$, so
    \[
    p_n(\beta) = \mathbb{P}(|A_{11}| \ge |A_{21}|) = \mathbb{P}\left(F_{\beta(n-1),1}\le \frac2{\beta(n-1)}\right) = I_{2/3}\left(\frac{\beta(n-1)}2,\frac12\right).
    \]
    For the asymptotics, we use the standard large-parameter behavior of the incomplete beta function: for fixed $z \in (0,1)$ and $b > 0$, as $a \to \infty$,
    \[
    I_z(a,b) \sim \frac{z^a(1-z)^{b-1}}{a\,B(a,b)}, \qquad B(a,b) \sim \Gamma(b)\,a^{-b}.
    \]
    With $z = 2/3$, $b = 1/2$, and $a = \beta(n-1)/2$, we have $(1-z)^{-1/2} = \sqrt3$ and $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt\pi$, so
    \[
    p_n(\beta) \sim \frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta(n-1)}}\left(\frac23\right)^{\beta(n-1)/2} = \sqrt{\frac6{\pi\beta(n-1)}}\left(\frac23\right)^{\beta(n-1)/2}. \qedhere
    \]

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{t: banded permuton}
Let $d = d_n \ge 1$ be an integer sequence with $d=o(n)$, and let $A =A_n \in \mathbb R^{n \times n}$ be any random matrix with $A_{ij}=0$ whenever $|i-j|\ge d$. Let $\pi_n=\pi(A)$ be the GEPP permutation. Then
\(        \sum_{j=1}^n |\pi_n(j)-j|
        \le 2(d-1)(n-1)
   \)
surely, and consequently $\mu_{\pi_n}$ converges weakly to the diagonal permuton, the law of $(U,U)$ for $U\sim\operatorname{Unif}([0,1])$.
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We first show that the pivot row $i_k$ chosen at step $k$ satisfies $i_k \in \{k, k+1, \dots, k+d-1\}$. Row swaps and elimination only mix rows $k,\dots,k+d-1$ at step $k$, so the entries of column $k+1$ below row $k+d$ are untouched and remain zero. Inductively, at step $k$ the entries of column $k$ in rows $i > k+d-1$ are zero. If the active column has a positive maximum, the pivot then lies in the window above; if the active column is zero, our convention gives $i_k=k$. Hence the claimed bound on $i_k$ holds in either case.

    Now $\pi_n$ is a product of the transpositions $(k\ i_k)$, $1 \le k \le n-1$. The footrule $D(\sigma) = \sum_j |\sigma(j)-j|$ satisfies $D(\sigma\tau) \le D(\sigma) + D(\tau)$, and $D((k\ i_k)) = 2|i_k - k| \le 2(d-1)$. Hence $D(\pi_n) \le 2(d-1)(n-1)$. Last, we couple $(J/n, \pi_n(J)/n)$ with $(J/n, J/n)$ for $J \sim \operatorname{Unif}\{1,\dots,n\}$. The expected $\ell^1$ distance is
    \[
    \frac1{n^2}\sum_{j=1}^n |\pi_n(j)-j| \le \frac{2(d-1)}{n} \to 0,
    \]
    and $(J/n,J/n)$ converges weakly to $(U,U)$.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{p: gue pn}
    Let $A \sim \operatorname{GUE}(n)$ and define $p_n^{(k)} = \mathbb{P}(\pi(A)(1) = 1)$ for $\pi(A) = \pi(A,k)$ the GEPP induced permutation that determines pivot candidates using $\ell^k$ evaluations of complex entries. Then $\pi(A) \not\sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$  for all $n \ge 2$ and $k \in \{1,2\}$, while $p_n^{(1)}$ is rational and $p_n^{(2)}$ is irrational, where
    \begin{equation}\label{eq: pn GUE}
        p_n^{(1)} = \frac1{2n-1}, \qquad p_n^{(2)} \sim \frac1{\sqrt{2n \log n}}.
    \end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Since $A \sim \operatorname{GUE}(n)$, we note $A_{11} \sim N(0,1)$ and $A_{j1} \sim N_{\mathbb C}(0,1)$, so $A_{j1}\stackrel{d}{=}(X_{j1} + i X_{j2})/\sqrt 2$ for $X_{ij} \stackrel{iid}{\sim}N(0,1)$. 
    
    For $p_n^{(1)}$, we note first
    \[
    |x_1|+|x_2| = \max(|x_1 + x_2|,|x_1-x_2|) = \sqrt 2 \max(|u|,|v|), \qquad u = \frac{x_1+x_2}{\sqrt 2}, v = \frac{x_1-x_2}{\sqrt 2},
    \]
    while $(u,v)$ is an orthogonal transformation of $(x_1,x_2)$ via a scaled Hadamard transformation. It follows
    \[
    p_n^{(1)} = \mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt 2 |X_{11}| \ge \max_{2 \le j \le n}[|X_{j1}| + |X_{j2}|]\right) = \mathbb{P}\left(|X_{11}| \ge \max_{2 \le j \le n} \max(|U_j|,|V_j|)\right),
    \]
    where we note the $\sqrt 2$ factor can now be canceled from each term. Now $X_{11},U_j,V_j$ for $2 \le j \le n$ comprise $1+2(n-1) = 2n-1$ iid $N(0,1)$ random variables (using the orthogonal invariance of Gaussian vectors). So $p_n^{(1)}$ is just the probability a particular one of these is maximal in magnitude, which is precisely $1/(2n-1)$ by exchangeability. So using the $\ell^1$ norm, then $p_n^{(1)} < 1/n$ for $n \ge 2$ yields $\pi(A) \not\sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$.
    
    Let $Y_j = X_{j1}^2 + X_{j2}^2$ for $2 \le j \le n$; these are iid exponential with mean $2$, so $\mathbb{P}(Y_j \le 2x^2) = 1 - e^{-x^2}$. Conditioning on $X_{11}$ gives
    \[
    p_n^{(2)} = \mathbb{P}\Big(2X_{11}^2 \ge \max_{2 \le j \le n} Y_j\Big) = \mathbb E\big[(1 - e^{-X_{11}^2})^{n-1}\big].
    \]
    Substituting $s = e^{-x^2}$ gives
    \begin{equation}\nonumber
    p_n^{(2)} = \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^1 s^{-1/2}(-\log s)^{-1/2}(1-s)^{n-1}\,ds \sim \frac1{\sqrt{2n\log n}},
    \end{equation}
    where the asymptotics follow as in \Cref{p: goe pn}: the factor $(1-s)^{n-1}$ localizes the integral to $s \asymp 1/n$, where $(-\log s)^{-1/2} \sim (\log n)^{-1/2}$, leaving 
    $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \log n}}\int_0^\infty s^{-1/2}e^{-ns}\,ds = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2n\log n}}.
    $$
    
    To see $p_n^{(2)} \ne 1/n$ for all $n \ge 2$, expand the binomial and use $\mathbb E[e^{-kX^2}] = (1+2k)^{-1/2}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq: gue exact l2}
    p_n^{(2)} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (-1)^k \binom{n-1}{k} (1+2k)^{-1/2}.
    \end{equation}
    By Bertrand's postulate there is a prime $q = 1+2k_0$ with $n < q < 2n$. No other odd integer $m \le 2n-1$ has squarefree part $q$, since such $m$ would be $j^2 q \ge 9q > 2n-1$. Since square roots of distinct squarefree integers are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$ (see, e.g., \cite{fractional_powers}), and $q^{-1/2}$ has nonzero coefficient $(-1)^{k_0}\binom{n-1}{k_0}$, the sum is irrational. Hence $p_n^{(2)} \ne 1/n$ and so $\pi(A) \not\sim \operatorname{Unif}(S_n)$ for the $\ell^2$ norm.
