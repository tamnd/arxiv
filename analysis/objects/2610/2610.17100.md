<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
For every Boolean function $f$ and every input $x$,
\[
C(f,x)=\tau(\mathcal B_f(x)),
\]
where $\tau$ denotes the minimum size of a set meeting every member of
$\mathcal B_f(x)$.  Equivalently, it is enough to hit the
inclusion-minimal members of $\mathcal B_f(x)$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
If $S$ is a certificate at $x$, then $S$ must meet every
$B\in\mathcal B_f(x)$; otherwise $x^B$ agrees with $x$ on all coordinates
of $S$ but satisfies $f(x^B)\ne f(x)$.

Conversely, suppose $S$ meets every member of $\mathcal B_f(x)$.  If $S$
were not a certificate, there would be an input $y$ agreeing with $x$ on
$S$ but satisfying $f(y)\ne f(x)$.  Let
\[
B=\{i\in\mathbb Z_n:x_i\ne y_i\}.
\]
Then $y=x^B$, so $B\in\mathcal B_f(x)$, while $B\cap S=\varnothing$, a
contradiction.  Hence $S$ is a certificate.  Finally, every sensitive block
contains an inclusion-minimal sensitive block, so the transversal number is
unchanged if only the inclusion-minimal members are retained.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
Let \(f\) be a rotation symmetric Boolean function and \(X=[x]\).  Then
\[
\boxed{\mathcal H_f(X)=\mathcal B_f(x).}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{C(f,X)=\tau(\mathcal H_f(X))}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{bs(f,X)=\nu(\mathcal H_f(X)),}
\]
where \(\nu\) denotes matching number.  In particular,
\[
\boxed{
C(f)=\max_{X\in\mathcal N_n}\tau(\mathcal H_f(X)),
\qquad
bs(f)=\max_{X\in\mathcal N_n}\nu(\mathcal H_f(X)).
}
\]
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Fix \(X=[x]\).  If
\[
B=\Delta_k(x,y)\in\mathcal D(X,Y)
\]
for a necklace \(Y=[y]\) with \(\bar f(Y)\ne\bar f(X)\), then
\[
x^B=R_ky.
\]
Rotation symmetry gives
\[
f(x^B)=f(R_ky)=f(y)=\bar f(Y)\ne\bar f(X)=f(x).
\]
Hence \(B\in\mathcal B_f(x)\), and therefore
\[
\mathcal H_f(X)\subseteq\mathcal B_f(x).
\]

Conversely, let \(B\in\mathcal B_f(x)\) and put \(y=x^B\).  Then
\(f(y)\ne f(x)\).  If \(Y=[y]\), rotation symmetry implies
\[
\bar f(Y)\ne\bar f(X),
\]
and
\[
B=\{i:x_i\ne y_i\}=\Delta_0(x,y).
\]
Thus \(B\in\mathcal D(X,Y)\subseteq\mathcal H_f(X)\).  Hence
\[
\mathcal B_f(x)\subseteq\mathcal H_f(X),
\]
proving equality.

The certificate formula follows from the preceding transversal
characterization.  The block-sensitivity formula follows because a family
of pairwise disjoint sensitive blocks is exactly a matching in
\(\mathcal B_f(x)=\mathcal H_f(X)\).  Finally, both quantities are invariant
under rotation of \(x\), so maximizing over inputs is equivalent to
maximizing over necklaces.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The transversal/matching interpretation of sensitive blocks is standard for
general Boolean functions.  The theorem above records the additional
rotation-symmetric structure by decomposing the sensitive-block hypergraph
into cyclic difference families indexed by opposite-value necklaces.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
Let \(X=[x]\) and \(Y=[y]\) be binary necklaces of length \(n\).  Then
\[
\Delta_k(x,y)=\Delta_\ell(x,y)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\ell-k\in H_y.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{|\mathcal D(X,Y)|=\frac{n}{h_y}=|Y|.}
\]

Moreover, \(H_x\) acts on \(\mathcal D(X,Y)\) by cyclic translation of
blocks.  Every \(H_x\)-orbit has size
\[
\boxed{\frac{h_x}{|H_x\cap H_y|},}
\]
and the number of such orbits is
\[
\boxed{
\frac{n|H_x\cap H_y|}{h_xh_y}
=
\frac{n}{|H_x+H_y|}.
}
\]
Since \(\mathbb Z_n\) is cyclic,
\[
|H_x\cap H_y|=\gcd(h_x,h_y),
\qquad
|H_x+H_y|=\operatorname{lcm}(h_x,h_y),
\]
so equivalently
\[
\boxed{
\text{orbit size}=
\frac{h_x}{\gcd(h_x,h_y)},
\qquad
\text{number of orbits}=
\frac{n}{\operatorname{lcm}(h_x,h_y)}.
}
\]
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By definition,
\[
\Delta_k(x,y)=\{i:x_i\ne(R_ky)_i\}.
\]
Thus
\[
\Delta_k(x,y)=\Delta_\ell(x,y)
\]
if and only if
\[
x\oplus R_ky=x\oplus R_\ell y,
\]
which is equivalent to
\[
R_ky=R_\ell y.
\]
Hence
\[
R_{\ell-k}y=y,
\]
or equivalently \(\ell-k\in H_y\).  Therefore the distinct members of
\(\mathcal D(X,Y)\) are naturally indexed by the cosets of \(H_y\) in
\(\mathbb Z_n\), giving
\[
|\mathcal D(X,Y)|=\frac{n}{h_y}.
\]

Now let \(a\in H_x\).  Since \(R_ax=x\), rotation of a difference block by
\(a\) gives
\[
\Delta_k(x,y)+a=\Delta_{k+a}(x,y)
\]
with the chosen rotation convention.  Thus \(H_x\) acts on
\(\mathcal D(X,Y)\).

The stabilizer in \(H_x\) of the block \(\Delta_k(x,y)\) consists of those
\(a\in H_x\) for which
\[
\Delta_{k+a}(x,y)=\Delta_k(x,y).
\]
By the first part, this is exactly \(H_x\cap H_y\).  The orbit-stabilizer
theorem therefore gives orbit size
\[
\frac{h_x}{|H_x\cap H_y|}.
\]
Dividing the total number \(n/h_y\) of distinct blocks by this orbit size
gives
\[
\frac{n|H_x\cap H_y|}{h_xh_y}.
\]
For finite subgroups,
\[
|H_x+H_y|
=
\frac{|H_x||H_y|}{|H_x\cap H_y|},
\]
which yields the second displayed form.  Finally, subgroups of the cyclic
group \(\mathbb Z_n\) are uniquely determined by their orders, so their
intersection and generated subgroup have orders
\(\gcd(h_x,h_y)\) and \(\operatorname{lcm}(h_x,h_y)\), respectively.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
Thus the opposite-necklace formula may be organized orbit by orbit.  For a
fixed pair \(X,Y\), the family \(\mathcal D(X,Y)\) has exactly
\(n/h_y\) distinct blocks, arranged into
\[
\frac{n}{\operatorname{lcm}(h_x,h_y)}
\]
orbits under the stabilizer of \(X\).  This gives a structural compression
of the sensitive-block hypergraph whenever \(X\) has nontrivial rotational
symmetry.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
Let \(f\) be rotation symmetric and \(X=[x]\).  If
\[
s(f,X)=C(f,X),
\]
then
\[
\boxed{s(f,X)=bs(f,X)=C(f,X).}
\]
At the global level, if
\[
s(f)=C(f),
\]
then
\[
\boxed{s(f)=bs(f)=C(f).}
\]
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Both statements follow immediately from
\[
s(f,X)\le bs(f,X)\le C(f,X)
\]
and
\[
s(f)\le bs(f)\le C(f),
\]
respectively.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
Let \(f\) be a rotation symmetric Boolean function and \(X=[x]\).  Put
\[
r=C(f,X)-s(f,X).
\]
Suppose there exist \(r\) pairwise disjoint sensitive blocks
\[
B_1,\ldots,B_r\subseteq N_f(x).
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{bs(f,X)=C(f,X).}
\]
Equivalently, it is enough to find \(r\) pairwise disjoint difference
blocks
\[
B_j=\Delta_{k_j}(x,y_j)
\]
with
\[
\bar f([y_j])\ne\bar f(X)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
B_j\subseteq N_f(x).
\]
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The \(s(f,X)\) sensitive coordinates give \(s(f,X)\) pairwise disjoint
sensitive singleton blocks
\[
\{i\},\qquad i\in S_f(x).
\]
By assumption, the blocks \(B_1,\ldots,B_r\) are pairwise disjoint and lie
entirely in \(N_f(x)\), so they are also disjoint from all of these
sensitive singleton blocks.  Hence
\[
bs(f,X)\ge s(f,X)+r=C(f,X).
\]
Since always \(bs(f,X)\le C(f,X)\), equality follows.

The equivalent necklace formulation follows from the
opposite-necklace difference theorem, which identifies the sensitive blocks
at \(x\) with the members of \(\mathcal H_f(X)\).
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
If
\[
C(f,X)=s(f,X)+1
\]
and the nonsensitive-coordinate set \(N_f(x)\) contains a sensitive block,
then
\[
\boxed{bs(f,X)=C(f,X)=s(f,X)+1.}
\]
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
The criterion explains the mechanism in several exact families below.  When
\(s<C\), the sensitive singleton blocks already account for \(s\) disjoint
blocks; the remaining task is to pack enough additional sensitive blocks
entirely inside the nonsensitive coordinates.  In the quadratic and
consecutive quartic families, this is precisely how the strict cases
\(s<bs=C\) arise.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
Consider
\[
Q_{6,1}(x)=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb Z_6}x_ix_{i+1},
\]
and let
\[
X=[000000],\qquad Y=[000011].
\]
We have
\[
Q_{6,1}(000000)=0,\qquad Q_{6,1}(000011)=1,
\]
so \(Y\) is an opposite-value necklace for \(X\).  Taking \(x=000000\)
and \(y=000011\), the six difference blocks are
\[
\mathcal D(X,Y)=
\bigl\{
\{4,5\},\{3,4\},\{2,3\},\{1,2\},\{0,1\},\{0,5\}
\bigr\}.
\]
Thus this single opposite necklace contributes the six edges of the cycle
\(C_6\) to the sensitive-block hypergraph at \(X\).

Here \(H_x=\mathbb Z_6\), while \(y\) has trivial stabilizer.  The
difference-orbit theorem therefore predicts six distinct blocks forming one
\(H_x\)-orbit of size six, exactly as displayed above.  This example also
shows concretely how an opposite necklace contributes an entire cyclic
family of sensitive blocks rather than a single block.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
Let $M_A$ be a monomial rotation symmetric Boolean function on $n$
variables. If $|A|$ is odd, then
\[
\boxed{s(M_A)=bs(M_A)=C(M_A)=n.}
\]
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $h=|\{t\in\mathbb Z_n:A+t=A\}|$ be the size of the
translation stabilizer of $A$.  A fixed coordinate $j$ lies in exactly $|A|/h$ members of $O(A)$:
there are $|A|$ choices of a member of $A$ that can be translated to $j$,
and each orbit member is counted $h$ times. Since $h$ divides $|A|$,
the number $|A|/h$ is odd when $|A|$ is odd. At the all-one input,
$D_jM_A(1^n)$ equals that number modulo two, and therefore equals $1$
for every $j$.  Hence $s(M_A)=n$.  The standard inequalities
\[
s(M_A)\le bs(M_A)\le C(M_A)\le n
\]
force equality throughout.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
Assume $k\ne n/2$. For every $x\in\{0,1\}^n$,
\[
\boxed{C(Q_{n,k},x)=n-\alpha(G_{n,k}[Z(x)]),}
\]
where $\alpha(H)$ denotes the independence number of $H$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let $U$ be the set of coordinates left unspecified by a certificate at
$x$. If two adjacent vertices $j$ and $j+k$ both belong to $U$, then after
all coordinates outside $U$ are fixed, the algebraic normal form still
contains the quadratic term $x_jx_{j+k}$. No other term has the same pair
of free variables. Thus the restricted function cannot be constant.
Therefore $U$ must be independent in $G_{n,k}$.

If $j\in U$, then both $j-k$ and $j+k$ are fixed. The two terms involving
$x_j$ have sum
\[
x_{j-k}x_j\oplus x_jx_{j+k}
=x_j(x_{j-k}\oplus x_{j+k}).
\]
Thus the restricted function is independent of $x_j$ exactly when
$x_{j-k}=x_{j+k}$, so $j\in Z(x)$.

Conversely, if $U$ is an independent subset of $Z(x)$, no quadratic term
contains two free coordinates, and the coefficient of every free variable
is zero. Hence the restricted function is constant. Therefore the maximum
number of coordinates that may be left unspecified is
$\alpha(G_{n,k}[Z(x)])$, proving the formula.
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
Let $1\le k\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. If $n$ is even and $k=n/2$, then
\[
\boxed{C(Q_{n,k})=n.}
\]
Otherwise, put
\[
d=\gcd(n,k),\qquad m=\frac nd.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
C(Q_{n,k})=
\begin{cases}
n,&m\equiv0\pmod4,\\[1mm]
n-d,&m\not\equiv0\pmod4.
\end{cases}}
\]
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
In the short-orbit case,
\[
Q_{n,n/2}(x)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n/2-1}x_ix_{i+n/2}.
\]
At $1^n$ every coordinate is sensitive, so
$C(Q_{n,n/2})=n$.

Now suppose $k\ne n/2$. The graph $G_{n,k}$ consists of $d$ disjoint
cycles of length $m$. On one component write the input values in cyclic
order as
\[
a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{m-1}
\]
and define
\[
y_i=a_i\oplus a_{i+1},\qquad i\pmod m.
\]
Since the sequence is cyclic,
\[
\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m-1}y_i=0. \tag{4.1}
\]
Moreover,
\[
a_{i-1}=a_{i+1}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
y_{i-1}=y_i. \tag{4.2}
\]

Suppose first that $4\mid m$. Choose
\[
y=(01)^{m/2}.
\]
It has $m/2$ ones, an even number, so (4.1) holds. No two cyclically
consecutive entries are equal. Thus $Z(x)$ is empty on this component.
Making this choice on every component gives $Z(x)=\varnothing$, and the
lemma yields $C(Q_{n,k},x)=n$. Hence $C(Q_{n,k})=n$.

Now assume $4\nmid m$. If a component contained no vertex of $Z(x)$, then
by (4.2) its transition sequence would alternate cyclically. Hence $m$
would be even. Such an alternating word has $m/2$ ones, and (4.1) would
force $m/2$ to be even. Thus $4\mid m$, a contradiction. Hence every
component contains at least one vertex of $Z(x)$. Choosing one from each
component gives an independent set, so
\[
\alpha(G_{n,k}[Z(x)])\ge d.
\]
Therefore
\[
C(Q_{n,k},x)\le n-d \tag{4.3}
\]
for every $x$.

It remains to attain equality. If $m=4q+1$, choose
\[
y=00(10)^{2q-1}1.
\]
It has $2q$ ones, and exactly one cyclic pair of consecutive entries is
equal. Thus the induced subgraph on $Z(x)$ has independence number $1$.

If $m=4q+3$, choose
\[
y=(01)^{2q+1}1.
\]

Put $n=6q+2$ and consider
\[
a=(111100)^q11.
\]
We first prove
\[
C(R_n,a)=n-1.
\]
For any coordinate $j$, the Boolean derivative is
\[
\begin{aligned}
D_jR_n(x)={}&x_{j+1}x_{j+2}x_{j+3}
\oplus x_{j-1}x_{j+1}x_{j+2}\\
&\oplus x_{j-2}x_{j-1}x_{j+1}
\oplus x_{j-3}x_{j-2}x_{j-1},
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.1}
\]
where indices are modulo $n$.

Leave coordinate $2$ unspecified and fix all other coordinates according
to $a$. Since
\[
D_2R_n
=x_3x_4x_5\oplus x_1x_3x_4
\oplus x_0x_1x_3\oplus x_{n-1}x_0x_1,
\]
and
\[
a_0=a_1=a_3=a_{n-1}=1,\qquad a_4=0,
\]
we obtain
\[
D_2R_n(a)=0\oplus0\oplus1\oplus1=0.
\]
The derivative does not involve $x_2$, so after all other coordinates are
fixed the value of $R_n$ is independent of $x_2$. Hence
\[
C(R_n,a)\le n-1.
\tag{5.2}
\]

Substitution of the six-periodic pattern $111100$ into (5.1), together
with the terminal symbols $11$, gives
\[
D_jR_n(a)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
j\in\{2,n-1\}.
\tag{5.3}
\]
Away from the cyclic boundary this follows by checking the six residue
classes modulo $6$; the boundary positions follow directly from the final
pattern $\cdots11110011$. Thus precisely $2$ and $n-1$ are nonsensitive at
$a$, and
\[
s(R_n,a)=n-2.
\]
Every sensitive coordinate belongs to every certificate at $a$. Therefore
a certificate of size at most $n-2$ would have to leave both $2$ and
$n-1$ unspecified. If all other coordinates are fixed according to $a$,
the cyclic window
\[
\{n-1,0,1,2\}
\]
contributes the quadratic monomial
\[
x_{n-1}x_2.
\]
No other length-four window contains both coordinates, so this term cannot
cancel. The restricted function is nonconstant. Hence the two coordinates
cannot both be omitted, and every certificate has size at least $n-1$.
Together with (5.2),
\[
C(R_n,a)=n-1.
\tag{5.4}
\]

We next determine the block sensitivity at $a$. All $n-2$ sensitive
coordinates other than $2$ and $n-1$ give pairwise disjoint sensitive
singleton blocks. Moreover,
\[
B=\{2,n-1\}
\]
is itself a sensitive block. Indeed, with all other variables fixed as in
$a$, the restricted algebraic normal form contains the variable-dependent
part
\[
x_2\oplus x_{n-1}\oplus x_2x_{n-1}.
\]
At the original values $(x_2,x_{n-1})=(1,1)$ this expression equals $1$,
whereas after flipping both coordinates it equals $0$. Thus
\[
R_n(a^B)\ne R_n(a).
\]
The block $B$ together with the $n-2$ sensitive singleton blocks gives
$n-1$ pairwise disjoint sensitive blocks. Therefore
\[
bs(R_n,a)\ge n-1.
\]
By the general inequality $bs(f,x)\le C(f,x)$ and (5.4),
\[
bs(R_n,a)=C(R_n,a)=n-1.
\tag{5.5}
\]
Hence globally
\[
bs(R_n)\ge n-1,\qquad C(R_n)\ge n-1.
\]

It remains to prove the matching global upper bound. Since the known
sensitivity formula gives
\[
s(R_n)=n-2<n,
\]
every input $x$ has at least one nonsensitive coordinate $j$. Fixing all
coordinates except $j$ is then a certificate: with the other $n-1$
coordinates fixed, the restricted Boolean function has only the variable
$x_j$, and nonsensitivity at $x$ says that its two possible values agree.
Consequently
\[
C(R_n,x)\le n-1
\]
for every $x$, and hence
\[
C(R_n)\le n-1.
\]
Together with (5.4), this gives
\[
C(R_n)=n-1.
\]
Finally,
\[
n-1\le bs(R_n)\le C(R_n)=n-1,
\]
so
\[
bs(R_n)=C(R_n)=n-1=6q+1.
\]
Combining this with $s(R_n)=6q$ completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
Let $n\ge10$, and suppose that $R_n$ has at least three nonsensitive
coordinates at an input $x$. Then there are two nonsensitive coordinates
$i,j$ such that
\[
D_iD_jR_n(x)=0.
\]
Consequently, after all coordinates except $i,j$ are fixed according to
$x$, the resulting two-variable restriction is constant.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
If two nonsensitive coordinates have cyclic distance at least $4$, no
length-four monomial contains both, so their mixed derivative vanishes.
Otherwise every pair among any three nonsensitive coordinates has cyclic
distance at most $3$. For $n\ge10$, three such positions must lie in
four consecutive positions: if their three cyclic gaps were all at most
$3$, their sum would be $n\le9$; if a gap is larger, the other two
place the three points in an arc of length at most $3$.
Up to rotation and reflection, the triple is therefore either
$\{0,1,2\}$ or $\{0,1,3\}$.

It remains to exclude the possibility that all three mixed derivatives
are $1$ while all three first derivatives vanish. This is a finite local
calculation involving only $x_{-3},x_{-2},\ldots,x_6$. Exhaustively
evaluating the $2^{10}$ assignments to the ten-variable window gives
\[
\begin{array}{c|r|r}
\text{triple}&\substack{\text{three first}\\\text{derivatives zero}}&
\substack{\text{first derivatives zero,}\\\text{three mixed derivatives one}}\\\hline
\{0,1,2\}&568&0\\
\{0,1,3\}&554&0
\end{array}
\]
For $\{0,1,2\}$, only nine of these ten variables occur in the three first derivatives; hence the count $568$ is twice the corresponding nine-variable count $284$. The tenth variable is retained to use a common enumeration for both triples.The calculation uses only the defining polynomial for $R_n$ and Boolean
differences $D_if(x)=f(x)\oplus f(x^{\{i\}})$. Its complete executable verification is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:verification}, and is also supplied as a standalone Python file.
In particular, the local
calculation is independent of $n\ge10$, because the relevant ten
positions are distinct. Thus some pair $i,j$ of the nonsensitive
coordinates has $D_iD_jR_n(x)=0$.

Fixing every coordinate except $i,j$ leaves a polynomial
$g(u,v)=c\oplus au\oplus bv\oplus duv$.
The mixed derivative condition yields $d=0$; nonsensitivity of $i,j$
then yields $a=b=0$. Hence $g$ is constant.

gives $C(R_n,x)\le n-2$.

It remains to consider an input with $s(R_n,x)=n-2$, so that exactly two
coordinates are nonsensitive.  We use the finite-state framework developed
in our companion work on the sensitivity of rotation symmetric Boolean
functions~\cite{LiZhangRSBFSensitivity}.  We use the finite-state excursion classification, uniqueness claims,
and avoidance bound established in that companion paper as an external
lemma; we reproduce the particular certificate and explain how it yields
the certificate- and block-sensitivity conclusion here. Thus the proof
below is conditional on the cited finite-state results, rather than an
independent proof of those results.  The weight-one state graph
has the unique recurrent six-cycle
\[
001111\to011110\to111100\to111001
\to110011\to100111\to001111.
\]
The extremal closed walk under consideration has exactly two weight-zero
transitions, corresponding to its two nonsensitive coordinates; the six-cycle
itself consists entirely of weight-one transitions. A
closed walk avoiding this six-cycle satisfies the avoidance bound
\[
s(R_n,x)\le\frac{3n}{5}<n-2,
\]
so an extremal walk must visit the cycle.

Decompose the walk into primitive excursions between successive visits to
the cycle. If $z(P)$ is the number of weight-zero transitions and
\[
\delta(P)=\phi(\operatorname{end}P)-\phi(\operatorname{start}P)-|P|
\pmod6,
\]
the finite excursion certificate from the sensitivity proof shows that,
among positive-cost primitive excursions of cost at most two, the possible
pairs $(z,\delta)$ are
\[
(1,1),\qquad(1,5),\qquad(2,4).
\]
Since the complete walk has length $6q+4$, its total phase defect is
$2\pmod6$. With total defect cost two, the only possible decomposition is
\[
(1,1)+(1,1).
\]
By the excursion uniqueness result in
\cite{LiZhangRSBFSensitivity}, the primitive excursion of type $(1,1)$ is
\[
011110\to111101\to111011\to110111
\to101111\to011110,
\]
with transition weights
\[
1,1,0,1,1.
\]
Thus each excursion contributes one nonsensitive coordinate, and even when
the two excursions are consecutive their zero-weight transitions are
separated by five positions. If $i,j$ are the two nonsensitive coordinates,
then $\operatorname{dist}(i,j)\ge5$. No length-four monomial contains both,
so $D_iD_jR_n(x)=0$. As in the lemma, fixing every coordinate except
$i,j$ makes the restricted function constant. Hence
\[
C(R_n,x)\le n-2.
\]

We have proved $C(R_n,x)\le n-2$ for every input $x$, and therefore
$C(R_n)\le n-2$. Finally,
\[
n-2=s(R_n)\le bs(R_n)\le C(R_n)\le n-2,
\]
which proves the result.
