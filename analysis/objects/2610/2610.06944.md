<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:eq}
Let $s>1$.  An \emph{$s$-equilibrium} is a set $X\subset\mathbb{R}$ with at least
two points that is locally finite (every bounded interval contains only
finitely many points of $X$) and satisfies, for every $x\in X$,
\begin{gather}
\sum_{y\in X\setminus\{x\}}|y-x|^{-s}<\infty, \label{eq:E1}\\
\sum_{y\in X\setminus\{x\}}\operatorname{sgn}(y-x)\,|y-x|^{-s}=0. \label{eq:E2}
\end{gather}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $1<s\le2$, and let $X$ be an $s$-equilibrium, that is, a locally finite
set $X\subset\mathbb{R}$ with at least two points, such that for every $x\in X$ the
total force \eqref{eq:E1} from both sides is finite and the net force
\eqref{eq:E2} is zero.  Then $X=\{\alpha n+\beta:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ for some
$\alpha>0$ and $\beta\in\mathbb{R}$.  Equivalently, if $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$ is the
increasing enumeration of $X$ (\cref{lem:order}), then $a_n=\alpha n+\beta$
for all $n$.  Conversely, every such set is an $s$-equilibrium.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:scope}
(a) The note \cite{Ben15} does not say in which sense the series converges.
We read $\sum_{i\ne n}$ as a sum over an unordered index set, that is, as
an absolutely convergent series; this gives \eqref{eq:E1} and
\eqref{eq:E2}.  The arXiv version of \cite{GK17} defines equilibrium in the
same way: the total force on each particle from each side is finite, and
the net force is zero.  The published version states the finiteness
assumption for the force law instead (the one-sided forces are finite when
the particles sit at the integers) and defines equilibrium as zero net
force.  Its proofs compare one-sided forces, which therefore have to be
finite.  A ``principal value'' reading, in which the one-sided forces are
infinite and only symmetric partial sums cancel, is not covered by
\cref{thm:main}.

(b) We take $(a_n)$ to be the increasing enumeration of the configuration,
as \cite{GK17} do when they ask about the distances between subsequent
particles.  For an arbitrary bijection $\mathbb{Z}\to X$ the identity
$a_n=\alpha n+\beta$ fails trivially, already for a re-enumerated
arithmetic progression.

(c) For $s\le1$ an arithmetic progression violates \eqref{eq:E1}.  Problem~1
of \cite{GK17} for a general strictly decreasing force law remains open, and
we make no claim for exponents $s>2$ (\cref{rem:bigs,rem:generalF}).
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:order}
The set $X$ is unbounded above and below.  Hence $X=\{a_n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ for a
strictly increasing sequence with $a_n\to\pm\infty$ as $n\to\pm\infty$, and
this enumeration is unique up to a shift of the index.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Suppose that $X$ is bounded above.  Since $X$ is locally finite, it has a
largest element $x$, and the sum in \eqref{eq:E2} at $x$ equals
$-\sum_{y\in X,\,y<x}(x-y)^{-s}<0$, because $X$ has at least two points.
This contradicts \eqref{eq:E2}, and the same argument applies below.  A
locally finite set that is unbounded in both directions has exactly one
increasing enumeration by $\mathbb{Z}$ with a prescribed point at index $0$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:ratio}
Let $r_s$ be the unique positive solution of $2r^{-s}+(1+r)^{-s}=1$.  Then
$r_s>1$, and
\[
r_s^{-1}g_{n-1}\le g_n\le r_s\,g_{n-1}\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z} .
\]
For $s=2$, $r_2=1.538615\ldots$
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Fix $n$, and write $y=a_n$, $z=a_{n+1}$, $u=g_{n-1}$ and $v=g_n$.  For
$i\ge n+2$ we have $a_i-y>a_i-z>0$, hence
\[
F_n^+=v^{-s}+\sum_{i\ge n+2}(a_i-y)^{-s}\le v^{-s}+F_{n+1}^+ .
\]
The terms $i=n$ and $i=n-1$ of $F_{n+1}^-$ are $v^{-s}$ and $(u+v)^{-s}$,
and for $i\le n-2$ we have $z-a_i>y-a_i>0$.  Hence
\[
F_{n+1}^-\le v^{-s}+(u+v)^{-s}+\sum_{i\le n-2}(y-a_i)^{-s}
=v^{-s}+(u+v)^{-s}+F_n^--u^{-s}.
\]
Using $F_n^-=F_n^+$ and $F_{n+1}^-=F_{n+1}^+$, we obtain
\[
F_n^-=F_n^+\le v^{-s}+F_{n+1}^-\le2v^{-s}+(u+v)^{-s}+F_n^--u^{-s}.
\]
Since $F_n^-<\infty$, this gives $u^{-s}\le2v^{-s}+(u+v)^{-s}$, that is,
$h_s(v/u)\ge1$ with $h_s(r)=2r^{-s}+(1+r)^{-s}$.  The function $h_s$
decreases strictly from $+\infty$ to $0$ on $(0,\infty)$.  So $r_s$ is well
defined, $v/u\le r_s$, and $r_s>1$ because $h_s(1)>1$.

The reflected set $-X$ is again an $s$-equilibrium.  Its increasing
enumeration $b_m=-a_{-m}$ has the gaps $b_{m+1}-b_m=g_{-m-1}$.  The bound
just proved, applied to $-X$ at the index $m=-n$, gives
$g_{n-1}\le r_sg_n$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:harmonic}
For integers $n\ne j$ put $D=|a_j-a_n|$ and $D'=|a_{j+1}-a_{n+1}|$, and let
\[
c(n,j)=\frac{D^{-s}-D'^{-s}}{D'-D}\quad\text{if }D\ne D',\qquad
c(n,j)=sD^{-s-1}\quad\text{if }D=D'.
\]
Then $c(n,j)=c(j,n)>0$, and for every $n\in\mathbb{Z}$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:harmonic}
\sum_{j\ne n}c(n,j)\,(g_j-g_n)=0,
\end{equation}
where the series converges absolutely.  For $s=2$,
$c(n,j)=(D+D')/(D^2D'^2)$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For $m\in\mathbb{Z}$ let $\Phi_m=\sum_{i\ne m}\operatorname{sgn}(a_i-a_m)|a_i-a_m|^{-s}$.  By
\eqref{eq:E1} and \eqref{eq:E2} this series converges absolutely, and
$\Phi_m=0$.  Substituting $i=j+1$ in $\Phi_{n+1}$ we get
\[
0=\Phi_n-\Phi_{n+1}=\sum_{j\ne n}\Bigl(\operatorname{sgn}(a_j-a_n)D^{-s}
-\operatorname{sgn}(a_{j+1}-a_{n+1})D'^{-s}\Bigr),
\]
where $D$ and $D'$ depend on $j$ as in the statement.  The subtraction may
be done termwise, because both series converge absolutely.  If $j>n$, both
signs are $+1$ and $D'-D=(a_{j+1}-a_j)-(a_{n+1}-a_n)=g_j-g_n$, so the
$j$-th term is $D^{-s}-D'^{-s}=c(n,j)(g_j-g_n)$.  If $j<n$, both signs are
$-1$ and $D-D'=(a_n-a_j)-(a_{n+1}-a_{j+1})=g_j-g_n$, so the $j$-th term is
$D'^{-s}-D^{-s}=c(n,j)(g_j-g_n)$.  (If $D=D'$, both expressions vanish.)
This proves \eqref{eq:harmonic}.

The numbers $D$ and $D'$ do not change when $n$ and $j$ are exchanged.
Moreover $c(n,j)$ is the divided difference of the strictly decreasing
function $t\mapsto t^{-s}$ at $D$ and $D'$, with the sign reversed.  Hence
$c(n,j)=c(j,n)>0$.  For $s=2$,
$(D^{-2}-D'^{-2})/(D'-D)=(D+D')/(D^2D'^2)$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:comparison}
For $n\ne j$ let $D,D'$ be as in \cref{lem:harmonic}.  Then
$\min\{D,D'\}\ge D/(1+r_s)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cbound}
c(n,j)\le K_s\,|a_j-a_n|^{-s-1},\qquad K_s:=s(1+r_s)^{s+1}.
\end{equation}
For $s=2$ one also has $c(n,j)\le(1+r_2)(2+r_2)|a_j-a_n|^{-3}$, with
$(1+r_2)(2+r_2)=8.983\ldots$  Consequently
$\pi(n):=\sum_{j\ne n}c(n,j)<\infty$, and
$\sum_{j\ne n}c(n,j)\,g_j=\pi(n)\,g_n$ for every $n$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By symmetry we may assume $j>n$.  If $j=n+1$, then $D=g_n$ and
$D'=g_{n+1}\ge g_n/r_s$ by \cref{lem:ratio}.  If $j\ge n+2$, then
$D\ge g_n+g_{n+1}\ge(1+r_s^{-1})g_n$, so $g_n\le r_sD/(1+r_s)$ and
$D'=D+g_j-g_n\ge D-g_n\ge D/(1+r_s)$.  In both cases
$\min\{D,D'\}\ge D/(1+r_s)$.  By the mean value theorem,
$c(n,j)=s\xi^{-s-1}$ for some $\xi$ between $D$ and $D'$, so
$c(n,j)\le s\min\{D,D'\}^{-s-1}\le K_sD^{-s-1}$.  For $s=2$,
$c(n,j)=D^{-1}D'^{-2}+D^{-2}D'^{-1}\le\bigl((1+r_2)^2+(1+r_2)\bigr)D^{-3}$.

Every $j\ne n$ satisfies $|a_j-a_n|\ge\delta_n:=\min\{g_{n-1},g_n\}>0$, so
$\pi(n)\le K_s\delta_n^{-1}(F_n^-+F_n^+)<\infty$.  Finally, the series
$\sum_jc(n,j)(g_j-g_n)$ converges absolutely with sum $0$ by
\cref{lem:harmonic}, and $\sum_jc(n,j)g_n=\pi(n)g_n$.  Adding the two
series gives the last identity.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:dirichlet}
Let $x_0\in V$, and let $f\colon V\to\mathbb{R}$ be finitely supported with
$f(x_0)=1$.  Then
\[
\pi(x_0)\,P_{x_0}\bigl[X_t\ne x_0\text{ for all }t\ge1\bigr]\le\mathcal{E}_c(f).
\]
Consequently, if $\inf\mathcal{E}_c(f)=0$ over all such $f$, the walk is
recurrent.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $B=\{x_0\}\cup\operatorname{supp}f$ and $A=B\setminus\{x_0\}$, both
finite.  For $S\subset V$ let $\tau_S=\inf\{t\ge0:X_t\in S\}$ and
$\tau_S^+=\inf\{t\ge1:X_t\in S\}$.  By irreducibility, from every $x\in A$
the walk leaves $A$ within $|A|$ steps with positive probability.  Since
$A$ is finite, the Markov property shows that, from any starting point,
the walk stays in $A$ forever with probability $0$.

Let $h(x)=P_x[\tau_{\{x_0\}}<\tau_{V\setminus B}]$.  Then $0\le h\le1$,
$h(x_0)=1$, $h=0$ off $B$, and $Ph(x)=h(x)$ for $x\in A$ (first-step
analysis).  By the same analysis at $x_0$,
\[
q:=P_{x_0}\bigl[\tau^+_{V\setminus B}<\tau^+_{\{x_0\}}\bigr]
=1-Ph(x_0)=h(x_0)-Ph(x_0).
\]
By \eqref{eq:green} with $u=w=h$,
\[
\mathcal{E}_c(h)=\sum_{x\in B}h(x)\,\pi(x)\bigl(h(x)-Ph(x)\bigr)=\pi(x_0)\,q .
\]
The function $\eta=f-h$ vanishes at $x_0$ and off $B$, so it is supported
in $A$.  By \eqref{eq:green} with $u=\eta$ and $w=h$,
\[
\frac12\sum_{x,y}c(x,y)\bigl(h(x)-h(y)\bigr)\bigl(\eta(x)-\eta(y)\bigr)
=\sum_{x\in A}\eta(x)\,\pi(x)\bigl(h(x)-Ph(x)\bigr)=0 .
\]
All energies involved are finite, so
$\mathcal{E}_c(f)=\mathcal{E}_c(h)+\mathcal{E}_c(\eta)\ge\pi(x_0)q$.  Finally, if $X_t\ne x_0$ for
all $t\ge1$, then either the walk visits $V\setminus B$ before returning
to $x_0$, or it stays in $A$ from time $1$ on; the second event has
probability $0$.  Hence the probability of no return is at most
$q\le\mathcal{E}_c(f)/\pi(x_0)$.  If $\inf\mathcal{E}_c(f)=0$, then $x_0$ is recurrent, and
so is every vertex, by irreducibility.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:liouville}
If the walk is recurrent, every positive harmonic function is constant.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $u>0$ be harmonic.  Under $P_x$ the process $u(X_t)$ is a nonnegative
martingale, so it converges almost surely to a finite limit.  By recurrence
and irreducibility, the walk visits every vertex infinitely often, almost
surely.  Hence $u(y)=u(z)$ for all $y,z\in V$.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:doob}
Let $h\colon V\to(0,\infty)$ satisfy $\sum_yc(x,y)h(y)=\pi(x)h(x)$ for all
$x$, that is, let $h$ be a positive harmonic function.  Put
$c^h(x,y)=c(x,y)h(x)h(y)$.  Then the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $\pi^h(x):=\sum_yc^h(x,y)=\pi(x)h(x)^2<\infty$, so $c^h$ is a
network.  Its walk is irreducible and has transition probabilities
$P^h(x,y)=P(x,y)h(y)/h(x)$.
\item[(b)] The function $1/h$ is harmonic for $P^h$.
\item[(c)] $(P^h)^t(x,y)=P^t(x,y)h(y)/h(x)$ for all $t\ge0$.  In
particular, the walks $P$ and $P^h$ have the same return probabilities, and
$c^h$ is recurrent if and only if $c$ is.
\item[(d)] $\mathcal{E}_c(h\varphi)=\mathcal{E}_{c^h}(\varphi)$ for every finitely supported
$\varphi\colon V\to\mathbb{R}$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
(a) We have $\pi^h(x)=h(x)\sum_yc(x,y)h(y)=\pi(x)h(x)^2$, and
$c^h(x,y)/\pi^h(x)=P(x,y)h(y)/h(x)$.  (b) Indeed,
$\sum_yP^h(x,y)/h(y)=\sum_yP(x,y)/h(x)=1/h(x)$.  (c) The formula follows
from (a) by induction on $t$.  Along every path from $x$ back to $x$ the
factors $h(y)/h(x)$ cancel, so the two walks return to $x$ with the same
probability.  (d) Put $u=h\varphi^2$, which is finitely supported.  For
all $x,y$,
\[
\bigl(h(x)\varphi(x)-h(y)\varphi(y)\bigr)^2
=h(x)h(y)\bigl(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\bigr)^2
+\bigl(h(x)-h(y)\bigr)\bigl(u(x)-u(y)\bigr).
\]
Multiply by $c(x,y)/2$ and sum.  The first terms give $\mathcal{E}_{c^h}(\varphi)$,
which is finite because $\varphi$ is finitely supported and $\pi^h<\infty$.
By \eqref{eq:green} with $w=h$, the second terms are absolutely summable,
with sum $\sum_xu(x)\bigl(\pi(x)h(x)-\sum_yc(x,y)h(y)\bigr)=0$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:energy}
Fix $R_1\ge|a_0|$ such that $(-2R_1,2R_1)$ contains at least two points of
$X$.  For $R\ge R_1$, the function $f_R$ is finitely supported,
$f_R(0)=1$, and
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{E}_{c^*}(f_R)&\le K\Bigl(\frac{7(1+r)}{2-s}+\frac{4(1+r)^{s+3}}{s}\Bigr)
R^{1-s}\qquad\text{if }s<2,\\
\mathcal{E}_{c^*}(f_R)&\le\frac KR\Bigl(7(1+r)\max\Bigl\{1,\,\log\frac{(1+r)
\bigl(1+16R^2(F_0^-+F_0^+)\bigr)}{7}\Bigr\}+2(1+r)^5\Bigr)
\qquad\text{if }s=2.
\end{align*}
In both cases $\mathcal{E}_{c^*}(f_R)\to0$ as $R\to\infty$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Since $|a_0|\le R$, we have $f_R(0)=1$, and $f_R$ vanishes outside the
finite set $\{n:|a_n|<2R\}$.  By \eqref{eq:cbound},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
\mathcal{E}_{c^*}(f_R)=\sum_{n<j}c(n,j)\,g_ng_j\bigl(f_R(n)-f_R(j)\bigr)^2
\le K(S_1+S_2),
\end{equation}
where $S_1$ is the sum of $g_ng_j(f_R(n)-f_R(j))^2(a_j-a_n)^{-s-1}$ over
the pairs $n<j$ with $a_j-a_n\le R$, and $S_2$ is the same sum over the
pairs $n<j$ with $a_j-a_n>R$.

\emph{Step 1: the cut-off.}  We have $0\le\varphi_R\le1$ and
$|\varphi_R(x)-\varphi_R(y)|\le|x-y|/R$.  Moreover, $\varphi_R$ is constant
on each of $(-\infty,-2R]$, $[-R,R]$ and $[2R,\infty)$, and it vanishes on
the first and the last.  Hence, if $n<j$ and $f_R(n)\ne f_R(j)$, then
$[a_n,a_j]$ meets $(-2R,-R)\cup(R,2R)$, and at least one of $a_n$, $a_j$
lies in $(-2R,2R)$.  If in addition $a_j-a_n\le R$, then both $a_n$ and
$a_j$ lie in $(-3R,3R)$.

\emph{Step 2: comparison with integrals.}  Let $p>0$ and $n<j$.  Then
\begin{align}
g_j\,(a_j-a_n)^{-p}&\le(1+r)^p\int_{a_j}^{a_{j+1}}(t-a_n)^{-p}\,dt,
\label{eq:int1}\\
g_n\,(a_j-a_n)^{-p}&\le r(1+r)^p\int_{a_{n-1}}^{a_n}(a_j-t)^{-p}\,dt.
\label{eq:int2}
\end{align}
For \eqref{eq:int1}, note that $g_j\le rg_{j-1}\le r(a_j-a_n)$ by
\cref{lem:ratio}, because $j-1\ge n$.  So $t-a_n\le a_{j+1}-a_n\le
(1+r)(a_j-a_n)$ for $t\in[a_j,a_{j+1}]$, and we integrate
$(t-a_n)^{-p}\ge(1+r)^{-p}(a_j-a_n)^{-p}$ over this interval of length
$g_j$.  For \eqref{eq:int2}, similarly, $g_{n-1}\le rg_n\le r(a_j-a_n)$,
so $a_j-t\le(1+r)(a_j-a_n)$ for $t\in[a_{n-1},a_n]$.  Integrating gives
$g_{n-1}(a_j-a_n)^{-p}\le(1+r)^p\int_{a_{n-1}}^{a_n}(a_j-t)^{-p}\,dt$, and
$g_n\le rg_{n-1}$.

\emph{Step 3: the near pairs.}  Let $I=\{n:|a_n|<3R,\ g_n\le R\}$, and for
$n\in I$ let $J(n)=\{j>n:a_j-a_n\le R\}$.  If a pair $n<j$ with
$a_j-a_n\le R$ contributes to $S_1$, then $|a_n|<3R$ by Step~1, and
$g_n\le a_j-a_n\le R$; so $n\in I$ and $j\in J(n)$.  Using
$(f_R(n)-f_R(j))^2\le(a_j-a_n)^2/R^2$ we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S1}
S_1\le R^{-2}\sum_{n\in I}g_n\sum_{j\in J(n)}g_j\,(a_j-a_n)^{1-s}.
\end{equation}
Let $n\in I$ with $J(n)\ne\emptyset$, and let $j^*=\max J(n)$.  The
intervals $[a_j,a_{j+1}]$, $j\in J(n)$, tile $[a_{n+1},a_{j^*+1}]$, and
$a_{j^*+1}-a_n\le(1+r)(a_{j^*}-a_n)\le(1+r)R$ by Step~2.  By
\eqref{eq:int1} with $p=s-1$ (and trivially if $J(n)=\emptyset$, since
$g_n\le R$),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inner}
\sum_{j\in J(n)}g_j\,(a_j-a_n)^{1-s}\le(1+r)^{s-1}\int_{g_n}^{(1+r)R}
\tau^{1-s}\,d\tau
\le\begin{cases}
(1+r)R^{2-s}/(2-s), & s<2,\\
(1+r)\log\bigl((1+r)R/g_n\bigr), & s=2.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The intervals $[a_n,a_{n+1}]$, $n\in I$, are disjoint and lie in
$(-3R,4R)$, so $\sum_{n\in I}g_n\le7R$.  For $s<2$, \eqref{eq:S1} and
\eqref{eq:inner} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S1-s}
S_1\le\frac{7(1+r)}{2-s}\,R^{1-s}.
\end{equation}
For $s=2$, if $I=\emptyset$ then $S_1=0$; otherwise let $N=|I|\ge1$ and $\Sigma=\sum_{n\in I}g_n\le7R$.  The function
$\psi(t)=t\log((1+r)R/t)$ is concave on $(0,\infty)$, so Jensen's
inequality gives $\sum_{n\in I}\psi(g_n)\le N\psi(\Sigma/N)=
\Sigma\log\bigl((1+r)RN/\Sigma\bigr)$.  The function
$\Sigma\mapsto\Sigma\log(\Lambda/\Sigma)$ increases on $(0,\Lambda/e]$ and
is bounded by $\Lambda/e$.  With $\Lambda=(1+r)RN$ we get
$\sum_{n\in I}\psi(g_n)\le7R\max\{1,\log((1+r)N/7)\}$, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S1-2}
S_1\le\frac{7(1+r)}{R}\max\Bigl\{1,\log\frac{(1+r)N}{7}\Bigr\}
\qquad(s=2).
\end{equation}
Finally, if $|a_n|<3R$, then $|a_n-a_0|<4R$ because $|a_0|\le R$.  Every
$n\ne0$ with $|a_n-a_0|\le4R$ contributes at least $(4R)^{-s}$ to
$F_0^-+F_0^+$.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count}
N\le\#\{n:|a_n|<3R\}\le1+(4R)^s\bigl(F_0^-+F_0^+\bigr),
\end{equation}
which is $1+16R^2(F_0^-+F_0^+)$ for $s=2$.

\emph{Step 4: the far pairs.}  By Step~1, every pair $n<j$ that contributes
to $S_2$ has $a_n\in(-2R,2R)$ or $a_j\in(-2R,2R)$.  We bound
$(f_R(n)-f_R(j))^2\le1$ and get
\[
S_2\le\sum_{|a_n|<2R}g_n\sum_{\substack{j>n\\a_j-a_n>R}}
\frac{g_j}{(a_j-a_n)^{s+1}}
+\sum_{|a_j|<2R}g_j\sum_{\substack{n<j\\a_j-a_n>R}}
\frac{g_n}{(a_j-a_n)^{s+1}} .
\]
For fixed $n$, the intervals $[a_j,a_{j+1}]$ with $a_j-a_n>R$ are disjoint
and lie in $[a_n+R,\infty)$.  So by \eqref{eq:int1} with $p=s+1$ the first
inner sum is at most $(1+r)^{s+1}\int_R^\infty\tau^{-s-1}d\tau=
(1+r)^{s+1}R^{-s}/s$.  For fixed $j$, the intervals $[a_{n-1},a_n]$ with
$a_j-a_n>R$ are disjoint and lie in $(-\infty,a_j-R]$.  So by
\eqref{eq:int2} the second inner sum is at most $r(1+r)^{s+1}R^{-s}/s$.
Let $m<M$ be the smallest and the largest index with $a_m,a_M\in(-2R,2R)$;
there are at least two such indices because $R\ge R_1$.  Then
$\sum_{|a_n|<2R}g_n=a_{M+1}-a_m=(a_M-a_m)+g_M$, where $a_M-a_m<4R$ and
$g_M\le rg_{M-1}\le r(a_M-a_m)$.  So $\sum_{|a_n|<2R}g_n\le4(1+r)R$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S2}
S_2\le4(1+r)R\cdot\frac{(1+r)^{s+1}+r(1+r)^{s+1}}{s\,R^{s}}
=\frac{4(1+r)^{s+3}}{s}\,R^{1-s}.
\end{equation}
For $s=2$ this is $2(1+r)^5/R$.  Combining \eqref{eq:split},
\eqref{eq:S1-s}, \eqref{eq:S1-2}, \eqref{eq:count} and \eqref{eq:S2} proves
the proposition.

Let $X$ be an $s$-equilibrium with $1<s\le2$.  By \cref{lem:doob}, applied
to the network $c$ and its positive harmonic function $g$, the transform
$c^*$ is a network, its walk $P^*$ is irreducible, and $1/g$ is a positive
harmonic function for $P^*$.  By \cref{prop:energy} and
\cref{lem:dirichlet}, applied to $c^*$ with $x_0=0$ and $f=f_R$,
\[

\xrightarrow[R\to\infty]{}0 ,
\]

$g_n=g_0$ for all $n$, and $a_n=a_0+g_0n$.

Conversely, let $X=\{\alpha n+\beta:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ with $\alpha>0$.  For every
$x\in X$, both one-sided sums equal $\sum_{k\ge1}(\alpha k)^{-s}<\infty$,

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:doob}
By \cref{lem:doob}(c) with $h=g$, the walk $P^*$ has the same return
probabilities as the walk of $c$.  So, for an equilibrium, $c$ is recurrent
if and only if $c^*$ is: the transform does not turn a transient walk into
a recurrent one.  Its role is to supply test functions.  By
\cref{lem:doob}(d), the finitely supported functions $n\mapsto
g_n\varphi_R(a_n)/g_0$ take the value $1$ at $0$ and have $c$-energy
$\mathcal{E}_{c^*}(f_R)/g_0^2\to0$.  Thus the proof can also be phrased without
$P^*$: the network $c$ is recurrent by \cref{lem:dirichlet}, and $g$, a
positive harmonic function for $c$, is constant by \cref{lem:liouville}.
Heuristically, $c^*(n,j)\approx s\,g_ng_j|a_j-a_n|^{-1-s}$ is a Riemann
sum, with mesh given by the gaps, of the translation-invariant kernel
$s|x-y|^{-1-s}\,dx\,dy$ on $\mathbb{R}$.  This kernel does not see the local
density of the points; for $s=2$ the density enters only through the
logarithm in \eqref{eq:S1-2}.

\Cref{prop:energy} uses equilibrium only through \cref{lem:ratio} and, for
$s=2$, through $F_0^-+F_0^+<\infty$.  Its proof applies to every increasing
sequence whose consecutive gap ratios lie in $[r_s^{-1},r_s]$ and which,
when $s=2$, satisfies the point count \eqref{eq:count}.  For such a
sequence that is not an equilibrium, $g$ is in general not harmonic for the
weights $c$ of \cref{lem:harmonic}, $c^*$ is not a Doob transform of $c$,
and \cref{prop:energy} says nothing about the recurrence of $c$.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rem:uses}
(a) Equilibrium of every particle is used in \cref{lem:ratio} and
\cref{lem:harmonic}.  If a single particle $a_k$ is not in equilibrium,
then \eqref{eq:harmonic} may fail at $n=k-1$ and $n=k$, and the argument
breaks down.  This is consistent with \cite[Theorem~7]{GK17}, which provides
non-trivial configurations in which all particles but one are in
equilibrium.

(b) The finiteness condition \eqref{eq:E1} is used in \cref{lem:harmonic}
and, for $s=2$, in the point count \eqref{eq:count}.  For $s=2$, any bound
with $\log\#\{n:|a_n|<3R\}=o(R)$ would suffice in place of
\eqref{eq:count}.

(c) Local finiteness, which follows from \eqref{eq:E1}, is used in
\cref{lem:order} and to make $f_R$ finitely supported.

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
\label{rem:bigs}
\Cref{lem:order,lem:ratio,lem:harmonic,lem:comparison} and the bound
\eqref{eq:S2} hold for every $s>1$, with the same proofs.  For $s>2$,
however, the integral in \eqref{eq:inner} is of order $g_n^{2-s}$.  Then
the argument above controls $S_1$ only through $R^{-2}\sum_{n\in I}g_n^{3-s}$, which is large
when many gaps are small.  We make no claim for $s>2$; whether the
conclusion of \cref{thm:main} holds there remains open.

<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
\label{rem:generalF}
Let $F\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)$ be strictly decreasing, and assume
that the one-sided forces $\sum_{y\in X,\,y>x}F(y-x)$ and
$\sum_{y\in X,\,y<x}F(x-y)$ are finite and equal for every $x\in X$.  The
proof of \cref{lem:harmonic} then gives \eqref{eq:harmonic} with
$c(n,j)=(F(D)-F(D'))/(D'-D)>0$ when $D\ne D'$ (when $D=D'$ the term
vanishes, and any positive value of $c(n,j)$ will do).  So
\cref{rem:max} recovers \cite[Theorem~1]{GK17} for such configurations.
What is missing for general $F$ is recurrence: our estimates use the
homogeneity of $d^{-s}$ in \cref{lem:ratio,lem:comparison}.  Problem~1 of
\cite{GK17} for general $F$ remains open, and so does the principal-value
reading mentioned in \cref{rem:scope}(a).
