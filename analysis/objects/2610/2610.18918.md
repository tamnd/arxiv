<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
The minimum number of circles satisfies
\[
 f(8)=17,\qquad f(n)=g(n)\quad\text{for every }n\ge15.
\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- relatedremark-1: body_md -->
\Cref{thm:main} was obtained in the summer of 2026 by (at least) five investigations 
within a short period: those of Rafa\l{} Wrona, Liyan Wang, Stijn Cambie,
Denis Paliy, and the forum (on~\cite{Forum506}) user \texttt{mzn}. These investigations were independent, assuming that their AI use would not influence each other (which is unclear). This paper summarises the main insights from those.\\
The block-counting/inversion approach for eight points and the uniform
incidence approach for $n\ge15$ also appear in~\cite{Wrona}.
The seven-point Lemma~\ref{lem:seven} and the pointed
Lemma~\ref{lem:pointed} yield short versions of these arguments.
The carrier estimate, inversion count, and Melchior argument after
adjoining the inversion centre have counterparts in
\cite[Lemmas~2.1, 2.2 and 6.1]{Wang}; the proof in~\cite{Wang} treats
$n=15$ and $n=16$ separately. Paliy's parallel work~\cite{PaliyCorrected}
developed an exact computational and SAT-based approach to the finite
cases, e.g. $n=12,13$, with reproducible verification files.
<!-- end relatedremark-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:seven}
Seven real planar points with no four collinear determine at most six
three-point lines. If there are six, every point outside the set lies
on at most one ordinary line and at most one three-point line.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The seven points are not collinear, since no four are collinear.
Let $t_2,t_3$ count the ordinary and three-point lines. Pair counting gives
$t_2+3t_3=21$, and the ordinary-line theorem gives $t_2>0$, so $t_3\le6$.

At equality, the graph of ordinary pairs has three edges and only even
degrees: a point on $d$ three-point lines has ordinary degree $6-2d$.
A simple graph with three edges and all degrees even must be a triangle.
Hence the three ordinary edges form a triangle, whose supporting lines meet
pairwise at selected points.

The three-point degrees are therefore $2,2,2,3,3,3,3$. Since
$3\binom22+4\binom32=\binom62$, every pair of three-point lines also
meets at a selected point. Two ordinary lines cannot meet outside the selected set, and neither
can two three-point lines.
<!-- end proof-1 -->

Put $u=\sqrt7$ and take the $8$ points with coordinates $(x,y)$ as follows
\[
\begingroup
\setlength{\arraycolsep}{4.1pt}
\renewcommand{1.28}{1.28}
\begin{array}{c|cccccccc}
      &A&B&C&D&E&F&G&H\\ \hline
x&-\tfrac32&-\tfrac12&\tfrac12&\tfrac32
  &\tfrac{1-u}{4}&-\tfrac{1+u}{4}
  &\tfrac{u-1}{4}&\tfrac{u+1}{4}\\
y&\tfrac u2&\tfrac u2&\tfrac u2&\tfrac u2
  &\tfrac{1+u}{4}&\tfrac{u-1}{4}
  &\tfrac{u+1}{4}&\tfrac{u-1}{4}
\end{array}
\endgroup
\]
The collinear triples are
$ABC,ABD,ACD,BCD,AEH,DFG$. The $11$ four-point circles are
$ABEF$, $ABGH$, $ACFG$, $ADEG$, $ADFH$, $BCEG$, $BCFH$, $BDEH$,
$CDEF$, $CDGH$, and $EFGH$. The remaining six circle blocks are
$ACE,ACH,BDF,BDG,BFG,CEH$.
A different eight-point configuration determining $17$ circles was
also reported by the forum user \texttt{mzn}~\cite{Forum506}.
The determinant criterion for
concyclicity, using $u^2=7$, verifies the incidences and the
distinctness of the circle equations.  Distinct circles share at most two points, so the listed circle blocks
account for disjoint sets of noncollinear triples. The checksum
$6+11\binom43+6=\binom83$ then confirms that the list is complete.
Thus the configuration determines seventeen circles. The
four-point circles are depicted in~\cref{fig:eight-rich}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:pointed}
Let $Q$ be a noncollinear set of $m$ real points, let $o\notin Q$, and
let $\mathcal L$ be the set of lines spanned by $Q$. With $j_\ell=|\ell\cap Q|$,
\[
 \sum_{\substack{\ell\in\mathcal L\\o\notin\ell}}\frac1{j_\ell+1}
 \ge \frac1{12}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L}j_\ell-\frac m9+\frac12.
\]
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Put $I=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L}j_\ell$,
$D=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L}(3-j_\ell)$ and
$R=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L,\,o\in\ell}(j_\ell+1)$.
Melchior's inequality on $Q$ gives $D\ge3$. Adjoining $o$ decreases the contribution
of each old line through $o$ by one. The lines through $o$ that
already contain two or more points of $Q$ partition those points into
disjoint subsets, and every other line joining $o$ to a point of $Q$
is new and ordinary. Thus $m-\sum_{o\in\ell}j_\ell$ ordinary
lines are created. Melchior on $Q\cup\{o\}$ gives $D+m-R\ge3$.
For every integer $j\ge2$,
$
 \frac1{j+1}-\frac{6-j}{12}
 =\frac{(j-2)(j-3)}{12(j+1)}\ge0$ and $\frac1{j+1}\le\frac{j+1}{9}.
$
Subtracting the weights of the lines through $o$ therefore yields
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{\substack{\ell\in\mathcal L\\o\notin\ell}}\frac1{j_\ell+1}
 &=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L}\frac1{j_\ell+1}
   -\sum_{\substack{\ell\in\mathcal L\\o\in\ell}}\frac1{j_\ell+1}\\
 &\ge\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L}\frac{6-j_\ell}{12}
   -\sum_{\substack{\ell\in\mathcal L\\o\in\ell}}\frac{j_\ell+1}{9}
  =\frac{I+2D}{12}-\frac R9\\
 &\ge\frac I{12}+\frac D{18}-\frac m9+\frac13
  \ge\frac I{12}-\frac m9+\frac12.
\end{align*}
Here $\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L}(6-j_\ell)=I+2D$ because
$3|\mathcal L|=I+D$; we used $R\le D+m-3$ and then $D\ge3$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The upper bound comes from a circle with $n-1$ selected points and its
center, as depicted in~\cref{fig:optimalConf} (rightmost) and explained before. 

Suppose first that a line or circle contains $k>n/2$ points, and put
$r=n-k\ge1$. The counting argument in~\eqref{eq:carrier-count} is valid
for every $n$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:carrier}
 C(P)\ge 1+r\binom{k}{2}-\binom{r+1}{2}
 \left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
 \ge H_n(k):=1+\frac{(n-k)k(3k-n-3)}4.
\end{equation}
Put $G(n)=1+(n-1)(n-3)/2$, so that $g(n)=\lceil G(n)\rceil$.
On $[(n+1)/2,n-1]$ we have $H_n''(k)=(8n+6-18k)/4<0$, while
\[
 H_n(n-1)=G(n),\qquad
 H_n\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)-G(n)
 =\frac{(n-1)(n-3)(n-15)}{32}\ge0.
\]
Concavity and integrality give $C(P)\ge g(n)$.
We may now assume that every line and circle contains at most $n/2$
points of $P$. Invert $P\setminus\{p\}$ about each $p\in P$, obtaining
$Q_p$ of order $m=n-1$. It is noncollinear, since otherwise all of $P$
would lie on one line or circle. A $j$-point line of $Q_p$ avoiding $p$
corresponds to a circle containing exactly $j+1$ original points,
one of them $p$. Consequently the sum, over all $p$, of the left side
in Lemma~\ref{lem:pointed} is exactly $C(P)$.
Every line of $Q_p$ has at most $n/2-1\le2m/3$ points, so
\eqref{eq:langer} and the lemma give
\[
 C(P)\ge\frac n{36}
 \left(3\left\lceil\frac{(n-1)(n+2)}3\right\rceil-4n+22\right).
\]
At $n=15$ this is $505/6>84=g(15)-1$, so integrality suffices.
For $n\ge16$, dropping the ceiling and using $g(n)\le1+(n-2)^2/2$ gives
\[
 C(P)-g(n)\ge
 \frac{n(n^2-3n+20)}{36}-\left(1+\frac{(n-2)^2}{2}\right)
 =\frac{n(n-16)(n-5)+12(n-9)}{36}>0.
\]
This completes the lower bound.
