<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{16}
Let $q\in\mathbb N$ and let $\xi\in V$ have rank one. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{17}
K_{(q)}^F(z,\xi)=\frac{\langle z,\xi\rangle^q}{q!}.
\end{equation}
As $\xi$ ranges over the rank-one elements of $V$, the
polynomials $\ell_\xi^q$ span $\mathcal P_{(q)}$, and the
operators $\partial_\xi^q$ span
$\{p^*(\partial):p\in\mathcal P_{(q)}\}$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Upmeier's description of conical polynomials
\cite[Section~4, formula~(4.2) and the following paragraph]{UpmeierKinv}
implies that $\mathcal P_{(q)}$ is spanned by
$\ell_c^q$, where $c$ ranges over minimal tripotents.
Every rank-one $\xi$ is a nonzero scalar multiple of such a $c$,
so the polynomials $\ell_\xi^q$ also span $\mathcal P_{(q)}$.

By \eqref{14}, $\ell_\xi^q/q!$ represents evaluation at
$\xi$ on the full space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
$q$. It also lies in $\mathcal P_{(q)}$, so it represents
evaluation on that subspace. This gives \eqref{17}.
Taking Fischer adjoints gives the statement about
$\partial_\xi^q$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{21}
Let $\Pi_H:L^2(S)\to H^2(S)$ be the orthogonal
projection. For $q\in\mathbb N$ and $p\in\mathcal P_{(q)}$,
\begin{equation}\label{22}
\Pi_H(p\bar z_j|_S)
=
\frac{\partial_jp}{n/r+q-1}\Big|_S,
\qquad
1\le j\le n.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{16}, $p$ is a linear combination of $\ell_\xi^q$
with $\xi$ of rank one. Since
$\partial_j\ell_\xi^q=q\overline{\xi_j}\ell_\xi^{q-1}$,
the same spanning statement gives
$\partial_jp\in\mathcal P_{(q-1)}$.
For any homogeneous holomorphic polynomial $u$ of degree $q-1$,
Fischer adjointness, orthogonality of the polynomial $K$-types,
and \eqref{15} give
\[
\langle z_ju,p\rangle_S
=\frac{\langle z_ju,p\rangle_F}{(n/r)_q}
=\frac{\langle u,\partial_jp\rangle_F}{(n/r)_q}
=\frac{\langle u,\partial_jp\rangle_S}{n/r+q-1}.
\]
For $u$ of any other homogeneous degree, both sides vanish.
Since polynomials are dense in $H^2(S)$, this identifies the
adjoint of multiplication by $z_j$ at $p$ with
$\partial_jp/(n/r+q-1)$. The adjoint is also
$\Pi_H(p\bar z_j|_S)$, proving \eqref{22}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{28}
Let $\widetilde g\in\widetilde G$ and put $g=\pi(\widetilde g)$.
The map $g$ and its complex Jacobian extend holomorphically to
a neighborhood of $\overline\Omega$, where the Jacobian does
not vanish. Every complex power specified by $\widetilde g$
extends to a possibly smaller neighborhood. Near the identity
in $\widetilde G$, the extensions can be chosen on a common
neighborhood and depend smoothly on $\widetilde g$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
In the Harish--Chandra realization, $\Omega$ lies in an affine
chart of its compact dual. The action of $g$ extends to an
automorphism of the compact dual
\cite[Chapter~VIII, Corollary~7.15]{Helgason}.
Because $g(\overline\Omega)=\overline\Omega$, this action and
its inverse are holomorphic near $\overline\Omega$ in the same
affine chart. Thus the Jacobian is nonzero there after the
neighborhood is made smaller.

The closed domain $\overline\Omega$ is convex, so we can choose
a convex neighborhood within the region where the Jacobian is
nonzero. Any two holomorphic logarithms of the Jacobian on $\Omega$
differ by a constant in $2\pi i\mathbb Z$. We choose the
constant in the logarithm on this neighborhood so that its
restriction to $\Omega$ is the branch specified by
$\widetilde g$. Exponentiation then extends the chosen
complex powers.

The compact-dual action depends smoothly on the group element.
Compactness of $\overline\Omega$ gives a common neighborhood for
$\widetilde g$ near the identity. After shrinking it, the
Jacobians are uniformly close to $1$, so their logarithms and
complex powers also depend smoothly on $\widetilde g$.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{32}
For $L\in\mathbb N_0$ and $F\in\operatorname{Pol}(V)$,
$\mathcal D_{L+1}F$ vanishes on $\Omega$ if and only if
$F\in\mathcal F_L$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
By definition, $\mathcal D_{L+1}F\equiv0$ if and only if
$p^*(\partial)F=0$ for every $p\in\mathcal P_{(L+1)}$.
For such $p$ and any $R\in\operatorname{Pol}(V)$, Fischer
adjointness gives
\[
\langle pR,F\rangle_F
=\langle R,p^*(\partial)F\rangle_F.
\]
Consequently, $\mathcal D_{L+1}F\equiv0$ implies
$F\perp\mathcal P_{(L+1)}\operatorname{Pol}(V)$.
Conversely, suppose that
$F\perp\mathcal P_{(L+1)}\operatorname{Pol}(V)$.
For each $p\in\mathcal P_{(L+1)}$, the function
$p^*(\partial)F$ is a polynomial. Taking
$R=p^*(\partial)F$ in the displayed identity gives
\[
\|p^*(\partial)F\|_F^2
=\langle p\,p^*(\partial)F,F\rangle_F=0.
\]
Thus $p^*(\partial)F=0$ for every such $p$, which means
$\mathcal D_{L+1}F\equiv0$.
By \eqref{30}, the orthogonal complement of
$\mathcal P_{(L+1)}\operatorname{Pol}(V)$ is
$\bigoplus_{\alpha_1\le L}\mathcal P_\alpha=\mathcal F_L$. The proof is complete.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
For $L\in\mathbb N_0$, the space $\mathcal F_L$ also has
the description
\begin{equation}
\label{31}
\mathcal F_L
=\operatorname{span}\{h(\,\cdot\,,w)^L:w\in\Omega\}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Denote the span on the right of \eqref{31} by $W_L$.
Because $\alpha$ is a partition,
$(-L)_\alpha\ne0$ exactly when $\alpha_1\le L$.
Formula~\eqref{13} gives the finite expansion
\[
h(\,\cdot\,,w)^L
=\sum_{\alpha_1\le L}(-L)_\alpha
K_\alpha^F(\,\cdot\,,w),
\qquad w\in\Omega.
\]
Thus $W_L\subset\mathcal F_L$. Suppose that
$p=\sum_{\alpha_1\le L}p_\alpha\in\mathcal F_L$
is Fischer-orthogonal to $W_L$. By the reproducing
property of $K_\alpha^F$, for every $w\in\Omega$,
\[
0=\langle p,h(\,\cdot\,,w)^L\rangle_F
 =\sum_{\alpha_1\le L}(-L)_\alpha p_\alpha(w).
\]
The polynomial on the right vanishes on $\Omega$, hence
identically equals to $0$ on $V$. Its terms belong to distinct spaces
$\mathcal P_\alpha$, so each $(-L)_\alpha p_\alpha=0$.
All these coefficients are nonzero; thus $p=0$.
Since $\mathcal F_L$ is finite-dimensional,
$W_L=\mathcal F_L$, proving \eqref{31}.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
Let $L\in\mathbb N_0$. For $\widetilde g\in\widetilde G$, put
$g=\pi(\widetilde g)$.  Then, for every $F\in C^{L+1}(\Omega)$,
the following identity holds in $\mathcal W_{L+1}^\vee$ at each $z\in\Omega$:
\begin{equation}\label{34}
\mathcal D_{L+1}\!\left[
J_{\widetilde g^{-1}}^{-L/p_\Omega}
(F\circ g^{-1})
\right](z)
=
J_{\widetilde g^{-1}}(z)^{-L/p_\Omega}
B_g(z)
(\mathcal D_{L+1}F)(g^{-1}z).
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Fix $\widetilde g\in\widetilde G$ and $z\in\Omega$, and put
\(y=g^{-1}z\) and \(A=(g^{-1})'(z)\).
By Lemma~\ref{16}, $\mathcal W_{L+1}$ is spanned by
$\varphi_\xi=(\ell_\xi^{L+1})^*$ with $\xi$ of rank one.
The definition of $\mathcal D_{L+1}$ gives
\[
\bigl(\mathcal D_{L+1}F(y)\bigr)(\varphi_\xi)
=\partial_\xi^{L+1}F(y).
\]
The product and chain rules give
\[
\partial_\xi^{L+1}\!\left[
J_{\widetilde g^{-1}}^{-L/p_\Omega}(F\circ g^{-1})
\right](z)
=J_{\widetilde g^{-1}}(z)^{-L/p_\Omega}
\partial_{A\xi}^{L+1}F(y)+R_{\xi,z}(F),
\]
where $R_{\xi,z}(F)$ depends only on derivatives of $F$ at $y$
of order at most $L$. Since
$C_g(z)\varphi_\xi=\varphi_{A\xi}$, the first term on the right is
\[
J_{\widetilde g^{-1}}(z)^{-L/p_\Omega}
\bigl(B_g(z)\mathcal D_{L+1}F(y)\bigr)(\varphi_\xi).
\]
The $\varphi_\xi$ span $\mathcal W_{L+1}$, so the difference
in \eqref{34} involves only $\partial^\gamma F(y)$,
$|\gamma|\le L$.

The space $\mathcal F_L$ is invariant under \(F\longmapsto J_{\widetilde g^{-1}}^{-L/p_\Omega} (F\circ g^{-1})\).
Indeed, by \eqref{24},
\[
J_{\widetilde g^{-1}}(z)^{-L/p_\Omega}
h(g^{-1}z,w)^L
=
\overline{
J_{\widetilde g^{-1}}(gw)
}^{\,L/p_\Omega}
h(z,gw)^L,
\]
and the assertion follows from \eqref{31}.

For $P\in\mathcal F_L$, the invariance just proved shows
that its weighted pullback also belongs to $\mathcal F_L$.
Lemma~\ref{32} therefore makes both sides of \eqref{34}
zero. Hence $R_{\xi,z}(P)=0$ for every $P\in\mathcal F_L$.

For a general $F$, define
\[
P_y(w)=\sum_{|\gamma|\le L}
\frac{\partial^\gamma F(y)}{\gamma!}(w-y)^\gamma.
\]
This polynomial has total degree at most $L$, so
$P_y\in\mathcal F_L$. By construction,
\[
\partial^\gamma P_y(y)=\partial^\gamma F(y),
\qquad |\gamma|\le L.
\]
Since $R_{\xi,z}$ depends only on these derivatives,
\[
R_{\xi,z}(F)=R_{\xi,z}(P_y)=0.
\]
Thus \eqref{34} holds after evaluation on every
$\varphi_\xi$. They span $\mathcal W_{L+1}$,
so \eqref{34} holds in $\mathcal W_{L+1}^\vee$.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{35}

=

B_g(z)

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{48}
Let $C>0$ and $A,B\in\mathbb C$.  The function \[\psi(\rho) = \rho^A{}_2F_1(A,B;C;1-\rho), \quad 0<\rho\le1,\]
extends smoothly across $\rho=0$ if and only if at least one of
\begin{equation}\label{49}
A=0;\qquad
B=C;\qquad
A\in\mathbb N_0,\ B\in-\mathbb N_0;\qquad
A-C\in\mathbb N_0,\ C-B\in\mathbb N_0
\end{equation}
holds.  In each of these cases $\psi$ is a polynomial in $\rho$.  Otherwise
$\psi$ fails to have a $C^m$ extension at $0$ for some
$m\in\mathbb N_0$.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
The four alternatives in \eqref{49} are sufficient. If $A=0$ or
$B=C$, then $\psi=1$. In the third case the Gauss series
terminates. In the fourth case Euler's transformation gives
\[
\psi(\rho)=
\rho^{C-B}{}_2F_1(C-A,C-B;C;1-\rho),
\]
which is again a polynomial.

Conversely, suppose that $\psi$ is smooth at $\rho=0$.
The Gauss hypergeometric equation gives
\[
\rho(1-\rho)\psi''
+\bigl(B+1-A-C+(A-B-1)\rho\bigr)\psi'
+\frac{A(C-B)}{\rho}\psi=0.
\]
Its only possible singularities are $0$, $1$, and $\infty$,
all regular. The Frobenius expansions show that smoothness
at $0$ forces $\psi$ to be analytic there; see
\cite[Section~2.7(i)]{DLMF}.
The defining series shows that $\psi$ is also analytic at $1$.
Continuation around both finite singularities is therefore
trivial, so $\psi$ extends to an entire function.

The point at infinity is a regular singularity. Its Frobenius
expansions therefore give constants $C_0,M>0$ such that
$|\psi(\rho)|\leq C_0(1+|\rho|)^M$ for large $|\rho|$.
For any integer $n>M$, Cauchy's estimate on $|\rho|=R$ gives
\[
|\psi^{(n)}(0)|
\leq \frac{n!C_0(1+R)^M}{R^n}.
\]
Letting $R\to\infty$ shows that $\psi^{(n)}(0)=0$.
Hence $\psi$ is a polynomial.

If $\psi$ is constant, then $\psi(1)=1$ and the equation gives
$A=0$ or $B=C$. Otherwise, let $N\geq1$ be its degree and
$m\geq0$ its order of vanishing at $0$. Comparing the
highest and lowest powers of $\rho$ gives
\[
N=A-B,\qquad (m-A)(m-C+B)=0.
\]
Since $0\leq m\leq N$, either
$A=m\in\mathbb N_0$ and $B=m-N\in-\mathbb N_0$, or
$C-B=m\in\mathbb N_0$ and $A-C=N-m\in\mathbb N_0$.
These are the remaining two conditions in \eqref{49}.

Write \(T_tf(\omega_0)=P_{\sigma,\frac nr}f(t\omega_0)\).
We use the uniform boundary limit for the Poisson--Szeg\H{o}
transform \(P_{\frac nr,\frac nr}\) on $C(S)$, given by

We first compare the kernels.  For $\omega_0,\omega\in S$, put
$p(\zeta)=h(\zeta\omega_0,\omega)$.  By \eqref{13} with $u=-1$,
$p$ is a polynomial of degree at most $r$, and $p(0)=1$.
The Szeg\H{o}-kernel formula \cite[(3.18)]{Koranyi} gives
$p(\zeta)\ne0$ for $|\zeta|<1$.  Thus

\[

compact-group average and preserves the assumed $C^m$ regularity
on  $\overline{\Omega_0(c)}$. The operator in \eqref{80} commutes with the
$U(b+1)$-action; hence $U_c$ also satisfies \eqref{80}.



Choose homogeneous harmonic polynomials $(Y_\ell)$ whose restrictions
form an orthonormal basis of $H^{p,1}(\mathbb C^{b+1})$ on the unit
sphere. For $w\ne0$, we can write $U_c$ as
\[
\sum_\ell Y_\ell(w)\Phi_\ell(x),
\qquad x=\|w\|^2.
\]
For a harmonic polynomial $Y$ of bidegree $(p,1)$,
$\sum_jw_j\partial_jY=pY$ and
$\sum_j\bar w_j\bar\partial_jY=Y$. Using these identities and
$\sigma-\frac nr=p-1$ in \eqref{80}, then using the linear
independence of the $Y_\ell$ on each sphere, we find that each
$\Phi_\ell$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{83}
x(1-x)\Phi''+(m+2-3x)\Phi'-\Phi=0.
\end{equation}
This is Gauss's hypergeometric equation with parameters
$1,1$ and $m+2$.

Since the restrictions of the $Y_\ell$ are orthonormal, therefore
\[
t^{p+1}\Phi_\ell(t^2)
=
\int_{\partial\Omega_0(c)}
U_c(t\zeta)\overline{Y_\ell(\zeta)}\,d\zeta,
\qquad 0<t<1,
\]
where $d\zeta$ is normalized surface measure. Because
$U_c\in C^m(\overline{\Omega_0(c)})$, the integral is bounded
as $t\downarrow0$ and is $C^m$ up to $t=1$. The change of
variable $x=t^2$ is smooth and invertible near $t=1$.
Consequently,
\[
x^{(p+1)/2}\Phi_\ell(x)=O(1)\quad(x\downarrow0),
\qquad
\Phi_\ell\in C^m\text{ near }x=1.
\]

At $x=0$, equation~\eqref{83} has the regular solution
${}_2F_1(1,1;m+2;x)$. An independent solution has leading
order $x^{-m-1}$ and violates the first condition, since
$p+1<2(m+1)$. Hence
\[
\Phi_\ell(x)=a_\ell\,{}_2F_1(1,1;m+2;x).
\]
It follows that
\[
U_c(w)=Y(w)\,{}_2F_1(1,1;m+2;\|w\|^2)
\]
for a harmonic polynomial $Y$ of bidegree $(p,1)$.

Lemma~\ref{551}, with $\lambda=m+1$, $\mu=m$, and
$\rho=1-x$, gives
\[
{}_2F_1(1,1;m+2;x)
=A(1-x)+(-1)^{m+1}(m+1)x^{-m-1}
(1-x)^m\log(1-x),
\]
where $A$ is analytic near $0$. If $Y\ne0$, choose
$\zeta\in\partial\mathbb B^{b+1}$ with $Y(\zeta)\ne0$.
Along $w=t\zeta$, the factor $Y(t\zeta)=t^{p+1}Y(\zeta)$
is smooth and nonzero near $t=1$, whereas
$(1-t^2)^m\log(1-t^2)$ is not $C^m$ there. This contradicts
the $C^m$ regularity of $U_c$. Hence $U_c=0$. Its boundary value is the
$H^{p,1}(\mathbb C^{b+1})$ component of $g_c$,
so this component vanishes.

\frac a2
\ell_\xi(s)^m\overline{\ell_v(s)}
+
\partial_{\operatorname{pr}_2(s)v}\ell_\xi^m(s)
\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
Dividing by $a/2+m$ gives
\[
\int_{\mathcal I_s}
\ell_\xi(e)^m\overline{\ell_v(e)}\,d\mu_s(e)
=
\frac{(a/2)_m}{(ar/2)_{m+1}}
\left[
\frac a2
\ell_\xi(s)^m\overline{\ell_v(s)}
+
\partial_{\operatorname{pr}_2(s)v}\ell_\xi^m(s)
\right].
\]
For $m\ge1$, the rank-one powers $\ell_\xi^m$ span
$\mathcal P_{(m)}$ by Lemma~\ref{16}; for $m=0$, they span the constants.
Hence \eqref{103} follows by linearity.

Finally, the Harish--Chandra decomposition
\(\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}=V\oplus\mathfrak k_{\mathbb C}\oplus\bar V\)
and the bracket relation \([x,\bar y]=D(x,\bar y)\) in
\cite[Section~2, formulas~(1)--(2)]{KZ} show that
\(D(x,\bar s)\in\mathfrak k_{\mathbb C}\).
Since $\mathcal P_{(m)}$ is $K$-invariant, it is also invariant under
the derived action of $\mathfrak k_{\mathbb C}$. More explicitly, if $A\in\mathfrak k$, then \(p(e^{tA}z)\in\mathcal P_{(m)}\)
for every $t$, and differentiation at $t=0$ gives \(z\longmapsto\partial_{Az}p(z)\in\mathcal P_{(m)}\).
By complex linearity, the same conclusion holds for
$A\in\mathfrak k_{\mathbb C}$. Hence \(z\longmapsto \partial_{D(x,\bar s)z}p(z) \in\mathcal P_{(m)}\).

By the outer symmetry of the Jordan triple product, \[D(x,\bar s)s = D(s,\bar s)x = x.\]
We may therefore apply \eqref{102} to the polynomial \(z\longmapsto \partial_{D(x,\bar s)z}p(z)\).
This gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathcal I_s}
\partial_{D(e,\bar s)x}p(e)\,d\mu_s(e)
&=
\int_{\mathcal I_s}
\partial_{D(x,\bar s)e}p(e)\,d\mu_s(e)=
\frac{(a/2)_m}{(ar/2)_m}
\partial_{D(x,\bar s)s}p(s)=
\frac{(a/2)_m}{(ar/2)_m}
\partial_xp(s).
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves \eqref{104}.

<!-- lemma-25: body_md -->
\label{107}
The operator $\mathsf T$ maps $\mathcal E_q$ into itself and is
invertible.
<!-- end lemma-25 -->
<!-- proof-36: body_md -->
To see that $\mathsf T$ maps $\mathcal E_q$ into itself, let \[U(z)=\sum_{j=1}^np_j(z)\bar z_j, \quad p_j\in\mathcal P_{(q)}.\]
Since
$
\bar z=\sum_{l=1}^n\bar z_l\bar v_l,
$
the outer symmetry gives
\[
D(z,\bar z)v_j
=
\sum_{l=1}^n
\bar z_lD(z,\bar v_l)v_j
=
\sum_{l=1}^n
\bar z_lD(v_j,\bar v_l)z.
\]
Hence
\[
\sum_{j=1}^n
\partial_{D(z,\bar z)v_j}p_j(z)
=
\sum_{l=1}^n
\left(
\sum_{j=1}^n
\partial_{D(v_j,\bar v_l)z}p_j(z)
\right)\bar z_l.
\]
The proof of Lemma~\ref{101} shows that \(z\longmapsto \partial_{D(v_j,\bar v_l)z}p_j(z)\)
belongs to $\mathcal P_{(q)}$. Therefore the coefficient of each
$\bar z_l$ in the preceding expression belongs to
$\mathcal P_{(q)}$, and hence \[\sum_{j=1}^n \partial_{D(z,\bar z)v_j}p_j(z) \in\mathcal E_q.\]
Thus $\mathsf T\mathcal E_q\subset\mathcal E_q$.

It remains to prove invertibility.  Since $K$ acts unitarily on $V$,
$\mathfrak k$ consists of skew-Hermitian endomorphisms.  Moreover,
$D(z,\bar z)\in\mathfrak k_{\mathbb C}$ and
$D(z,\bar z)^*=D(z,\bar z)$.  Writing
$\mathfrak k_{\mathbb C}=\mathfrak k\oplus i\mathfrak k$ as real vector
spaces, the Hermitian condition therefore gives \(D(z,\bar z)\in i\mathfrak k\).

Let $\mathcal B$ be the positive multiple of the complexified Killing form
for which \[\mathcal B(u,\bar v)=\langle u,v\rangle, \quad u,v\in V.\]
Its restriction to the real space $i\mathfrak k$ is positive definite.
Choose a $\mathcal B$-orthonormal basis $(Z_\gamma)$ of $i\mathfrak k$.
Then every $Z_\gamma$ acts Hermitianly on $V$.  Using \([z,\bar w]=D(z,\bar w)\)
and the invariance of $\mathcal B$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal B(D(z,\bar z),Z_\gamma)
&=
\mathcal B([z,\bar z],Z_\gamma)
=
\mathcal B([Z_\gamma,z],\bar z)
=
\langle Z_\gamma z,z\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{107a}
D(z,\bar z)
=
\sum_\gamma
\langle Z_\gamma z,z\rangle Z_\gamma.
\end{equation}

For fixed $\gamma$, define
\[
\mathsf C_\gamma U
=
\sum_{j=1}^n
\partial_{Z_\gamma v_j}p_j,
\qquad
U=\sum_{j=1}^np_j\bar z_j.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{16}, \(\mathsf C_\gamma U\in\mathcal P_{(q-1)}\).
If \(Z_\gamma v_j = \sum_l c^\gamma_{lj}v_l\),
then, because $Z_\gamma$ is Hermitian, \(c^\gamma_{jl}=\overline{c^\gamma_{lj}}\).
For $h\in\mathcal P_{(q-1)}$, Fischer adjointness in the two variables gives
\begin{equation}\label{107b}
\left\langle
\langle Z_\gamma z,z\rangle h,
U
\right\rangle_F
=
\langle h,\mathsf C_\gamma U\rangle_F.
\end{equation}
Indeed, \(\langle Z_\gamma z,z\rangle = \sum_{l,j}c^\gamma_{lj}z_j\bar z_l\),
so the left-hand side of \eqref{107b} is \(\sum_{l,j}c^\gamma_{lj} \langle h,\partial_jp_l\rangle_F\).
On the other hand, after interchanging $j$ and $l$,
\[
\mathsf C_\gamma U
=
\sum_{l,j}c^\gamma_{jl}\partial_jp_l
=
\sum_{l,j}\overline{c^\gamma_{lj}}\partial_jp_l.
\]
Since the Fischer inner product is conjugate-linear in its second variable,
this gives exactly \eqref{107b}.
Notice that we only use the pairing identity \eqref{107b}; no assertion is
needed that multiplication by $\langle Z_\gamma z,z\rangle$ preserves
$\mathcal E_q$.

By \eqref{107a},
\[
\sum_{j=1}^n
\partial_{D(z,\bar z)v_j}p_j
=
\sum_\gamma
\langle Z_\gamma z,z\rangle
\mathsf C_\gamma U.
\]
Taking the Fischer inner product with $U$ and applying \eqref{107b} with
$h=\mathsf C_\gamma U$ gives
\[
\left\langle
\sum_{j=1}^n
\partial_{D(z,\bar z)v_j}p_j,
U
\right\rangle_F
=
\sum_\gamma
\|\mathsf C_\gamma U\|_F^2.
\]
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{108}
\langle\mathsf TU,U\rangle_F
=
\frac{ab}{2}\|U\|_F^2
+
\left(b+\frac{ar}{2}+q\right)
\sum_\gamma
\|\mathsf C_\gamma U\|_F^2.
\end{equation}
Since $a>0$ and $b>0$, the right-hand side is positive whenever
$U\ne0$.  Thus $\mathsf T$ is injective.  Since $\mathcal E_q$ is
finite-dimensional, $\mathsf T$ is invertible.

<!-- lemma-26: body_md -->
\label{109}
For $\mathcal A_q$ defined in \eqref{95}, \(\operatorname{Ran}\mathcal A_q = \mathcal M_q\).
<!-- end lemma-26 -->

We first consider \(U = \ell_\xi^q\overline{\ell_v}\), where
\(\xi,v\in V\) and \(\operatorname{rank}\xi=1\).
By \eqref{99}, \eqref{103}, and \eqref{104}, with \(x=\operatorname{pr}_1(s)v\),
we have
\[
\begin{aligned}
(\mathcal A_qU)(s)
=
\frac{(a/2)_q}{(b+q)(ar/2)_{q+1}}
\Bigg\{
b\left[
\frac a2\ell_\xi(s)^q\overline{\ell_v(s)}
+
\partial_{\operatorname{pr}_2(s)v}\ell_\xi^q(s)
\right]
-
\left(\frac{ar}{2}+q\right)
\partial_{\operatorname{pr}_1(s)v}\ell_\xi^q(s)
\Bigg\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $s$ is maximal, \[V = V_2(s)\oplus V_1(s),\ 
\mbox{and}\ D(s,\bar s) = 2\operatorname{pr}_2(s) + \operatorname{pr}_1(s).\]
Therefore
\[
\operatorname{pr}_2(s)
=
D(s,\bar s)-I,
\qquad
\operatorname{pr}_1(s)
=
2I-D(s,\bar s),
\]
and hence
\begin{align}
(\mathcal A_qU)(s)
={}&
\frac{(a/2)_q}{(b+q)(ar/2)_{q+1}}
\Bigg[
\frac{ab}{2}
\ell_\xi(s)^q\overline{\ell_v(s)}
+
\left(b+\frac{ar}{2}+q\right)
\partial_{D(s,\bar s)v}\ell_\xi^q(s)
\notag\\
&\hspace{40mm}
-
(b+ar+2q)\partial_v\ell_\xi^q(s)
\Bigg].
\label{110}
\end{align}

Write \(v=\sum_{j=1}^n a_jv_j, \quad a_j=\langle v,v_j\rangle\).
Then \(\overline{\ell_v(z)} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_j\bar z_j\).
By the definition of $\mathsf T$, the first two terms in the brackets
in \eqref{110} are $(\mathsf TU)(s)$. Also, \[\partial_v\ell_\xi^q = q\langle v,\xi\rangle\ell_\xi^{q-1} \in \mathcal P_{(q-1)}.\]
Thus
\[
\mathcal A_qU
-
\frac{(a/2)_q}{(b+q)(ar/2)_{q+1}}
(\mathsf TU)|_S
\in
\mathcal P_{(q-1)}|_S.
\]

By Lemma~\ref{16},
$\mathcal P_{(q)}$ is spanned by the polynomials
$\ell_\xi^q$ with $\operatorname{rank}\xi=1$. Hence
\[
\mathcal E_q
=
\operatorname{span}
\left\{
\ell_\xi^q\overline{\ell_v}:
\xi,v\in V,\ \operatorname{rank}\xi=1
\right\}.
\]
It follows by linearity that
\begin{equation}\label{111}
\mathcal A_qU
-
\frac{(a/2)_q}{(b+q)(ar/2)_{q+1}}
(\mathsf TU)|_S
\in
\mathcal P_{(q-1)}|_S,
\qquad
U\in\mathcal E_q.
\end{equation}

We next record a formula for the pairing with $\mathcal A_qU$.
For $g\in C(S)$ and $U\in\mathcal E_q$,
\begin{equation}\label{112}
\langle g,\mathcal A_qU\rangle_S
=
\int_K
\left\langle
R_{kc_0}g,
R_{kc_0}(U|_S)
\right\rangle
\,dk.
\end{equation}
To prove this, first suppose that \(U=\ell_\xi^q\overline{\ell_v}\) with\( \operatorname{rank}\xi=1\).
By \eqref{99} and \(\operatorname{pr}_1(s)=2I-D(s,\bar s)\),
the function \((s,e) \longmapsto (R_{s-e}(U|_S))(e)\)
is continuous on $X$. The same is therefore true for every
$U\in\mathcal E_q$ by linearity. Hence \eqref{95} and \eqref{94} give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle g,\mathcal A_qU\rangle_S
=
\int_K\int_{\partial\Omega_0(c_0)}
&g\bigl(k(c_0+\omega)\bigr)
\overline{(R_{kc_0}(U|_S))(k\omega)}
\,d\omega\,dk.
\end{aligned}
\]
For fixed $k\in K$, the map \(\omega\longmapsto k\omega\)
carries the normalized sphere measure on
$\partial\Omega_0(c_0)$ onto that on
$\partial\Omega_0(kc_0)$. Thus the inner integral is
\[
\int_{\partial\Omega_0(kc_0)}
g(kc_0+e)
\overline{(R_{kc_0}(U|_S))(e)}
\,de.
\]
Since $R_{kc_0}$ is the orthogonal projection onto
$H^{q,1}(V_0(kc_0))$, this is \(\left\langle R_{kc_0}g, R_{kc_0}(U|_S) \right\rangle\).
This proves \eqref{112}.

We now show that
\begin{equation}\label{113}
\mathcal A_qU
\perp
\mathcal P_{(q-1)}|_S,
\qquad
U\in\mathcal E_q.
\end{equation}
Let $h\in\mathcal P_{(q-1)}$. For every tripotent $c$ of rank $r-1$,
the function \(e\longmapsto h(c+e)\)
is a holomorphic polynomial of degree at most $q-1$ on $V_0(c)$.
Its homogeneous components belong to \(H^{m,0}(V_0(c)), \quad 0\le m\le q-1\),
and are orthogonal on the sphere to
$H^{q,1}(V_0(c))$. Hence \(R_ch=0\).
Applying \eqref{112} with $g=h$ gives \(\langle h,\mathcal A_qU\rangle_S=0\),
and \eqref{113} follows.

Since $\mathsf TU\in\mathcal E_q$, \eqref{111} and \eqref{91} imply \(\mathcal A_qU\in\mathcal E_q|_S\).
Now \eqref{91} and \eqref{113} give \(\mathcal A_qU\in\mathcal M_q\).

By Lemma~\ref{21}, the restriction of $\Pi_H$ to
$\mathcal E_q|_S$ is the orthogonal projection onto
$\mathcal P_{(q-1)}|_S$. Taking the $\mathcal M_q$ component in
\eqref{111}, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{114}
\mathcal A_qU
=
\frac{(a/2)_q}{(b+q)(ar/2)_{q+1}}
(I-\Pi_H)\bigl((\mathsf TU)|_S\bigr).
\end{equation}
The scalar in \eqref{114} is nonzero. Since $\mathsf T$ is invertible, \(\mathsf T(\mathcal E_q) = \mathcal E_q\).
Therefore \eqref{91} and \eqref{114} give \(\operatorname{Ran}\mathcal A_q = (I-\Pi_H)(\mathcal E_q|_S) = \mathcal M_q\).
