<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:J-main}
Deciding~\eqref{eq:decision} is NP-hard under deterministic polynomial-time
many-one reductions. NP-hardness remains true under all of the following restrictions
on the table $p$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the number of rows is $n=2^r$, with $n\ge2$;
\item every row has exactly $d\ge1$ positive entries;
\item every positive entry equals $1/(nd)$;
\item every column has at most $d$ positive entries.
\end{enumerate}
Additionally, on this restricted class, the decision problem is NP-complete.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:problem}
\textup{\textsc{Common Entropy}}: given a rational probability table $p$ and a rational number $r$, decide whether
$G(p)\le r$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:G-main}
\textup{\textsc{Common Entropy}}\ is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This remains true for instances in
which every row of $p$ has at most four nonzero entries and all nonzero entries of $p$ take one of
two values.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:decomposition}
A \emph{rank-one decomposition} of a nonnegative matrix $A$ is a finite family $(F_w)_{w\in\mathcal{W}}$ of
nonnegative rank-one matrices such that $\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}}F_w=A$. The matrices $F_w$ are its
\emph{components}, and $(\mu(F_w))_{w\in\mathcal{W}}$ is its \emph{mass vector}.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:decomp}
Let $p$ be a probability table on $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If $W$ is an admissible auxiliary for $p$, then the matrices
$F_w(x,y)=\Pr(X=x,\,Y=y,\,W=w)$, for $w$ with $\Pr(W=w)>0$, form a rank-one decomposition of $p$
with $\mu(F_w)=\Pr(W=w)$.
\item Conversely, for every rank-one decomposition $(F_w)_{w\in\mathcal{W}}$ of $p$ there is an admissible
auxiliary $W$ with $\Pr(W=w)=\mu(F_w)$ for every $w\in\mathcal{W}$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
(a) For each $w$ with $\Pr(W=w)>0$, conditional independence gives $F_w=g_wh_w^{\top}$ with
$g_w(x)=\Pr(W=w)\Pr(X=x\mid W=w)$ and $h_w(y)=\Pr(Y=y\mid W=w)$, which are nonzero nonnegative
vectors. Thus $F_w$ is a nonnegative rank-one matrix with $\mu(F_w)=\Pr(W=w)$, and
$\sum_wF_w=p$ by the law of total probability.

(b) Write $F_w=g_wh_w^{\top}$ with nonnegative vectors $g_w,h_w$, scaled so that the entries of
$h_w$ sum to one; then the entries of $g_w$ sum to $\mu(F_w)>0$. The function
$(x,y,w)\mapsto F_w(x,y)$ is a probability distribution on $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{W}$, because
$\sum_w\mu(F_w)=\mu(p)=1$. Let $(X,Y,W)$ have this distribution. The pair $(X,Y)$ has distribution
$\sum_wF_w=p$, the variable $W$ has distribution $(\mu(F_w))_{w}$, and conditionally on $W=w$ the
pair $(X,Y)$ has distribution $F_w/\mu(F_w)=(g_w/\mu(F_w))h_w^{\top}$, which is a product of a
distribution on $\mathcal{X}$ and a distribution on $\mathcal{Y}$. Hence $X\perp Y\mid W$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:no-rectangle}
If every rectangle in $E$ has at most $d$ entries, then the table
\eqref{eq:uniform} satisfies $J(p)=\log n$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Fix an admissible $W$. For each $w$ with $\Pr(W=w)>0$, let
$$q_w(x,y)=\Pr(X=x,Y=y\mid W=w)$$ and define
\[
A_w=\{x:\Pr(X=x\mid W=w)>0\},
\qquad
B_w=\{y:\Pr(Y=y\mid W=w)>0\}.
\]
Since 
$$q_w(x,y)=\Pr(X=x,Y=y\mid W=w)=\Pr(X=x\mid W=w)\Pr(Y=y\mid W=w), $$
 the support of $q_w$ is exactly
$A_w\times B_w$. Moreover, one has that
\[
p(x,y)=\sum_{v:\,\Pr(W=v)>0}\Pr(W=v)q_v(x,y)
\ge \Pr(W=w)q_w(x,y).
\]
Since all summands are nonnegative, it holds that $q_w$ is zero wherever $p$ is zero.
By \eqref{eq:uniform}, 
 $A_w\times B_w\subseteq E$. Therefore, by the hypothesis of the lemma, it holds that  $|A_w||B_w|\le d$.

Recall that a probability vector with $s$ positive entries has entropy at most $\log s$.
Applying this inequality to each joint conditional distribution $q_w$ gives
$H(X,Y\mid W=w)\le\log d$. Averaging over $w$ yields
$H(X,Y\mid W)\le\log d$, and therefore
\[
I(X,Y;W)\ge\log N-\log d=\log n.
\]
This holds for every admissible $W$, while $W=X$ attains $\log n$
by~\eqref{eq:row-upper} and~\eqref{eq:entropies}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:large-rectangle}
If $E$ contains a rectangle with more than $d$ entries, then the table
\eqref{eq:uniform} satisfies $J(p)<\log n$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Choose $A\times B\subseteq E$ and write
$a=|A|$, $b=|B|$, and $m=ab>d$.
Since $B\subseteq E_u$ for every $u\in A$, we have $b\le d$.
For $0\le t<1$, define he random variable $W_t$ that takes  values in
$\mathcal X\cup\{\ast\}$, where $\ast\notin\mathcal X$, as follows.
Draw $(X,Y)$ from $p$. If $(X,Y)\in A\times B$, output $\ast$ with
probability $t$ and the row label $X$ with probability $1-t$;
otherwise output $X$. Explicitly, the resulting joint distribution is
\begin{equation}
\Pr(X=x,Y=y,W_t=w)=
\begin{cases}
t/N,&(x,y)\in A\times B,\quad w=\ast,\\
(1-t)/N,&(x,y)\in A\times B,\quad w=x,\\
1/N,&(x,y)\in E\setminus(A\times B),\quad w=x,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:joint-construction}
\end{equation}
One can easily see that summing over $w$ gives $p(x,y)$.
                                                                                                       
In particular, $W_0=X$.

\smallskip
We now compute the conditional distributions and check for the admissibility of $W_t.$
For $t>0$, the  label $\ast$ has probability
\[
\Pr(W_t=\ast)=\sum_{(x,y)\in A\times B}\frac{t}{N}
=\frac{mt}{N}.
\]
Let $\mathbf1_C$ denote the indicator function of a set $C$.
We first compute the conditional joint distribution given
$W_t=\ast$. For $t>0$, the construction and $m=ab$ give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(X=x,Y=y\mid W_t=\ast)
&=\frac{\Pr(X=x,Y=y,W_t=\ast)}
        {\Pr(W_t=\ast)}\\
&=\frac{(t/N)\mathbf1_A(x)\mathbf1_B(y)}{mt/N}\\
&=\frac{\mathbf1_A(x)\mathbf1_B(y)}{ab}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus this conditional joint distribution is uniform on
$A\times B$ and zero outside it.

We next compute its two conditional marginals.
Summing over $y$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(X=x\mid W_t=\ast)
&=\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}
  \Pr(X=x,Y=y\mid W_t=\ast)\\
&=\frac{\mathbf1_A(x)}{ab}
  \sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}\mathbf1_B(y)
=\frac{\mathbf1_A(x)}a,
\end{aligned}
\]
because $|B|=b$. Similarly, since $|A|=a$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(Y=y\mid W_t=\ast)
&=\sum_{x\in\mathcal X}
  \Pr(X=x,Y=y\mid W_t=\ast)\\
&=\frac{\mathbf1_B(y)}{ab}
  \sum_{x\in\mathcal X}\mathbf1_A(x)
=\frac{\mathbf1_B(y)}b.
\end{aligned}
\]
                                                                       
Hence, for every $x\in\mathcal X$ and
$y\in\mathcal Y$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(X=x,Y=y\mid W_t=\ast)
&=\frac{\mathbf1_A(x)\mathbf1_B(y)}{ab}\\
&=\Pr(X=x\mid W_t=\ast)
  \Pr(Y=y\mid W_t=\ast).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:star-product}
\end{equation}
                                                                       
For a row label $u\in\mathcal X$, summing the masses that retain this label
gives
\[
\Pr(W_t=u)=
\begin{cases}
[b(1-t)+(d-b)]/N=(d-bt)/N,&u\in A,\\
d/N,&u\notin A.
\end{cases}
\]
All these probabilities are positive, since
$d-bt\ge d(1-t)>0$, including when $b=d$.
Write $q_{u,t}(y)=\Pr(Y=y\mid W_t=u)$.
If $u\notin A$, then $q_{u,t}(y)=\mathbf1_{E_u}(y)/d$.
If $u\in A$, division by $(d-bt)/N$ gives
\begin{equation}
q_{u,t}(y)=
\begin{cases}
(1-t)/(d-bt),&y\in B,\\
1/(d-bt),&y\in E_u\setminus B,\\
0,&y\notin E_u.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:affected-row}
\end{equation}
The event $W_t=u$ implies $X=u$. Thus, for every $x,y$,
\[
\Pr(X=x,Y=y,W_t=u) =\Pr(X=x)\Pr(Y=y,W_t=u\mid X=x)
=
\mathbf1_{\{u\}}(x)\Pr(Y=y,W_t=u).
\]
In fact, if  $x\ne u$, both sides are zero; if $x=u$, the two
events whose probabilities appear are identical.
Dividing by $\Pr(W_t=u)>0$ gives
\[
\Pr(X=x,Y=y\mid W_t=u)
=
\mathbf1_{\{u\}}(x)\Pr(Y=y\mid W_t=u)
=
\mathbf1_{\{u\}}(x)q_{u,t}(y).
\]
Moreover, since $X=u$ is certain given that  $W_t=u$, it holds that
\[
\Pr(X=x\mid W_t=u)=\mathbf1_{\{u\}}(x).
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Pr(X=x,Y=y\mid W_t=u)
&=\mathbf1_{\{u\}}(x)q_{u,t}(y)\\
&=\Pr(X=x\mid W_t=u)\Pr(Y=y\mid W_t=u).
\end{split}
\label{eq:row-product}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:star-product} and~\eqref{eq:row-product} prove the
required factorization for every label of positive probability. 

At \(t=0\), the construction gives \(W_0=X\) with probability one. The label \(\ast\) never occurs, so no conditional distribution given that label is needed. Every row label \(u\) has probability \(d/N>0\), and the factorization~\eqref{eq:row-product} remains valid at \(t=0\). Hence \(W_0\) is admissible as well. Thus $W_t$ is admissible for every $0\le t<1$.

\smallskip


In formula~\eqref{eq:conditional}, each label $w$ contributes its probability multiplied
by the entropy of its joint conditional distribution. The common label $\ast$  is
uniform on $m$ pairs and contributes $(mt/N)\log m$ (zero at $t=0$).
Each of the $n-a$ row labels outside $A$ contributes $(d/N)\log d$.
For $u\in A$, the $b$ probabilities of the first type
in~\eqref{eq:affected-row} and the $d-b$ of the second type give
\begin{align*}
&\Pr(W_t=u)H(X,Y\mid W_t=u)\\
&\quad=-\frac1N\left[
 b(1-t)\log\frac{1-t}{d-bt}
 +(d-b)\log\frac1{d-bt}\right]\\
&\quad=\frac1N\left[
 (d-bt)\log(d-bt)-b(1-t)\log(1-t)\right].
\end{align*}
This expression is the same for every $u\in A$, regardless of the names of
the elements of $E_u\setminus B$.




There are $a$ affected row labels. Adding their contributions
to those of the  label $\ast$ and the $n-a$ unaffected row
labels, and using $ab=m$, gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
N H(X,Y\mid W_t)
={}&mt\log m+(n-a)d\log d\\
&+a(d-bt)\log(d-bt)
-m(1-t)\log(1-t).
\end{aligned}
\]
To isolate the contribution coming  from $H(X,Y\mid W_0)=\log d$, use
$N=nd$ to write
\[
(n-a)d\log d=N\log d-ad\log d.
\]
Substituting this identity into the preceding expression
and subtracting $N\log d$ yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
N\bigl(H(X,Y\mid W_t)-\log d\bigr)
={}&mt\log m\\
&+a\bigl[(d-bt)\log(d-bt)-d\log d\bigr]\\
&-m(1-t)\log(1-t).
\end{aligned}
\]
Denote the right-hand side of the above formula by $\Phi(t)$. Dividing by $N$
gives the conditional-entropy identity

$$
H(X,Y\mid W_t)
=\log d+\frac{\Phi(t)}N.
$$
 Since
$H(X,Y)=\log N$ and $\log N-\log d=\log n$, the next 
mutual-information identity follows:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
I(X,Y;W_t)
&=H(X,Y)-H(X,Y\mid W_t)\\
&=\log N-\log d-\frac{\Phi(t)}N
 =\log n-\frac{\Phi(t)}N,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gain-identity}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\Phi(t)={}&mt\log m
+a\bigl[(d-bt)\log(d-bt)-d\log d\bigr]\\
&\ -m(1-t)\log(1-t).
\end{split}
\label{eq:gain-function}
\end{equation}
Thus $\Phi(t)/N$ is the increase in conditional entropy
over $\log d$, and equally the decrease in mutual
information below $\log n$. At $t=0$, these identities
hold with $\Phi(0)=0$ and $W_0=X$.


\smallskip
We now claim that, for $0\le t<1$,
\begin{equation}
\Phi(t)\ge mt\left(\log\frac md-\frac{t}{\ln2}\right).
\label{eq:gain-bound}
\end{equation}
We use the well known inequality
$\ln u\le u-1$ for $u>0$.
Applying it to $u=d/z$, with $z>0$, yields
$z\log(z/d)\ge(z-d)/\ln2$ and hence
\[
z\log z-d\log d
=(z-d)\log d+z\log(z/d)
\ge(z-d)\left(\log d+\frac1{\ln2}\right).
\]
Taking $z=d-bt>0$ gives
\[
(d-bt)\log(d-bt)-d\log d
\ge-bt\left(\log d+\frac1{\ln2}\right).
\]
Also, $\ln(1-t)\le-t$, so
\[
-(1-t)\log(1-t)\ge\frac{t(1-t)}{\ln2}.
\]
Substituting these inequalities in~\eqref{eq:gain-function} and using
$ab=m$, one gets 
\[
\Phi(t)\ge mt\log m-mt\left(\log d+\frac1{\ln2}\right)
       +\frac{mt(1-t)}{\ln2}
=mt\left(\log\frac md-\frac{t}{\ln2}\right),
\]
proving ~\eqref{eq:gain-bound}.
Since $m>d$, this lower bound is positive whenever
$0<t<\min\{1,\ln(m/d)\}$.
For any such $t$, admissibility and~\eqref{eq:gain-identity} imply
\[
J(p)\le I(X,Y;W_t)=\log n-\frac{\Phi(t)}N<\log n.\qedhere
\]

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:reduction}
Given an instance $(\Gamma,k)$ of Maximum Edge Biclique,
where $\Gamma=(L,R,E_\Gamma)$ is an explicitly given finite bipartite
graph and $k\ge1$ is encoded in binary, one can construct,
in polynomial time, integers $d\ge1$, $r\ge1$, and a
bipartite graph
\[
\Gamma'=(\mathcal X,\mathcal Y,E),
\qquad E\subseteq\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y,
\]
with disjoint left and right vertex sets $\mathcal X$
and $\mathcal Y$, respectively, such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $|\mathcal X|=n=2^r$, every vertex of $\mathcal X$
has degree $d$, and every vertex of $\mathcal Y$ has
degree between $1$ and $d$;
\item $\Gamma$ has a biclique with at least $k$ edges if and
only if there exist nonempty sets $A\subseteq\mathcal X$
and $B\subseteq\mathcal Y$ such that
$A\times B\subseteq E$ and $|A||B|>d$;
\item the complete rational probability table indexed by
$\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y$ and uniform on $E$, together
with the integer $r$, has polynomial encoding length.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $e=|E_\Gamma|$. We call a vertex in $R$  \emph{private} if it has exactly one
neighbor. We will use the following observation: in a graph whose left
degrees are all equal to $d$, a rectangle containing a private right vertex has
only one left vertex, and therefore at most $d$ edges.
Indeed, suppose a rectangle $A\times B$ contains a private
right vertex $y_0$, whose unique neighbor is $x_0$.
Every vertex of $A$ must be adjacent to $y_0$, so
$A=\{x_0\}$, since $A$ is nonempty.
Moreover, every vertex of $B$ must be adjacent to $x_0$.
As $x_0$ has degree $d$, we obtain
$|A||B|=|B|\le d$.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1}
                                         
If $k>e$ (in particular, if $e=0$), then $(\Gamma,k)$ is a
no-instance of Maximum Edge Biclique.
In this case, construct $\Gamma'=(\mathcal X,\mathcal Y,E)$ by
setting
\[
\mathcal X=\{x_1,x_2\},\qquad
\mathcal Y=\{y_1,y_2\},\qquad
E=\{(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2)\},
\]
where the four vertices are distinct, and put
$d=1$, $r=1$, and $n=2$.

We verify the required conditions.
For condition~1, we have $|\mathcal X|=2=2^r$,
and every left and right vertex has degree $1=d$.

For condition~2, the original graph $\Gamma$ has no biclique
with at least $k$ edges. We show that $E$ likewise has
no rectangle with more than $d$ entries.
Let $A\times B\subseteq E$ be a nonempty rectangle.
The set $A$ cannot contain both $x_1$ and $x_2$,
because these vertices have no common neighbor and
$B$ is nonempty. Hence $A=\{x_i\}$ for some
$i\in\{1,2\}$. Since $y_i$ is the unique neighbor
of $x_i$, necessarily $B=\{y_i\}$. Thus
$|A||B|=1=d$.
Both sides of the equivalence in condition~2 are
therefore false, so the equivalence holds.

Finally, the probability table uniform on $E$ is
\[
p=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2 & 0\\
0 & 1/2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This table and $r=1$ have constant encoding length,
which verifies condition~3.

Suppose now that $k\le e$ and that some vertex of $\Gamma$
has degree at least $k$. Then $(\Gamma,k)$ is a yes-instance
of Maximum Edge Biclique. Indeed, if such a vertex
$x$ belongs to $L$, choose any $k$ of its neighbors
and let $B$ be their set. The sets $A=\{x\}$ and $B$
form a biclique with $|A||B|=k$ edges.
If the vertex belongs to $R$, the same argument applies
with the two sides exchanged.

In this case, construct $\Gamma'=(\mathcal X,\mathcal Y,E)$
by setting
\[
\mathcal X=\{x_1,x_2\},\qquad
\mathcal Y=\{y_1,y_2\},\qquad
E=\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y,
\]
where the four vertices are distinct, and put
$d=2$, $r=1$, and $n=2$.

For condition~1, we have $|\mathcal X|=2=2^r$,
and every left and right vertex has degree $2=d$.

For condition~2, we have already shown that $\Gamma$
contains a biclique with at least $k$ edges.
The constructed graph also contains a rectangle
with more than $d$ entries: take
$A=\mathcal X$ and $B=\mathcal Y$. Then
\[
A\times B=E,\qquad |A||B|=4>2=d.
\]
Both sides of the equivalence in condition~2 are
therefore true, so the equivalence holds.

Finally, the probability table uniform on $E$ is
\[
p=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/4 & 1/4\\
1/4 & 1/4
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This table and $r=1$ have constant encoding length,
which verifies condition~3.

\medskip
In every remaining case, the following conditions hold
\begin{equation}
2\le k\le e,\qquad \deg_\Gamma(v)\le k-1\quad\text{for every vertex }v.
\label{eq:nontrivial}
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $k=1$ and $e>0$, the preceding yes-case applies.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 2}
                                                     
Remove isolated vertices from $\Gamma$; in fact, none can belong to a biclique with both sides
nonempty. Let $L_0,R_0$ be the remaining vertex sets, put $n_0=|L_0|$,
and set $d=k-1$. Each surviving vertex has degree between $1$ and $d$.
Since $\sum_{x\in L_0}\deg_\Gamma(x)=e$, we have $n_0\le e$.
Moreover, we $n_0\ge2$: otherwise, i.e., if there were only one left vertex, its degree would be
$e\ge k$, contrary to~\eqref{eq:nontrivial}.

 Let $n=2^r$ be the smallest power of two at least $n_0$.
The integers $n$ and $r$ are computable directly from
the binary representation of $n_0$ in polynomial time.
By minimality,
\begin{equation}
2^{r-1}<n_0\le 2^r,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
n_0\le n<2n_0,\quad r\ge1.
\label{eq:rounding}
\end{equation}
Construct the final graph by the following two operations:
\begin{itemize}
\item For each $x\in L_0$, add $d-\deg_\Gamma(x)$ new right vertices,
each adjacent only to $x$.
\item Add $n-n_0$ new left vertices. For each such new vertex $\alpha$,
create 
$d$ new right neighbors,
each adjacent \textit{only} to that left vertex $\alpha$.
\end{itemize}
All introduced vertices are distinct, and no other edges are added.
Write $\mathcal X,\mathcal Y$ for the final vertex sets and $E$ for the final
edge set. Every left degree is now $d$. An original right degree is unchanged
and lies between $1$ and $d$; every new right degree is one. 

This proves
the first assertion  of the lemma. 
We verify both implications of the second assertion directly in the final
graph. 

Suppose first that $\Gamma$ contains a biclique $A\times B$
with at least $k$ edges. None of its vertices is isolated,
so all survive the deletion of isolated vertices.
All its edges are retained in $\Gamma'$, and therefore
$A\times B\subseteq E$ with $|A||B|\ge k=d+1>d$.

Conversely, suppose $A\times B\subseteq E$ and
$|A||B|>d$. We show that this rectangle uses only
original vertices and edges.

Firstly, let us recall that 
every newly added right vertex is \textit{private}, that is, it has exactly one left neighbor. 
If $B$ contained a newly added right vertex $y_0$,
let $x_0$ be its unique neighbor. Since every vertex
of $A$ must be adjacent to $y_0$, we would have
$A=\{x_0\}$. Then $B$ would be contained in the
$d$-element neighborhood of $x_0$, giving
$|A||B|=|B|\le d$, a contradiction.
Hence $B\subseteq R_0$. 

Secondly, every newly added left vertex is adjacent only to
newly added right vertices, so it has no neighbor in
the nonempty set $B$. Since every vertex of $A$
must be adjacent to every vertex of $B$, no newly
added left vertex can belong to $A$. Thus $A\subseteq L_0$.
Finally, the construction adds no edges between
$L_0$ and $R_0$, so
\[
A\times B\subseteq E\cap(L_0\times R_0)=E_\Gamma.
\]
This is a biclique of the original graph with
$|A||B|>d=k-1$, hence with at least $k$ edges.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 3.}
Now, we estimate  the size of the constructed instance and the running time of the algorithm.

Let $\ell$ be the total bit length of $(\Gamma,k)$. The graph is given explicitly,
so $e=O(\ell)$. In the nontrivial case,
\[
n<2n_0\le2e,\qquad 1\le d=k-1<e,\qquad N=|E|=nd\le2e^2.
\]
Every right vertex has positive degree, and the sum of the right degrees is
$N$. Hence $c:=|\mathcal Y|\le N$. The complete probability table, including
zeros, has at most
\[
nc\le nN\le4e^3
\]
cells. Its entries are $0$ or $1/N$, each requiring $O(\log(e+1))$ bits;
therefore its total encoding length is $O(e^3\log(e+1))$.
Also $r=\log_2 n\le\log_2(2e)$, so $r$ has polynomial encoding length.

Degrees and isolated vertices are found by scanning the input. The
construction adds polynomially many vertices and edges, which may be
relabeled consecutively, and writes polynomially many table entries.
By~\eqref{eq:rounding}, the power-of-two padding increases
the row count by less than a factor of two, and
$n<2n_0\le2e$. Thus the requirement $n=2^r$ preserves
the polynomial bound on the output size.
Finally, the test $k>e$ occurs before any loop whose length depends on the
value of $k$. All subsequent such loops have $k\le e$, so a large
binary-encoded threshold cannot cause a pseudopolynomial running time.
This proves the third assertion and the claimed polynomial-time construction.

Given $(\Gamma,k)$, apply Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction}, form the uniform table $p$ on
$E$, and output $(p,r)$. The output satisfies all the stated restrictions,
and $H(X)=\log n=r$ exactly. By Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:criterion},
\[
\begin{split}
\Gamma\text{ has a biclique with at least }k\text{ edges}
&\ \Longleftrightarrow\ E\text{ has a rectangle larger than }d\\
&\ \Longleftrightarrow\ J(p)<r.
\end{split}
\]
 The construction is polynomial-time and  proves NP-hardness by a many-one
reduction.

\medskip
For NP membership on the restricted class, treat it as a language on all
encodings and reject those failing its restrictions. First check that the
input specifies a probability table and a nonnegative integer $r$.
The remaining checks are also polynomial-time: count rows, check that their number is a power of
two whose exponent equals $r$, count positive entries in each row and column,
and compare each positive rational entry with $1/(nd)$. The exponent is
read from the binary row count; there is no need to construct $2^r$ for an
arbitrarily large supplied $r$.
For an input satisfying the restrictions, a certificate consists of two
nonempty index sets $A,B$. Verify that every entry in $A\times B$ is positive
and that $|A||B|>d$. This certificate has polynomial length, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:criterion} proves that such a certificate exists exactly
when $J(p)<r$. No real-valued certificate or entropy comparison is required.
Thus the restricted language is NP-complete.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:strict}
Every constructed instance satisfies $J(p)\le r$, with equality exactly in
the no case. Consequently, replacing $J(p)<r$ by $J(p)\le r$ at this same
threshold would make every constructed instance a yes-instance. Section~\ref{sec:gap} will establish a quantitative gap
between the two cases. This will allow us to replace $r$
by the smaller rational threshold
$\theta=r-1/(8N^2)$, where $N$ is the number of positive
table entries, so that yes-instances satisfy
$J(p)<\theta$ and no-instances satisfy $J(p)>\theta$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:gap}
Let $p$ be the table~\eqref{eq:uniform}, with $N=nd$. If its support
contains a rectangle with more than $d$ entries, then
\begin{equation}
J(p)<\log n-\frac1{4(d+1)N}
\le\log n-\frac1{4N^2}.
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
If no such rectangle exists, $J(p)=\log n$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
The equality assertion is Lemma~\ref{lem:no-rectangle}.
Otherwise choose a rectangle of size $m>d$ and use the auxiliary and gain
function of Lemma~\ref{lem:large-rectangle}. Since $m,d$ are integers,
$m\ge d+1$. Set
\[
t=\frac1{2(d+1)}\in(0,1).
\]
Applying $\ln u\le u-1$ with $u=d/(d+1)$ gives
\[
\ln\frac{d+1}{d}\ge\frac1{d+1}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\log\frac md\ge\log\frac{d+1}{d}
\ge\frac1{(d+1)\ln2}=\frac{2t}{\ln2}.
\]
The estimate~\eqref{eq:gain-bound} now yields
\[
\Phi(t)\ge\frac{mt^2}{\ln2}
\ge\frac{(d+1)t^2}{\ln2}
=\frac1{4(d+1)\ln2}
>\frac1{4(d+1)},
\]
where the last inequality uses $0<\ln2<1$.
Since $W_t$ is admissible,~\eqref{eq:gain-identity} implies
\[
J(p)\le I(X,Y;W_t)=\log n-\frac{\Phi(t)}N
<\log n-\frac1{4(d+1)N}.
\]
Finally, the rectangle is contained in $E$, so $d+1\le m\le N$.
This proves the second inequality in~\eqref{eq:gap}.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:approximation}
On the class of tables in Theorem~\ref{thm:J-main}, let $N$ be the number of
positive entries. Producing a rational $q$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
|q-J(p)|\le\frac1{16N^2}
\label{eq:required-error}
\end{equation}
is NP-hard under a polynomial-time reduction making one call to a solver
for this approximation task. In particular, a polynomial-time algorithm
guaranteed to return such a $q$ would imply $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.

Consequently, unless $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$, no algorithm can, for every such $p$ and
every positive integer $s$ given in unary, return a rational $q$ with
$|q-J(p)|\le2^{-s}$ in time polynomial in the table length and $s$.
The obstruction already occurs for $s=O(\log N)$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Given $(\Gamma,k)$, construct $(p,r)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction}, compute $N=nd$,
and obtain any rational $q$ satisfying~\eqref{eq:required-error}. Compute
\begin{equation}
\theta=r-\frac1{8N^2}.
\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
On a yes-instance,~\eqref{eq:dichotomy} gives
\[
q\le J(p)+\frac1{16N^2}<r-\frac3{16N^2}<\theta.
\]
On a no-instance it gives
\[
q\ge J(p)-\frac1{16N^2}=r-\frac1{16N^2}>\theta.
\]
Thus the original instance is yes exactly when $q<\theta$, for every
allowed output of the approximation solver. If the solver runs in polynomial
time, $q$ has polynomial encoding length; comparison with $\theta$ uses
integer cross-multiplication and takes polynomial time. This proves the
first assertion.

For the precision formulation, let $s$ be the number of binary digits of
the positive integer $16N^2$. Then
\[
2^{-s}<\frac1{16N^2},\qquad s=O(\log N).
\]
This choice uses only integer operations. Since $N$ is polynomially bounded
in the original input length, the unary encoding of $s$ also has polynomial
length. An algorithm meeting the stated precision specification would
therefore supply a $q$ satisfying~\eqref{eq:required-error}, contradicting
the first assertion unless $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.

<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\label{def:restricted}
Let $U$ be a finite set and $\mathcal{S}=(S_1,\dots,S_m)$ an indexed family of subsets of $U$. An
\emph{exact cover} is a set $I\subseteq[m]$ such that the sets $S_j$, $j\in I$, are pairwise
disjoint and their union is $U$. The pair $(U,\mathcal{S})$ is \emph{restricted} if
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(R\arabic*)}]
\item\label{R1} $\lvert S_j\rvert=3$ for every $j\in[m]$;
\item\label{R2} every element of $U$ belongs to at most three of the sets $S_j$;
\item\label{R3} $\lvert S_j\cap S_k\rvert\le1$ whenever $j\ne k$;
\item\label{R4} $\lvert U\rvert=3t$ for an integer $t$ with $1\le t<m$.
\end{enumerate}

\label{thm:mr}

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:restricted}
There is a polynomial-time algorithm that maps every instance of \textup{\textsc{Cubic X3C}}\ with $n\ge1$ sets to a
restricted pair $(U,\mathcal{S})$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sizes}
  \lvert U\rvert=N=21n,\qquad t=7n,\qquad m=15n,
\end{equation}
such that $(U,\mathcal{S})$ has an exact cover if and only if the given instance has one.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $(U_0,(T_1,\dots,T_n))$ be the given instance of \textup{\textsc{Cubic X3C}}, so $\lvert U_0\rvert=n$.

\emph{Three copies.} The disjoint union of three copies of the given instance is an instance of
\textup{\textsc{Cubic X3C}}\ with a ground set $U'$ of size $3n$ and sets $T'_1,\dots,T'_{3n}$. It has an exact cover if
and only if the original instance has one, since an exact cover of the union restricts to an exact
cover of each copy, and exact covers of the copies combine to one of the union. (This step only
serves to make $\lvert U\rvert$ divisible by three in \eqref{eq:sizes}.)

\emph{Gadgets.} For each $j\in[3n]$ write $T'_j=\{u_{j,1},u_{j,2},u_{j,3}\}$, introduce six new
elements $c_{j,i,k}$ ($i\in[3]$, $k\in[2]$), the cells of a $3\times2$ grid, and replace $T'_j$ by
the three \emph{rows} and the two \emph{columns} of this grid, each row enlarged by one element of
$T'_j$:
\[
  A_{j,i}=\{u_{j,i},\,c_{j,i,1},\,c_{j,i,2}\}\quad(i\in[3]),\qquad
  B_{j,k}=\{c_{j,1,k},\,c_{j,2,k},\,c_{j,3,k}\}\quad(k\in[2]).
\]
Let $U$ consist of $U'$ and the $18n$ new elements, and let $\mathcal{S}$ be the family of all $15n$ rows
and columns. Then \eqref{eq:sizes} holds, and the construction takes polynomial time.

\emph{Restrictions.} Property \ref{R1} is clear, and \ref{R4} holds because $7n<15n$. An element
$u\in U'$ belongs to the row $A_{j,i}$ exactly when $u=u_{j,i}$; so it belongs to one row of each
of the three gadgets $j$ with $u\in T'_j$, and to no other set. A cell $c_{j,i,k}$ belongs only to
$A_{j,i}$ and $B_{j,k}$. This proves \ref{R2}. As for \ref{R3}, two sets from different gadgets can
only share elements of $U'$, and each set contains at most one element of $U'$; within a gadget,
the rows are pairwise disjoint, the two columns are disjoint, and
$A_{j,i}\cap B_{j,k}=\{c_{j,i,k}\}$.

\emph{Equivalence.} Let $I$ be an exact cover of $U$ and fix $j$. Every row of gadget $j$ meets
every column of gadget $j$, so $I$ cannot contain both a row and a column of this gadget. If $I$
contains a row, it contains no column, and each cell $c_{j,i,1}$ must be covered by its row; thus
all three rows belong to $I$. If $I$ contains no row, the cells $c_{j,1,1}$ and $c_{j,1,2}$ must be
covered by the columns; thus both columns belong to $I$.

Let $J$ be the set of indices $j$ such that the rows of gadget $j$ belong to $I$. Each $u\in U'$ is
covered by exactly one member of $I$, and the members of $\mathcal{S}$ containing $u$ are the rows
$A_{j,i}$ with $u_{j,i}=u$. Hence $u$ belongs to $T'_j$ for exactly one $j\in J$; that is, $J$ is an
exact cover of $U'$. Conversely, if $J$ is an exact cover of $U'$, choose the rows of every gadget
$j\in J$ and the columns of every gadget $j\notin J$. Every cell is covered exactly once, by its
row or by its column, and every $u\in U'$ is covered exactly once, by a row of the unique gadget
$j\in J$ with $u\in T'_j$. This is an exact cover of $U$.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:component}
Let $F$ be a nonzero nonnegative rank-one matrix with $F\le M$. Then
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item\label{c:a} $\mu(F)\le3+\mu_0(F)$;
\item\label{c:b} $\mu(F)\le3+3\varepsilon$;
\item\label{c:c} if $\mu(F)>3+\varepsilon$, then $\operatorname{supp} F\subseteq S_j\times\{0,j\}$ for some $j\in[m]$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
By \eqref{eq:rectangle}, $\operatorname{supp} F=R\times C$ for some nonempty $R\subseteq U$ and
$C\subseteq\{0,\dots,m\}$, and $\operatorname{supp} F\subseteq\operatorname{supp} M$ because $0\le F\le M$. Let $J=C\cap[m]$ be
the set of set columns used by $F$.

If $J=\emptyset$, then $F$ is supported in the extra column, so $\mu(F)=\mu_0(F)\le\mu_0(M)=1$.

If $J=\{j\}$, then $R\times\{j\}\subseteq\operatorname{supp} F\subseteq\operatorname{supp} M$, that is, $u\in S_j$ for every
$u\in R$. Hence $\operatorname{supp} F\subseteq S_j\times\{0,j\}$, and $F\le M$ gives
$\mu(F)-\mu_0(F)\le\lvert S_j\rvert=3$ and $\mu_0(F)\le3\varepsilon$.

If $J$ contains two distinct indices $j$ and $k$, then $R\subseteq S_j\cap S_k$, so $R=\{u\}$ for a
single element $u$ by \ref{R3}. By \ref{R2}, row $u$ of $M$ contains at most three ones, so
$\mu(F)-\mu_0(F)\le3$, while $\mu_0(F)\le\varepsilon$; thus $\mu(F)\le3+\varepsilon$.

Assertions \ref{c:a} and \ref{c:b} hold in all three cases, and $\mu(F)>3+\varepsilon$ is possible only in
the second case, which proves \ref{c:c}.
<!-- end proof-9 -->
<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:majorization}
The normalized mass vector $q$ of every rank-one decomposition of $M$ satisfies $q\preceqq^{\star}$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Let $F_1,F_2,\dots$ be the components, ordered as above, so that $Zq_i=\mu(F_i)$. Fix $k\ge1$; in
the following sums, $i$ ranges over the indices $i\le k$ of existing components. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:component}\ref{c:b}, $\sum_i\mu(F_i)\le k(3+3\varepsilon)$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:component}\ref{c:a}, $\sum_i\mu(F_i)\le3k+\sum_i\mu_0(F_i)\le3k+1$, because the
extra-column masses of all components add up to $\mu_0(M)=1$. Trivially, $\sum_i\mu(F_i)\le Z$.
Therefore
\[
  q_1+\dots+q_k\le\min\Bigl\{ka,\ \frac{3k+1}{Z},\ 1\Bigr\}.
\]
On the other hand, the partial sums of $q^{\star}$ are $ka$ for $k\le t$, then $ta+(k-t)b=(3k+1)/Z$ for
$t\le k\le m$, and $1$ for $k\ge m$. In each range of $k$, one of the three bounds above equals the
partial sum of $q^{\star}$.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:cover}
If $(U,\mathcal{S})$ has an exact cover, then $M$ has a rank-one decomposition with $m$ components whose
normalized mass vector is $q^{\star}$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Let $I\subseteq[m]$ be an exact cover, so $\lvert I\rvert=t$. For $j\in[m]$, let
$\mathbf{1}_{S_j}\in\mathbb{R}^U$ be the indicator vector of $S_j$, let $e_0,\dots,e_m$ be the standard basis
vectors of $\mathbb{R}^{\{0,\dots,m\}}$, and put
\[
  E_j=\mathbf{1}_{S_j}(e_j+\varepsilon e_0)^{\top}\quad(j\in I),\qquad E_j=\mathbf{1}_{S_j}e_j^{\top}\quad(j\notin I).
\]
Each $E_j$ is a nonnegative matrix of rank one. The matrices $E_j$ reproduce every set column of
$M$, and their extra columns add up to $\varepsilon\sum_{j\in I}\mathbf{1}_{S_j}=\varepsilon\mathbf{1}_U$, because the sets
$S_j$, $j\in I$, partition $U$. Thus $\sum_jE_j=M$. Finally, $\mu(E_j)=3+3\varepsilon=Za$ for $j\in I$
and $\mu(E_j)=3=Zb$ for $j\notin I$; listed in nonincreasing order, the normalized masses form
$q^{\star}$.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:deficit}
If $(U,\mathcal{S})$ has no exact cover, then $D_t(q)\ge1/(NZ)$ for the normalized mass vector $q$ of every
rank-one decomposition of $M$.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Let $F_1,F_2,\dots$ be the components of a rank-one decomposition of $M$, ordered as above, and let
$q$ be its normalized mass vector, so that $\mu(F_i)=Zq_i$. Suppose that $Z\,D_t(q)<\varepsilon$; we show
that an exact cover exists.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:component}\ref{c:b}, each of the numbers $Zq_1,\dots,Zq_t$ is at most $3+3\varepsilon$,
and together they fall short of $t(3+3\varepsilon)$ by $Z\,D_t(q)<\varepsilon$. Hence each of them exceeds $3+2\varepsilon$;
in particular, the components $F_1,\dots,F_t$ exist, and Lemma~\ref{lem:component}\ref{c:c} yields
indices $j_1,\dots,j_t\in[m]$ with $\operatorname{supp} F_i\subseteq S_{j_i}\times\{0,j_i\}$. Put
$V=S_{j_1}\cup\dots\cup S_{j_t}$, and compare two bounds on the extra-column mass of
$F_1,\dots,F_t$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:component}\ref{c:a},
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^{t}\mu_0(F_i)\ge\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bigl(\mu(F_i)-3\bigr)=t(3+3\varepsilon)-Z\,D_t(q)-3t
  =1-Z\,D_t(q)>1-\varepsilon.
\]
On the other hand, $F_i(u,0)=0$ for $u\notin V$, and $\sum_{i\le t}F_i(u,0)\le M(u,0)=\varepsilon$ for every
$u$, because the components are nonnegative and sum to $M$. Hence
$\sum_{i\le t}\mu_0(F_i)\le\varepsilon\lvert V\rvert$.

Combining the two bounds gives $\lvert V\rvert>(1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon=N-1$, so $V=U$. Thus $U$ is the union
of the $t$ sets $S_{j_1},\dots,S_{j_t}$ of size three, while $\lvert U\rvert=3t$. These sets are
therefore pairwise disjoint (in particular, the indices $j_i$ are distinct), and
$\{j_1,\dots,j_t\}$ is an exact cover.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:entropy}
Let $1\le t<m$ be integers and $a>b>0$ real numbers with $ta+(m-t)b=1$, and let $q^{\star}$ be the
probability vector with $t$ entries equal to $a$ and $m-t$ entries equal to $b$. Let $q$ be a
probability vector with $q\preceqq^{\star}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $H(q)\ge H(q^{\star})$.
\item If $D_t(q)=ta-(q_1+\dots+q_t)\ge\delta$ for some $\delta$ with $0<\delta\le(a-b)/2$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:entropy-gap}
  H(q)-H(q^{\star})\ge\delta\log\frac{a-\delta}{b+\delta}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Part~(a) is the Schur concavity of entropy recalled in Section~\ref{subsec:majorization}.

For part~(b), let $q^\circ$ be the probability vector obtained from $q^{\star}$ by moving mass $\delta$
from one entry equal to $a$ to one entry equal to $b$:
\[
  q^\circ=(\underbrace{a,\dots,a}_{t-1},\,a-\delta,\,b+\delta,\,\underbrace{b,\dots,b}_{m-t-1}).
\]
Since $\delta\le(a-b)/2$, we have $a\ge a-\delta\ge b+\delta\ge b$, so the entries of $q^\circ$
are listed in nonincreasing order. Its partial sums are $ka$ for $k\le t-1$, $ta-\delta$ for
$k=t$, and those of $q^{\star}$ for $k\ge t+1$. Since $q\preceqq^{\star}$, the partial sums of $q$ are at most
$ka$ for $k\le t-1$ and at most those of $q^{\star}$ for $k\ge t+1$; moreover,
$q_1+\dots+q_t=ta-D_t(q)\le ta-\delta$. Hence $q\preceq q^\circ$, and Schur concavity gives
$H(q)\ge H(q^\circ)$.

It remains to compare $q^\circ$ with $q^{\star}$. Let $g(x)=x\log(1/x)$ for $x>0$. Then
\[
  H(q^\circ)-H(q^{\star})=\bigl[g(b+\delta)-g(b)\bigr]-\bigl[g(a)-g(a-\delta)\bigr].
\]
The derivative $g'(x)=\log(1/x)-\log e$ is decreasing. By the mean value theorem,
$g(b+\delta)-g(b)\ge\delta\,g'(b+\delta)$ and $g(a)-g(a-\delta)\le\delta\,g'(a-\delta)$. Therefore
\[
  H(q)-H(q^{\star})\ \ge\ H(q^\circ)-H(q^{\star})\ \ge\ \delta\bigl(g'(b+\delta)-g'(a-\delta)\bigr)
  =\delta\log\frac{a-\delta}{b+\delta}.\qedhere
\]

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:gap}
Let $(U,\mathcal{S})$ be restricted, let $p$ and $h_0$ be given by \eqref{eq:M} and \eqref{eq:h0}, and put
$\Delta=1/(4N^2Z)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If $(U,\mathcal{S})$ has an exact cover, then $G(p)=h_0$, and the infimum in \eqref{eq:G} is attained
by an auxiliary with $m$ values and rational probabilities.
\item If $(U,\mathcal{S})$ has no exact cover, then $H(W)\ge h_0+\Delta$ for every admissible auxiliary $W$
for $p$; in particular, $G(p)\ge h_0+\Delta$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
Let $W$ be an admissible auxiliary for $p$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:decomp}(a), applied to $p$ and rescaled
by the factor $Z$, the positive probabilities $\Pr(W=w)$ form the normalized mass vector $q$ of a
rank-one decomposition of $M$; in particular, $H(W)=H(q)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:majorization},
$q\preceqq^{\star}$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:entropy} applies.

(a) By Lemma~\ref{lem:entropy}(a), $H(W)\ge h_0$ for every admissible auxiliary $W$. Conversely,
Lemma~\ref{lem:cover} and Lemma~\ref{lem:decomp}(b) provide an admissible auxiliary with distribution
$q^{\star}$, whose entropy is $h_0$.

(b) Here $D_t(q)\ge1/(NZ)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:deficit}. Put $\delta=1/(NZ)$. Since $\varepsilon=1/N$, we
have $a=(3N+3)/(NZ)$ and $b=3N/(NZ)$; hence $\delta\le(a-b)/2$, $a-\delta=(3N+2)/(NZ)$ and
$b+\delta=(3N+1)/(NZ)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:entropy}(b) and the inequality $\ln(1+x)\ge x/(1+x)$, with
$x=1/(3N+1)$, give
\[
  H(W)-h_0\ \ge\ \frac1{NZ}\log\frac{3N+2}{3N+1}\ \ge\ \frac{1}{(3N+2)\ln2\cdot NZ}
  \ >\ \frac{1}{4N^2Z}=\Delta,
\]
where the last inequality holds because $(3N+2)\ln2<\frac{7}{10}(3N+2)\le\frac72N<4N$ for $N\ge1$.
This holds for every admissible auxiliary $W$, and so $G(p)\ge h_0+\Delta$.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:G-approx}
Producing, for a rational probability table $p$ with $L$ entries, a rational
number $q$ with
\[
|q-G(p)|\le\frac1{36L^2}
\]
is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard under a polynomial-time reduction making one call to a solver for

Theorem~\ref{thm:G-main}.

<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
Instances of \textup{\textsc{Cubic X3C}}\ with $n=0$ sets are yes-instances and are decided directly.
Given an instance with $n\ge1$ sets, construct $p$ as in step~(i) above; it has
$L=N(m+1)$ entries. Since $Z=3m+1\le3(m+1)$ and $N\le L$, we have
$4N^2Z\le12NL\le12L^2$, so every allowed output $q$ satisfies
$|q-G(p)|\le\Delta/3$. Let $s'=3s$, and let $k'$ be the least nonnegative
integer satisfying~\eqref{eq:threshold} with $k$ and $s$ replaced by $k'$ and
$s'$. As in the proof above, $k'=\lceil s'h_0\rceil$, so that
$h_0\le k'/s'<h_0+\Delta/3$, and $k'$ is computed in polynomial time by the same
binary search. Put $\theta=k'/s'+\Delta/3$, a rational number.

If the given instance has an exact cover, then $G(p)=h_0$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:gap}(a), and $q\le h_0+\Delta/3\le\theta$. Otherwise
$G(p)\ge h_0+\Delta$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:gap}(b), and
$q\ge h_0+2\Delta/3>k'/s'+\Delta/3=\theta$. Hence the given instance has an
exact cover if and only if $q\le\theta$, and the comparison takes polynomial
time.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:J-vs-G}
Let $\mathcal X=\{x_1,x_2\}$ and $\mathcal Y=\{y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4\}$, and let $p$
be the uniform table on
\[
E=\{x_1\}\times\{y_1,y_2,y_3\}\ \cup\ \{x_2\}\times\{y_1,y_2,y_4\}.
\]
Then $p$ satisfies the restrictions of Theorem~\ref{thm:J-main} with $n=2$,
$d=3$ and $r=1$, and
\[
J(p)\;<\;1\;=\;G(p).
\]
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
The table has $n=2=2^1$ rows, three positive entries in each row, all equal to
$1/6=1/(nd)$, and at most two positive entries in each column. The rectangle
$\{x_1,x_2\}\times\{y_1,y_2\}\subseteq E$ has $4>d$ entries, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:large-rectangle} gives $J(p)<\log2=1$. The auxiliary $W=X$ gives
$G(p)\le H(X)=1$.

Conversely, let $W$ be an admissible auxiliary, and let $(F_w)$ be the rank-one
decomposition of $p$ given by Lemma~\ref{lem:decomp}(a). By~\eqref{eq:rectangle},
$\operatorname{supp} F_w=A_w\times B_w\subseteq E$ for nonempty sets $A_w\subseteq\mathcal X$
and $B_w\subseteq\mathcal Y$. For $i=1,2,3$, let $\tau_i$ be the probability that
$A_W$ equals $\{x_1\}$, $\{x_2\}$ and $\{x_1,x_2\}$, respectively. Since
$(x_2,y_3)\notin E$, a component whose support contains $(x_1,y_3)$ has
$A_w=\{x_1\}$; hence
$1/6=p(x_1,y_3)\le\sum_{w:\,A_w=\{x_1\}}\mu(F_w)=\tau_1$. On the other hand, the
components with $A_w=\{x_1\}$ are supported in the row of $x_1$, so
$\tau_1\le\Pr(X=x_1)=1/2$. Symmetrically, using $(x_2,y_4)$, we have
$1/6\le\tau_2\le1/2$. Since $(\tau_1,\tau_2,\tau_3)$ is the distribution of a
function of $W$,
\[
H(W)\;\ge\;f(\tau_1,\tau_2):=H(\tau_1,\tau_2,1-\tau_1-\tau_2).
\]
The function $f$ is concave on the square $[1/6,1/2]^2$, so its minimum there is
attained at a vertex. Now $f(1/2,1/2)=1$,
$f(1/2,1/6)=f(1/6,1/2)=\tfrac23+\tfrac12\log3>1$, and
$f(1/6,1/6)=\log3-\tfrac13>1$. Hence $H(W)\ge1$ for every admissible $W$, and
$G(p)=1$.
