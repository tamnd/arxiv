<!-- defn-1: body_md -->
We say that a tile $T$ can be \emph{surrounded $n$ times} if there exist finite collections of figures $\mathcal C_1,\mathcal C_2,\dots,\mathcal C_n$ such that:
\begin{itemize}
\item for each $i$, $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$, each figure from $\mathcal C_i$ is congruent to $T$;
\item every two different figures from $\{T\}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^n\mathcal C_i$ have disjoint interiors;
\item for each $i$, $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$, each figure from $\mathcal C_i$ has a common boundary point with some figure from $\mathcal C_{i-1}$ (where, by convention, we let $\mathcal C_0=\{T\}$);
\item for each $i$, $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$, $\bigcup\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^i{\mathcal C_j}\right)$ contains $\bigcup\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^{i-1}{\mathcal C_j}\right)$ completely in its interior.
\end{itemize}

The collection $\mathcal C_i$ is called the \emph{$i^{\text{th}}$ corona}.
<!-- end defn-1 -->
<!-- defn-2: body_md -->
The Heesch number of a tile $T$ is the biggest number of coronas that one can create around $T.$ If no such number exist, we say that the Heesch number of the tile is infinite.
<!-- end defn-2 -->
<!-- question-1: body_md -->
Is there an upper bound to all the finite values that can appear as Heesch numbers of tiles?
<!-- end question-1 -->
<!-- defn-3: body_md -->
\label{ratio}
We say that a tiling $\mathcal{T}$ by two prototiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ and $\mathcal{P}_2$ is of ratio $q$ (of $\mathcal{P}_1$ w.r.t. $\mathcal{P}_2$), if $\lim_{r \rightarrow\infty} \frac{t_1(r,P)}{t_2(r,P)} = q$, for every point $P$. By $t_i(r,P)$ we denote the number of tiles congruent to $\mathcal{P}_i$ in the patch generated by the disk of radius $r$ centered at $P$. We further say that a protoset is of ratio $q$ if every tiling it admits has ratio $q$, or that it is of ratio at least $q$ if each tiling it admits has ratio at least $q.$

Suppose the contrary, that $\mathcal{P}$ can tessellate the space. That, in particular, means that we can create arbitrarily many coronas around $\mathcal{P}.$ We shall see how to choose $\delta$ later (depending only on $q$) but assume for now that we have chosen it to be sufficiently small. Lemma \ref{lem} gives us an integer $n_{\delta}$ and we take an arbitrary $n \geqslant n_{\delta}$. Since $\mathcal{P}$ tessellates the space, we can create $n$ coronas around it.

Taking the original prism, $\mathcal{P}$, to have its edges parallel to the $x$-- and $y$--axes, and its centroid (not taking into account the bumps and nicks) at $(0,0,0)$, we slice the structure of $n$ coronas by the planes that are orthogonal to and cut the $x$-- and $y$--axes at integer coordinates, thus obtaining $2(2n+1)$ planes with a nonempty intersection with $\mathcal{P}$. The intersections with the planes that cut the $y$--axis at integer points we will call the $x$--layers, and similarly for the $y$--layers. Observe that each layer in fact represents the surrounding of the prototiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ or $\mathcal{P}_2$ (in particular, $n$ coronas around them -- this is easily proven by induction; the important thing to note is that we have to force the tiles to all be vertically oriented. This is somewhat obvious, but we can make the height of each level of the prism, say, irrational, which would prohibit valid surroundings if one of the tiles was in horizontal orientation. Perhaps easier, the height of each layer could be made so small compared to the base of the prism that no tile could be oriented in a horizontal way). Consider, for instance, the $x$--layers. Due to the choice of $n$, each of the layers has the ratio of tiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ to $\mathcal{P}_2$ at least $q-\delta$. Looking at all the $x$--layers together, we obtain that in all of them jointly there are at least $q-\delta$ times more tiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ than tiles $\mathcal{P}_2.$ Notice that each prism in these $n$ coronas is cut into a $\mathcal{P}_1$ by a plane from one set (that is to say, in an $x$--layer or $y$--layer) and into $\mathcal{P}_2$ by a plane from the other set. What this in turn means is that in all the $y$--layers there will be at least $q-\delta$ times more tiles $\mathcal{P}_2$ than tiles $\mathcal{P}_1$, i.e. at most $\frac{1}{q-\delta}$ times more tiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ than tiles $\mathcal{P}_2$. Recall that we took the rectangle $\mathcal{P}_1$ to be one of the two possible, such that $q > 1.$ For $\delta < q-1$, we have that $q-\delta > 1$ and therefore $\frac{1}{q-\delta} < 1,$ and, in particular, $\frac{1}{q-\delta} < q-\delta$. Recall that jointly, in all the $y$--layers, there are at most $\frac{1}{q-\delta}$ times more tiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ than tiles $\mathcal{P}_2$. Therefore there exists a layer in which the ratio is smaller or equal than $\frac{1}{q-\delta}$ (for otherwise, in each layer we would have more than $\frac{1}{q-\delta}$ times many tiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ than tiles $\mathcal{P}_2$, which would, in turn, mean that in all the $y$--layers, looking at them jointly, there would be more than $\frac{1}{q-\delta}$ times more tiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ than tiles $\mathcal{P}_2$). Since $\frac{1}{q-\delta} < q-\delta$ by our choice of $\delta$, this is in contradiction with the choice of $n$ and this finishes the proof.

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We argue by contradiction. Suppose that we can find arbitrarily large bad patches $\mathcal{A}_j$ with $N_1 \leq bN_2$. The general proof strategy is the following: we will first show that any sufficiently big patch generated by a disk has to contain a good disk concentric to it. Then we show that for arbitrarily large patches, we can cover almost all of their area with good disks (meaning that we can find such disks inside of the patches). However, counting the tiles in those disks gives us that the original patch has good ratio, which is a contradiction. Along the way, we prove many technical lemmas and statements which have their own merit.

We first prove the following statement, which essentially tells us that we can always find a good small disk inside a big enough disk. The proof relies on the knowledge of the Extension theorem so we refer the reader to an exposition of it in \cite{gs1987,adams2022}

\begin{stmt}\label{stmt1}
    For each prescribed radius $L > 2U,$ there is a finite $T > L + U,$ s.t. every patch covering $\mathcal{B}_T(x)$ has a good disk centered at $x$ with radius between $L$ and $T-U.$
\end{stmt}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Statement \ref{stmt1}]

Assume the contrary, that no $T$ works. Then we can cover larger and larger disks about the origin but with no good concentric disks of the indicated size. Compactness argument from the Extension theorem of Gr\:{u}nbaum and Shepahard \cite{gs1987,adams2022} gives us a tiling from these patches. Since this is a tiling of the whole plane, it's ratio is at least $q$, so any sufficiently large disk will be good. Take one such disk centered at $0,$ $\mathcal{B}_r(0).$ Choose $r > L$ so that $\mathcal{B}_r(0)$ is good and no tile in the limit tiling is tangent to $\mathcal{B}_r(0)$. The reason for the latter requirement is that a tile just touching the boundary could result in it potentially not being counted in some of the patches from the converging subsequence. And indeed, the tiles in patches only converge to the ones in the tiling, and as such could move both away from and towards $\mathcal{B}_r(0),$ which could affect the count of the number of tiles in the patch. Take $R > r + D$. Now every tile touching $\mathcal{B}_r(0)$ is wholly contained in $\mathcal{B}_R(0).$ Thus by convergence on this larger disk $\mathcal{B}_R(0),$ we have that all the tiles touching $\mathcal{B}_r(0)$ in sufficiently late patches will have the same type and count as the tiles in the limit tiling.
However, for such patches we get a contradiction since a disk with the appropriate ratio inside them is good. And indeed, we can take $r > L$ in addition to the previous constraint, and choose $T$ such that the radius of the good disk, $r$,  is smaller than $T - U.$ This gives us the desired contradiction.
    
\end{proof}

Next we show that almost the whole patch can be covered by good disks.

To that end, we introduce $p$ as follows. For any disk size $L,$ we look at how much of the area of the whole patch $\mathcal{A}_j$, for $j$ sufficiently big, can be covered by good disks of radius at least $L.$ If for all sufficiently big $j$ we can cover at least the area of $a |\Omega_j|,$ then to $L$ we associate $a.$ Among all such values of $a,$ for all distinct $L,$ we take the supremum, and denote it by $p.$ Thus $p$ is the supremum of all $a\in [0,1]$ such that, for every $L>2U$, all sufficiently late patches $\mathcal A_j$ contain disjoint good disks of radii at least $L$ covering at least $a|\Omega_j|$.
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  

Clearly, $0 \leq p \leq 1$, and we will show $p=1.$

Our ultimate goal is to count the tiles in these good disks to show that the whole patch is good. However, patches generated by disks can contain tiles not fully contained in their interior. Thus, a tile could be shared between several disks and counted with multiplicity in this way, which we have to take care of. One solution would be to not take those tiles into the final count for the starting patch, if we could show that the ratio of tiles in each shrunken disk remains relatively close to what it was before. This can indeed give us the adequate density of tiles in the final patch, but we find the following solution to be more elegant. What we will prove is that we can additionally require the disks to be at least $D$ apart (consider this to also be a part of the definition of $p$), which will make each tile belonging to one of the patches generated by one of them not belong to any other.

\begin{stmt}\label{stmt2}
    For any fixed $L > 2U,$ and $\eta > 0,$ every sufficiently late patch $\mathcal{A}_j$ has a finite collection of disks $\mathscr{B}_j$ such that:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The disks lie in the patch and have pairwise distance greater than $D$;
        \item Every radius is at least $L;$
        \item Each disk is good;
        \item Together they occupy at least $(1-\eta)|\Omega_j|$ of the area of the patch.
    \end{itemize}
    In other words, $p=1.$
\end{stmt}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the Statement \ref{stmt2}]
We will prove that $p=1$ by improving any fraction below $1.$

To start, fix $L$ and take $T$ given by Statement \ref{stmt1}. By the definition of $p,$ sufficiently late patches have disjoint good disks of radius $M \gg T+D$ covering a fraction of $p-\varepsilon$ of their area. If $p=0$ we start with no disks.

Next we find new disks that will increase the total fraction of the good disks in the patch. As per Statement \ref{stmt1}, every uncovered point at distance at least $T$ from $\partial\Omega_j$ and at least $T+D$ from selected disks is the center of another good disk of radius between $L$ and $T-U.$ Note that this is not enough, as we have to prove that the fraction of the total area has increased, for which, for instance, taking a single additional good disk is not enough. Note also that $\mathcal{A}$ may contain holes, in which case $\partial \Omega$ contains both the outer boundary and the boundaries of those holes.

For the next step we need an obvious-looking geometric statement, whose proof we postpone for after this Lemma's. It essentially tells us that a boundary band of any fixed width occupies a vanishing fraction of the patch's area, for all patches of $n$ coronas. Formally,

\begin{lema}\label{band}
    For every fixed $w >0$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there is an $N$ such that, for every $n \geq N$ and every patch of $n$ coronas, $\mathcal{A},$ with support $\Omega,$ $$|\left \{ x: \text{dist}(x, \partial \Omega) < w \right \}| <\varepsilon \cdot |\Omega|. $$
 
\end{lema}

Call a point \textit{eligible} if it satisfies the above requirement of being sufficiently far away from both the boundary and existing disks. Then the set of all eligible points $E,$ is defined by $$E = \left \{ x \in \Omega_j: \text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega_j) > T, \, \,  \text{dist}(x, \text{old disks}) > T + D  \right \}.$$ As distance to a fixed set is a continuous function, for any point $x\in E$ we can find an open ball around it, meaning that $E$ is open. $E$ is thus measurable (we can represent it as a countable union of squares whose closures belong to $\text{int }E$ and since squares are measurable, and a countable union of measurable sets is still measurable, $E$ is as well).

Next we claim that all but at most $(O((T+D)/M) + o_j(1)) \cdot |\Omega_j|$ of the uncovered area belongs to $E.$  Indeed, the only parts of patches that are not eligible are the $T$-wide band around the boundary of the patch (which, according to Lemma \ref{band}, has a negligible area compared to the total, $o_j(\Omega_j)$), and the $T$-neighbourhoods outside of the disks. Each such annulus has an area at most $O((T+D)/M)$ times the area of the disk it is circumscribing, which gives us a total area of $O((T+D)/M)|\Omega_j|.$ Recall that we took $M \gg T+D$, so both types of non--eligible areas are indeed arbitrarily small. If we denote by $a_j$ the fraction of the patch $\mathcal{A}_j$ that our starting set of good disks, $\mathscr{B}_j$ has covered, we have $a_j \geq p-\varepsilon,$ so the lower bound on $|E|$ is given by 

\begin{equation}\label{eq1}
|E| \geq (1-a_j -O((T+D)/M) - o_j(1)) \cdot |\Omega_j|.
\end{equation} This bound will come in handy slightly later in the proof.

Now we choose disjoint disks from these candidates greedily. While we cannot guarantee the existence of a good disk with the largest possible radius (they could be approaching a supremum without ever reaching it), we can always choose a disk whose radius exceeds half of the supremum of the largest possible radii of the good circles that can still be added to our set $\mathscr{B}_j.$ We add that disk to our collection and discard all remaining candidate disks whose distance from it is at most $D$ -- we are keeping track of all the possible disks that can be added to our collection and just discard these from it (note, in particular, that we do not change $E$ in this process). We claim that the center of every discarded disk lies in the fourfold enlargement of the selected disk. Here is why. Assume that our greedy selection procedure selects a disk of radius $r$ and discards a candidate disk of radius $s$ because they intersect. Since $r > \frac{R}{2},$ and since the disk of radius $s$ was a candidate, it satisfied $s \leq R < 2r$. As the disks intersect, their centers are at a distance of at most $r + s + D\leq 3r +D < 4r$, since we took $r \geq L > 2U \geq D,$ which is what we wanted. Observe also that this greedy algorithm terminates in finite time since the patch is bounded and each disk has radius at least $L.$

Thus we have that $E \subseteq \bigcup_i 4D_i,$ where $D_i$ is the new collection of selected disks that we obtained from the algorithm. This gives: $$|E| \leq \sum_i |4 D_i| = 16 \sum_i |D_i| = 16\Bigl|\bigcup_i D_i\Bigr|,$$ where the last equality comes from disks being disjoint. Thus, using (\ref{eq1}), the new disks cover an area of at least $\frac{1}{16}|E| \geq \frac{1}{16}(1-a_j -O((T+D)/M) - o_j(1)) \cdot |\Omega_j|$, which will be almost $\frac{1}{16}(1-p).$ Together with the starting collection of disks, $\mathscr{B}_j,$ we would have a total percentage of almost $p + \frac{1}{16}(1-p)$ covered by good disks, which will be an increase, and a contradiction. However, there are a few error terms that we have to take care of.

Going back to the calculation, together with the starting good disks from $\mathscr{B}_j$, which cover an area of $a_j |\Omega_j|,$ and using that $a_j \geq p-\varepsilon,$ we have that at least the following percentage of $|\Omega_j|$ is covered by good disks: $a_j + \frac{1}{16} (1-a_j -O((T+D)/M) - o_j(1)) \geq p + \frac{1-p}{16} - \frac{15\varepsilon + O((T+D)/M) + o_j(1)}{16}.$ Term $\frac{1-p}{16}$ is a constant, and we can make the last term, $\frac{15\varepsilon + O((T+D)/M) + o_j(1)}{16}$ be smaller than it by choosing first a small enough epsilon, then taking $M \gg T+D$ and taking $j$ sufficiently large so that all the sufficiently large patches will have this term be smaller than $\frac{1-p}{16}.$ This ultimately gives us a contradiction since we have shown that we can cover a percentage larger than $p$ with good disks of any sufficiently large patch, which goes against the definition of $p$.

\end{proof}



Now we are ready to finish the proof. The main idea left is to take the area estimate of the good disks in the patch and transform it into tile count, which will ultimately give that the patch has a good ratio of tiles, a contradiction.

Choose the good disks from Statement \ref{stmt2}, $\mathcal{B}_{r_i}(x_i)$, with $r_i > L > D$, covering at least $(1 -\eta)|\Omega_j|$ area. Assign each tile that touches any of the disks to the disk it touches. Since we required in the statement of Statement \ref{stmt2} that disks be at a pairwise distance at least $D,$ this assignment is unique.
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        

Next, let $N$ be the total number of tiles and $R$ the number of tiles left unassigned. Then the area estimate gives us: $R \cdot a \leq \eta|\Omega_j| \leq \eta\cdot N \cdot A$ and ultimately \begin{equation}\label{eq3}\frac{R}{N} \leq \frac{A}{a}\cdot \eta.\end{equation} A crucial observation is that we can make this fraction as small as needed by choosing some $L$ and a sufficiently small $\eta$. Then Statement \ref{stmt2} gives us $j$ large enough so that the area estimate we rely on here really holds. We thus make the fraction of unassigned tiles arbitrarily small.

Since each assigned collection is good, we have $N_{1,i} > q' N_{2,i},$ for a collection corresponding to $\mathcal{B}_{r_i}(x_i).$ Denote $m = N_{1,i} + N_{2,i}.$ Then $N_{2,i} < \frac{m}{q'+1}$, so we calculate $N_{1,i} - b N_{2,i} = m - (b+1)N_{2,i} > m(1 - \frac{b+1}{q'+1}) = \frac{q' - b}{q' + 1}\cdot m \equiv \gamma (N_{1,i} + N_{2,i}),$ for $\gamma = \frac{q'-b}{q'+1} > 0.$

Now we estimate $N_1 - b N_2.$ For assigned tiles, we have the previous bound, and each unassigned tile can contribute at worst $-b$ to $N_1 - bN_2$. Adding all the contributions, we get $$N_1 - bN_2 > \gamma (N-R) - bR = \gamma N - (\gamma + b)R.$$ We want the right-hand side to be positive to get a contradiction, so, equivalently, we want $\frac{R}{N} < \frac{\gamma}{\gamma + b}.$ Now choosing the fraction of the unassigned tiles to be below $\frac{\gamma}{\gamma + b},$ which we can do per (\ref{eq3}), we get a contradiction with $N_1 \leq bN_2.$ This finishes the proof.

For every fixed $w >0$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, there is an $N$ such that, for every $n \geq N$ and every patch of $n$ coronas, $\mathcal{A},$ with support $\Omega,$ $$|\left \{ x: \text{dist}(x, \partial \Omega) < w \right \}| <\varepsilon \cdot |\Omega|. $$

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
We first prove the following Statement.
Prior to that, we introduce the \textit{contact graph} of the patch $\mathcal{A}.$ Contact graph $G$ of a patch $\mathcal{A}$ is obtained by taking a vertex for each tile from $\mathcal{A}$ and connecting two vertices if and only if the corresponding tiles touch, potentially only at one point.
\begin{stmt}\label{stmt3}
    Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a patch of $n$ coronas around tile $T_0.$ Denote by $\mathscr{B}_s(T)$ a ball in the contact graph: $$\mathscr{B}_s(T) = \left \{ T' \in \mathcal{A} : d_G(T, T') \leq s \right \}.$$ Then there exist constants $s_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ and $c,C >0$ which depend only on the protoset s.t. the following holds. For every $s_0 \leq s \leq n$ and every tile $T$ satisfying $d_G(T,T_0)\leq n-s$, i.e. for all the tiles at least $s$ layers from the boundary, $$cs^2 \leq |\mathscr{B}_s(T)| \leq C(s+1)^2.$$ In particular, the whole patch has $\Theta(n^2)$ many tiles.
 \end{stmt}

 \begin{proof}[Proof of Statement \ref{stmt3}]

 To see why the upper bound holds, observe that all the tiles corresponding to the vertices in a radius--$s$ ball in $G$ are contained in a disk of radius $(s+1)D$. Since each tile has area at least $a,$ we have at most $\frac{D^2\pi}{a}(s+1)^2$ many tiles. 

 To get the lower bound, observe first that any tile meeting a line segment of length $\ell$ lives in its $D$-neighbourhood. Total area of this neighbourhood is $2D\ell + D^2\pi$ and since tiles are disjoint, at most $\frac{2D\ell + D^2\pi}{a} = O(\ell+1)$ tiles can meet the segment.

 Tiles intersecting this segment form a path in $G.$ Therefore, a line of length $\ell$ meets at most $C_1\ell+ C_0$ tiles, for some constants $C_1, C_0.$ Taking $\alpha >0$ sufficiently small, so that $C_1 \alpha s + C_0 < s,$ we have that a line segment of length $\alpha s$ meets less than $s$ tiles. We next argue that there are no holes inside of the patch generated by $\mathcal{B}_{\alpha s}(x),$ for a point $x$ inside $T$. Let $y$ be any point at distance at most $\alpha s$ from $x.$ Suppose that the segment between $x$ and $y$ leaves the support $\Omega$ of $\mathcal{A},$ and stop at a first point $z$ on $\partial \Omega$. Since earlier coronas are completely surrounded, a tile $Q$ containing $z$ belongs to the last corona $\mathcal{C}_n,$ so $d_G(T_0,Q) = n.$ Then triangle inequality gives $d_G(T,Q) \geq n - d_G(T,T_0) \geq s.$ However, by the previous argument, the line segment from $x$ to $z$ meets fewer than $s$ tiles; these tiles make a path of length less than $s$ in $G$, i.e. $d_G(T,Q) < s,$ which is a contradiction.
 For every $y \in \mathcal{B}_{\alpha s} (x)$ line connecting $x$ and $y$ touches less than $s$ tiles, so the whole patch will be contained in $\mathscr{B}_s(T).$ Each tile has area at most $A$ so $t(\mathscr{B}_{s}(T)) \geq \frac{\pi \alpha^2}{A}s^2$.

 In particular, taking $s = n$ and $T = T_0$, we get that the total number of tiles is $\Theta(n^2).$
     
 \end{proof}

 Reflecting on the previous Statement, and in light of the pathological examples of protosets where holes can arise in valid coronas, which could later be extended, it is quite interesting that we can guarantee that a linear width around the starting tile will not contain any holes. To get this, take any $\alpha$ so that $C_1 \alpha n + C_0 < n$ has a solution for sufficiently large $n,$ i.e. such that $C_1 \alpha < 1,$ or $\alpha < \frac{a}{2D}.$ Then for $n > \frac{\pi D^2}{a -2D\alpha}$ the patch generated by $\mathcal{B}_{n\alpha}(x)$ will not contain any holes, for any point $x$ in the interior of $T_0$. What's more, we can easily turn this into an area estimate: denote by $r = \text{dist}(x, \partial \Omega).$ Then a disk centered at $x$ and of radius $r$ is hole free, and from the previous calculations, it meets at most $\frac{2D\ell +D^2 \pi}{a}$ tiles. Since it goes to the boundary, it meets $n+1$ tiles, so $n+1 \leq \frac{2D\ell +D^2 \pi}{a}$, which simplifies to $r \geq \frac{a}{2D}n - \frac{D^2 \pi - a}{2D} \equiv \frac{a}{2D}n - c',$ for a constant $c'$. In particular, the radius of this disk is linear in $n$ and so we have a disk whose area is a constant fraction of the whole (since the total area is on the order of $n^2$ -- which follows from the previous Statement) that is hole-free.  Since this bears its own merit, we state it as a corollary:

 \begin{cor}
     For any $0< \alpha < \frac{a}{2D}$ and any sufficiently large $n$, the disk $\mathcal{B}_{n\alpha}(x)$ is fully contained in any $n$-corona patch around $T_0,$ for any point $x$ in the interior of $T_0$. As a consequence, there is a disk which covers a fraction of the total area of the patch that is hole-free.
 \end{cor}

Next, we prove that the number of tiles in the last constant number of coronas is a vanishing portion of the whole.

\begin{stmt}\label{stmt4}
    For a fixed $h \geq 1,$ and any patch of $n$ coronas, $\mathcal{A},$ let $T_h$ represent the tiles in the last $h$ coronas (coronas $n-h+1, ..., n$). Then $$\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \frac{t(T_h)}{t(\mathcal{A)}}=0.$$
\end{stmt}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Statement \ref{stmt4}]

Choose an integer $s \geq \max\left \{ h, s_0\right \}$ s.t. $8s \leq n.$ Choose also a maximal family of tiles in $T_h$ whose pairwise graph distances in $G$ exceed $ 20s$. Due to their maximality, the radius--$20s$ balls cover $T_h.$ Statement \ref{stmt3} gives us that each such ball contains $O(s^2)$ many tiles. Denoting by $N$ the number of centers of distinct balls in $T_h,$ we get: $N \cdot c_1 \cdot s^2 \geq t(T_h)$, so \begin{equation}\label{eq2}
    N \geq \frac{t(T_h)}{c_1\cdot s^2} \equiv c_2 \frac{t(T_h)}{s^2},
\end{equation} 
for $c_2 = \frac{1}{c_1}$ constant.

Next, from each center we move $5s$ steps towards the root, along the shortest path. Take the radius--$s$ balls from these points, and denote them by $\mathscr{B}_s'.$ The new centers are still more than $10s$ apart, so the balls in $\mathscr{B}_s'$ are disjoint. Statement \ref{stmt3} gives that each such ball has at least $c\cdot s^2$ many tiles (and we can apply it since $s \geq s_0$), and all of them lie between depths $4s$ and $7s$ from the outermost corona (more precisely, between depths $4s$ and $6s + h-1$ since we start from the corona of depth at most $h-1$). Recalling (\ref{eq2}), this gives that they contain at least $$cs^2 \cdot c_2 \frac{t(T_h)}{s^2} \equiv \beta \cdot t(T_h),$$ for $\beta$ a constant, which is a positive fraction of the number of tiles in $T_h.$

Now we repeat this procedure with $t$ replacing $s$, for $t = 8s, 8^2s, 8^3s,\dots,$ as long as $8t \leq n.$ More precisely, we again choose a maximal set of centers in $T_h,$ pairwise separated by more than $20t,$ then move each center $5t$ steps inward and put a radius--$t$ graph ball around each new center. The resulting balls lie progressively deeper in the patch for bigger values of $t$, in bands of depth $[4t, 7t).$ These bands are pairwise disjoint since $4 \cdot 8^{i+1}s > 7 \cdot 8^is.$ Each again contains at least the same positive fraction of the tiles in $T_h$, $\beta \cdot T_h$. Since they all lie in the original patch, as $n$ increases, $t(T_h)$ must present a vanishing fraction of its total tile count.

More formally, let $m$ be the number of scales $s, 8s, 8^2s, ...$ for which the construction is repeated. At each step we obtain a band containing at least $\beta \cdot t(T_h)$ many tiles, for a constant $\beta$ independent of $t.$ Since these bands are pairwise disjoint and lie in $\mathcal{A},$ $t(\mathcal{A}) \geq m \cdot \beta \cdot t(T_h),$ and so $$\frac{t(T_h)}{t(\mathcal{A})} \leq \frac{1}{m \beta} \rightarrow 0$$ since $m \rightarrow \infty$ as $n\rightarrow \infty.$

\end{proof}

We are now ready to finish the proof of Lemma \ref{band}.

To that end, denote the band of width $w$ around and inside $\partial \Omega$ by $I_w$. Every point of $\partial \Omega$ belongs to a tile in the last corona $\mathcal{C}_n.$ Choose a point $z_T$ in each such tile $T.$ Since diameters of tiles are at most $D,$ $I_w \subseteq \bigcup _{T \in \mathcal{C}_n} \mathcal{B}_{w + D}(z_T).$
Consequently, $$|I_w| \leq \pi (w +D)^2 \cdot t(\mathcal{C}_n) = \pi (w +D)^2 \varepsilon_n \cdot t(\Omega) \leq \frac{ \pi (w +D)^2 \varepsilon_n}{a} \cdot |\Omega| = o(|\Omega|),$$ where we used Statement \ref{stmt4} with $h=1$ to write $t(\mathcal{C}_n) = \varepsilon_nt(\Omega),$ for $\varepsilon_n \rightarrow 0$ and the fact that tiles have area at least $a.$

<!-- lema-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:norm}
If $\mathcal{T}$ is a normal tiling by $\mathcal{P} = \left \{\mathcal{P}_1, \mathcal{P}_2 \right \}$, and if $\mathcal{T}$ has ratio $q$ at $P,$ then for arbitrary $x > 0:$ $$\lim_{r \rightarrow \infty} \frac{t_1(r+x,P)}{t_1(r,P)} = 1.$$ Equivalently, \begin{equation}\label{norm}
    \lim_{r\rightarrow \infty} \frac{t_1(r+x,P) - t_1(r,P)}{t_1(r,P)} = 0.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lema-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We will show the equation (\ref{norm}). Denote by $u$ the inparameter of the tiling $\mathcal{T}$ and by $U$ the circumparameter. Then, since the patch of tiles generated by $\mathcal{B}_{r}(P)$ contains (by definition) $\mathcal{B}_{r}(P),$ and since the area of each tile is at most $U^2\pi$, we have that $t(r,P) \geqslant \frac{r^2\pi}{U^2\pi} = \left (\frac{r}{U}\right )^2,$ for arbitrary $r > 0.$ Now we make use of Lemma \ref{lem}. Tako some $\delta,$ and take $r_{\delta}$ to be the smallest radius such that each union of the first $n_{\delta}$ coronas around any of $\mathcal{P}_1$ or $\mathcal{P}_2$ is contained in $\mathcal{B}_{r_{\delta}}(P_0),$ where $P_0$ is the centroid of the zeroth corona (which is just a single tile $\mathcal{P}_1$ or $\mathcal{P}_2$), again not accounting in the bumps and nicks. Lemma \ref{lem} then tells us that $\frac{t_1(r,P)}{t_2(r,P)} \geqslant q-\delta,$ for any $r > r_{\delta}.$ Since $t_1(r,P) + t_2(r,P) = t(r,P),$ we have that $t_1(r,P) \geqslant \frac{q-\delta}{q-\delta+1}t(r,P) \geqslant \frac{q-\delta}{q-\delta+1}\left (\frac{r}{U}\right )^2.$ On the other hand, $t_1(r+x,P)-t_1(r,P) \leqslant \frac{(r+x+2U)^2\pi - r^2\pi}{u^2\pi} = \frac{\pi(x+2U)(2r + x + 2U)}{u^2\pi},$ since we are counting the number of tiles in the annulus between two discs. The last two inequalities together give $$\frac{t_1(r+x,P)-t_1(r,P)}{t_1(r,P)} \leqslant \frac{(x+2U)(2r + x + 2U)U^2}{\frac{q-\delta}{q-\delta+1}u^2r^2},$$ which tends to zero as $r \rightarrow \infty$ ($\frac{q-\delta}{q-\delta+1}$ is a constant).

<!-- lema-4: body_md -->
\label{welldef}
    If in any tiling $\mathcal{T}$ by two prototiles $\mathcal{P}_1$ and $\mathcal{P}_2$ $\lim_{r \rightarrow\infty} \frac{t_1(r,P)}{t_2(r,P)} = q$, for some point $P$ in the plane, then for any other point $Q$ we have $\lim_{r \rightarrow\infty} \frac{t_1(r,Q)}{t_2(r,Q)} = q.$
<!-- end lema-4 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
We follow a similar line of reasoning as that in \cite[Theorem~3.3.2]{gs1987}. Let $P$ and $Q$ be arbitrary, and denote by $\frac{x}{2}$ the distance between them. Then obviously $\mathcal{B}_{r}(P) \subset \mathcal{B}_{r+\frac{x}{2}}(Q) \subset \mathcal{B}_{r+x}(P),$ and therefore the analogous relations hold for the patches generated by them. It then follows that $t_1(r,P) \leqslant t_1(r+\frac{x}{2},Q) \leqslant t_1(r+x,P)$ and $t_2(r,P) \leqslant t_2(r+\frac{x}{2},Q) \leqslant t_2(r+x,P)$. The following inequalities are then easily checked to be true:

    \begin{equation}
        \frac{t_1(r+x,P)}{t_2(r+x,P)}\cdot \frac{t_1(r,P)}{t_1(r+x,P)} \leqslant \frac{t_1(r+\frac{x}{2},Q)}{t_2(r+\frac{x}{2},Q)} \leqslant\frac{t_1(r+x,P)}{t_2(r,P)}\cdot \frac{t_1(r,P)}{t_1(r,P)}.
    \end{equation}

    Taking the limit of both sides as $r\rightarrow \infty$, and using Lemma \ref{lem:norm}, we get that both the leftmost and the rightmost side is equal to $q$, proving the claim.

<!-- prop-2: body_md -->

To see why this is true, notice that $p\mathcal{H}$ is also disbalanced: indeed, if the disbalance (difference really) between the bumps and nicks in $\mathcal{H}$ is $d,$ then the disbalance between bumps and nicks in $p\mathcal{H}$ will be $pd.$ And using an argument analogous to the one first introduced by Amman (see Section \ref{overview}), we have that it has a finite Heesch number (important to note here is that the growth rate of the boundary of the coronas of a square prism is quadratic, while the volume is cubic, which is an easy and tedious task which we left to the reader).
