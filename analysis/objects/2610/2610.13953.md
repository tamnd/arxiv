<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $K\in \mathcal{K}^3$. In $\mathbb{R}^3\times\mathbb{R}^3\times\mathbb{R}^3$, we write
		\[
		\begin{gathered}
		D =\{(x,x,x):x\in K\},\\
		J_0=K\times\{0\}\times\{0\},\\
		J_1=\{0\}\times K\times\{0\},\\
		J_2=\{0\}\times\{0\}\times K,\\
	Q=J_0+J_1+J_2=K\times K\times K,
		\end{gathered}
		\]
where the addition is Minkowski addition. Define
		\[
		\begin{aligned}
		M&=V_9( D[3],J_0[2],J_1[2],J_2[2]),\\
		N&=V_9( Q[3],J_0[2],J_1[2],J_2[2]).
		\end{aligned}
		\]
		
		The calculation leading to equations~(5.7) and (5.8) in \cite {ChenFengLiXiXu2026} and the derivation of (5.9) in \cite {ChenFengLiXiXu2026} therein yield
		\[
		M=\frac{vb}{7560},\qquad M\leq N,\qquad N=\frac{v^3}{280}.
		\]
	Therefore,
		\[
		27v^2-b=\frac{7560}{v}(N-M).
		\]
		Thus
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:b-MN-equality}
			b=27v^2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M=N.
		\end{equation}
	Consider the ordered nine-tuples
		\[
		\begin{split}
			\mathscr P=( D, D, D,
			J_0,J_0,J_1,J_1,J_2,J_2),\ \ \ \ 
			\mathscr Q=(Q, Q,Q,
			J_0,J_0,J_1,J_1,J_2,J_2).
		\end{split}
		\]
		Let $u=(u_0,u_1,u_2)\in(\mathbb{R}^3\times \mathbb{R}^3\times \mathbb{R}^3)\setminus\{0\}$. Define
		\[
		F_i:=F(K,u_i)=\{x\in K:\langle x,u_i\rangle=h_K(u_i)\},
		\qquad F(K,0):=K.
		\]
		Then
		\[
	 Q^u:=F(Q, u)=F_0\times F_1\times F_2,
		\]
		and 
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:triple-face-intersection}
			D\cap Q^u\neq\varnothing
			\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
			F_0\cap F_1\cap F_2\neq\varnothing.
		\end{equation}
		
	Let
		$(P_i)_{i=1}^9$ and $(Q_i)_{i=1}^9$ be the members of $\mathscr P$ and
		$\mathscr Q$, respectively, and define
		$T_u=\{i:P_i\cap Q_i^u\neq\varnothing\}$. The strict-monotonicity criterion of Bihan and Soprunov
		\cite[Theorem~3.3]{BihanSoprunov2019} implies that $M<N$ if and only if, for some $u\neq 0$, the collection
		\[
		\{Q_i^u:i\in T_u\}\cup\{Q_i:i\notin T_u\}
		\]
		is essential. 
		
		If
		\eqref{eq:triple-face-intersection} holds, every member of $\mathscr P$
		touches the exposed face of the corresponding member of $\mathscr Q$.
		The nine exposed faces lie in translates of the common hyperplane $u^\perp$,
		Therefore, $\{Q_i^u:1\leq i\leq9\}$ is not essential. If
		\eqref{eq:triple-face-intersection} fails, the multiset tested by the
		criterion is
		\[
		\{Q, Q, Q,
		\iota_0F_0,\iota_0F_0,
		\iota_1F_1,\iota_1F_1,
		\iota_2F_2,\iota_2F_2\},
		\]
		where $\iota_i$ embeds $\mathbb{R}^3$ into the $i$th coordinate subspace of
		 $\mathbb{R}^3\times \mathbb{R}^3\times \mathbb{R}^3$.  Any subcollection containing a copy of $Q$ has
		nine-dimensional Minkowski sum. For a subcollection containing no copy of $Q$, all its members lie in the three coordinate subspaces, which form a direct sum. Examining the two repeated copies in each coordinate shows that the displayed multiset is essential if and only if
		\[
		\dim F_i\geq2\qquad(i=0,1,2).
		\]
		Thus, the inequality \(M\leq N\) is strict precisely when there exist \(F_0,F_1,F_2\), each of which is either a facet of \(K\) or \(K\) itself, such that
		\[
		F_0\cap F_1\cap F_2=\varnothing,
		\]
		where repetitions among the facets are allowed. If one
		\(F_i\) equals \(K\), then the other two facets must be disjoint.
		If two of the \(F_i\) coincide, then the repeated facet must be
		disjoint from the remaining one. In either case, replacing the
		occurrence of \(K\), or one of the repeated copies, by another
		facet yields three distinct facets with empty intersection.
		Conversely, if three distinct facets of \(K\) have empty intersection, then choosing an outer normal for each of them yields the strict inequality \(M<N\). Therefore,
		\[
		M=N
		\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
		\text{every three distinct facets of \(K\) have a common point}.
		\]
		In view of \eqref{eq:b-MN-equality}, this proves \eqref{eq:b-facet-condition}.
			
		To prove the final assertion of the proposition, it remains to show
		that every three-dimensional polytope satisfying this condition is
		a tetrahedron.  Translate \(K\) so that \(0\in\operatorname{int}K\), and let $L=K^\circ$. Under polarity, the facets of \(K\) correspond to the vertices of \(L\), and condition \eqref{eq:b-facet-condition} becomes the assertion that every three vertices of \(L\) lie in a common proper face. Fix a facet \(G\) of \(L\) and choose a vertex \(z\notin G\). For any two distinct vertices \(x,y\) of \(G\), the preceding property yields a proper face \(H\) of \(L\) containing \(x,y,z\). Since \(z\notin G\), whereas \(x,y\in G\), the three vertices \(x,y,z\) are not collinear. Consequently, \(H\) is two-dimensional and hence is a facet of \(L\). Moreover, \(H\neq G\), and \(H\cap G\) is a face containing both \(x\) and \(y\). Since two distinct facets of a three-dimensional polytope intersect in at most an edge, \(H\cap G\) must be an edge. Thus \(x\) and \(y\) are adjacent. It follows that every pair of vertices of \(G\) is adjacent, forcing \(G\) to be a triangle. Since \(G\) was arbitrary, \(L\) is simplicial. Finally, every triple of vertices of \(L\) lies in a proper face, which, by simpliciality, must be the triangular facet spanned by those three vertices. Hence, every triple of vertices of \(L\) forms a facet.
		
		Let \(m=f_0(L)\) denote the number of vertices of \(L\). Since every triple of vertices of \(L\) forms a triangular facet, we have
		\[
		f_2(L)=\binom{m}{3}.
		\]
		Since \(L\) is a simplicial, each facet has three edges, and each edge belongs to exactly two facets. 
		Counting the edge-facet incidences yields
		\[
		3f_2(L)=2f_1(L).
		\]
		Combining this identity with Euler's relation
		\[
		f_0(L)-f_1(L)+f_2(L)=2
		\]
		and using \(f_0(L)=m\), we obtain
		\[
		f_2(L)=2m-4.
		\]

By translation invariance, we may assume that \(0\in\operatorname{int}K\) and let \(L=K^\circ\). It follows from 	\eqref{eq:both-equalities} and Proposition

		\]

Then \(F\cap P_E\) is an exposed face of \(P_E\) containing \(x\) and \(y\). Since every proper face of \(L\) has dimension at most two, this face is proper. By the classification theorem, it is combinatorially equivalent to either a pyramid or a triangular prism.

We therefore assume that \(\operatorname{ext}L\) is infinite. Choose a seven-element subset \(E_0\subset\operatorname{ext}L\) such that
	\[
	\dim\operatorname{conv}E_0=3.
	\]

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
On the four-cube $C_4=[-1/2,1/2]^4$, take
		\[
			T(x)=x_1+\cdots+x_4,
		\]
		and let $q$ be the nondecreasing rearrangement of $T$ on the unit interval.
		Central reflection in the second factor of $C=C_4\times C_4$ shows that
		$T(x)+T(y)$ and $T(x)-T(y)$ have the same distribution.  Using equimeasurability and decomposing the unit square into the
		two regions \(s\ge t\) and \(s\le t\), we obtain
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:quantile-folding}
			\int_{C_4\times C_4}|T(x)+T(y)|\,dx\,dy
			=\int_0^1\!\int_0^1|q(s)-q(t)|\,ds\,dt
			=2\int_0^1(2s-1)q(s)\,ds.
		\end{equation}
		By symmetry under coordinate reflections, all cross terms in the
		expansion of \(T(x)^2\) integrate to zero. Hence,
		\[
			\int_0^1q(s)^2\,ds
			=\int_{C_4}T(x)^2\,dx
			=4\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}r^2\,dr
			=\int_0^1(2s-1)^2\,ds.
		\]
		Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to \eqref{eq:quantile-folding} and using the
		equality of the second moments above, it follows that
		\[
			\begin{split}
			\int_{C_4\times C_4}|T(x)+T(y)|\,dx\,dy
			&\leq2\int_0^1q(s)^2\,ds\\
			&=\int_{C_4\times C_4}(T(x)+T(y))^2\,dx\,dy.
			\end{split}
		\]
		We claim that the first inequality above is strict. Equality in the
		Cauchy--Schwarz inequality would imply that \(q(s)\) is proportional
		to \(2s-1\) almost everywhere. Since \(q\) is the quantile function of
		\(T\), this would force \(T\) to be uniformly distributed on an
		interval. This conclusion is incompatible with the geometry of the upper level sets of \(T\) near its
		maximum value \(2\). For every sufficiently small \(\delta>0\), the
		set
		\[
		T^{-1}\bigl((2-\delta,2]\bigr)
		\]
		is a vertex cap of \(C_4\) and is homothetic to a fixed four-dimensional simplex with homothety ratio
		\(\delta\). Its volume is therefore
		proportional to \(\delta^4\). By contrast, a uniformly distributed random variable would assign mass of order \(\delta\) to the interval \((2-\delta,2]\). Hence equality cannot occur, and the first inequality
		is strict. Since \(T(x)+T(y)\) is the diagonal coordinate \(h\) on
		\(C\), this proves \eqref{eq:cube-folding}.
