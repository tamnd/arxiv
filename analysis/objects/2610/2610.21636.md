<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{Theorem1}
    Let $E_m(n)$ be the maximum size of an Eventown family of $m$-bounded multisets on $[n]$. Then
    \[E_m(n)=
\begin{cases}
(q+1)^n,
& m=2q,\\[2mm]
\bigl((q+1)^2+1\bigr)^{n/2},
& m=2q+1,\quad n\text{ even},\\[2mm]
(q+1)\bigl((q+1)^2+1\bigr)^{(n-1)/2},
& m=2q+1,\quad n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:two-groups} 
Let $C\le \mathbb{F}_2^n$ be a $d$-dimensional self-orthogonal subspace, so that,
\[c \cdot c'=0 \ \ \ \ \ \ \ (c,c'\in C).\]
Then there exist disjoint coordinate sets $I,K\subseteq[n]$, each of cardinality $d$, such that the coordinate restriction maps to either $I$ or $K$ are injective on $C$. In particular, the coordinates indexed by either $I$ or $K$ uniquely determine every vector in $C$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
If $d=0$, take $I=K=\varnothing$. Assume $d>0$, and let $G$
be a $d\times n$ matrix whose rows form a basis of $C$. For $J\subseteq[n]$, let $G_J$ denote the submatrix of $G$
consisting of the columns indexed by $J$.
Choose $d$ independent columns, indexed by $I$.
Since $G_I$ is invertible, the restriction or projection map
\[\pi_I:C\longrightarrow \mathbb{F}_2^I\]
is a bijection. Here, $\mathbb{F}_2^I$ denotes the space of binary vectors indexed by $I$.
We claim that restriction to the complementary coordinates is
also injective. Indeed, suppose $z\in C$ is supported on $I$, that is, $z_i=0$ for all $i\notin I$. By the self-orthogonality of $C$, we have
\[
0=z\cdot c=\sum_{i\in I}z_i c_i,
\qquad\text{for every }c\in C.
\]
Since $c|_I$ ranges over all of $\mathbb{F}_2^I$, this forces
$z|_I=0$, and hence $z=0$.

Therefore restriction to $I^c=[n]\setminus I$ is injective, and thus
 $G_{I^c}$ has rank $d$.
Choosing $d$ independent columns of this matrix gives a set
$K\subseteq I^c$, of size $d$, such that restriction to $K$
is also injective.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{ordering lemma}
Let $m=2q+\delta$, where $\delta\in\{0,1\}$, and put $r=q+1$.
Let $\mathcal F\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,m\}^n$ be an Eventown family.
Then, for every coordinate $j\in[n]$,
\[
a_j+b_j\le r+\delta.
\]
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
If $a_j=0$ or $b_j=0$, the conclusion follows from the fact that $\{0,1,...,m\}$ contains $r$ even values and $q+\delta \le r$ odd values.

    Otherwise, choose $u,v$ such that $|E_j(u)|=a_j$ and $|O_j(v)|=b_j$. Let $E_j(u)=\{s_1<s_2<...<s_{a_j}\}$ and $O_j(v)=\{t_1<t_2<...<t_{b_j}\}$. Then, we have 
    \[\min(s_i,t_{i'})+I(u,v) \equiv 0 \pmod2\ \ \ \ \forall \ i\in[a_j],\ i'\in[b_j].\]
    Thus all $\min(s_i,t_{i'})$ have same parity. Since $s_i$'s are even and $t_{i'}$'s are odd, either $s_1<...<s_{a_j}<t_1<...<t_{b_j}$ or $t_1<...<t_{b_j}<s_1<...<s_{a_j}$. So, either every element of $E_j(u)$ lies below every element of $O_j(v)$, or conversely.

    In the first case, write $\max E_j(u)=2l$. There are at most $l+1$ even values at most $2l$, and $q+\delta -l$ odd values greater than $2l$. Hence
    \[a_j \le l+1,\ \ \ \ \ \ b_j \le q+\delta-l,\]
    so $a_j+b_j\le r+\delta.$

    In the second case, write $\max O_j(v)=2l-1$. Then,
    \[b_j \le l,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ a_j \le q-l+1,\]
    and therefore $a_j+b_j \le r\le r+\delta$. This proves the lemma.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:counting-G}
Let $\mathcal G\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,m\}^n$ be an Eventown family of multisets
such that, in each coordinate, the multiplicities lie in either $Q_e$ or $Q_o$, with the choice fixed for that coordinate. Then
\[
|\mathcal G|
\le r^{n-2k}(1+r^2)^k.
\]
Moreover, if $r>1$, equivalently $m\ge3$, equality holds if and
only if, up to a permutation of the coordinates,
\[
\mathcal G
=
\mathcal A^{\,t}\times
\mathcal B^{\,k-t}\times
V_e^{\,n-2k}
\qquad\text{for some }t\in\{0,\ldots,k\}.
\]
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Observe that the identity $\min(s,t)\bmod2=(s\bmod2)(t\bmod2)\label{1}$ holds within each of $Q_e,Q_o$. For $x \in \mathcal{G}$, define its parity vector as 
\[\pi(x)=(x_1 \bmod2,x_2 \bmod2,...,x_n \bmod2)\in\mathbb{F}_2^n.\]
Using the above identity we get for $x,y\in \mathcal{G}$,
\[\pi(x) \cdot \pi(y)=\sum_{i=1}^n\min(x_i,y_i)=0\ \ \ \ \ \text{in}\ \mathbb{F}_2.\]
Let $C=\text{span}_{\mathbb{F}_2}\{\pi(x):x \in \mathcal{G}\}$. Then $C$ is a self-orthogonal subspace of $\mathbb{F}_2^n$. Therefore if dimension of $C$ is $d$, then $d\le k$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:two-groups}, there exist disjoint coordinate sets $I,J\subseteq[n]$, each of size $d$, such that either restriction determines a vector of $C$. Put $L=[n]\setminus(I\cup J)$, so $|L|=n-2d$. Note that these restrictions determine the parity vector, not the actual multiplicities.

In each coordinate, the allowed multiplicities have parity counts $(r,1)$ or $(1,r)$. Thus, one parity has $r$ multiplicities, and the other has one. Let $P$ be the set of parity vectors occurring in $\mathcal{G}$. For each $c \in P$, define
\begin{align*}
    a_c &\coloneqq\  \text{the number of allowed multiplicity assignments on} \ I \ \text{having parity pattern }\ c|_I, \\  b_c &\coloneqq\  \text{the number of allowed multiplicity assignments on} \ J \ \text{having parity pattern }\ c|_J.
\end{align*}
Once $c$ is fixed, each coordinate in $L$ has at most $r$ multiplicity choices. Hence the number of members of $\mathcal{G}$ with parity vector $c$ is at most $a_cb_cr^{n-2d}$. Consequently,
\[|\mathcal{G}|\le r^{n-2d}\sum_{c \in P} a_cb_c.\]

Next, we shall bound $\sum_{c \in P} a_c^2$. For every binary pattern $u$ on $I$, let $A(u)$ be the number of allowed multiplicity assignments with that pattern. Since $I$ contains $d$ coordinates, there are $2^d$ possible patterns for $u$. If $u$ selects the parity with $r$ choices in exactly $p$ coordinates, then
\[A(u)=r^{p}.\]
Since $l'$ coordinates are chosen from $d$ coordinates, there are exactly $\binom{d}{p}$ such patterns. Consequently,
\[
\sum_{u\in\{0,1\}^{I}}A(u)^2
=\sum_{p=0}^{d}\binom {d}{p} r^{2p}
=(1+r^2)^d.
\]
Since $I$ determines the complete parity vector, distinct $c \in P$ provide distinct restrictions $c_I$. Therefore, we have,
\[\sum_{c \in P} a_c^2 \le(1+r^2)^d.\]
 Similarly from  $J$ we have 
 \[\sum_{c \in P} b_c^2 \le(1+r^2)^d.\]
Hence
\begin{align*}
    |\mathcal{G}|&\le r^{n-2d}\sum_{c \in P}a_cb_c \\ &\le r^{n-2d} \frac{1}{2}(\sum_{c \in P} a_c^2+\sum_{c \in P} b_c^2) \\ &\le r^{n-2d} (1+r^2)^d \\ & \le r^{n-2k} (1+r^2)^k\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ [\text{as}\ r\ge1]
\end{align*}
Now we characterize the extremal families. Since $|\mathcal{G}|=r^{n-2k}(1+r^2)^k$, every inequality above is an equality. Consequently, we have the following
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $d=k$,
    \item $P=C$, and 
    \item $a_c=b_c$ for all $c \in C$.
\end{enumerate}
Also, if a parity pattern occurs in $P$, then every allowed choice of multiplicity vector realizing that pattern belongs to $\mathcal{G}$. 

If $n$ is odd, there is one coordinate outside $I \cup J$. This coordinate must contribute exactly $r$ choices for each parity pattern that occurs. Since $0\in C=P$, the even parity must provide $r$ choices. Hence, the multiplicity assigned to this coordinate must belong to $Q_e$. In contrast, the odd parity provides only one choice, so no member of the family can have odd multiplicity in this coordinate. Consequently, the remaining coordinate takes values precisely from $V_e$, independently of the other coordinates.

Now since $I,J$ both determines $C$, their restrictions have the form
\[\{(u,Tu):u \in \mathbb{F}_2^k\},\]
where $T=\pi_J \circ \pi_I^{-1}$ is an invertible linear map. Define $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_2^{|I|} $ as follows
\[\alpha_i=\begin{cases}
0,
& \text{the $i$-th coordinate of $I$ uses $Q_e$},\\[2mm]
1,
& \text{the $i$-th coordinate of $I$ uses $Q_o$}.
\end{cases}\]
Similarly, define $\beta\in \mathbb{F}_2^{|J|}$ on $J$. Thus, a coordinate of $I$ gives $r$ choices precisely when $u_i=\alpha_i$. Therefore,
\[A(u)=r^{k-\text{wt}(u+\alpha)}.\]
Similarly,
\[B(Tu)=r^{k-\text{wt}(Tu+\beta)},\]
where $B(Tu)$ is the analogous multiplicity assignment count on $J$. By using $a_c=b_c$ for $c\in C$ and $r>1$, we get,
 \[\text{wt}(u+\alpha)=\text{wt}(Tu+\beta)\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for every}\  u.\]
Taking $u=\alpha$ we have $T\alpha=\beta$. Substituting $u=v+\alpha$ into the preceding equation, we obtain
\[
\operatorname{wt}(Tv)=\operatorname{wt}(v)
\qquad\text{for every }v.
\]
Each standard basis vector has Hamming weight $1$. Since $T$ preserves Hamming weight, it maps every standard basis vector to a standard basis vector. The invertibility of $T$ ensures that these images are distinct. Hence, $T$ is a coordinate permutation, inducing a pairing between the coordinates of $I$ and those of $J$. Within each pair, the two coordinates have the same parity, and both take values exclusively from either $Q_e$ or $Q_o$.

A pair of $Q_e$-coordinates, therefore, gives
\[V_e^2 \cup \{(m,m)\}=\mathcal{B}\]
and a pair of $Q_o$-coordinates gives
\[\{(0,0)\}\cup V_o^2=\mathcal{A}.\]
Since all parity patterns and all permitted assignments occur, we have,
\[\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{A}^t\times\mathcal{B}^{k-t}\times V_e^{n-2k},\]
after a coordinate permutation. The converse is immediate.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For a fixed coordinate $i$ and a family $\mathcal{F}$, define
\begin{align}
    T_e^i&=\{x:\iota_i(s,x)\in \mathcal{F},\text{for some even}\ s\in \{0,1,...,m\}\} \\ T_o^i&=\{y:\iota_i(t,y)\in \mathcal{F},\text{for some odd}\ t\in \{0,1,...,m\}\}
\end{align}
For $i=1$, we drop the superscript and just write $T_e,T_o$.

\textbf{Case I: m is even}

Suppose $\delta=0$, so $m=2q$. If $q=0$, the conclusion is immediate, otherwise, by Lemma \ref{ordering lemma}, we have,
\[
|\mathcal F|\le r\max(|T_e|,|T_o|).
\]
Now, we fix the first coordinate and replace $\mathcal F$ by
\[
\mathcal F^{1}=
\begin{cases}
\{(0,x):x\in T_e\},& if \ |T_e|\ge |T_o|,\\[1mm]
\{(1,y):y\in T_o\},& if\ |T_o|>|T_e|.
\end{cases}
\]
This replacement is clearly an Eventown family.
Moreover,
\[
|\mathcal F^1|=\max(|T_e|,|T_o|)\ge\frac{|\mathcal F|}{r}.
\]
The selected first coordinate is now fixed, and all other coordinates remain unchanged.


Starting with $\mathcal F$, we perform this operation successively on
all $n$ coordinates. 
The resulting family $\mathcal G$ contains at most one member, and at each iteration, the size of the family decreases by a factor of at most $r$.
Consequently,
\[
|\mathcal F|\le r^n |\mathcal{G}|\le r^n=(q+1)^n.
\]
This proves the theorem when $m$ is even.

\textbf{Case II: m is odd}

Suppose $\delta=1$, so $m=2q+1$. Define
\[Q_e=\{0,2,...,2q\}\cup\{m\},\ \ \ \ \ \ Q_o=\{0\}\cup\{1,3,...,2q+1\}\]
both of size $r+1$.

We first transform the family $\mathcal{F}$ into another Eventown family $\mathcal{G}$, without decreasing its size, such that the value in each coordinate is either from $Q_e$ or $Q_o$.

Fix the first coordinate. First suppose that,
\[|\mathcal{F}|\le r \max(|T_e|,|T_0|).\]
If $|T_e|\ge |T_o|$, replace $\mathcal{F}$ by $\{(s,x):s\in\{0,2,...,2q\},x\in T_e\}=V_e \times T_e$; otherwise replace it by $\{(t,y):t\in\{1,3,...,2q+1\},y\in T_o\}=V_o \times T_o$. The replacement family is also an Eventown family, where the chosen first coordinate of each member
lies in $Q_e$ or $Q_o$.
The  size of the new family is $r \max(T_e,T_o)\ge |\mathcal{F}|$.  

Now, suppose $|\mathcal{F}|>r \max(|T_e|,|T_o|)$. This forces $a+b >r$. Therefore, by Lemma \ref{ordering lemma}, we get,
\[a+b=r+1\]
and for every $x\in T_e$ with $a$ number of even extensions and any $y \in T_o$ with $b$ number of odd extensions, the even extensions come before the odd extensions. Therefore, selected tuples have exactly the following extensions:
\begin{align*}
    x&:0,2,...,2l\\ y&:2l+1,2l+3,...,2q+1
\end{align*}
for some $l \in \{0\}\cup[q]$.

Now, every extension of a tuple in $T_e$ precedes every extension of $y$. Consequently, each tuple in $T_e$ has  extensions
at most $2l$.
Similarly, a tuple in $T_o$ has all its extensions above  $2l$.  
Consequently, every even extension precedes every odd extension in  $\mathcal{F}$.


Now, if $|T_e|\ge |T_o|$ we replace $\mathcal{F}$ by \[\mathcal{F}_1^{(1)}=\{(s,x):s\in\{0,2,...,2q\},x\in T_e\} \cup \{(2q+1,y):y \in T_o\},\] otherwise by \[\mathcal{F}_2^{(1)}=\{(0,x):x \in T_e\} \cup \{(t,y):t\in\{1,3,...,2q+1\},y\in T_o\}.\] Both of the families, $\mathcal{F}_1^{(1)}$ and $\mathcal{F}_2^{(1)}$ are Eventown families with the sizes $r|T_e|+|T_o|$ and $|T_e|+r|T_o|$, respectively.

So, when $|T_e|\ge |T_o|$ we have
\[r|T_e|+|T_o|-(a|T_e|+b|T_o|)=(b-1)(|T_e|-|T_o|)\ge0,\]
and for  $|T_o|\ge|T_e|$, we have
\[|T_e|+r|T_o|-(aT_e+b|T_o|)=(a-1)(|T_o|-|T_e|)\ge0.\]
Thus the chosen replacement family, has size at least $a|T_e|+b|T_o|\ge|\mathcal F|$,
preserves the Eventown condition and restricts the first coordinate
to $Q_e$ or $Q_o$.

We apply the above procedure successively to all coordinates, with each iteration modifying only the chosen coordinate of the family members. After all $n$ iterations, we obtain an Eventown family $\mathcal G$ satisfying
\[
|\mathcal F|\le |\mathcal G|,
\]

such that, in each coordinate, every member of $\mathcal G$ takes values exclusively from either $Q_e$ or $Q_o$.
Hence by Lemma \ref{lem:counting-G}, we have,
\[|\mathcal{F}|\le |\mathcal G|
\le r^{n-2k}(1+r^2)^k.\]
Therefore,
\[|\mathcal{F}|\le\begin{cases}
\bigl((q+1)^2+1\bigr)^{n/2},  \qquad\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ for } n\text{ even},\\[2mm]
(q+1)\bigl((q+1)^2+1\bigr)^{(n-1)/2},\qquad \text{ when }n\text{ is odd}.
\end{cases}\]
