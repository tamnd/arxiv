\label{qHlemma}
 (Sierpi\'nski, 1948) For each positive integer $H$, there are arbitrarily large primes $q > H$ such that $q$
 is the unique prime in 
$ [q-H, q+H]$ \cite{Sierpinski1948}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lemlargeprimes}
 Let $A$ denote a nonempty and finite set of primes that is product-minimal. 
 Then there are arbitrarily large primes $q > s(A)$
 such that $A \cup \{ q \}$ is product-minimal.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Define $H = 4 v^2(A)$. 
 According to 
 Lemma \ref{qHlemma}, 
 it is possible to select an arbitrarily large prime $q > \max\{ s(A), H \}$
 that is the unique prime in $[q - H, q + H]$. 
 Since each member of $A$ is at most $s(A) < q$, we see that $q \not\in A$. 
 Consequently, the relations
 among 
\begin{equation}\label{sAcupq}
 s(A \cup \{ q \}) = s(A) + q
\end{equation}
 and 
\begin{equation}\label{vAcupq}
 v(A \cup \{ q \}) = q v(A) 
\end{equation}
 hold. 
 So, from the relations in \eqref{sAcupq} and 
 \eqref{vAcupq}, we find that 
 there is at least one finite set $B$ of primes
 such that 
\begin{equation}\label{sBequals} 
 s(B) = s(A) + q
\end{equation}
 and 
\begin{equation}\label{vBleqqM}
 v(B) \leq q \, v(A). 
\end{equation} 
 We then let $B$ be an arbitrary set of this form. 

 We claim that it is necessarily the case that $B = A \cup \{ q \}$. 
 In this direction, we set 
 $R = v(B \setminus \{ \max(B) \})$ and $u = s(B \setminus \{ \max(B) \})$. 
 We thus have that 
\begin{equation}\label{zplusuequals} 
 \max(B) + u = s(A) + q 
\end{equation}
 and 
\begin{equation}\label{zRleqqM}
 \max(B) R \leq q \, v(A). 
\end{equation}
 Of course, each element in $B$ is at most $\max(B)$. 
 This gives us that 
\begin{equation}\label{dplusqleq} 
 s(A) + q \leq |B| \max(B). 
\end{equation}
 A combined application of \eqref{zRleqqM} and 
 \eqref{dplusqleq} gives us that 
\begin{equation}\label{multinequalities}
 R \leq \frac{q \, v(A)}{\max(B)} \leq \frac{|B| q \, v(A)}{s(A) + q} < |B| v(A). 
\end{equation}
 Of course, since $B \setminus \{ \max(B) \}$ has $|B| - 1$ members, 
 each of which exceeds $1$, we find that 
\begin{equation}\label{Rgeq2power}
 R \geq 2^{|B|-1}. 
\end{equation}
 We may verify (using an inductive argument) that 
\begin{equation}\label{squareleqpower}
\ell^2 \leq 2^{\ell+1} 
\end{equation}
 holds for each positive integer $\ell$. 
 A combined application of \eqref{Rgeq2power} and \eqref{squareleqpower} then produces 
\begin{equation}\label{kleq2sqrtR} 
 |B| \leq 2 \sqrt{R}. 
\end{equation}
 From \eqref{multinequalities} and \eqref{kleq2sqrtR} together, we deduce that 
\begin{equation}\label{Rless2Mroot} 
 R < 2 v(A) \sqrt{R}. 
\end{equation}
 Observe that $v(A) > 0$. 
 So, the relation in 
 \eqref{Rless2Mroot} leads us to 
 $ R < 4 v^2(A) = H$. By then exploiting the relation in \eqref{sAleqvA}, we arrive at the relations among
\begin{equation}\label{uleqRlessH} 
 u \leq R < H 
\end{equation}
 and 
\begin{equation}\label{dleqMlessH}
 s(A) \leq v(A) < H. 
\end{equation}
 Using \eqref{zplusuequals}, \eqref{uleqRlessH}, and \eqref{dleqMlessH} together, 
 this gives us that 
\begin{equation}\label{equalsabs} 
 |\max(B) - q| = |s(A) - u| < H. 
\end{equation}
 Now, observe that $\max(B)$ is prime. 
 Also, recall that we chose $q$ as the unique prime 
 in $[q - H, q + H]$, by virtue of Lemma \ref{qHlemma}. 
 So, from \eqref{equalsabs}, 
 we can conclude that the primes $\max(B)$ and $q$ are necessarily equal. 
 So, from \eqref{sBequals} and \eqref{vBleqqM}, respectively, 
 we can deduce that 
 $ s(B \setminus \{ q \}) = s(A) $ 
 and that 
 $ v(B \setminus \{ q \}) \leq v(A)$. 
 The given assumption on $A$ concerning product-minimality allows us 
 to conclude that 
 $ v(B \setminus \{ q \}) = v(A)$, 
 whereas the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic 
 gives us that 
 $ B \setminus \{ q \} = A$. 
 Consequently, the equality $B = A \cup \{ q \}$ holds. 
 Since no other (finite) prime set summing to $s(A) + q$ 
 is such that the product of its elements is at most $q \, v(A)$, 
 we obtain the desired result.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lemmatd}
 For each integer $t$ exceeding $1$, there exist arbitrarily 
 large odd integers $d$ whose product-minimal prime set is of size $t$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
If $t$ is even, then we begin by setting $A_0 = \{ 2 \}$. 
 If $t$ is odd, then we start with $A_0 = \{ 3 \}$. 
 Observe that both singleton sets are product-minimal for their sums, 
 as each of $2$ and $3$ admit only singleton prime partitions. 

 We then apply Lemma \ref{lemlargeprimes} successively $t-1$ times. 
 With each application of Lemma \ref{lemlargeprimes}, the appended prime is larger than the
 preceding sum, and is thus distinct from all of the previous primes. 
 Also observe that each appended prime is necessarily odd. 

 After completing the above appending procedure, we find that the resulting product-minimal set $A$
 has precisely $t$ elements.
 If $t$ is even, then it consists of $2$ along with $t-1$ odd primes, 
 so the associated sum is odd. 
 If $t$ is odd, it consists of $t$ odd primes, so we again obtain an odd sum. 

 In either case, regardless of the parity of $t$, the final application of 
 Lemma \ref{lemlargeprimes} in the above procedure
 can be taken as being arbitrarily large. So, the resulting odd integer $d = s(A)$ can be
 made to be arbitrarily large.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lemVinogradov}
 Each sufficiently large odd integer is the sum of three pairwise distinct primes (cf.\ \cite{Vinogradov1937}).
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For $R_3(N)$ as defined in \eqref{defineR3N}, 
 we find that there exist at most $N$ triples
 of the form indicated in \eqref{defineR3N}
 such that $p_1 = p_2$, since, by fixing a choice for $p_1$, 
 the final entry is determined
 by $p_3 = N - 2 p_1$. 
 We obtain the same bound for triples satisfying
 $p_1 = p_3$ and those satisfying $p_2 = p_3$. 
 So, there are at most $3N$ triples of the form indicated in \eqref{defineR3N}
 containing a repeated prime. 
 Since $\frac{N}{\log^3 N} \to \infty$ as $N \to \infty$, 
 the formulation of Vinogradov's theorem in \eqref{displayVinogradov}
 gives us that 
 $R_3(N) > 3 N$ for all sufficiently large odd $N$. 
 So, at least one triple associated with 
 $R_3(N) $ (again for sufficiently large odd $N$) 
 necessarily has three pairwise distinct entries.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{eleminequality}
 Let $x \geq 2$, and let $G > 0$. 
 Let $U$ and $V$ be finite sets of integers satisfying 
\begin{equation}\label{2lequleqx}
 2 \leq u \leq x
\end{equation}
 for $u \in U$ and 
\begin{equation}\label{conditionG}
 v \geq x + G
\end{equation}
for $v \in V$
 and $V \neq \varnothing$ and 
\begin{equation}\label{sUminussV} 
 s(U) - s(V) = d > 0. 
\end{equation}
 Then the relations in $ \frac{v(U)}{v(V)} \geq \frac{2}{e^3} (d + G) > \frac{d + G}{11} $ hold.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We begin by noting that $|V| \geq 1$, from one of the assumptions given above. 
 Also, we observe that it is necessarily the case that $|U| > |V|$, because, otherwise, 
 we would have that 
 $s(U) \leq |V| x < |V|(x + G) \leq s(V)$, 
 but this contradicts the hypothesis in \eqref{sUminussV}. 
 So, there exists a positive integer $a$ such that 
 the elements in $U$ and $V$ can be written so that 
\begin{equation}\label{orderU} 
 U = \{ u_1 < u_2 < \cdots < u_{a+|V|} \} 
\end{equation}
 and so that 
 $ V = \{ v_1 < v_2 < \cdots < v_{|V|} \}$. By then writing 
\begin{equation}\label{tausumu} 
 \tau = \sum_{i=1}^{a} u_{i}. 
\end{equation}
 The condition in \eqref{sUminussV} then gives us that 
\begin{equation}\label{tauminusd}
 \tau - d = \sum_{j=1}^{|V|} (v_j - u_{a + j}). 
\end{equation}
 The hypothesis associated with \eqref{conditionG} gives us that each 
 difference on the right of \eqref{tauminusd} is at least $G$, 
 and we thus obtain that 
\begin{equation}\label{taugeqdplusbG} 
 \tau \geq d + |V| G \geq d + G. 
\end{equation}
 Observe that $u_{a + j} \geq u_a$ (recalling \eqref{orderU}). 
 Also, from 
 \eqref{orderU} and \eqref{tausumu}, 
 we can deduce that $\tau \leq a u_{a}$. So, this leads us to the relations such that 
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \frac{v_{j}}{u_{a+j}} 
 & = |V| + \sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \frac{v_{j} - u_{a + j}}{u_{a+j}} \\ 
 & \leq |V| + \frac{\tau - d}{u_{a}} \\
 & \leq |V| + a.
\end{align*}
 By applying the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality
 to the $|V|$ positive numbers
 of the form $\frac{v_{j}}{u_{a + j}}$ for $j \in \{ 1, 2, \ldots, |V| \}$, 
 and by then applying the elementary inequality 
 $\big( 1 + \frac{a}{|V|} \big)^{|V|} \leq e^a$, we find that 
 $$ \prod_{j=1}^{|V|} \frac{v_{j}}{u_{a+j}} \leq \left( \frac{1}{|V|} \sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \frac{v_{j}}{u_{a + j}} \right)^{|V|}
 \leq \left( 1 + \frac{a}{|V|} \right)^{|V|} \leq e^{a}. $$
 As a consequence, we obtain that 
\begin{equation}\label{prodquot}
 \frac{v(U)}{v(V)} 
 = \frac{ \prod_{i=1}^{a} u_{i} }{ \prod_{j=1}^{|V|} \frac{v_{j}}{u_{a + j}} } 
 \geq e^{-a} \prod_{i=1}^{a} u_{i}.
\end{equation}
 The expressions among $u_1$, $u_2$, $\ldots$, $u_a$
 are pairwise unequal and all exceed $1$ (recalling \eqref{2lequleqx}), 
 and thus $u_{i} \geq i + 1$. Consequently, we have that 
\begin{equation}\label{geqfactorial}
 \prod_{i=1}^{a} u_{i} \geq u_{a} \prod_{i=1}^{a-1} (i+1) 
 = a! u_{a} \geq (a-1)! \tau. 
\end{equation}
 Now, observe that 
 the ratio of consecutive terms 
 in the sequence of expressions of the form 
 $(\alpha - 1)! e^{-\alpha}$ for $\alpha \in \mathbb{N}$ is $\alpha/e$, 
 and thus its minimum occurs at $\alpha = 3$. 
 Consequently, the relation 
\begin{equation}\label{fromratio} 
 \frac{(\alpha-1)!}{e^\alpha} \geq \frac{2}{e^3} 
\end{equation}
 holds for positive integers $\alpha$. 

 Now, a combined application of \eqref{taugeqdplusbG}, 
 \eqref{prodquot}, \eqref{geqfactorial}, and \eqref{fromratio} gives us that 
 $ \frac{v(U)}{v(V)} \geq \frac{(a-1)!}{e^a} \tau \geq 
 \frac{2}{e^3} \tau \geq \frac{2}{e^3} (d + G)$. 
 Since $\frac{2}{e^3} > \frac{1}{11}$, we obtain the desired result.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lemmaPn}
 We write $A$ in place of the product-minimal prime set of sum $d > 0$, and we then let $M = v(A)$. Now, let $x > d$ be a prime, and 
 let $y$ be the next prime after $x$. 
 Also, let $G = y - x$, 
 and assume that 
\begin{equation}\label{Gstrict}
 G > 11 M.
\end{equation} 
 Also, let $n = \sigma_x -d$. Then $n$ admits a partition into distinct primes, 
 and its unique maximum-product prime set is
 $\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$, which, moreover, 
 equals $P_n$, for $P_n$ as defined in Section \ref{secintro}.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Each element of $A$ is at most $d < x$. 
 We thus have that $A \subseteq \mathcal{B}_{x}$. 
 So, the difference $\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$ consists of distinct primes
 and is such that 
 $s(\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A) = \sigma_{x} - d = n$. 
 Also observe that $\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$ is nonempty, since $x \not\in A$. 
 By construction, we also have that 
 $ v(\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A) = \frac{T_{x}}{M}$. 
 
 Now, let $E$ denote an arbitrary finite set of primes such that $s(E) = n$. 
 We then write $U = \mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus E$ and $V = E \setminus \mathcal{B}_{x}$. 

 From the above definitions of $U$ and $V$, the set $E$ is obtained from $\mathcal{B}_{x}$
 by removing the elements of $U$ and adjoining the elements of $V$, i.e., so that 
\begin{equation}\label{sEequals}
 s(E) = s(\mathcal{B}_{x}) - s(U) + s(V) = \sigma_{x} - s(U) + s(V). 
\end{equation}
 Also, from the choice of $E$ and the definition of $n$, we   find that   $s(E) = n = \sigma_{x} - d$. This and \eqref{sEequals}    together give  
  us that 
\begin{equation}\label{complementsumidentity}
 s(U) - s(V) = d. 
\end{equation} 
 Our construction also gives that 
\begin{equation}\label{vEandTx}
 v(E) = T_x \frac{v(V)}{v(U)}. 
\end{equation}
 This leads us toward the following case analysis. 

 \ 

\noindent {\bf Case 1:} First suppose that $V = \varnothing$. From \eqref{complementsumidentity}, we obtain that $s(U) = d$. Now, since 
 $A$ is product-minimal  for prime sets summing to $d$, we 
 find that $v(U) \geq M$. So, from \eqref{vEandTx}, we find that 
\begin{equation}\label{vEleq} 
 v(E) \leq \frac{T_{x}}{M}. 
\end{equation}
 Equality in \eqref{vEleq} holds if and only if $v(U) = M = v(A)$. By the Fundamental Theorem
 of Arithmetic, this is equivalent to $U = A$. Since $V = \varnothing$, 
 it follows that equality holds if and only if $E = \mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$. 

 \ 

\noindent {\bf Case 2:} Now, suppose that $V$ is nonempty. Observe that each element of $U$ is in $[2, x]$. 
 Each element of $V$ is a prime greater than $x$, and is thus at least
 $y = x + G$. Using Lemma \ref{eleminequality}
 and \eqref{complementsumidentity}, we obtain that 
\begin{equation}\label{justifylast}
 \frac{v(U)}{v(V)} > \frac{d+G}{11} > M, 
\end{equation}
 where the last inequality in \eqref{justifylast} is implied by 
 \eqref{Gstrict}. From \eqref{vEandTx}, we find that 
 $ v(E) < \frac{T_{x}}{M}$. 

 \ 

 So, if $E$ contains a prime greater than $x$, then Case 2 gives $ v(E) < \frac{T_{x}}{M}$, and, if each element of $E$ is at most $x$, 
 then Case 1 gives $ v(E) \leq \frac{T_{x}}{M}$, 
 with equality if and only if $E = \mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$. 
 Since $E$ was an arbitrary finite set of primes whose elements sum to $n$ 
 these two cases show that 
 $ v(E) \leq v(\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A) = \frac{T_{x}}{M} $ 
 for each such $E$, with equality only when $E = \mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$. 
 As above, the elements of $\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$ sum to $n$. So, this set uniquely attains
 the maximum product, and, hence, by the definition of $P_{n}$, 
 we have that $P_n = \mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$, as desired.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
For each positive integer $r$, there exist infinitely many positive
 integers $n$ admitting partitions into distinct primes for which 
 $L(n) - K(n) \geq r$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Fix a positive integer $r$, and define $t = r + 3$. By Lemma \ref{lemmatd}, we are permitted to choose an odd integer $d$, sufficiently 
 large so that Lemma \ref{lemVinogradov} is applicable, such that the product-minimal prime set $A$ of $d$ has precisely $t$ elements, 
 i.e., so that $s(A) = d$ and $|A| = t$. We then define $M$ so that $M = v(A)$. 
 According to Lemma \ref{lemVinogradov}, there exists a set $C$ consisting
 of three distinct primes satisfying 
 $s(C) = d$ and $|C| = 3$. 
 We henceforth let $d$ and $A$ and $C$ be fixed. 

 Now, we proceed to apply 
 the Sierpi\'nski Lemma, as formulated in Lemma \ref{qHlemma}, letting $H = 11 M$. 
 So, there are infinitely many primes $x$ exceeding $d$ for which $x$ is the unique 
 prime in 
 $ [x - 11M, x + 11M]$. 
 So, for each such $x$, if $y$ is the next prime, then
 $ y - x > 11 M$. 
 We then set $n = \sigma_{x} - d$, with $m = |\mathcal{B}_{x}|$. 
 By Lemma \ref{lemmaPn}, we obtain that 
 $ P_n = \mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus A$. 
 Consequently, the equalities among
\begin{equation}\label{Pnmminust} 
 |P_n| = |\mathcal{B}_{x}| - |A| = m - t 
\end{equation} 
 hold. Each prime in $C$ is at most $d < x$, so that 
 $ C \subseteq \mathcal{B}_{x}$. 
 As a consequence, the prime set
 $ \mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus C $
 is such that 
 $ s(\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus C) = \sigma_{x} - s(C) = \sigma_{x} - d = n $
 and is such that 
 $ |\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus C| = m - 3$. 

 Now, recall the definitions of $S_n$ and $P_n$ 
 given in Section \ref{secintro}. By definition, 
 the cardinality of $S_n$ is at least that of every finite set of primes whose elements
 sum to $n$, 
 with 
\begin{equation}\label{cardSngeq} 
 |S_{n}| \geq |\mathcal{B}_{x} \setminus C| = m - 3. 
\end{equation}
 A combined application of \eqref{Pnmminust} and \eqref{cardSngeq}
 then give us that 
\begin{align*}
 L(n) - K(n) & = |S_n| - |P_n| \\ 
 & \geq (m - 3) - (m - t) \\ 
 & = t - 3 \\
 & = r.
\end{align*}

 There are infinitely many eligible primes $x$. 
 Also, recall that 
 $$ \sigma_{x} = s(\mathcal{B}_{x}) = \sum_{\substack{p \leq x \\ \text{$p$ prime}}} p.$$
 For eligible primes $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that $x_1 < x_2$, 
 we have that 
\begin{equation}\label{substackdiffer} 
 \sigma_{x_2} - \sigma_{x_1} = \sum_{\substack{x_1 < p \leq x_2 \\ \text{$p$ prime}}} p. 
\end{equation}
 Since the right-hand side of 
 \eqref{substackdiffer} containsat least the positive term $p = x_2$, since $x_2$ is prime,
 we find that $\sigma_{x_{2}} - \sigma_{x_{1}} \geq x_2 > 0$. 
 So, as $x$ increases through distinct primes, 
 the value $\sigma_x$ strictly increases. 
 So, since $d$ is fixed, the integers of the form $n = \sigma_x - d$ are distinct (as $x$ increases through distinct primes). 
 So, for $r$ as given above, infinitely many positive integers $n$ are such that 
 $L(n) - K(n) \geq r$. Since $r$ was arbitrary, we obtain the desired result.
