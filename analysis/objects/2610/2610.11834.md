<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Since signals are normalized to the unit sphere
\(\mathbb S^{n-1}\),
we measure signal distance by the normalized geodesic distance
$\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x_1,x_2):=\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\langle x_1,x_2\rangle$, where $x_1,x_2\in\mathbb S^{n-1}$.
For binary observations \(u,v\in\{-1,+1\}^m\), we measure their discrepancy by the normalized Hamming distance \(\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(u,v):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \mathbf 1\{u_i\neq v_i\}\).
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
For \(x_0\in\mathbb S^{n-1}\) and \(0<\eta\le 1\), define the geodesic $\eta$-cap
$\mathcal B(x_0,\eta):=\{x\in\mathbb S^{n-1}: \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x,x_0)\le \eta\}$. For a distribution \(R\) on \(\mathbb S^{n-1}\) and \(\delta\in[0,1)\), the
\((\eta,\delta)\)-approximate covering number is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R):=\min\left\{k\in \mathbb N:R\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^k \mathcal B(x_i,\eta)\right)\ge 1-\delta,
\ x_i\in\mathbb S^{n-1}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be probability distributions on \(\mathbb S^{n-1}\), and let
\(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) denote the set of all couplings of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\).
For \(p\ge 1\), the geodesic Wasserstein-\(p\) distance between \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal W_{p,\mathrm{geo}}(\mu,\nu)
:=
\left(
\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}
\int_{\mathbb S^{n-1}\times\mathbb S^{n-1}}
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x,y)^p\, d\gamma(x,y)
\right)^{1/p}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding geodesic Wasserstein-\(\infty\) distance is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(\mu,\nu)
:=
\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}
\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{(x,y)\sim\gamma}
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x,y).
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\label{separation_gap}
For the noisy Gaussian one-bit model with noise level \(\sigma\), let \(f_\sigma(w)=\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\!\left(\frac{\cos(\pi w)}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}}\right)\), \(w\in[0,1]\). For \(u\in(0,1)\) and \(s\in(1,1/u)\), the one-bit separation gap is defined by \(\Delta_\sigma(s,u):=f_\sigma(su)-f_\sigma(u)\). Since \(s>1\), we have \(su>u\), by the monotonicity of
\(f_\sigma\), \(\Delta_\sigma(s,u)\ge 0\).
<!-- end definition-4 -->
<!-- definition-5: body_md -->
Let $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and let $a_i^\top$ denote the $i$-th row of $A$. We define
$\|A\|_{2,\infty}:=\max_{i\in[m]}\|a_i\|_2.$
<!-- end definition-5 -->
<!-- definition-6: body_md -->
Under the one-bit probit model, the conditional likelihood of observing 
$y\in\{-1,+1\}^m$ given $x$ and $A$ is defined as
$\Pr(y| x,A):=\prod_{i=1}^m\Phi\left(\frac{y_i a_i^\top x}{\sigma}\right)$, where \(\Phi\) is the standard Gaussian cumulative distribution function and \(\sigma>0\) is the noise level.
<!-- end definition-6 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:main}
Let \(R\) and \(P\) be two probability distributions on the unit sphere \(\mathbb S^{n-1}\).\footnote{\(R\) is regarded as the true signal distribution and \(P\) is regarded as learned distribution corresponding to the generative model.} Let \(p\ge 1\), \(0<\eta<1\), \(0<\delta<1/4\), and assume 
$\mathcal W_{p,\mathrm{geo}}(R,P)\le \rho $. Define the effective mismatch level
$\varepsilon_\delta:=\rho/\delta^{1/p}$,
and define the parameter $
\bar\eta
:=
\eta+\varepsilon_\delta $.
We take $c\in\left(1,\frac{1-\varepsilon_\delta}{\bar\eta}-1\right)$, assume further that \(2\eta+3\varepsilon_\delta<1\), which guarantees that

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The condition $\frac{r_{\varepsilon_\delta}^2}{\sigma^2}\le\frac{1}{6}\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2$
requires the model mismatch to be small compared with the effective noise level and the one-bit near--far separation gap. Since $
r_{\varepsilon_\delta}=2\sin\left(\frac{\pi\varepsilon_\delta}{2}\right),\varepsilon_\delta=\frac{\rho}{\delta^{1/p}},$
for small \(\varepsilon_\delta\) this condition is equivalent up to constants to $\rho\lesssim\delta^{1/p}\sigma\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)$.
Thus, the learned sampling prior \(P\) must be sufficiently close to the true prior \(R\). If the mismatch is too large, the main probability masses of \(R\) and \(P\) may be separated on the sphere, so even after Gaussian smoothing their induced one-bit observation distributions may not overlap enough. Consequently, the posterior induced by \(P\) may place little mass near the true signal \(x^*\sim R\), causing the stability guarantee for posterior sampling to fail. Moreover, extensive studies have shown the generated distribution \(P\) can be close to the true distribution \(R\) under appropriate conditions~\citep{chen2023sampling,lee2023convergence,gao2025wasserstein,lu2020universal,gao2023approximating}.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
Compared with the linear posterior sampling upper bound~\citep{jalal2021instance}, the one-bit setting contains an additional factor
\(\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^{-2}\). This is due to the one-bit observation process, which preserves only sign information and discards the amplitude information available in linear measurements. Consequently, the geometric separation between two covering caps is not directly reflected in the observation space, but can only be detected through the difference between their induced expected Hamming disagreements. This difference is precisely
$\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)=f_\sigma(c\bar\eta)-f_\sigma(\bar\eta)$.
To reliably distinguish the covering caps, the empirical Hamming distance must concentrate at the scale of \(\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)\), which requires
$m\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2\gtrsim \log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)$.
Therefore, the sample complexity naturally contains the factor
$\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^{-2}$.
This term reflects the intrinsic loss of information caused by one-bit quantization and noise.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{hammingdistance}
    Consider two signals $x_1,x_2\in\mathbb S^{n-1}$ defined on the unit Euclidean sphere. Define the observations of these two signals as $u=\operatorname{sign}(Ax_1+\xi)$ and $v=\operatorname{sign}(Ax_2)$. We have:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(u,v)] = \frac{1}{\pi} \arccos\left(\frac{\cos(\pi \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x_1,x_2))}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}}\right).
\end{equation}

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:near_far_test}
    Fix a reference point \(x_0\in\mathbb S^{n-1}\). Let the testing threshold be $\tau_{\eta,c,\sigma}:=\frac{f_\sigma(\eta)+f_\sigma(c\eta)}{2}$,
and define the test function
$\phi_{x_0,\eta}(y;A):=
\mathbf 1\left\{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(y,\operatorname{sign}(Ax_0)\right)\ge\tau_{\eta,c,\sigma}\right\}$.
Then the following two testing error bounds hold in the annealed sense:
\begin{equation}
    \sup_{x:\ \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x,x_0)\le \eta}
\Pr_{A,\xi| x}
\left(\phi_{x_0,\eta}(y;A)=1\right)
\le
\exp\left(-\frac{m}{2}\Delta_\sigma(c,\eta)^2\right),
\end{equation}

and
\begin{equation}
\sup_{x:\ \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x,x_0)\ge c\eta}\Pr_{A,\xi| x}
\left(\phi_{x_0,\eta}(y;A)=0\right)
\le
\exp\left(-\frac{m}{2}\Delta_\sigma(c,\eta)^2\right).
\end{equation}
Here the probability is taken with respect to the joint randomness of the measurement matrix \(A\) and the noise \(\xi\), conditional on the fixed reference point \(x\). Hence the result is annealed, in the sense that the probability is averaged over the measurement matrix \(A\), rather than a conditional in \(A\) bound for a fixed realization of \(A\).

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->

When $\sigma=0$, we have $f_0(w)=w$, so ~\Cref{hammingdistance} reduces to the classical random-hyperplane identity $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(\operatorname{sign}(Ax_1),\operatorname{sign}(Ax_2))]=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x_1,x_2)$, with $\Delta_0(c,\eta)=(c-1)\eta$, while ~\Cref{lem:near_far_test} yields concentration of the empirical Hamming distance and hence a near--far separation guarantee. <!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:one_bit_sphere_lower_bound}
Let $R$ be a prior distribution defined on the high dimensional unit Euclidean sphere $\mathbb{S}^{n-1}$, where $x^* \sim R$. Define the recovery accuracy \(\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{3})\), suppose that $\delta<0.1$ and that there exists a recovery algorithm capable of outputting $\widehat{x} \in \mathbb{S}^{n-1}$ based solely on the observation $y$ and the matrix $A$, such that:
\begin{equation}
    \Pr\left(d_{S}(\widehat{x}, x^*) \le \eta\right) \ge 1-\delta.
\end{equation}
If $A$ is a deterministic matrix, then the number of measurements $m$ must satisfy:
\begin{equation}
    m \ge \frac{0.072\pi\sigma^2}{2(\log 2)\|A\|_{2,\infty}^2} \left( \log \mathrm{Cov}_{3\eta, 4\delta}(R) + \log(6\delta)-25 \right).
\end{equation}
If $A_{ij}\sim \mathcal N(0,1)$, then the number of measurements $m$ must satisfy:
\begin{equation}
    m \ge \frac{0.072\pi}{2(\log 2)\arcsin\left(\frac{1}{1+\sigma^2}\right)} \left( \log \mathrm{Cov}_{3\eta, 4\delta}(R) + \log(6\delta)-25\right).
\end{equation}

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
We now compare the upper bound in \Cref{cor:main} with the lower bound in
\Cref{thm:one_bit_sphere_lower_bound}. Then \Cref{cor:main} shows that when
$m\gtrsim\frac{\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)}{\Delta_\sigma(c,\eta)^2}$, posterior sampling achieves accurate recovery with high probability. In contrast, \Cref{thm:one_bit_sphere_lower_bound} shows that any estimator
that achieves \(O(\eta)\) accurate recovery with constant success probability
must satisfy
$m\gtrsim\log \mathrm{Cov}_{3\eta,4\delta}(R)$,
where the constants may depend on the one-bit channel and the noise level.

Therefore, the two bounds nearly match in the leading term $\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)$ depending on the prior $R$.
This shows that the approximate covering number is the correct distributional
complexity measure for one-bit compressed sensing with arbitrary data priors.
In particular, the complexity term appearing in the posterior sampling upper
bound generally cannot be removed or substantially improved.

The remaining difference is mainly the factor
\(\Delta_\sigma(c,\eta)^{-2}\) in the upper bound. This factor comes from
the one-bit observation process. Since the measurements only retain the signs,
two signals separated on the sphere can be distinguished only through the gap
between their expected Hamming distances. This gap is exactly measured by
\(\Delta_\sigma(c,\eta)\). If $c$ is chosen as a fixed numerical constant, for instance $c=2$, then in the small-noise regime, we have $\Delta_\sigma(c,\eta) \asymp \eta$. Therefore, the upper bound requires sufficiently many measurements for this Hamming-distance gap to concentrate.
Thus, the upper and lower bounds are nearly matched in terms of \(\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)\).

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
For the lower bound, we explicitly consider arbitrary fixed deterministic sensing matrices and i.i.d. Gaussian random matrices, which already cover many commonly used sensing settings. In particular, since the deterministic result holds for any fixed \(A\), it also allows \(A\) to be optimized using knowledge of the prior distribution \(R\), provided that \(A\) is independent of the realized signal \(x^\ast\). In such settings, the sensing matrix may depend on additional randomness or side information correlated with the realized signal, in which case the independence argument used in ~\Cref{lemma:4.3} no longer applies directly and a separate information-theoretic analysis is required.

<!-- head-1: body_md -->
The proof consists of four steps. First, using the rotational invariance of Gaussian measurements, we show that the expected Hamming distance between one-bit observations is determined only by the geodesic distance between the underlying signals. Second, based on this relation, we establish a one-bit near--far test to quantify the separability between nearby and faraway signals in the one-bit observation space, and prove that its testing error decays exponentially with the number of measurements $m$. Third, we construct an approximate geodesic cover for the high probability mass of the signal distribution, and apply the above test together with a union bound over all covering centers, thereby obtaining the posterior sampling recovery guarantee in the matched-prior setting. Fourth, to handle model mismatch between the true distribution and the learned distribution, we use a geodesic Wasserstein truncation argument to decompose the two distributions into high probability regular components and small residual components, the regular components are close in $W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}$ and can be controlled by a discrete distribution whose support size is at most $e^k$. Finally, combining this with a R\'enyi stability lemma transfers the matched-prior recovery guarantee to the mismatched prior setting, while the residual components contribute only an additional failure probability of $2\delta$.

By assumption, \(\mathcal W_{p,\mathrm{geo}}(R,P)\le \rho\),
and we also have
\begin{equation}
\min\{
\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(P),
\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)
\}
\le \log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R).
\end{equation}
By \(\Cref{lem:cov_lemma}\), there exist probability distributions
\(R'\), \(R''\), \(P'\), \(P''\), and a finite discrete distribution \(Q\), such that
\begin{equation}
|\operatorname{supp}(Q)|\le \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:good_winfty_mismatch_cn}
\mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(R',P')
\le
\frac{\rho}{\delta^{1/p}}
=
\varepsilon_\delta .
\end{equation}
Moreover, \(R\) and \(P\) can be decomposed as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mixture_decomp_cn}
R=(1-2\delta)R' + 2\delta R'',
\qquad
P=(1-2\delta)P' + 2\delta P'' .
\end{equation}
In addition, the lemma also guarantees that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:min_q_close_cn}
\min\{
\mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(P',Q),
\mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(R',Q)
\}
\le \eta .
\end{equation}

This step converts the global \(W_p\) mismatch into a
\(W_\infty\) mismatch on the good parts. The residual components \(R''\) and \(P''\)
may be arbitrary distributions, but their weights in the original distributions are at most \(2\delta\).


We next prove that \(P'\) can be fully covered by at most \(\mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)\) spherical caps of radius \(\bar\eta\).

By Eq.~\eqref{eq:min_q_close_cn}, there are two cases.

The first case is \(\mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(P',Q)\le \eta\).


By the definition of the \(W_\infty\) distance, there exists a coupling
\(\gamma\in\Pi(P',Q)\) such that
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x,q)\le \eta
    \qquad \gamma\text{-a.s.}
\end{equation}
Since \(Q\) is a finite discrete distribution and the second marginal of \(\gamma\) is \(Q\),
we have
\begin{equation}
    \gamma\left(
    \left\{(x,q): q\in \operatorname{supp}(Q),
    \ \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x,q)\le \eta \right\}
    \right)=1 .
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    P'\left(
    \bigcup_{q\in\operatorname{supp}(Q)}
    \mathcal B(q,\eta)
    \right)=1 .
\end{equation}
By the definition of the \((\eta,0)\)-covering number, it follows that
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}_{\eta,0}(P')
    \le |\operatorname{supp}(Q)|
    \le \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R) .
\end{equation}
Moreover, since \(\bar\eta\ge \eta\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}_{\bar\eta,0}(P')
    \le
    \operatorname{Cov}_{\eta,0}(P')
    \le \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R) .
\end{equation}

The second case is \(\mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(R',Q)\le \eta\).


Combining this with Eq.~\eqref{eq:good_winfty_mismatch_cn} and using the triangle inequality for
the \(W_\infty\) distance, we have
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(P',Q)
    \le
    \mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(P',R')
    +
    \mathcal W_{\infty,\mathrm{geo}}(R',Q)
    \le
    \varepsilon_\delta+\eta
    =
    \bar\eta .
\end{equation}
Thus, there exists a coupling
\(\tilde\gamma\in\Pi(P',Q)\) such that
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x,q)\le \bar\eta
    \qquad \tilde\gamma\text{-a.s.}
\end{equation}
Similarly, we obtain
\begin{equation}
    P'\left(
    \bigcup_{q\in\operatorname{supp}(Q)}
    \mathcal B(q,\bar\eta)
    \right)=1 .
\end{equation}
Hence,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}_{\bar\eta,0}(P')
    \le |\operatorname{supp}(Q)|
    \le \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R) .
\end{equation}

Combining the two cases, we have
\begin{equation}
\label{cov_pandR}
    \operatorname{Cov}_{\bar\eta,0}(P')\le \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R) .
\end{equation}
Moreover, defining
\begin{equation}
    G_P:=
    \bigcup_{q\in\operatorname{supp}(Q)}
    \mathcal B(q,\bar\eta),
\end{equation}
the previous argument shows that
\begin{equation}
    P'(G_P)=1.
\end{equation}
Since \(P=(1-2\delta)P'+2\delta P''\), it follows that
\begin{equation}
    P(G_P)\ge 1-2\delta .
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}_{\bar\eta,2\delta}(P)\le \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R) .
\end{equation}


Consider the following matched good-component experiment:
\begin{equation}
z^*\sim P',
\qquad
u=\operatorname{sign}(Az^*+\xi'),
\end{equation}
where \(\xi'\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I_m)\), and \(\xi'\) is independent of all other random variables.

\(
\widehat z
\sim
P(\cdot| u,A)
\)
be the posterior sampling estimator based on the full learned prior distribution\(P\).

By Eq.~\eqref{cov_pandR}, \(P'\) can be fully covered by at most \(\mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)\)
spherical caps of radius \(\bar\eta\). Moreover, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:mixture_decomp_cn},
\[
P=(1-2\delta)P' + 2\delta P'' .
\]
Therefore, applying \(\Cref{lemma:orup}\) with full prior \(P\), good component \(P'\),
bad component \(P''\), mixture parameter \(\alpha=2\delta\), covering radius
\(\bar\eta\), we get \(N=|\operatorname{supp}(Q)|\le \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)\).Then we obtain

Let $\Delta:=\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta),r:=r_{\varepsilon_\delta}$.
By the definition of the one-bit separation gap, we have
\(\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)=f_\sigma(c\bar\eta)-f_\sigma(\bar\eta)\).
Since \(c>1\), and \(f_\sigma\) is monotonically increasing on the corresponding interval, it follows that \(\Delta>0\).
Moreover, since the range of \(f_\sigma\) is contained in \([0,1]\), we have
\[
    0<\Delta\le 1.
\]

Set
\begin{equation}
    \beta
    :=
    \exp\left(
    -\frac{m}{48}\Delta^2
    \right).
\end{equation}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:main}, for any parameter
\(0<\lambda\le
\min\left\{
1,\,
\frac{\sigma^2}{3r^2}
\right\}\),
with probability at least \(1-\beta\) over the draw of \(A\), we have
\begin{align}
&
\Pr_{x^*\sim R,\xi,\widehat{x}| A}
\left(
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x^*,\widehat x)
\ge
(c+1)\bar\eta+\varepsilon_\delta
\right)
\nonumber,\\
&\qquad\le
2\delta
+
\frac{1}{\beta}
\left(
1-
\frac{\lambda(1+\lambda)r^2}{\sigma^2}
\right)^{-\frac{m}{2(1+\lambda)}}
\exp\left(
\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}
\left(
\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log{\frac{1}{1-2\delta}}-\frac m2\Delta^2
\right)
\right)
\nonumber,\\
&\qquad\le
2\delta
+
\frac{1}{\beta}
\left(
1-
\frac{\lambda(1+\lambda)r^2}{\sigma^2}
\right)^{-\frac{m}{2(1+\lambda)}}
\exp\left(
\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}
\left(
\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2-\frac m2\Delta^2
\right)
\right),
\label{eq:cor_proof_start_cn}
\end{align}
where the last step uses \(\delta<1/4\), and hence
\(\log\frac{1}{1-2\delta}\le \log2\).

We now take \(\lambda=1\).
First, we verify that this choice is admissible. By the small model-mismatch assumption in the corollary, we have
\begin{equation}
    \frac{r^2}{\sigma^2}
    \le
    \frac{1}{6}\Delta^2
    \le
    \frac16.
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
    1
    \le
    \min\left\{
    1,\,
    \frac{\sigma^2}{3r^2}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}
Hence \(\lambda=1\) lies in the range allowed by the main theorem. Substituting \(\lambda=1\) into \eqref{eq:cor_proof_start_cn} yields
\begin{align}
&
\Pr_{x^*\sim R,\xi,\widehat{x}| A}
\left(
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x^*,\widehat x)
\ge
(c+1)\bar\eta+\varepsilon_\delta
\right)
\nonumber\\
&\qquad\le
2\delta
+
\frac{1}{\beta}
\left(
1-\frac{2r^2}{\sigma^2}
\right)^{-m/4}
\exp\left(
\frac12 (\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2)-\frac m4\Delta^2
\right).
\label{eq:lambda_one_bound_cn}
\end{align}

Next, we control the multiplicative factor induced by the model mismatch. Let
\(u:=\frac{2r^2}{\sigma^2}\).
By the assumption,
\begin{equation}
    0\le u\le \frac{\Delta^2}{3}\le \frac13.
\end{equation}
Using the elementary inequality
\begin{equation}
    -\log(1-u)\le \frac{u}{1-u}\le \frac32 u,
    \qquad 0\le u\le \frac13,
\end{equation}
we obtain
\begin{equation}
    -\log\left(1-\frac{2r^2}{\sigma^2}\right)
    \le
    3\frac{r^2}{\sigma^2}.
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:prefactor_control_cn}
\left(
1-\frac{2r^2}{\sigma^2}
\right)^{-m/4}
&=
\exp\left(
-\frac m4
\log\left(
1-\frac{2r^2}{\sigma^2}
\right)
\right)
\le
\exp\left(
\frac{3m}{4}\frac{r^2}{\sigma^2}
\right)\le
\exp\left(
\frac{m}{8}\Delta^2
\right),
\end{align}
where the last step uses
\(\frac{r^2}{\sigma^2}\le
\frac{1}{6}\Delta^2\).

Substituting \eqref{eq:prefactor_control_cn} into \eqref{eq:lambda_one_bound_cn}, we get
\begin{align}
\label{eq.242}
&
\Pr_{x^*\sim R,\xi,\widehat{x}| A}
\left(
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x^*,\widehat x)
\ge
(c+1)\bar\eta+\varepsilon_\delta
\right)
\\
&\qquad\le
2\delta
+
\frac{1}{\beta}
\exp\left(
\frac12 (\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2)-\frac m4\Delta^2+\frac m8\Delta^2
\right)
\\
&\qquad=
2\delta
+
\frac{1}{\beta}
\exp\left(
\frac12 (\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2)-\frac m8\Delta^2
\right).
\end{align}

If, furthermore, \(m\ge \frac{8(\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2)}{\Delta^2}\),
then
\begin{equation}
    \frac12 (\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2)-\frac m8\Delta^2
    \le
    -\frac{m}{16}\Delta^2.
\end{equation}
Together with the choice
\(\beta=\exp\left(-\frac{m}{48}\Delta^2\right)\), this gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\beta}
    \exp\left(
    \frac12 (\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2)-\frac m8\Delta^2
    \right)
    \le
    \exp\left(
    \frac{m}{48}\Delta^2-\frac{m}{16}\Delta^2
    \right)
    =
    \exp\left(
    -\frac{m}{24}\Delta^2
    \right).
\end{equation}
It follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq.242} that, with probability at least
\(1-\exp\left(-\frac{m}{48}\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2\right)\)
over the draw of \(A\),
\begin{equation}
    \Pr_{x^*\sim R,\xi,\widehat{x}| A}
    \left(
    \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x^*,\widehat x)
    \ge
    (c+1)\bar\eta+\varepsilon_\delta
    \right)
    \le
    2\delta
    +
    \exp\left(
    -\frac{m}{24}\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2
    \right).
\end{equation}
This proves the first probability upper bound.

We next prove the order-wise sample complexity statement. To ensure that the second term on the right-hand side and the exceptional probability over \(A\) are no larger than the prescribed
\(\zeta\in(0,1)\), it suffices that
\begin{equation}
    \exp\left(
    -\frac{m}{24}\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2
    \right)
    \le
    \zeta.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, it suffices that
\(m\ge24\frac{\log(1/\zeta)}{\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2}\).
On the other hand, to use the exponential bound proved above, we also need
\(m\ge8\frac{\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2}{\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2}\).
Therefore, it suffices that
\begin{equation}
    m
    \ge
    C
    \frac{\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log(1/\zeta)+\log2}
    {\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2}
\end{equation}
where \(C>0\) is an absolute constant. For example, one may take \(C=48\). Hence, with probability at least \(1-\zeta\) over the draw of \(A\),
\begin{equation}
    \Pr_{x^*\sim R,\xi,\widehat{x}| A}
    \left(
    \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{S}}(x^*,\widehat x)
    \ge
    (c+1)\bar\eta+\varepsilon_\delta
    \right)
    \le
    2\delta+\zeta.
\end{equation}

Finally, we analyze the order of \(\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)\) under different noise levels.
From
\begin{equation}
    f_\sigma(d)
    =
    \frac1\pi
    \arccos\left(
    \frac{\cos(\pi d)}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}}
    \right)
\end{equation}
direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
    f_\sigma'(d)
    =
    \frac{\sin(\pi d)}
    {\sqrt{\sigma^2+\sin^2(\pi d)}}.
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)
    =
    \int_{\bar\eta}^{c\bar\eta}
    \frac{\sin(\pi t)}
    {\sqrt{\sigma^2+\sin^2(\pi t)}}\,dt.
\end{equation}
Since \(c>1\) is fixed and \(\bar\eta=\Theta(\eta)\), the integral is over an interval of length \(\Theta(\eta)\), and \(t=\Theta(\eta)\) throughout the interval. Therefore,
\[
    \sin(\pi t)\asymp t\asymp \eta.
\]
If \(\sigma\lesssim\eta\), then
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\sin(\pi t)}
    {\sqrt{\sigma^2+\sin^2(\pi t)}}
    \asymp
    1,
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)
    \asymp
    \eta.
\end{equation}
If \(\sigma\gtrsim\eta\), then
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\sin(\pi t)}
    {\sqrt{\sigma^2+\sin^2(\pi t)}}
    \asymp
    \frac{\eta}{\sigma},
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)
    \asymp
    \frac{\eta^2}{\sigma}.
\end{equation}
Substituting these estimates into
\(m\ge C\frac{\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log2+\log(1/\zeta)}
{\Delta_\sigma(c,\bar\eta)^2}\)
gives
\begin{equation}
    m=
\begin{cases}
O\!\left((\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log(1/\zeta))\,\eta^{-2}\right),
& \sigma\lesssim \eta,\\[2mm]
O\!\left((\log \mathrm{Cov}_{\eta,\delta}(R)+\log(1/\zeta))\,\sigma^2\eta^{-4}\right),
& \sigma\gtrsim \eta .
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
This completes the proof.

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We introduce two new standard normal random variables $U_1, U_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ that are mutually independent and independent of $Z$.
Then, conditioned on $Z$:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(cZ) = \Pr\left(U_1 \le cZ | Z\right) = \Pr\left(U_1 - cZ \le 0 | Z\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\Phi(cZ)^2 = \Pr\left(U_1 \le cZ, U_2 \le cZ | Z\right) = \Pr\left(U_1 - cZ \le 0, U_2 - cZ \le 0 | Z\right).
\end{equation}
Define the random variables: $W_1 = U_1 - cZ$, $W_2 = U_2 - cZ$. Since $U_1, U_2, Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, $W_1$ and $W_2$ follow a joint zero-mean bivariate normal distribution.
By the additivity of Gaussian distributions, we have
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Var}(W_1) = \mathrm{Var}(U_1) + c^2\mathrm{Var}(Z) = 1 + c^2.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Cov}(W_1, W_2) = \mathbb{E}[(U_1 - cZ)(U_2 - cZ)] = c^2 \mathbb{E}[Z^2] = c^2.
\end{equation}
    Therefore, the correlation coefficient between $W_1$ and $W_2$ is:
\begin{align}
    \rho &= \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(W_1, W_2)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(W_1)\mathrm{Var}(W_2)}},\\
\label{Eq.26}
    &= \frac{c^2}{1+c^2}.
\end{align}
For joint bivariate normal variables with mean $0$ and correlation coefficient $\rho$, the probability that both are simultaneously less than or equal to $0$ is strictly given by the orthant probability formula for the standard bivariate normal distribution~\cite{o2014analysis}:
\begin{equation}
\label{Eq.22}
    \Pr\left(W_1 \le 0, W_2 \le 0\right) = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2\pi} \arcsin(\rho).
\end{equation}
Combining the above equations, we have:
\begin{align}
     \mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\left(\Phi(cZ) - \frac{1}{2}\right)^2\right] &= \mathbb{E}_{Z}[\Phi(cZ)^2] - \mathbb{E}_{Z}[\Phi(cZ)] + \frac{1}{4},\\
     &=\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2\pi} \arcsin(\rho)-\frac{1}{2}+ \frac{1}{4},\\
     &=\frac{1}{2\pi} \arcsin(\frac{c^2}{1+c^2}).
\end{align}
