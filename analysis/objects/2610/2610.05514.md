<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{normalization}
Let $D=C(\lambda,a,b)$ be a purely secant circle to $B$. Then under the full automorphism group fixing $\{0,\infty\}$, $D$ is conjugate to one of the circles $C(1,1,b)$ for some $b \in GF(q)$ with $b(b+1)$ a non-zero square in $GF(q)$, or to $C(0,a,b)$ for some $a,b \in GF(q)\setminus\{0\}$. Conversely, every circle of one of these two forms is purely secant to $B$.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
If $\lambda = 0$, then $D=C(0,a,b)$ is already of the claimed form, and note that for any $C=C(\mu,0,0) \in B$, $\Delta(C,D) = -ab\mu^{q+1}$ is non-zero. Hence $D$ meets every circle in $B$ in either 0 or 2 points, and contains neither $0$ nor $\infty$.

Now assume $\lambda \ne 0$. As neither $0$ nor $\infty$ lies on $D$, neither $a$ nor $b$ can be 0. For any non-zero $d \in GF(q^2)$, the mapping $x^{\phi_d} = dx$ is an automorphism of $M(q)$ preserving $0$ and $\infty$, thus leaving the bundle $B$ invariant. Taking $d$ to be $\lambda^q/a$, the image of $D$ under $\phi_d$ is the circle with circle inversion
$$x \rightarrow \frac{\lambda^{q}}{a} \frac{\lambda(ax/\lambda^q)^q+a}{b (ax/\lambda^q)^q - \lambda^q}$$
Noting that $\lambda^{q^2} = \lambda$ and $a^q=a$, we can simplify to obtain
$$x \rightarrow \frac{\lambda^{q}}{a} \frac{ax^q+a}{ab/\lambda x^q- \lambda^q}$$
Cancellation yields
$$x \rightarrow \frac{x^q+1}{ab/\lambda^{q+1} x^q-1}$$ so that $D^{\phi_d} = C(1,1,b')$ for some $b' = \frac{ab}{\lambda^{q+1}}\in GF(q)\setminus\{0\}$.

So we may assume without loss of generality that $D = C(1,1,b)$ for some $b \in GF(q)\setminus\{0\}$. For each $C=C(\mu,0,0)$ in the bundle $B$, we calculate
$$\Delta(D,C) = \frac14(-\mu-\mu^q)^2-(-\mu^{q+1})(-1-b)$$
Writing $u = \mu^{q-1}$, we obtain
$$\Delta(D,C) = \frac14\mu^2(1+u)^2+\mu^2u(-1-b)$$
Factoring leaves
$$\Delta(D,C) = \frac14\mu^2\left[(1+u)^2-4u(1+b)\right]$$
Now this expression is only 0 if the polynomial $(1+u)^2-4u(1+b) = u^2-(4b+2)u +1=h(u)$ is 0. As $h$ has coefficients in $GF(q)$, it definitely has two roots in $GF(q^2)$; the question is if these roots are $(q-1)$-th powers in $GF(q^2)$. The discriminant of $h$ is $16b(b+1)$. We know $b$ is not zero, and $b \ne -1$ since $D$ is a circle and $b=-1$ would cause $C(1,1,b)$ to fail the degeneracy condition $1^{q+1}+1(-1) \ne 0$; hence this discriminant is non-zero.

If $b(b+1)$ is non-square, then $h$ is irreducible over $GF(q)$, which means it has two roots $u_0$ and $u_1$ in $GF(q^2)$, which necessarily satisfy $u_1 = u_0^q$. However, from the constant coefficient of $h$ we know that $u_0u_1 = 1$, whence $u_0^{q+1} = 1$, and there exists $\mu$ such that $\mu^{q-1} = u_0$. This forces $D$ to be tangent to $C(\mu,0,0)$, a contradiction. Thus we must have $b(b+1)$ a non-zero square.

Conversely if $b(b+1)$ is a non-zero square, $C(1,1,b)$ clearly does not contain either $0$ or $\infty$, and the polynomial $h$ has two roots in $GF(q)$. Note that neither root is $1$ or $-1$, as $h(1) = -4b \ne 0$ and $h(-1) = 4(b+1) \ne 0$. However, $1$ and $-1$ are the only $(q-1)$-th powers in $GF(q)$; if $u \in GF(q)$ and $u = \mu^{q-1}$ for some $\mu \in GF(q^2)$, then $1=\mu^{q^2-1} = u^{q+1} = u^2$. Hence $C(1,1,b)$ meets every circle of $B$ in either 0 or 2 points, finishing the proof.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{nest}
Let $D$ be a purely secant circle to $B$. Then the orbit of $D$ under the group $H$ forms a $(q-1)$-nest if and only if $D$ is conjugate to $C(1,1,b)$ for $b \in GF(q)$ with $b(b+1)$ a non-zero square.
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
By Proposition~\ref{normalization}, we may assume without loss of generality that either $D = C(1,1,b)$ for $b \in GF(q)$ with $b(b+1)$ a non-zero square, or $D=C(0,a,c)$ for some $a,c \in GF(q)$, $a,c \ne 0$. As mentioned earlier, the group $H$ leaves each circle in $B$ invariant, and cyclically permutes the points distinct from $0$ and $\infty$ on each circle in $B$. Hence the orbit of any purely secant circle, {\em when considered as a multiset}, will be a set of $q-1$ circles such that every point is contained in either 0 or 2 of the circles of the multiset. So the orbit of $D$ under $H$ will form a nest if and only if the orbit has length $q-1$.

Let $C$ be any circle of $B$ secant to $D$, and let $P$ and $Q$ be the points of $C \cap D$. Suppose there exists non-identity $\psi \in H$ such that $D^\psi = D$, which necessarily maps $\{P,Q\}$ onto $\{P,Q\}$. Since $H$ acts regularly on the points of $C$ distinct from $0$ and $\infty$, the only element of $H$ mapping $P$ to itself is the identity, so we must have $P^\psi = Q$ and $Q^\psi = P$. But then $P^{\psi^2} = P$, which means $\psi^2$ is the identity. As $H$ is cyclic of order $q-1$, the only way we can have a short orbit is if $D$ is fixed by $\psi:x \rightarrow \beta^{i(q+1)} x$ for $i =\frac{q-1}{2}$. Simplifying, we have $\psi:x \rightarrow -x$.

Suppose first $D = C(1,1,b)$ for $b \in GF(q)$ with $b(b+1)$ a non-zero square. Then the image $D^\psi$ is exactly $C(1,-1,-b)$, which is distinct from $D$. On the other hand, if $D=C(0,a,b)$, then the image $D^\psi=D$, and we have a short orbit. Hence the orbit of $D$ under $H$ is a nest if and only if $D$ is of the form $C(1,1,b)$ for $b \in GF(q)$ with $b(b+1)$ a non-zero square.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:replaceable}
Let $D$ be a purely secant circle to $B$ whose orbit under $H$ forms a $(q-1)$-nest. This nest is Bruck replaceable.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By Proposition~\ref{nest}, we may assume without loss of generality that $D = C(1,1,b)$ for $b \in GF(q)$ with $b(b+1)$ a non-zero square. Let $\Psi$ be the Bruck kernel map leaving each line of the regular spread ${\bf S}$ invariant; without loss of generality, we may assume $\Psi$ acts via $\Psi(x,y) = (\beta x,\beta y)$, where $\beta$ is the primitive element of $GF(q^2)$ used to define the group $H$ previously.

From Bruck~\cite{bruck}, the circle $N_1=\{x:x^{q+1} = 1\}$ in $M(q)$ maps to the regulus ${\bf R}=\{{\bf J}(k):k^{q+1} = 1\}$ in ${\bf S}$, and Bruck calculates the opposite regulus to this regulus as ${\bf R}' = \{{\bf J}'(k):k^{q+1} = 1\}$ where ${\bf J}'(k) = \{(x,kx^q):x \in GF(q^2)\}$. Note that there exists non-zero $r \in GF(q^2)$ such that $r^{q+1} = \frac{b+1}{b^2}$ as $b \in GF(q)$ and $b(b+1)$ is non-zero. Thus the mapping $\phi:u \rightarrow \frac1b + ru$ is a non-singular transformation in $Aut(M(q))$, and it is an easy calculation to show that it maps $N_1$ onto $D$. In turn, it is another easy calculation to show that $\Phi:(x,y) \rightarrow (x,\frac xb + ry)$ is a linear transformation of $V$ which maps ${\bf J}(\infty)$ onto itself and ${\bf J}(k)$ onto ${\bf J}(\frac1b + rk)$, placing $\Phi$ in the stabilizer of ${\bf S}$ and specifically in the pre-image of $\phi$ under $\Upsilon$. This allows us to conclude that ${\bf L} = \{(x,\frac xb + rx^q):x \in GF(q^2)\}$ is a line of the opposite regulus of the regulus corresponding to the circle $D$.

We recall that the group $H \subset Aut(M(q))$ is generated by the mapping $\xi:u \rightarrow  \beta^{q+1} u$ in $Aut(M(q))$, so that $\Xi:(x,y) \rightarrow (x,\beta^{q+1}y)$ is a pre-image of $\xi$ under $\Upsilon$. This finally allows us to define our putative nest replacement set as the orbit of ${\bf L}$ under the group $G$ generated by $T=\Psi\Xi$. Note first that $\Psi$ and $\Xi$ commute, so that every element of $G$ can be written as $\Psi^j\Xi^j$ for some integer $j$.

From the actions of $\Psi$ and $\Xi$, it is clear that the orbit of ${\bf L}$ under $G$ is a set of lines of the opposite reguli to the reguli in the nest. For this to be a replacement set, we must have the right number of lines, namely $\frac{1}{2}(q^2-1)$ and they must be pairwise disjoint. Moreover, we must also show that they constitute the union of opposite half-reguli under the square of the Bruck kernel map $\Psi$. Since all of the points contained in lines of the orbit of ${\bf L}$ under $G$ also lie in lines contained in the nest reguli to be removed, all of these lines are clearly disjoint from any lines of the regular spread remaining after replacement.

First we calculate the order of $T$, which acts via $T:(x,y) \rightarrow (\beta x,\beta^{q+2}y)$. Now if $T^n(x,y) = (\beta^nx, \beta^{(q+2)n}y)$ is the identity, then $T^n(1,0) = (\beta^n,0)$, which forces $\beta^n \in GF(q)$ and thus $q+1 | n$. On the other hand $T^n(1,1) = (\beta^n,\beta^{n(q+2)})$, so we must have $n \equiv n(q+2) \pmod {q^2-1}$, or $q^2-1 | n(q+1)$, from which we see $q-1 | n$. As $gcd(q+1,q-1) = 2$, $n$ must be at least $\frac12(q^2-1)$. But as $T^n = \Psi^n \Xi^n$, we see $T^{\frac12(q^2-1)}$ is the identity, since $\Psi$ has order $q+1$ and $\Xi$ order $q-1$.

Now let's examine the orbit of ${\bf L}$. Suppose ${\bf L}^{T^n} = {\bf L}$ for some $n$, which we write as $({\bf L}^{\Psi^n})^{\Xi^n} = {\bf L}$. Since $\Psi$ leaves each regulus of the nest invariant, ${\bf L}^{\Psi^n}$ is opposite to the same regulus of the nest as ${\bf L}$. Now $\Xi$ acts regularly on the reguli of the nest, so if $\Xi^n$ maps ${\bf L}^{\Psi^n}$ onto ${\bf L}$, it must also leave each regulus of the nest invariant, which implies $q-1 | n$, and thus $\Xi^n$ is the identity. Thus we have ${\bf L}^{\Psi^n} = {\bf L}$. $\Psi$ acts regularly on the lines of each opposite regulus to a regulus in the nest, which forces $q+1 | n$. As before, this forces the orbit of ${\bf L}$ to have length $\frac12 (q^2-1)$.

Using the same logic as the previous paragraph, for ${\bf M} = {\bf L}^{T^k}$ with $0 \le k \le q-2$, the other lines in the orbit of ${\bf L}$ that are in the same opposite regulus as ${\bf M}$ are exactly $\{{\bf L}^{T^{k+j(q-1)}}:j \in \{0 \ldots \frac12(q-1)\}\}$. Writing $T^{k+j(q-1)} = \Xi^{k+j(q-1)} \Psi^{k+j(q-1)} = \Xi^k \Psi^{k+j(q-1)}$, we find the lines in the orbit of ${\bf L}$ in the same opposite regulus as ${\bf M}$ are $\{{\bf M}^{\Psi^{j(q-1)}}:j \in \{0 \ldots \frac12(q-1)\}\}$. Now $j(q-1) \equiv q+1-2j \pmod {q+1}$, so as $j$ varies over $\{0 \ldots \frac12(q-1)\}$, we have$\{{\bf M}^{\Psi^{j(q-1)}}:j \in \{0 \ldots \frac12(q-1)\}\} = \{{\bf M}^{\Psi^{(q+1)-2j}}:j \in \{0 \ldots \frac12(q-1)\}\}$, which is exactly the orbit of ${\bf M}$ under the square of the Bruck kernel map $\Psi$.

Finally, we need to show that the lines in the orbit of ${\bf L}$ are pairwise disjoint, for which it suffices to show that ${\bf L}$ and ${\bf L}^{T^n}$ are disjoint for all $0 < n < \frac12(q^2-1)$. Suppose $(e,f) \in {\bf L} \cap {\bf L}^{T^n}$. Then we can write $(e,f) = (u,\frac ub + ru^q)$ for some $u \in GF(q^2)$, as well as $(e,f) = (\beta^nz,\beta^{n(q+2)}(\frac zb + rz^q))$ for some $z \in GF(q^2)$, where neither $u$ nor $z$ is zero. We must have $u = \beta^nz$, and thus $$\frac{\beta^nz}{b} + r(\beta^nz)^q = \beta^{n(q+2)}(\frac zb + rz^q)$$ We may divide both sides by $\beta^n$ and multiply both sides by $b$ as neither quantity is zero to obtain $$z+br\beta^{n(q-1)}z^q = \beta^{n(q+1)}(z+brz^q)$$ Letting $t = \beta^{n(q+1)}$ and $s = \beta^{n(q-1)}$ and collecting like terms in $z$ yields $$(1-t)z = br(t-s)z^q$$ Noting that $t-s = 0$ implies $\beta^{2n}-1 = 0$, which cannot occur as $n <  \frac12(q^2-1)$, we may divide both sides by $z$ and $br(t-s)$ to find $$z^{q-1} = \frac{1-t}{br(t-s)}$$ This also forces $t \neq 1$.

Letting ${\bf N}$ denote the norm function of $GF(q^2)$ over $GF(q)$ and ${\bf Tr}$ the trace function of $GF(q^2)$ over $GF(q)$, we note that ${\bf N}(z^{q-1}) = 1$, so that ${\bf N}(1-t) = {\bf N}(br(t-s))$. We know $b,t \in GF(q)$, so ${\bf N}(1-t) = (1-t)^2$, and ${\bf N}(r) =  \frac{b+1}{b^2}$, and thus we obtain $(1-t)^2 =(b+1)(t-s)^{q+1}$. Expanding and using the facts that $t^q = t$ and ${\bf N}(s) = 1$, we have $(t-1)^2 = (b+1)(t^2-{\bf Tr}(s)t+1)$. We rearrange to separate the $t$ and $s$ terms to get 
$${\bf Tr}(s) -2 = \frac{b(b+1)(t-1)^2}{(b+1)^2t}$$ As $b(b+1)$, $(t-1)^2$ and $(b+1)^2$ are all non-zero squares, we find ${\bf Tr}(s)-2$ has the same quadratic character as $t$ in $GF(q)$. In particular, this implies $s \ne 1$, as ${\bf Tr}(s)-2$ cannot be zero.

Since ${\bf N}(s) = 1$, we have $s^q = s^{-1}$ and we can write ${\bf Tr}(s)-2 = s-2+s^{-1}$. Multiplying and dividing by $s$, which is non-zero, yields ${\bf Tr}(s)-2 = \frac{(s-1)^2}{s}$. To determine the quadratic character of ${\bf Tr}(s)-2$, we can exponentiate by $\frac{q-1}{2}$, where a $1$ indicates ${\bf Tr}(s)-2$ is a non-zero square, and $-1$ indicates a nonsquare. Hence we calculate $$({\bf Tr}(s)-2)^{\frac12(q-1)} = \frac{(s-1)^{q-1}}{s^{\frac12(q-1)}}$$ We can multiply each of the numerator and denominator on the right by $s-1$ as $s \ne 1$, yielding $$({\bf Tr}(s)-2)^{\frac12(q-1)} = \frac{s^q-1}{s^{\frac12(q-1)}(s-1)}$$ Since $s^{q+1} = 1$ we have $s^q = s^{-1}$, and multiplying numerator and denominator by $s$ gives $$({\bf Tr}(s)-2)^{\frac12(q-1)} = \frac{1-s}{s^{\frac12(q+1)}(s-1)} = \frac{-1}{s^{\frac12(q+1)}}$$

Now recall the definitions of $t = \beta^{n(q+1)}$ and $s = \beta^{n(q-1)}$. Since $\beta^{q+1}$ is a primitive element of $GF(q)$, $t$ is a non-zero square or a nonsquare in $GF(q)$ as $n$ is even or odd, respectively. On the other hand $$s^{\frac12(q+1)} = (\beta^{\frac12(q^2-1)})^n = (-1)^n$$ so ${\bf Tr}(s)-2$ has exactly the opposite quadratic character as $t$ in $GF(q)$. This contradiction shows that ${\bf L}$ can have no point in common with any line in its orbit under $G$, which finishes the proof.

Let $D$ be a purely secant circle to $B$ whose orbit under $H$ forms a $(q-1)$-nest $N$. Then there exists a circle $\hat{D}$ whose orbit under $H$ also forms a replaceable $(q-1)$-nest $\hat{N}$ such that every circle in $N$ is disjoint from every circle in $\hat{N}$.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
As in Theorem~\ref{thm:replaceable}, we may assume without loss of generality that $D = C(1,1,b)$ for $b \in GF(q)$ with $b(b+1)$ a non-zero square. Define $\hat{D}$ to be the circle $C(\epsilon,(b+1)\epsilon^2,1)$, where $\epsilon = \beta^{\frac12(q+1)}$. A straightforward calculation shows that the map $\nu:z \rightarrow \frac{-\epsilon}{z}$ leaves $\{0,\infty\}$ invariant, and maps $\hat{D}$ onto $E=C(1,1,-1-b)$. Noting that $(-1-b)(-1-b+1) = b(b+1)$ is a non-zero square, by Theorem~\ref{thm:replaceable} the orbit of $E$ under $H$ is a replaceable $(q-1)$-nest. As $\nu$ normalizes the group $H$ and $\nu = \nu^{-1}$, the orbit of $\hat{D}$ under $H$ is also a replaceable $(q-1)$-nest.

From the proof of Proposition~\ref{normalization}, we know that $D=C(1,1,b)$ is secant to $C=C(\mu,0,0)$ if $\Delta(D,C) = \frac14\mu^2\left[(1+u)^2-4u(1+b)\right]$ is a non-square in $GF(q)$, where $u=\mu^{q-1}$. Applying this to $E=C(1,1,-1-b)$, we find $E$ is secant to $C$ if and only if $\Delta(E,C) = \frac14\mu^2\left[(1+u)^2+4ub\right]$ is a non-square in $GF(q)$. To map this back to $\hat{D}$, we note that $\nu$ leaves $\{0,\infty\}$ invariant, which means it must leave the bundle of circles containing $0$ and $\infty$ invariant, i.e., $\nu$ maps $C(\mu,0,0)$ onto $C(\hat{\mu},0,0)$ for some $\hat{\mu}$. Every point $z$ of $C$ satisfies $z^q = -\mu^{q-1} z$, so every point of $x=z^\nu$ of $C^\nu$ must satisfy $x^q = -\hat{\mu}^{q-1} x$. Substituting $x = -\epsilon/z$ and $z^q=-\mu^{q-1} z$, and recalling that $\epsilon^{q} = -\epsilon$, yields $$\frac{\epsilon}{-\mu^{q-1}z} = \hat{\mu}^{q-1} \frac{\epsilon}{z}$$ from which we can conclude that $\hat{\mu}^{q-1} = -\mu^{1-q}$.

Treating this as a polynomial in $\hat{\mu}$, we see there are at most $q-1$ possible solutions. However, for any $\hat{\mu}$, $f\hat{\mu}$ is also a solution for any non-zero $f \in GF(q)$. Hence all possible values for $\hat{\mu}$ are scalar multiples of each other, and all represent the same circle. Thus we may assume without loss of generality that $\hat{\mu} = \epsilon \mu^{-1}$, as $\epsilon^{q-1} = -1$.

Now $\hat{D}$ and $C(\mu,0,0)$ are secant if and only if $\Delta' = \frac14\hat{\mu}^2\left[(1+\hat{u})^2+4\hat{u}b\right]$ is a non-square in $GF(q)$, where $\hat{u} = \hat{\mu}^{q-1} = -\mu^{1-q}$. We substitute $\hat{u} = -\mu^{1-q} = -u^{-1}$  and $\hat{\mu} = \epsilon \mu^{-1}$ to obtain $$\Delta' = \frac14 \epsilon^2 \mu^{-2}\left[(1-u^{-1})^2-4(u^{-1})b\right]$$ We can factor out $u^{-2}$ from the bracketed expression as $(\mu^{q-1})^{-2}$  to obtain
$$\Delta' = \frac14 \frac{\epsilon^2}{\mu^{2q}}\left[(u-1)^2-4ub\right]$$

Finally, we add and subtract $4u$ in the bracketed expression, and pull out an additional $\mu^2$ in front to get
$$\Delta' = \frac{\epsilon^2}{(\mu^{q+1})^2} \frac14\mu^2\left[(1+u)^2-4u(1+b)\right] = \frac{\epsilon^2}{(\mu^{q+1})^2} \Delta(C,D)$$

Now $\mu^{q+1}$ is a non-zero element of $GF(q)$, so its square is definitely a non-zero square in $GF(q)$. But $\epsilon^2 = \beta^{q+1}$ is a primitive element of $GF(q)$, making it a non-square. Hence $\Delta'$ always has the opposite quadratic character to $\Delta(C,D)$, meaning that the circle $\hat{D}$ is secant to precisely those bundle circles to which $D$ is disjoint. Thus no circle in either nest intersects a circle of the other, proving the result.
