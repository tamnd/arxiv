<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:diagonal}
For topologies $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ on the same set $X$, let $\tau_1\vee\tau_2$ denote their join in the lattice of topologies on $X$. Then the diagonal $\Delta(X)=\{(x, x) : x\in X\}$ of $(X,\tau_1)\times(X,\tau_2)$, with its subspace topology, is homeomorphic to $(X,\tau_1\vee\tau_2)$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $\tau$ be the product topology of $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$. Then $\{U_1\times U_2 : U_1\in \tau_1, U_2\in\tau_2\}$ is a base of $\tau$ and $\{U_1\cap U_2 : U_1\in \tau_1, U_2\in\tau_2\}$ is a base of $\tau_1\vee\tau_2$.

Define
\[
 \varphi:(X,\tau_1\vee\tau_2)\longrightarrow
 (\Delta(X),\tau|_{\Delta(X)}),\quad x\longmapsto(x,x).
\]
Clearly, $\varphi$ is a bijection. For any $W_1\in \tau_1, W_2\in\tau_2$, we have $\varphi^{-1}((W_1\times W_2)\cap \Delta(X))=W_1\cap W_2$ or, equivalently, $\varphi(W_1\cap W_2)=(W_1\times W_2)\cap \Delta(X)$. Hence $\varphi$ is a homeomorphism.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:basic}
The family $\tau_A$ is a compact $T_1$ topology contained in $\tau$. It has the following additional properties.
\begin{enumerate}[\rm (1)]
\item Every subset of $X$ meeting $A$ is compact in $(X,\tau_A)$.
\item The restrictions of $\tau_A$ and $\tau$ to $X\setminus A$ coincide.
\item A subset $K\subseteq X$ is compact in $(X,\tau_A)$ if and only if either $K\cap A\neq\varnothing$, or $K\cap A=\varnothing$ and $K$ is compact in $(X,\tau)$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Since $(X,\tau)$ is $T_1$, every co-finite subset of $X$ is $\tau$-open, so $\tau_A\subseteq\tau$. If a union of members of $\tau_A$ meets $A$, one of its members meets $A$ and is co-finite; hence the union is co-finite. If a finite intersection meets $A$, every factor containing the common point is co-finite, so the intersection is co-finite. If the intersection misses $A$, it is a $\tau$-open set disjoint from $A$. Thus $\tau_A$ is a topology. It contains the co-finite topology and is therefore $T_1$.

Let $K\cap A\neq\varnothing$, and let $\mathcal U$ be a $\tau_A$-open cover of $K$. Choose $a\in K\cap A$ and $U\in\mathcal U$ with $a\in U$. The set $U$ is co-finite, so finitely many further members of $\mathcal U$ cover the remaining points of $K$. This proves (1), and the case $K=X$ proves compactness of $(X,\tau_A)$.

For (2), if $U\in\tau$, then $U\setminus A$ is $\tau$-open because $A$ is closed, and it belongs to $\tau_A$. Since
\[
 U\cap(X\setminus A)=(U\setminus A)\cap(X\setminus A),
\]
the restrictions coincide. Assertion (3) follows from (1) and (2).
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:classification}
Let $(X,\tau)$ be $T_1$, and let $A\subseteq X$ be nonempty and closed. Then
\[
 \operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X,\tau_A)
 =\{\{a\}:a\in A\}
 \cup\{K\in\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X,\tau):K\cap A=\varnothing\}.
 \tag{3.2}
\]
Consequently, $(X,\tau_A)$ is co-sober if and only if $A$ meets every non-singleton member of $\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X,\tau)$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $K\in\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X,\tau_A)$. Suppose first that $K\cap A\neq\varnothing$ and $|K|>1$. Choose $a\in K\cap A$ and $x\in K\setminus\{a\}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}, $K\setminus\{x\}$ is compact because it still meets $A$, while $\{x\}$ is compact. Both sets are saturated because $(X,\tau_A)$ is $T_1$. Thus
\[
 K=\{x\}\cup(K\setminus\{x\})
\]
is a union of two proper members of $Q(X,\tau_A)$, a contradiction. Hence every member of $\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X,\tau_A)$ meeting $A$ is a singleton contained in $A$.

Now suppose that $K\cap A=\varnothing$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}, the subspace topology on $K$, and on every subset of $K$, is the same for $\tau_A$ and $\tau$. Since both spaces are $T_1$, saturation is automatic. Therefore $K$ is compact and $k$-irreducible for $\tau_A$ if and only if it is compact and $k$-irreducible for $\tau$. This proves (3.2), and the final assertion follows from the $T_1$ characterization of co-sobriety.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:preservation}
If $(X,\tau)$ is Fr\'echet--Urysohn, then $(X,\tau_A)$ is Fr\'echet--Urysohn. If $(X,\tau)$ is anti-Hausdorff, then $(X,\tau_A)$ is anti-Hausdorff.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The anti-Hausdorff assertion follows from $\tau_A\subseteq\tau$.

Let $x\in\overline C^{\tau_A}$. If $x\in A$, then either $x\in C$, in which case the constant sequence suffices, or $C$ is infinite; otherwise the co-finite neighborhood $X\setminus C$ of $x$ would miss $C$. In the latter case, choose pairwise distinct points $c_n\in C$. Every $\tau_A$-neighborhood of $x$ is co-finite, so $c_n\to x$.

If $x\notin A$, then for every $\tau$-neighborhood $U$ of $x$, the set $U\setminus A$ is a $\tau_A$-neighborhood of $x$ and therefore meets $C$. Hence
\[
 x\in\overline{C\setminus A}^{\tau}.
\]
The Fr\'echet--Urysohn property of $(X,\tau)$ gives a sequence in $C\setminus A$ converging to $x$ in $\tau$. It also converges in the coarser topology $\tau_A$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:criterion}
Let $(X,\tau)$ be a $T_1$-space with a non-singleton $k$-irreducible compact subset. Suppose that there are disjoint nonempty closed sets $A,B\subseteq X$ such that every non-singleton member of $\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X,\tau)$ meets both $A$ and $B$. Then $(X,\tau_A)$ and $(X,\tau_B)$ are compact $T_1$ co-sober spaces, whereas their product is not co-sober.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Lemma~\ref{lem:basic} and Theorem~\ref{thm:classification} show that both modified spaces are compact, $T_1$ and co-sober.

We claim that
\[
 \tau_A\vee\tau_B=\tau.
 \tag{4.1}
\]
The inclusion from left to right follows from $\tau_A,\tau_B\subseteq\tau$. Conversely, for every $U\in\tau$,
\[
 U=(U\setminus A)\cup(U\setminus B),
\]
because $A\cap B=\varnothing$. Since $A$ and $B$ are closed, $U\setminus A\in\tau_A$ and $U\setminus B\in\tau_B$. This proves (4.1).

Choose a non-singleton $C\in\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X,\tau)$. By Lemma \ref{lem:diagonal},
\[
 \Delta(C)=\{(x,x):x\in C\}
\]
is homeomorphic to $C$, and hence compact. Suppose that $\Delta(C)=K_1\cup K_2$, where $K_1$ and $K_2$ are compact saturated subsets of the product. Since $K_i\subseteq \Delta(C)$, their inverse images under the diagonal homeomorphism are compact subsets of $(X,\tau)$. They are saturated because $(X,\tau)$ is $T_1$. The k-irreducibility of $C$ therefore gives $K_1=\Delta(C)$ or $K_2=\Delta(C)$. Thus $\Delta(C)$ is $k$-irreducible in the product. The product is $T_1$, so $\Delta(C)$ is saturated, and its having more than one point prevents it from being the saturation of a point. Hence the product is not co-sober.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:kv}
There exists a countably infinite compact Fr\'echet--Urysohn anti-Hausdorff KC-space $(M,\tau)$ such that
\[
 \operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(M,\tau)=\{\{x\}:x\in M\}\cup\{M\}.
 \tag{5.1}
\]
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Kov\'a\v{r} \cite[p.~2]{Kovar} considers a structure $(M,\mathcal T)$ in which $\mathcal T$ is closed under finite intersections and nonempty unions but need not contain $\varnothing$. He associates with it the ordinary topology
\[
 \tau=\mathcal T\cup\{\varnothing\}.
\]
Let
\[
 \mathcal C=\{H\subseteq M:M\setminus H\in\mathcal T\}.
\]
The closed sets of $(M,\tau)$ are precisely the members of $\mathcal C$ together with $M$.

In \cite[Example~2.2]{Kovar}, Kov\'a\v{r} constructs $\mathcal C$ from a family $\mathcal A$ and a map $l$ satisfying $l(A)\notin A$. In the proof, for every infinite $H\in\mathcal C$ an infinite subset $D\subseteq H$ is obtained such that $H\setminus\{x\}\in\mathcal C$ for every $x\in D$. Choosing distinct $p,q\in D$, both $H\setminus\{p\}$ and $H\setminus\{q\}$ are proper closed subsets and
\[
 H=(H\setminus\{p\})\cup(H\setminus\{q\}).
\]
Hence every infinite member of $\mathcal C$ is reducible. In the paragraph following the example, he takes $M=\mathbb N$ and an $\omega$-MAD family and records that the associated topology is anti-Hausdorff and Fr\'echet--Urysohn, that convergent sequences have unique limits, and that the topology is maximal compact \cite[Example~2.2 and the following paragraph, p.~6]{Kovar}; see also \cite{vanDouwen}.

Thus $(M,\tau)$ is countably infinite, compact, Fr\'echet--Urysohn, anti-Hausdorff and US, and therefore $T_1$. Every infinite proper closed set belongs to $\mathcal C$ and is reducible. Every finite closed set with at least two points is reducible because the space is $T_1$. The whole space $M$ is irreducible because it is anti-Hausdorff. Consequently,
\[
 \operatorname{Irr}_{c}(M,\tau)=\{\{x\}:x\in M\}\cup\{M\}.
 \tag{5.2}
\]

By Lemma \ref{lem-Frechet-US-KC}, $(M,\tau)$ is a KC-space. Since it is compact, its compact subsets are exactly its closed subsets. For a compact subset of $M$, k-irreducibility is therefore equivalent to irreducibility as a closed set. Saturation is automatic because $M$ is $T_1$; compact pieces are closed by the KC property, and closed pieces are compact because $M$ is compact. Combining this observation with (5.2) gives (5.1).
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:scope}
Equation~(5.1) does not follow merely from compactness, the Fr\'echet--Urysohn and US properties, and anti-Hausdorffness. It uses Kov\'a\v{r}'s description of the proper closed sets in the chosen $\omega$-MAD realization.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:binary}
There exist two countable compact Fr\'echet--Urysohn $T_1$ anti-Hausdorff co-sober spaces whose product is not co-sober.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Take $(M,\tau)$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:kv}, choose distinct points $a,b\in M$, and put $A=\{a\}$ and $B=\{b\}$. The only non-singleton member of $\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(M,\tau)$ is $M$, which meets both $A$ and $B$. Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion} gives compact $T_1$ co-sober spaces
\[
 P=(M,\tau_A),~ Q=(M,\tau_B)
\]
such that $P\times Q$ is not co-sober. They are countable, and Lemma~\ref{lem:preservation} shows that they remain Fr\'echet--Urysohn and anti-Hausdorff.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:core}
The family $\lambda$ is a topology. If $P$ and $Q$ are $T_1$, then $(Z,\lambda)$ is $T_1$ and anti-Hausdorff, and $P$ and $Q$ are closed subspaces with their original topologies. Moreover:
\begin{enumerate}[\rm (1)]
\item A subset $K\subseteq Z$ is compact if and only if $K\cap P$ is compact in $P$ and $K\cap Q$ is compact in $Q$.
\item If $P$ and $Q$ are compact Fr\'echet--Urysohn spaces, then $Z$ is compact and Fr\'echet--Urysohn.
\item If $P$ and $Q$ are $T_1$ co-sober spaces, then $Z$ is co-sober.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Finite intersections and nonempty unions of co-finite subsets of the infinite set $R$ are co-finite. Together with the topology axioms on $P$ and $Q$, this proves that $\lambda$ is a topology.

Assume that $P$ and $Q$ are $T_1$. The complement of $p\in P$ is
\[
 (P\setminus\{p\})\cup Q\cup R,
\]
and is open; the same holds for points of $Q$. For $r\in R$, the complement $P\cup Q\cup(R\setminus\{r\})$ is open. Thus $Z$ is $T_1$. Every nonempty open set contains a co-finite subset of $R$, so any two nonempty open sets intersect. For $U\in\mathcal{O}(P)$, the set $U\cup R$ is open in $Z$ and has trace $U$ on $P$, and similarly for $Q$. Finally, $Z\setminus P=Q\cup R$ and $Z\setminus Q=P\cup R$ are open, so both subspaces are closed.

To prove (1), suppose first that $K$ is compact. If $\mathcal U$ is an open cover of $K\cap P$ in $P$, then
\[
 \{U\cup R:U\in\mathcal U\}\cup\{Q\cup R\}
\]
is an open cover of $K$ in $Z$. A finite subcover shows that $K\cap P$ is compact. The argument for $K\cap Q$ is symmetric.

Conversely, assume that the two traces are compact and let $\mathcal V$ be an open cover of $K$. Finitely many members cover $K\cap P$, and finitely many cover $K\cap Q$. If some member has been chosen, their union contains all but finitely many points of $R$, and finitely many further members cover the remaining points of $K\cap R$. If both traces are empty and $K$ is nonempty, choose one member of $\mathcal V$ meeting $K\subseteq R$; it covers all but finitely many points of $K$, and finitely many additional members complete the cover. The empty set is compact. This proves (1), and compactness in (2) follows by taking $K=Z$.

For the Fr\'echet--Urysohn assertion, let $z\in\overline C^Z$. If $C\cap R$ is infinite, a sequence of distinct points of $C\cap R$ converges to every point of $Z$, because every nonempty neighborhood contains all but finitely many points of $R$. Suppose that $C\cap R$ is finite. If $z\in P$ and $z\notin\overline{C\cap P}^{P}$, choose an open neighborhood $U$ of $z$ in $P$ disjoint from $C\cap P$. Then
\[
 U\cup\bigl(R\setminus(C\cap R)\bigr),
\]
with empty $Q$-part, is an open neighborhood of $z$ in $Z$ disjoint from $C$, a contradiction. Hence $z\in\overline{C\cap P}^{P}$, and the Fr\'echet--Urysohn property of $P$ supplies a sequence in $C\cap P$ converging to $z$. The case $z\in Q$ is symmetric. If $z\in R$ and $z\notin C$, then $R\setminus(C\cap R)$, with empty $P$- and $Q$-parts, is an open neighborhood of $z$ disjoint from $C$. Thus $z\in C$, and the constant sequence suffices. This proves (2).

For (3), let $K$ be a $k$-irreducible compact subset of $Z$. Saturation is automatic because $Z$ is $T_1$. If $K$ meets both $P$ and $Z\setminus P$, then
\[
 K=(K\cap P)\cup(K\setminus P)
\]
is a union of two nonempty proper compact subsets by (1), a contradiction. Repeating the argument with $Q$ shows that $K$ is contained in one of $P$, $Q$ or $R$. If $K\subseteq P$, then (1) and the definition of k-irreducibility show that $K$ is $k$-irreducible in $P$, so it is a singleton. The same holds in $Q$. If $K\subseteq R$ and $|K|>1$, then $K=\{r\}\cup(K\setminus\{r\})$ is a union of two proper compact subsets by (1). Hence every $k$-irreducible compact subset of $Z$ is a singleton, and $Z$ is co-sober.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:square}
There exists a countable compact Fr\'echet--Urysohn $T_1$ anti-Hausdorff co-sober space whose square is not co-sober.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let $(M,\tau)$ and the distinct points $a,b\in M$ be as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:binary}. Put $A=\{a\}$ and $B=\{b\}$. To obtain disjoint copies of the two factors, set
\[
 P=M\times\{0\},\quad Q=M\times\{1\},
\]
and transport the topologies $\tau_A$ and $\tau_B$ to $P$ and $Q$, respectively. Take $R$ countably infinite and disjoint from $P\sqcup Q$, and define $Z$ by (6.1). Lemma~\ref{lem:core} shows that $Z$ has all the asserted properties.

Define
\[
 \Gamma=\{((x,0),(x,1)):x\in M\}\subseteq P\times Q\subseteq Z^2.
\]
By (4.1) and Lemma~\ref{lem:diagonal}, the map
\[
 h:(M,\tau)\longrightarrow\Gamma,\quad
 h(x)=((x,0),(x,1)),
\]
is a homeomorphism. Since $M\in\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(M,\tau)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:kv}, the set $\Gamma$ is compact. If
\[
 \Gamma=K_1\cup K_2
\]
with $K_1,K_2$ compact saturated subsets of $Z^2$, then $K_i\subseteq\Gamma$. Thus $h^{-1}(K_i)$ is compact in $(M,\tau)$; it is also saturated because $(M,\tau)$ is $T_1$. The $k$-irreducibility of $M$ gives $h^{-1}(K_i)=M$ for some $i$, and hence $K_i=\Gamma$. Therefore $\Gamma$ is $k$-irreducible in $Z^2$. Since $Z^2$ is $T_1$ and $\Gamma$ has more than one point, it is not the saturation of a point. Consequently, $Z^2$ is not co-sober.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:powers}
The same witness placed in any two coordinates shows that $Z^I$ is not co-sober whenever $|I|\geq2$; the other coordinates may be fixed at one point of $Z$. This is an immediate extension of Theorem~\ref{thm:square}, not an additional construction. The added core does not preserve the US property: every injective sequence in $R$ converges to every point of $Z$. No assertion is made that $Z^2$ is Fr\'echet--Urysohn.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:nonreflective}
The full subcategory $\mathbf{CoSob}$ is not reflective in $\mathbf{Top}_0$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Let $Z$ be the co-sober space in Theorem~\ref{thm:square}. We show directly that $Z^2$ has no $\mathbf{CoSob}$-reflection. Suppose that $\eta:Z^2\to C$ were such a reflection. Since $Z$ is co-sober, the coordinate projections $\pi_i:Z^2\to Z$ factor through $\eta$: there are continuous maps $p_i:C\to Z$ satisfying $p_i\circ\eta=\pi_i$ for $i=1,2$. The continuous map
\[
 r:C\longrightarrow Z^2,\qquad r(c)=(p_1(c),p_2(c)),
\]
satisfies $r\circ\eta=\operatorname{id}_{Z^2}$. Therefore
\[
 (\eta\circ r)\circ\eta=\eta=\operatorname{id}_C\circ\eta.
\]
By the uniqueness in the reflection property, applied to the map $\eta:Z^2\to C$, we have $\eta\circ r=\operatorname{id}_C$. Thus $\eta$ is a homeomorphism, so $Z^2$ is co-sober because $C$ is co-sober. This contradicts Theorem~\ref{thm:square}. Hence $Z^2$ has no $\mathbf{CoSob}$-reflection, and $\mathbf{CoSob}$ is not reflective.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:kc}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be co-sober spaces. If $X$ is a KC-space, then $X\times Y$ is co-sober.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
A KC-space is $T_1$, since every singleton is compact and hence closed. Let $K\in\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X\times Y)$ and put $A=\pi_X(K)$. The set $A$ is compact. It is also saturated: if $x\in A$ and $x\leq_X x'$, choose $y$ with $(x,y)\in K$. The specialization order of a product is coordinatewise, so $(x',y)\in K$ and $x'\in A$.

Suppose that
\[
 A=A_1\cup A_2,~ A_1,A_2\in Q(X).
\]
Each $A_i$ is closed because $X$ is KC. Hence
\[
 K_i=K\cap(A_i\times Y)
\]
is a closed subset of the compact set $K$, and is therefore compact. It is nonempty because $A_i\subseteq\pi_X(K)$, and it is saturated because both $K$ and $A_i\times Y$ are saturated. Moreover, $K=K_1\cup K_2$. The k-irreducibility of $K$ gives $K=K_i$ for some $i$, and projection gives $A=A_i$. Thus $A\in\operatorname{K\text{-}Irr}(X)$.

By co-sobriety of $X$, $A=\mathord{\uparrow} x$ for some $x\in X$. Since $X$ is $T_1$, $A=\{x\}$. Hence
\[
 K=\{x\}\times L,
~ L=\pi_Y(K).
\]
The set $L$ is compact. It is also saturated: if $y\in L$ and $y\leq_Y y'$, choose $x$ with $(x,y)\in K$. Since the specialization order on $X\times Y$ is coordinatewise and $K$ is saturated, $(x,y')\in K$, so $y'\in L$. The set $L$ is $k$-irreducible, because any decomposition of $L$ into compact saturated subsets would give a corresponding decomposition of $K$ after taking the product with $\{x\}$. Since $Y$ is co-sober, $L=\mathord{\uparrow} y$ for some $y\in Y$. Consequently,
\[
 K=\{x\}\times\mathord{\uparrow} y=\mathord{\uparrow}(x,y).
\]
Thus $X\times Y$ is co-sober.
