\label{def:2}
    Let $G$ be a graph, $S=(s_1,s_2,\ldots)$ a non-decreasing sequence of positive integers and $k$ a positive integer, where $1\le k\le n(G)$. The minimum $t$ for which there exists an $S$-packing $k$-domatic coloring of $G$ using colors in $[t]$ is an \textit{$S$-packing $k$-domatic chromatic number} of $G$, denoted by $\chi_{S,k}(G)$. If there is no such $t\in \mathbb{N}$, we set $\chi_{S,k}(G)=\infty$.

\label{def:3}
Let $G$ be a graph and $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_t)$ a finite non-decreasing sequence of positive integers such that $G$ is $S$-packing colorable. The maximum $k$ for which there exists an $S$-packing $k$-domatic coloring of $G$ is the {\em $S$-packing domatic number} of $G$, denoted ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)$. 
    
If $S=(s_1,s_2,\ldots)$ is an infinite non-decreasing sequence of positive integers, then let ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)=dom_{\chi_{S'}}(G)$, where $S'=(s_1,\ldots,s_{\chi_S(G)})$. That is, we consider the first $\chi_S(G)$ terms in the sequence $S$ using the definition for a finite sequence.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- observation-1: body_md -->
\label{obs:monotonicity}
    If $S$ is a sequence of positive integers and $G$ is a graph with ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)\ge k\ge 2$, then 
$$\chi_{S,k-1}(G)\le \chi_{S,k}(G).$$
<!-- end observation-1 -->
<!-- observation-2: body_md -->
If $S$ is an unbounded non-decreasing sequence of positive integers, then $\chi_{S,k}(G)<\infty$ holds for any connected graph $G$ and any $k\le n(G)$.
<!-- end observation-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $S$ be an unbounded non-decreasing sequence of positive integers, and let $i\in \mathbb{N}$ be the smallest index such that $s_i\ge{\rm diam}(G)$. Note that for any $v\in V(G)$, letting $f(v)=s_i$ and $f(u)=0$ for all $u\ne v$ gives a dominating broadcast of $G$. Thus, assigning pairwise distinct values from $\{s_i,s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{i+n-1}\}$ to the vertices of $G$, where $n=n(G)$, yields an $n$-dominating broadcast of $G$, which is at the same an $S$-packing coloring. Therefore, $$\chi_{S,n}(G)\le i+n(G)-1,$$
and by Observation~\ref{obs:monotonicity}, we infer that $\chi_{S,k}(G)<\infty$ for all $k\le n(G)$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- observation-3: body_md -->
\label{ob:domandchi}
Let $G$ be a graph, $S=(s_1,s_2,\ldots)$ a non-decreasing sequence of positive integers and $k$ a positive integer. Then, $\chi_{S,k}(G)=\chi_S(G)$ if and only if ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)\ge k$.
<!-- end observation-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $t=\chi_{S,k}(G)<\infty$. Thus there is an $S$-packing $k$-domatic coloring $f$ of $G$ using colors in $[t]$. If $t=\chi_S(G)$, we derive that $f$ is an $S$-packing coloring of $G$ using $\chi_S(G)$ colors, which is at the same time a $k$-dominating broadcast. Thus, ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)\ge k$. 

 For the converse, note that since $S$ is infinite, ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)$ is defined as ${\rm dom}_{\chi_{S'}}(G)$, where $S'=(s_1,\ldots,s_{\chi_S(G)})$. Let $r={\rm dom}_{\chi_{S'}}(G)$, and note that $r\ge k$. By definition, there exists an $S$-packing $r$-domatic coloring of $G$ using $\chi_S(G)$ colors, thus $\chi_{S,r}(G)=\chi_S(G)$. By applying Observation~\ref{obs:monotonicity}, we infer that $\chi_{S,k}(G)\le \chi_{S,r}(G)=\chi_S(G)$, which gives the desired equality.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- observation-4: body_md -->
\label{obs:domatic}
If $G$ is a graph with no isolated vertices and $S=(s_1,s_2,\ldots)$ an infinite non-decreasing sequence of positive integers, then $${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)\ge 2.$$

\label{obs:domgen}
If $G$ is a graph with no isolated vertices and $S=(1,1,\ldots)$ is the sequence with all terms equal to $1$, then $${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)=dom(G).$$

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prp:diam2}
If $G$ is a graph with ${\rm diam}(G)=2$ and $S$ is a non-decreasing sequence of positive integers with $s_1=1$ and $s_2\ge 2$, then ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)=n(G)-\alpha(G)+1=\beta(G)+1$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
It is easy to see that $\chi_S(G)=n(G)-\alpha(G)+1$. Let $f$ be the corresponding $S$-packing coloring. Now, consider the partition $\pi=\{D_1,\ldots,D_{n-\alpha(G)+1}\}$ of $V(G)$, where $D_1$ is a maximum independent set of $G$ and $D_i$, where $i\ge 2$, is a singleton. Clearly, every set $D_i$ $f$-dominates $G$, hence $f$ is a packing $(n-\alpha+1)$-domatic coloring, thus ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)\ge n-\alpha+1$. It is easy to see that ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)$ cannot be bigger than $n(G)-\alpha(G)+1$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:paths_lower}
    If $k \geq 3$ and $n \geq 3$, then $\chi_{\rho, k}(P_n) \geq k$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $k \geq 3$ and $n \geq 3$ be arbitrary integers. Suppose that $\chi_{\rho, k}(P_n) \leq k-1$ and denote by $f$ a packing $k$-domatic coloring using $k-1$ colors. Further, let $\Pi=\{A_1, A_2,\ldots,A_k\}$ be an arbitrary partition of $V(P_n)$ such that for each $i \in [k]$, $f|_{A_i}$ is a dominating broadcast of $P_n$. In particular, for each $i \in [k]$, there exists a vertex $v \in A_i$ such that $d(u_1, v) \leq f(v)$. Since $f(v) \leq k-1$ for any $v$, each of the sets $A_i$ contains at least one vertex from $N_{k-1}[u_1]=\{u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_k\}$. Since the sets in $\Pi$ are pairwise disjoint and $|\Pi|=|N_{k-1}[u_1]|=k$, each set $A_i$ contains exactly one of the vertices from $N_{k-1} [u_1]$. This implies that $f(u_k)=k-1$, $f(u_{k-1})=k-2$, $\ldots$, $f(u_2)=1$ and $f(u_1)=f(u_i)=i-1$ for some $i \in \{2,3,\ldots, k\}$, which contradicts the fact that $f$ is a packing coloring since $d(u_1, u_i)=i-1$. Thus, $\chi_{\rho, k}(P_n) \geq k$.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop_chi_rho_3_finite_paths}
For any path $P_n$, where $n \geq 3$, 
$\chi_{\rho, 3}(P_n) =3.$
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $n \geq 3$ be an arbitrary integer. By Lemma~\ref{lem:paths_lower}, we have $\chi_{\rho, 3}(P_n) \geq 3$. To prove that $\chi_{\rho, 3}(P_n) \leq 3$, we form a packing $3$-domatic coloring $f$ of $P_n$ using $3$ colors as follows. If $n=3$, then let $f(u_1)=2$, $f(u_2)=1$ and $f(u_3)=3$. In the case when $n >3$ and $n \not\equiv 1 \ (\mathrm{mod} \ 4)$, color the consecutive vertices of $P_n$ one after another by applying the color pattern $[1,2,1,3]$. Finally, if $n >3$ and $n \equiv 1 \ (\mathrm{mod} \ 4)$, then let $f(u_1)=2$, $f(u_2)=1$, $f(u_3)=3$ and color the remaining vertices of $P_n$ one after another (starting with $u_4$) by repeatedly applying the color pattern $[1,2,1,3]$. Note that the above construction yields a packing coloring of $P_n$ for any $n \geq 3$. Furthermore, for each $i \in [3]$, let $A_i=\{u_j:\,f(u_j)=i\}$. Since $\Pi=\{A_1, A_2, A_3\}$ is a partition of $V(P_n)$ and for each $i \in [3]$, the restriction $f|_{A_i}$ is a dominating broadcast, we conclude that $\chi_{\rho, 3}(P_n) = 3$ for any $n \geq 3$.

    Next, let $n \geq 5$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:paths_lower}, $\chi_{\rho, 4}(P_n) \geq 4$. In order to prove that $\chi_{\rho, 4}(P_n) \leq 4$, we form a packing $4$-domatic coloring $f$ of $P_n$ using $4$ colors. To present this coloring, we distinguish five cases with respect to $n$. \\
     
    \textbf{Case 1. $n \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ 5)$.} \\
    In this case, color the consecutive vertices of $P_n$ one after another by repeating the color pattern $[1, 3, 2, 1, 4]$. \\
    \textbf{Case 2. $n \equiv 1 \ (\mathrm{mod} \ 5)$.} \\
    First, let $f$ assign the colors $2, 1,3,4,1,2$ to the vertices $u_1, u_2, \ldots$ and $u_6$, respectively. Next, if $n>6$, apply the color pattern $[1, 3, 2, 1, 4]$ to color the remaining consecutive vertices of $P_n$. \\

    Let the vertices $u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_7$ receive colors $1, 2, 1,3,4,1,2$, respectively. If $n>7$, color the remaining vertices of $P_n$ one after another by applying the color pattern $[1, 3, 2, 1, 4]$. \\
    \textbf{Case 4. $n \equiv 3 \ (\mathrm{mod} \ 5)$.} \\
    In this case, we first color the vertices $u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_8$ with colors $3,1,2,1,4,3,1,2$, respectively. Then, if necessary, apply the color pattern $[1, 3, 2, 1, 4]$ for coloring the remaining consecutive vertices of $P_n$. \\
    \textbf{Case 5. $n \equiv 4 \ (\mathrm{mod} \ 5)$.} \\
    Let the vertices $u_1,u_2,u_3,u_4$ receive colors $3,1,2,4$, respectively. Next, color the remaining vertices one after another using the color pattern $[1, 3, 2, 1, 4]$. 
 
  It is straightforward to verify that in each of the cases, $f$ is a packing coloring. Next, for each $i \in [4]$, let $A_i=\{u_j:\,f(u_j)=i\}$. Since $\Pi=\{A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4\}$ is a partition of $V(P_n)$ and for each $i \in [4]$, $f|_{A_i}$ is a dominating broadcast, we conclude that $\chi_{\rho, 4}(P_n) = 4$.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop_chi_rho_5_finite_paths}

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
First, consider the case when $n=5$.  It is clear that colors in $[5]$ do not suffice for a packing $5$-domatic coloring of $P_5$, thus $\chi_{\rho, 5}(P_5) > 5$.  
By setting $f(u_1)=5$, $f(u_2)=4$, $f(u_3)=2$, $f(u_4)=3$ and $f(u_5)=6$, we form a packing $5$-domatic coloring $f$ of $P_5$ using colors from $[6]$. Therefore, $\chi_{\rho, 5}(P_5) = 6$.

Now, let $n=6$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:paths_lower}, we have $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_6)\geq5$. Suppose that $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_6)=5$ and denote by $f$ a packing $5$-domatic coloring of $P_6$ which uses colors from $[5]$. Further, let $\Pi=\{A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4,A_5\}$ be a partition of $V(P_6)$ such that $f|_{A_i}$ is a dominating broadcast for every $i\in[5]$. Since $|V(P_6)|=6$, at least four sets in $\Pi$ contain only one element. Hence, at least four vertices of $P_6$ individually $f$-dominate all vertices of $P_6$. Since the radius of $P_6$ is $3$, each of these four vertices receives a color from $\{3,4,5\}$ by $f$. Therefore, two of these vertices receive the same color from $j \in \{3,4,5\}$. Moreover, both of them $f$-dominate $u_1$, which means that they are at distance at most $j$, a contradiction to the fact that $f$ is a packing coloring. Thus, $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_6)\geq6$. \\
Now, let $f(u_1)=1$, $f(u_2)=4$, $f(u_3)=3$, $f(u_4)=2$, $f(u_5)=5$, and $f(u_6)=6$. Furthermore, let $A_1=\{u_1,u_4\}$, $A_2=\{u_2\}$, $A_3=\{u_3\}$, $A_4=\{u_5\}$, and $A_5=\{u_6\}$. It is straightforward to verify that $f$ is a packing coloring and that $f|_{A_i}$ is a dominating broadcast of $P_6$ for every $i\in[5]$. Therefore, $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_6)\leq6$, and consequently $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_6)=6$.

Next, consider the case when $n=7$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:paths_lower}, we have $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_7)\geq5$. Suppose that there exists a packing $5$-domatic coloring $f$ of $P_7$ using colors from $[5]$. Let $\Pi=\{A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4,A_5\}$ be a partition of $V(P_7)$ such that $f|_{A_i}$ is a dominating broadcast for every $i\in[5]$. We observe that there are at least three sets in $\Pi$ such that each contains exactly one vertex of $P_7$, since $|V(P_7)|=7$. This means that $V(P_7)$ contains at least three vertices such that each of them $f$-dominates all vertices of $P_7$.
Since the radius of $P_7$ is $3$, each of these vertices must receive a color from $\{3,4,5\}$ by $f$. Note that all of these vertices $f$-dominate $u_1$, hence they receive pairwise distinct colors from $\{3,4,5\}$, which also means that we have exactly three sets in $\Pi$ such that each of them contains only one element. Therefore, we can assume that $|A_1|=1$ and the vertex in $A_1$ is colored with $3$ by $f$, $|A_2|=1$ and the vertex in $A_2$ is colored with $4$ by $f$, $|A_3|=1$ and the vertex in $A_3$ is colored with $5$ by $f$, and $|A_4|\geq 2$, $|A_5|\geq 2$. These assumptions imply that $A_1=\{u_4\}$, $A_2 \subseteq \{u_3, u_5\}$, $A_3 \subseteq \{u_2, u_3, u_5, u_6\}$. Additionally, since $f$ is a packing coloring, the vertices in $A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3$ are the only vertices of $P_7$ receiving colors from $\{3,4,5\}$. Consequently, the remaining four vertices from $V(P_7) \setminus (A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3)$ receive colors from $\{1,2\}$ and belong to $A_4 \cup A_5$. \\
 
By symmetry, we may assume that $A_2=\{u_3\}$, which implies that $A_3 \subseteq \{u_2, u_5, u_6\}$. If $A_3=\{u_2\}$, then the vertices $u_1,u_5,u_6,u_7$ receive colors from $\{1,2\}$ and at least two of them $f$-dominate $u_1$, which is not possible. Hence, $A_3 \neq \{u_2\}$. Now, suppose that $A_3=\{u_5\}$, which means that the vertices $u_1,u_2,u_6,u_7$ receive colors from $\{1,2\}$ and $A_4 \cup A_5 =\{u_1,u_2,u_6,u_7\}$. Since $A_4$ and $A_5$ must $f$-dominate $u_4$, we derive that $f(u_2)=f(u_6)=2$ and we can assume that $u_2 \in A_4$ and $u_6 \in A_5$. Consequently $u_1 \in A_5$ and $f(u_1)=1$, which implies that $A_5$ does not $f$-dominate $u_3$, a contradiction. Hence, $A_3=\{u_6\}$. Then neither $u_1$ nor $u_2$ $f$-dominates $u_7$, thus we can assume that $u_5 \in A_4$ and $u_7 \in A_5$, which also implies that $f(u_5)=2$ and $f(u_7)=1$. But then no vertex in $A_5$ $f$-dominates $u_5$, again a contradiction. Thus, $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_7)\geq6$.

It is straightforward to verify that $f$ is a packing coloring and that $f|_{A_i}$ is a dominating broadcast of $P_7$ for every $i\in[5]$. Therefore, $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_7)\leq6$, and consequently $\chi_{\rho,5}(P_7)=6$.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{theorem_chi_rho_k_12}
    For any $k \geq 12$ and any $n \geq k$, $\chi_{\rho,k}(P_{n})>k$.

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $k\geq12$ and $n\geq k$ be integers. By Lemma~\ref{lem:paths_lower}, we have $\chi_{\rho,k}(P_n)\geq k$.
 
Suppose that $\chi_{\rho,k}(P_n)=k$, and let $f$ be a packing $k$-domatic coloring using colors from $[k]$. Furthermore, let $\Phi=\{A_1,\ldots,A_k\}$ be a partition of $V(P_n)$ such that $f|_{A_j}$ is a dominating broadcast of $P_n$ for every $j\in[k]$.

We first claim that for each $i\in[k]$, at most one vertex of color $i$ can $f$-dominate $u_1$. Suppose to the contrary that two distinct vertices, $u_j$ and $u_{j'}$, of color $i$ both $f$-dominate $u_1$. Then, assuming $j<j'$, we have $d(u_{j'},u_j)\le d(u_{j'},u_1)\le i$, which is a contradiction with $f$ being a packing coloring. 
This proves the claim.

Next, since $f|{A_j}$ is a dominating broadcast for every $j\in[k]$, each set $A_j$ contains at least one vertex that $f$-dominates $u_1$. As the sets in $\Phi$ are pairwise disjoint, at least $k$ distinct vertices $f$-dominate $u_1$. On the other hand, at most one vertex of each of the $k$ available colors can $f$-dominate $u_1$. Consequently, exactly $k$ vertices $f$-dominate $u_1$, exactly one of each color in $[k]$. For each $i\in[k]$, let $u_{a_i}$ denote the unique vertex of color $i$ that $f$-dominates $u_1$. Note that $d(u_1,u_{a_i})=a_i-1\le i$ and hence, $a_i\le i+1$ for any $i \in [k]$. 

Now, if $n=k$, then $\{u_{a_1},\ldots,u_{a_k}\}=V(P_n)$. In particular, $u_{a_1}$ has color $1$ and satisfies $a_1\leq2$. Hence, $d(u_{a_1},u_4)=4-a_1\geq2>1$, so $u_{a_1}$ does not $f$-dominate $u_4$. Consequently, at most $k-1$ vertices $f$-dominate $u_4$. However, each of the $k$ pairwise disjoint sets in $\Phi$ contains a vertex that $f$-dominates $u_4$, so at least $k$ distinct vertices must $f$-dominate $u_4$, a contradiction. We may therefore assume that $n\geq k+1$.

Note that $\{u_{a_1},\ldots,u_{a_k}\} \subseteq \{u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1}\}$, hence that there is exactly one vertex in $\{u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1}\}$ that does not $f$-dominate $u_1$. Denote this vertex by $u_H$. Since $\{u_{a_1},\ldots,u_{a_k}\}$ contains one vertex of each color in $[k]$, there exists some $i\in[k]$ such that $f(u_H)=f(u_{a_i})$.

We now consider the vertex $u_6$. Since $f|{A_j}$ is a dominating broadcast for every $j\in[k]$, each set $A_j$ contains at least one vertex that $f$-dominates $u_6$. As the sets in $\Phi$ are pairwise disjoint, at least $k$ distinct vertices $f$-dominate $u_6$. Since $a_1\leq2$, we have $d(u_{a_1},u_6)=6-a_1\geq4>1$, and hence $u_{a_1}$ does not $f$-dominate $u_6$. Similarly, since $a_2\leq3$, we have $d(u_{a_2},u_6)=6-a_2\geq3>2$, so $u_{a_2}$ does not $f$-dominate $u_6$ either. Consequently, at most $k-2$ vertices in $\{u_{a_1},\ldots,u_{a_k}\}$ can $f$-dominate $u_6$. Therefore, at least two vertices outside this set must $f$-dominate $u_6$.
 
Among the vertices in $\{u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1}\}$, the only vertex outside $\{u_{a_1},\ldots,u_{a_k}\}$ is $u_H$. Hence, there exists at least one additional vertex $u_p$ with $p\geq k+2$ that $f$-dominates $u_6$. If $n=k+1$, no such vertex exists, a contradiction. We may therefore assume that $n\geq k+2$.

Let $q=f(u_p)$, where $p\geq k+2$ and $u_p$ $f$-dominates $u_6$. Since $u_p$ $f$-dominates $u_6$, we have $p-6=d(u_p,u_6)\leq q$, or equivalently, $p-q\leq6$. 
                                                                                                      
In addition, since $p\geq k+2$ and $k\geq12$, we obtain 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:k12}
q\geq p-6\geq k-4\geq8.
\end{equation}
 
Recall that $p\geq k+2$ and $q\leq k$. Hence, $d(u_p,u_1)=p-1\geq k+1>q$, so $u_p$ does not $f$-dominate $u_1$. On the other hand, the vertex $u_{a_q}\in \{u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1}\}$ has color $q$ and $f$-dominates $u_1$. Since $f(u_p)=f(u_{a_q})=q$ and $p>a_q$, the packing condition implies that $p-a_q=d(u_p,u_{a_q})>q$. Therefore, $a_q<p-q\leq6$, and hence $a_q\leq5$. We infer that exactly one of the vertices from $N_4[u_1]=\{u_1, u_2, u_3, u_4, u_5\}$ receives the color $q \geq 8$ by $f$.
 
Further, recall that $a_i\leq i+1$ for every $i\in[k]$. In particular, $a_i\leq 5$ for every $i\in\{1,2,3,4\}$. Hence, $\{u_{a_1},u_{a_2},u_{a_3},u_{a_4}, u_{a_q}\}= N_4[u_1]$. In other words, the vertices in $N_4[u_1]$ are colored with pairwise distinct colors from $\{1,2,3,4,q\}$.
 

Next, we claim that $u_p$ is the unique vertex in $\{u_{k+2},\ldots, u_n\}$ that $f$-dominates $u_6$. Clearly, any vertex in this set that $f$-dominates $u_6$ must be at distance at most $k$ from $u_6$ and therefore belongs to $\{u_{k+2},\ldots, u_{\min\{n,k+6\}}\}$. Suppose, to the contrary, that another vertex $u_r$ in this set $f$-dominates $u_6$, and let $s=f(u_r)$. As above, we obtain $s\geq8$ and $a_s\leq5$. This means that to a vertex in $N_4[u_1]$ is assigned a color $s$ by $f$. Since the colors assigned to the vertices in $N_4[u_1]$ are precisely $1,2,3,4$, and $q$, and we know that $s \geq 8$, it follows that $s=q$. Hence, $f(u_p)=f(u_r)=q$. Further, since both $u_p$ and $u_r$ belong to $\{u_{k+2},\ldots,u_{\min\{n,k+6\}}\}$, $d(u_p,u_r)\leq4<q$, contradicting the fact that $f$ is a packing coloring. Therefore, $u_p$ is the unique vertex in $\{u_{k+2},\ldots, u_n\}$ that $f$-dominates $u_6$. Note that this implies that $u_H$ must $f$-dominate $u_6$, since at least two vertices outside $\{u_{a_1},\ldots,u_{a_k}\}$ $f$-dominate $u_6$. 

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                

 Now, we consider the position of $u_H$. Recall that the vertices in $N_4[u_1]$ are colored with pairwise distinct colors from $\{1,2,3,4,q\}$ and each of them $f$-dominates $u_1$. Further, since $a_5\leq6$, we have $a_5=6$ which means that $f(u_6)=5$. Similarly, $a_6\leq7$ implies $f(u_7)=6$. Continuing with this argument, we obtain $f(u_{i+1})=i$ for every $i\in\{5,\ldots,q-1\}$. Note that for each $i \in [q]$, $u_{i}$ $f$-dominates $u_1$. Since $u_H$ does not dominates $u_1$, we know that $H \geq q+1$.

Let $c=f(u_H)$. Since $u_H$ $f$-dominates $u_6$, we have $H-6\leq c$.
Furthermore, there is a unique vertex $u_{a_c}$ of color $c$ that $f$-dominates $u_1$. Since $u_H$ does not $f$-dominate $u_1$ it is clear that $a_c < H$.
                                                                                               
The packing condition applied to $u_H$ and $u_{a_c}$ now gives $H-a_c>c$. 
Combining this inequality with $H-6\leq c$, we obtain $H-a_c>H-6$, and therefore $a_c\leq5$. 
Since $\{f(u_1),\ldots,f(u_5)\}=\{1,2,3,4,q\}$, we conclude that $c\in\{1,2,3,4,q\}$.
Now, we show that $c\neq q$. Recall that $H\geq q+1$ and $p\leq q+6$. Moreover, $u_H \in \{u_1,\ldots,u_{k+1}\}$, whereas $u_p \in \{u_{k+2},\ldots,u_{\min\{n,k+6\}}\}$, so $H<p$. It follows that $d(u_H,u_p)=p-H\leq(q+6)-(q+1)=5$. If $c=q$, then $u_H$ and $u_p$ are distinct vertices of color $q$ satisfying $d(u_H,u_p)\leq5$, contradicting the packing condition since $q\geq8$. Therefore, $c\neq q$, and consequently $c \leq 4$. 

We now show that $k\in\{12,13\}$. Since $u_H$ $f$-dominates $u_6$, we have $H-6\leq c=f(u_H)$. Hence, $H\leq c+6\leq10$, since $c\leq4$. Combining this with $H\geq q+1$, we obtain $q+1\leq H\leq10$, and therefore $q\leq9$. Since we have already shown that $q\geq k-4$ (see~\eqref{eq:k12}), it follows that $k-4\leq q\leq9$, and hence $k\leq13$. Together with $k\geq12$, this yields $k\in \{12,13\}$. In continuation of the proof we distinguish two cases. 

First, suppose that $k=13$. Then $q\geq k-4=9$, and since $q\leq9$, we obtain $q=9$. The inequalities $q+1\leq H\leq10$ then imply that $H=10$. Moreover, $H-6\leq c\leq4$ yields $c=4$. Finally, $p\geq k+2=15$ and $p\leq q+6=15$, so $p=15$. Thus, when $k=13$, we necessarily have $p=15$, $q=9$, $H=10$, and $c=4$. In other words, $f(u_{10})=4$ and $f(u_{15})=9$.
 
We now consider the vertex $u_4$. The vertex $u_{a_1}$ of color $1$ does not $f$-dominate $u_4$, since $a_1\leq2$ and hence $d(u_{a_1},u_4)=4-a_1\geq2>1$. Moreover, neither $u_H=u_{10}$ nor $u_p=u_{15}$ $f$-dominates $u_4$, because $d(u_{10},u_4)=6>4=f(u_{10})$ and $d(u_{15},u_4)=11>9=f(u_{15})$.
 
No other vertex in $V(P_n)\setminus \{u_{a_1},\ldots,u_{a_{13}}\}$ can $f$-dominate $u_4$. Indeed, every such vertex different from $u_H$ belongs to $\{u_{15},u_{16},\ldots,u_n\}$. If such a vertex $f$-dominated $u_4$, then it would also $f$-dominate the closer vertex $u_6$. However, $u_{15}$ is the unique vertex in this set that $f$-dominates $u_6$, and we have already shown that it does not $f$-dominate $u_4$.
 
Therefore, only vertices in $\{u_{a_2},\ldots,u_{a_{13}}\}$ can $f$-dominate $u_4$, so at most $12$ vertices $f$-dominate $u_4$. On the other hand, each of the $13$ pairwise disjoint sets in $\Phi$ contains a vertex that $f$-dominates $u_4$. Thus, at least $13$ distinct vertices must $f$-dominate $u_4$, a contradiction.

Now suppose that $k=12$. Recall that $q+1\leq H\leq10$ and $8\leq q\leq9$. If $q=8$, then $H\in \{9,10\}$, whereas if $q=9$, then $H=10$. Moreover, since $H-6\leq c\leq4$, $H=9$ implies that $c\in \{3,4\}$, while $H=10$ implies that $c=4$.
 
We also have $p\geq k+2=14$ and $p\leq q+6$. If $q=8$, then $p=14$, while if $q=9$, then $p\in \{14,15\}$. Hence, the possible pairs $(p,q)$ are $(14,8)$, $(14,9)$, and $(15,9)$, while the possible pairs $(H,c)$ are $(9,3)$, $(9,4)$, and $(10,4)$.
 
In each case, $u_H$ does not $f$-dominate $u_4$, since $d(u_H,u_4)=H-4\geq5>c$. The vertex $u_p$ does not $f$-dominate $u_4$ either. Indeed, if $(p,q)=(14,8)$, then $d(u_p,u_4)=10>8=q$; if $(p,q)=(14,9)$, then $d(u_p,u_4)=10>9=q$; and if $(p,q)=(15,9)$, then $d(u_p,u_4)=11>9=q$.
 
Furthermore, no other vertex in $V(P_n)\setminus \{u_{a_1},\ldots,u_{a_{12}}\}$ can $f$-dominate $u_4$. Indeed, every such vertex different from $u_H$ lies to the right of $u_{13}$. If it $f$-dominated $u_4$, then it would also $f$-dominate the closer vertex $u_6$. By the uniqueness of $u_p$, this vertex would have to be $u_p$, but we have already shown that $u_p$ does not $f$-dominate $u_4$.
 
Finally, the vertex $u_{a_1}$ of color $1$ does not $f$-dominate $u_4$, since $a_1\leq2$ and therefore $d(u_{a_1},u_4)=4-a_1\geq2>1$. Hence, at most $11$ vertices $f$-dominate $u_4$, which is a contradiction since each of the $12$ pairwise disjoint sets in $\Phi$ must contain a vertex that $f$-dominates $u_4$.

Indeed, $f|_{D_1}$, where $D_1=\{u \in V(P_\infty) :~f(u)=1\}$, is a dominating broadcast. By an analogous argument, $f|_{D_3}$ and $f|_{D_4}$ are also dominating broadcasts. Therefore, $f$ is a packing $4$-domatic coloring of $P_\infty$ and consequently, $\chi_{\rho, 4}(P_\infty)=3$.

Hence, it is impossible to partition $V(P_\infty)$ into five sets, each inducing a dominating broadcast. Therefore, $\chi_{\rho, 5}(P_\infty) > 3$.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:domlower}
If $G$ is a connected graph with $\delta(G)\ge 2$, then $${\rm dom}_{\chi_\rho}(G)\ge 3.$$
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
Note that $\chi_\rho(G)\ge 3$ for any connected graph unless $G$ is a star $K_{1,r}$. Thus, by the assumption $\delta(G)\ge 2$, we infer that $\chi_\rho(G)\ge 3$. Note that for an infinite sequence $S$, ${\rm dom}_{\chi_S}(G)$ is defined as ${\rm dom}_{\chi_{S'}}(G)$, where $S'=(s_1,\ldots,s_{\chi_S(G)})$ is the finite sequence consisting of the first $\chi_S(G)$ terms of $S$. Thus, we can write $S'=(1,2,\ldots,\chi_\rho(G))$. 

Let $f$ be an arbitrary $S'$-packing coloring of $G$. We will show that $f$ is a $3$-dominating broadcast by presenting a partition $\pi=\{D_1,D_2,D_3\}$ of $V(G)$ such that $f|_{D_i}$ is a dominating broadcast for all $i\in [3]$. 

We claim that we may assume that the set $A_1=\{v\in V(G):\, f(v)=1\}$ is a maximal independent set. Indeed, if $A_1$ is not a maximal independent set we may select an arbitrary maximal independent set $B_1$ such that $A_1\subsetneq B_1$ and for any $v\in B_1\setminus A_1$ replace the color of $v$ assigned by $f$ to color $1$. It is clear that the resulting coloring is still an $S'$-packing coloring. Now, under this assumption, letting $D_1=A_1$, we note that $D_1$ is an independent dominating set, and so $f|_{D_1}$ is a dominating broadcast, as desired. 

In addition to the above assumption, let $f$ be chosen in such a way that the set $A_2=\{v\in V(G):\, f(v)=2\}$ is as large as possible. We claim that, under this assumption, every vertex $x\in V(G)$ with $f(x)>2$ has a vertex $y\in A_2$ such that $d_G(x,y)\le 2$. Indeed, if a vertex $x\in V(G)$ with $f(x)>2$ has no vertices $y$ with $f(y)=2$ within distance $2$, then we can redefine $f$ by changing the value at $x$ to $2$, and the resulting function $f'$ is still an $S'$-packing coloring of $G$ with $A_1$ forming a dominating set, yet $|\{v:\,f'(v)=2\}|>|A_2|$, a contradiction.  

Let $$A_1''=\{v\in A_1:\,\textrm{ if }d_G(x,v)\le 2,\textrm{ then }f(x)\ne 2\}.$$
In other words, $A_1''$ are the vertices in $A_1$ that are not $f$-dominated by $A_2$. Let $A_1'=A_1\setminus A_1''$. If $A_1''\ne\emptyset$, then define $W$ as a smallest subset of $N_G(A_1'')$ such that every $x\in A_1''$ has a neighbor in $W$. Note that $W\cap A_2=\emptyset$. In addition, now that $W$ is defined, define a subset $U$ of vertices in $A_2$ as follows: $u\in U$ if $u\in A_2$, $N_G(u)\subset A_1$, and for every $v\in N_G(u)$ we have $N_G(v)\setminus \{u\}\subset W$. In other words, all neighbors of $u$ are in $D_1$ and their neighbors (with the obvious exception of $u$) belong to $W$. 

Let $D_2=(A_2\setminus U)\cup W$, and $D_3=(A_{\ge 3}\setminus W)\cup U$, where $$A_{\ge 3}=\{v\in V(G):\,f(v)\ge 3\}.$$  
Note that $\pi=\{D_1,D_2,D_3\}$ is a partition of $V(G)$. As mentioned earlier, $f|_{D_1}$ is a dominating broadcast, since $D_1$ is a dominating set. Let us now consider the vertices in $D_2$. 

\begin{claim}
\label{cl:0} $f|_{D_2}$ is a dominating broadcast.
\end{claim}
\noindent {\it Proof of Claim~\ref{cl:0}.}
As proved earlier, vertices of $A_2$ $f$-dominate vertices in $A_{\ge3}$. Hence, vertices in $A_2\setminus U$ dominate all vertices of $A_{\ge 3}$ with the possible exception of vertices in $A_{\ge 3}$ that are $f$-dominated by a vertex in $U$. However, by definition of a vertex  $u\in U$, among vertices in $A_{\ge 3}$ vertex $u$ only $f$-dominates a vertex $w\in W$, which is itself in $D_2$. Hence, vertices in $A_2\setminus U$, $f$-dominate all vertices in $A_{\ge 3}\setminus W$. In addition, vertices in $U$ are $f$-dominated by vertices in $W$. Combining these two findings we infer that vertices in $D_2=(A_2\setminus U)\cup W$ $f$-dominate all vertices in $(A_{\ge 3}\setminus W)\cup U=D_3$.

Vertices in $A_2\setminus U$ also $f$-dominate vertices in $A_1'$ by the definition $A_1'$, again with a possible exception of the vertices $R$ in $A_1'$ that are adjacent to a vertex $u\in U$; however, vertices in $R$ are dominated by a vertex $w\in W$, which is in $N_G(R)$. In addition, vertices of $W$ $f$-dominate vertices in $A_1''$. Hence, vertices in $D_2=(A_2\setminus U)\cup W$ $f$-dominate all vertices in $A_1$. Thus, $f|_{D_2}$ is a dominating broadcast. \hskip 0.6cm {\tiny ($\Box$)}

\medskip 

Next, let us consider $f|_{D_3}$; we divide the proof into two claims. 

\begin{claim}
\label{cl:1}
Every vertex in $A_1$ is $f$-dominated by a vertex in $D_3$.    
\end{claim}
\noindent {\it Proof of Claim~\ref{cl:1}.} Let $x\in A_1$. Note that $x$ has at least one neighbor $y$ in $A_{\ge 3}$. If $y\in D_3$, then $x$ is $f$-dominated by a vertex in $D_3$, and we are done. Thus, assume that $y\in W$. Hence, $y$ has a neighbor in $A_1''$ and let $Z=N_G(y)\cap A_1''$. Note that all neighbors of vertices in $Z$ belong to $A_{\ge 3}$. We claim that there exists at least one vertex $z\in Z$, which has a neighbor in $V(G)\setminus W$. 

Suppose to the contrary that for every vertex $z$ in $Z$ its neighborhood belongs to $W$. Then, $W\setminus \{y\}$ still has the property that every vertex in $A_1''$ has a neighbor in  $W\setminus \{y\}$. Indeed, since $\deg_G(z)\ge 2$ for each $z\in Z$, there exists at least one neighbor of $z$   that belongs to $W\setminus\{y\}$.  This is a contradiction to the minimality of $W$, thus our supposition was false.   

Hence there exists a vertex $z\in Z$, which has a neighbor $u$ in $V(G)\setminus W$, and note that $f(u)\ge 3$ since $z\in A_1''$. Thus, $u\in D_3$ and $$d_G(x,u)\le d_G(x,y)+d_G(y,u)\le 3\le f(u),$$ 
that is, $x$ is $f$-dominated by $u$.  \hskip 0.6cm {\tiny ($\Box$)}

\begin{claim}
\label{cl:2}
Every vertex in $D_2$ is $f$-dominated by a vertex in $D_3$.    
\end{claim}
\noindent {\it Proof of Claim~\ref{cl:2}.} Note that $D_2=(A_2\setminus U)\cup W$. 
The proof that every vertex in $W$ is $f$-dominated by a vertex in $D_3$ is similar as the second part of the proof of Claim~\ref{cl:1}. Notably, if $y\in W$, then $y$ has a neighbor in $A_1''$ and we again define $Z=N_G(y)\cap A_1''$. By following the same lines as in the above proof, we derive that there exists at least one vertex $z\in Z$, which has a neighbor $u$ in $V(G)\setminus W$. Since $u$ belongs to $A_{\ge 3}$ and $u\notin W$, we get $u\in D_3$. Clearly, $y$ is $f$-dominated by $u$, since it is at distance at most $2$ from $u$ with $f(u)\ge 3$. 

Now, consider a vertex $y\in A_2\setminus U$. If $y$ has a neighbor $x$ in $A_{\ge 3}$, then either $x\in D_3$ or $x\in W$. In the former case, $y$ is $f$-dominated by $x\in D_3$ and we are done. In the latter case, when $x\in W$ we derive in the same way as in the previous paragraph, that there is a vertex $u\in D_3$, which is at distance at most $2$ from $x$. Thus $u\in D_3$ $f$-dominates $y$, since $d_G(y,u)\le d_G(y,x)+d_G(x,u)\le 3\le f(u)$. 

Finally, assume that $y\in A_2\setminus U$ and $N_G(y)\subset D_1$. Let $T=N_G(y)$. Since $y\notin U$, there exists a vertex $t\in T$ such that $N_G(t)$ contains a vertex $x\notin W$. Since $d_G(y,x)=2$, we derive $f(x)\ge 3$, and consequently $x\in D_3$. Clearly, $x$ $f$-dominates $y$, since $d_G(y,x)=2< 3\le f(x)$. 

All in all, we proved that every vertex in $D_2$ is $f$-dominated by a vertex in $D_3$. \hskip 0.6cm {\tiny ($\Box$)}

\medskip 

Combining Claims~\ref{cl:1} and~\ref{cl:2} we derive that $f|_{D_3}$ is a dominating broadcast. Thus, $f$ is a packing $3$-domatic coloring of $G$, which implies ${\rm dom}_{\chi_\rho}(G)\ge 3$.
