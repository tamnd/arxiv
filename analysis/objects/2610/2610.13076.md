<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Note that if $H, K\in \mathcal{N}(G)$, with $[H]=[K]$ in AutCl($G$), then $[H]=[K]$ in $\text{QIso}(G)$ but not conversely. For instance, in $G=\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_4$, the subgroups $H=\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \bigl<(0,2)\bigr>$ and $K=\{0\} \times \mathbb{Z}_4$ serves as counterexample.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
Following are few examples of the poset $\text{QIso}(G)$:
\begin{enumerate}
		\item For any prime $p$, $\text{QIso}(\mathbb{Z}_p)\cong C_2$.
	\item For any prime $p$, $\text{QIso}(\mathbb{Z}_p \times \mathbb{Z}_p)\cong \text{QIso}(\mathbb{Z}_{p^2})\cong \text{QIso}(A_4)\cong C_3$.
	\item $\text{QIso}(\mathbb{Z}_2^3)\cong \text{QIso}(\mathbb{Z}_8) \cong \text{QIso}(D_4)\cong \text{QIso}(S_4)\cong C_4 $. 
	\item For $n\ge 5$, $\text{QIso}(A_n)\cong C_2$ and $\text{QIso}(S_n)\cong C_3$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finite nilpotent group with number of distinct prime divisors of $|G|$ is $n$, then the width of $\text{QIso}(G)$ is at least $n$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $p_1,\dots, p_n$ be the distinct prime divisors of $|G|$. Since $G$ is nilpotent, $G$ is the direct product of its non-trivial Sylow subgroups, that is $G\cong P_1 P_2 \dots 
		P_n$, where $P_i\in Syl_{p_i}(G)$. Since each Sylow subgroup of $G$ is normal, we have $G/P_i \cong P_1 P_2\dots  P_{i-1} P_{i+1}\dots P_{n-1} P_n$, for all $1\le i\le n$. Certainly, the set $\{G/P_i : 1\le i\le n\}$ contains $n$ incomparable groups with respect to monomorphisms and hence $[P_i]$ are all distinct and incomparable in $\text{QIso}(G)$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finite nilpotent group with number of prime divisors of $|G|$ is $n$ and $\Phi(G)$ be its Frattini subgroup, then the width of the poset $\text{QIso}(G/\Phi(G))$ is at least $n$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Since $G$ is nilpotent, it follows that $G/\Phi(G)$ is abelian. Moreover, all Sylow $p$-subgroups of $\Phi(G)$ are normal in $G$. By Frattini argument, $G=N_G(P)\Phi(G)$, for any $P\in Syl_p(\Phi(G))$, so $G=N_G(P)$. Now suppose that $p$ divides $|G|$ but not $|G/\Phi(G)|$, then $\Phi(G)$ has a Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ with $P\in \mathcal{N}(G)$. By Schur-Zassenhaus theorem (see Theorem 3.8 of \cite{isaacs2008finite}), there exists a complement $H\le G$ for $P$ in $G$. Now for a maximal subgroup $M$ of $G$ containing $H$, we have $p$ divides $|G/M|$ and therefore $p$ divides $|G/\Phi(G)|$, a contradiction. The result now follows from Proposition 2.1.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finite abelian group. Then the poset QIso($G$) up to duality, is isomorphic to the poset $\text{Iso}(G)$ and in particular, QIso($G$) is a distributive lattice.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$, then $H$ is isomorphic to a quotient of $G$ and conversely every quotient of $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $G$. This is because the map $G\longmapsto \text{Hom}(G, S^1)$, where $S^1$ is the circle group, is a contravariant exact functor in the category of abelian groups. Therefore it maps the exact sequence $0 \to H \to G \to G/H \to 0 $ to the sequence
		\begin{equation}\label{exp3}
			0 \to \text{Hom}(G/H,S^1) \to \text{Hom}(G,S^1)  \to \text{Hom}(H,S^1) \to 0.
		\end{equation}
		Moreover, as any finite abelian group $G$ is isomorphic to $\text{Hom}(G,S^1)$, so from (\ref{exp3}), it follows that $G/H$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $G$.
		 Thus by realizing a class $[H]$ of QIso($G$) with the class $[H^*]$ in $\text{Iso}(G)$, where $H^* \le G$ with $H^*\cong G/H$, we have a one-to-one correspondance between elements of QIso($G$) and Iso($G$). It now remains to check that this one-to-one correspondance is a dual lattice isomorphism. So if $[H]\le [K]$ in QIso($G$), then there is a isomorphic copy of $G/K$ in $G/H$, therefore $[K^*]\le [H^*]$ in Iso$(G$). Conversely, if $[K^*]\le [H^*]$ in Iso($G$), then there is a monomorphism from $G/K$ to $G/H$, so $[H]\le [K]$ in QIso($G$). The distributivity of $\text{QIso}(G)$ follows from the fact that $\text{Iso}(G)$ is distributive (see Proposition 1 of \cite{tarnauceanu2015posetsclassesisomorphicsubgroups}).
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
Note that for a finite abelian group $G$, with $	G\cong  \prod_{i=1}^{k} G_i$, where $G_i$'s are prime power order. From \cite{tarnauceanu2015posetsclassesisomorphicsubgroups}, we have $	\text{QIso}(G)\cong \prod_{i=1}^{k} \text{QIso}(G_i).$
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
There exists an infinite family of non-abelian groups $\{G\}$ for which the poset $\text{Iso}(G)^{\text{op}}$ and $\text{QIso}(G)$ are isomorphic.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For any fixed odd prime $p$, consider the following subgroups of $GL(3,p)$:
	\begin{align*}
		N_p=\biggl\{\begin{pmatrix}
			1 & a & b\\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1
		\end{pmatrix} : a,b \in \mathbb{Z}_p
		\biggr\}\hspace{0.2cm} \text{and} \hspace{0.2cm}
		H_p=\biggl\{\begin{pmatrix}
			1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & c \\ 0 & 0 & 1
		\end{pmatrix} : c \in \mathbb{Z}_p
		\biggr\}.
	\end{align*}
	Let $G_p:= N_p \rtimes H_p$, where the action of $H_p$ on $N_p$ is via conjugation. Certainly,$\bigl<1\bigr>$, $N_p$ and $G_p$ are normal in $G_p$. Furthermore, note that the centre $Z(G_p)\cong \mathbb{Z}_p$ and $G_p/ Z(G_p)\cong \mathbb{Z}_p \times \mathbb{Z}_p$. It follows that $\text{QIso}(G_p)\cong C_4$. Since $G_p$ is a non-abelian group of order $p^3$ and exponent $p$, by Theorem 5 of \cite{tarnauceanu2015posetsclassesisomorphicsubgroups}, it follows that $\text{Iso}(G_p)$ is a chain. Since $G_p$ has subgroup of order $1,p,p^2$ and $p^3$, we have $\text{Iso}(G_p)\cong C_4$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
(see \cite{AN201135}) Let $G$ be a group (finite or infinite).
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item A group $G$ is s-self dual if every subgroup of $G$ is isomorphic to a quotient of $G$.
		\item A group $G$ is q-self dual if every quotient of $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $G$.
		\item A group is self dual if it is both s-self dual and q-self dual.
	\end{enumerate}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
For a finite group $G$, if QIso($G$) is a lattice, then $G$ is a q-self dual group.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let QIso($G$) be a lattice, then QIso($G$) has a minimum element $[M]$ as $G$ is finite. Certainly, $[M]\le [\bigl<1\bigr>]$, which implies there is a subgroup isomorphic to $G$ in $G/M$. As $|G/M|\le |G|$, this implies $G \cong G/M$ and therefore, $M=\bigl<1\bigr>$. So, $[\bigl<1\bigr>]\le [H]$, for all $[H]\in $ QIso($G$), which means $G/H$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $G$, for any normal subgroup $H$ of $G$. So, $G$ is a q-self dual group.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
Note that q-self duality is not the sufficient condition for QIso($G$) to be a lattice. For instance, consider the q-self dual group $G=D_3 \times \mathbb{Z}_6$. Certainly, QIso($G$) is not a lattice as the class determined by $D_3 \times 0$ and $1 \times \mathbb{Z}_6$ does not possess a supremum. In contrast to s-self duality, q-self duality does not imply nilpotency.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finite nilpotent group, then $G$ is a q-self dual group if and only if all its Sylow subgroups are q-self dual.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $|G|=p_1^{\alpha_1}\dots p_n^{\alpha_n}$ be the prime factorization of $|G|$, then $G=P_1P_2\dots P_n$, where $P_i\in Syl_{p_i}(G)$. Suppose $N$ is any normal subgroup of $G$, then $N$ itself is nilpotent. So $N=N_1N_2\dots N_n$, where $N_i \le P_i$. Now let $g_i\in P_i$, then for $g=g_1g_2\dots g_n\in G$, we have $N^g=N_1^{g_1}N_2^{g_2}\dots N_n^{g_n}=N$, so $N_i^{g_i}=N_i$, that is $N_i$ is normal in $P_i$. Therefore the quotient $G/N\cong (P_1/N_1)(P_2/N_2)\dots (P_n/N_n)\cong H_1H_2\dots H_n$, where $H_i$ is a subgroup of $P_i$ with $P_i/N_i\cong H_i$. So $G$ is a q-self dual group. \par Conversely, let $N_i$ be a normal subgroup of $P_i$, then since $G$ is a q-self dual group, so $P_i/N_i\cong G/(P_1\dots P_{i-1}N_iP_{i+1}\dots P_n)\cong H_i'$, for some $H_i'\le G$. Since $H_i'$ is a $p_i$-subgroup of $G$, so $H_i'\le P_i$ and consequently $P_i$ is q-self dual.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
Let $H,K$ be subgroups of $G$.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item By maximal subgroup up to monomorphism in $H,K$, we mean a subgroup $S$ of $G$ with the property that there are monomorphisms from $S$ to $H,K$ and if $S'$ is any other subgroup with this property then there is a monomorphism from $S'$ to $S$.
		\item  By minimal subgroup up to monomorphism from $H,K$, we mean a subgroup $\tilde S$ of $G$ with the property that there are monomorphisms from $H,K$ to $\tilde S$ and if $S''$ is any other subgroup with this property then there is a monomorphism from $S$ to $S''$. 
	\end{enumerate}
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finite self dual group with the property that any two subgroup $H,K$ of $G$ satisfies Definition 2.2, then the poset $\text{QIso}(G)$ is a lattice.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a self dual group and $[H],[K]$ be any two arbitrary elements of $\text{QIso}(G)$. Since $G$ is q-self dual, denote the quotients $G/H$ and $G/K$ by the subgroups of $G$, namely $S_H$ and $S_K$, respectively. Now consider maximal subgroup $\widetilde{S}$ up to monomorphism in $S_H$ and $S_K$ and minimal subgroup $\widehat S$ up to monomorphism from $S_H$ and $S_K$. The derived subgroup $P_{10}'=\bigl<a,b\bigr>$ is a subgroup of order $p^2$ with $P_{10}/P_{10}' \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2\cong P_{10}'$. Let $N$ be a normal subgroup of order $p^2$, then by Theorem 1.19 of \cite{isaacs2008finite}, we have $\bigl<a\bigr>\le N$. Since $N/\bigl<a\bigr>$ is normal in $P_{10}/\bigl<a\bigr>$, we have $Z(P_{10}/\bigl<a\bigr>)=\bigl<b \bigl<a\bigr> \bigr>=N/\bigl<a\bigr>$, so $N=\bigl<a,b\bigr>=P_{10}'$. Therefore $P_{10}$ has a unique normal subgroup of order $p^2$ and $\text{QIso}(P_{10})$ is a chain. 
 \end{enumerate} 
 This completes the proof.
