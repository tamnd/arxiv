<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:stars}
For a probability measure $\nu$ on $[0,\pi]$, the cyclic monotonicity inequalities hold if and only if there is a real scalar $\varphi_0$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:star-feasible}
 -\bar c_{t0}(\nu)\leq(1-q^t)\varphi_0\leq\bar c_{0t}(\nu),
 \qquad\text{for every integer }t\geq1.
\end{equation}
In that case, we may choose $\varphi_i=q^i\varphi_0$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:fixed-domain-rate}
For $s>1$, problem~\eqref{eq:disk-rate-lp} attains its maximum, and every optimal measure is supported on the circle $|z|^2=q_\star(s)$.
For each $q\in(0,1)$, membership in $\mathcal Q(s)$ is equivalent to the
existence of a feasible disk measure supported on $|z|^2=q$.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qcyc}
 q_\star(s)=\max\mathcal Q(s),
 \qquad q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)\leqq_\star(s)\leq1-1/s.
\end{equation}

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For every $\kappa>1$, the two worst-case asymptotic factors defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem} satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:main}
 \rho_{\mathrm{fix}}(\kappa)=\rho_{\mathrm{hor}}(\kappa)=q_\star(\sqrt\kappa).
\end{equation}
This factor is attained by a single function in a finite-dimensional space and an initial point, both independent of the iteration count.
The exact rate satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:classical-bracket}
 \left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt\kappa}\right)^2
 \leq q_\star(\sqrt\kappa)
 \leq 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt\kappa}.
\end{equation}
In particular, the rate tends to zero as $\kappa\downarrow1$ and to one as $\kappa\to\infty$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Set $s=\sqrt\kappa>1$.
For the lower bound, suppose $q_\star(s)>q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)$.
An optimal angular pair can then be chosen with a finitely supported measure, so the construction in Section~\ref{sec:variational} uses finitely many planar trajectories.
We construct a function in $\mathcal F_{1,\kappa}$ for which Nesterov's method, initialized with $x_{-1}=x_0$, attains this asymptotic factor.
If $q_\star(s)=q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)$, the quadratic $f(x)=x^2/2$ attains the factor.
For the upper bound, fix $q\in(q_\star(s),1)$.
The interpolation conditions are infeasible at this factor.
A separation argument gives a nonnegative combination of finitely many cyclic monotonicity inequalities.
After rescaling the iterates, we sum these inequalities and use the NAG recurrence to bound their squared norms.
The key identity averages a finite sum of integer powers over equally spaced points on the unit circle.
A finite induction first establishes that the sums of squared norms are finite, and then gives
\[
 \|x_t-x_\star\|^2\leq bq^t\|x_0-x_\star\|^2,
\]
where $b$ depends only on $s$ and $q$.
Taking $t$th roots and letting $t\to\infty$ gives $\rho_{\mathrm{hor}}(s^2)\leq q$.
Letting $q\downarrowq_\star(s)$ proves the upper bound.
The full proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:generic-bridges}.

\label{rem:no-radical-formula}
At $\kappa=(1421/1250)^2$, the exact factor $q_\star(1421/1250)$ has a minimal
polynomial of degree $20$ over $\mathbb Q$, with Galois group $S_{20}$.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:large-kappa}
The exact factor satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:large-kappa}
 \lim_{\kappa\to\infty}
 \sqrt\kappa\left(1-q_\star(\sqrt\kappa)\right)=\frac43,
 \qquad
 q_\star(\sqrt\kappa)
 =1-\frac{4}{3\sqrt\kappa}+o(\kappa^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
The corresponding asymptotic factor for the distance $\|x_t-x_\star\|$ is
$1-2/(3\sqrt\kappa)+o(\kappa^{-1/2})$.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:metrics}
Replacing $R_t(f,x_0)$ in the two asymptotic factor definitions \eqref{eq:rho-fix}--\eqref{eq:rho-hor} by either
\[
 \frac{f(x_t)-f(x_\star)}{f(x_0)-f(x_\star)}
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad
 \frac{\|\nabla f(x_t)\|^2}{\|\nabla f(x_0)\|^2}
\]
gives the same exact factor as in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
The asymptotic factors for the distance $\|x_t-x_\star\|$ and the gradient norm $\|\nabla f(x_t)\|$ equal the square root of this factor.

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For every $f\in\mathcal F_{1,\kappa}$ and $x$, strong convexity and smoothness give
\[
 \frac12\|x-x_\star\|^2\leq f(x)-f(x_\star)
 \leq\frac\kappa2\|x-x_\star\|^2,
 \qquad
 \|x-x_\star\|^2\leq\|\nabla f(x)\|^2
 \leq\kappa^2\|x-x_\star\|^2.
\]
Dividing by the corresponding initial values bounds the first ratio between $\kappa^{-1}R_t$ and $\kappa R_t$, and the second between $\kappa^{-2}R_t$ and $\kappa^2R_t$.
These constants depend only on $\kappa$, so the bounds remain valid when taking the supremum over dimensions, functions, and initial points.
Their $t$th roots tend to one, which proves equality of the asymptotic factors in both definitions.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Fix $s>1$, $q\in(0,1)$, and a probability measure $\nu$.
For integers $t,u\geq1$, define
\[
 b_t=\frac{\bar c_{0t}(\nu)}{1-q^t},
 \qquad a_u=-\frac{\bar c_{u0}(\nu)}{1-q^u}.
\]
The interpolation conditions ask for a scalar between every lower bound $a_u$ and every upper bound $b_t$.
Assume cyclic monotonicity and consider the finite cycle
\[
 0,t,2t,\ldots,ut,(t-1)u,(t-2)u,\ldots,u,0.
\]
It first takes $u$ steps of length $t$ upwards, then $t$ steps of length $u$ downwards.
Repeated indices are allowed.
Using $\bar c_{i+k,j+k}=q^k\bar c_{ij}$, its cost is
\[
 \bar c_{0t}\sum_{j=0}^{u-1}q^{jt}
 +\bar c_{u0}\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}q^{ju}
 =(1-q^{tu})(b_t-a_u)\geq0.
\]
Since $q<1$, this gives $a_u\leq b_t$ for every $t,u\geq1$.
Consequently,
\[
 \varphi_0:=\sup_{u\geq1}a_u
\]
is finite, lies above every $a_u$, and lies below every $b_t$.
This proves \eqref{eq:star-feasible}.

Conversely, suppose \eqref{eq:star-feasible} holds and set $\varphi_i=q^i\varphi_0$.
For $j>i$, apply the upper bound in \eqref{eq:star-feasible} with $t=j-i$ to obtain
\[
 \varphi_i-\varphi_j
 =q^i(1-q^{j-i})\varphi_0
 \leq q^i\bar c_{0,j-i}=\bar c_{ij}.
\]
For $i>j$, the lower bound in \eqref{eq:star-feasible} with $t=i-j$ gives the same inequality.
The case $i=j$ is immediate.
Summing these inequalities around any finite cycle proves cyclic monotonicity.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:closed-rate-problem}
For $s>1$, the set $\mathcal Q(s)$ in \eqref{eq:feasible-factors} is nonempty and has a largest element.
Every feasible factor $q$ satisfies $q\leq1-1/s$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Fix $(q,\nu,\varphi_0)$ satisfying \eqref{eq:star-feasible}.
Since $|z|=\sqrt q<1$, the edge formula gives the uniform limits
\[
 c_{t0}(\theta)\longrightarrow0,
 \qquad c_{0t}(\theta)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Re}d_s(z).
\]
Writing $m=\int\operatorname{Re}d_s(z)\,d\nu$, the interpolation conditions imply
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:star-origin-bounds}
 0\leq\varphi_0\leq m,
 \qquad \bar c_{01}(\nu)\geq(1-q)\varphi_0\geq0.
\end{equation}
These bounds also justify including the optimum, with $(\sigma,r,\varphi)=(0,0,0)$.
Indeed, for the prescribed trajectory they give
$0\leq\varphi_i=q^i\varphi_0\leq q^im=\langle r_i,\sigma_i\rangle$, precisely the two interpolation inequalities with the optimum.

We show directly that $q\geq1-1/s$ is impossible.
Put $a=1-1/s\in(0,1)$.
Expanding $c_{02}(\theta)$, which compares iterations $0$ and $2$, gives
\[
 \frac{c_{02}(\theta)}{s^2-1}
 =a^2-1+\frac{2a(1-q)}{\sqrt q}\cos\theta
       +\frac{q^2-a^2}{q}\cos(2\theta).
\]
If $q\geq a$, both cosine coefficients are nonnegative.
Since $\sqrt q\geq\sqrt a>a$, it follows that
\[
 c_{02}(\theta)\leq c_{02}(0)
 =-(s^2-1)(1-q)(1-a/\sqrt q)^2<0.
\]
This contradicts
$\bar c_{02}(\nu)\geq(1-q^2)\varphi_0\geq0$, which follows from
\eqref{eq:star-feasible} and \eqref{eq:star-origin-bounds}.
Thus every feasible factor satisfies $q<1-1/s$.

The choice $q=q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)$ and $\nu=\delta_0$ has $d_s(z)=0$ and all cycle costs zero, so it is feasible with $\varphi_0=0$.
To prove attainment, restrict $q$ to the compact interval $[q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s),1-1/s]$ and impose all cycle inequalities on $(q,\nu)$.
For each fixed cycle its cost is jointly continuous in $q$ and $\theta$ on this interval times $[0,\pi]$.
Each averaged inequality therefore defines a closed set under convergence of $q$ and weak convergence of $\nu$.
Their intersection is a nonempty closed subset of the compact product
$[q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s),1-1/s]\times\mathcal P([0,\pi])$.
The continuous objective $q$ attains its maximum there.
Lemma~\ref{lem:stars} supplies the corresponding scalar $\varphi_0$.
Since smaller factors cannot improve on $q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)$, this is the largest feasible factor.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:disk-radial-reweighting}
Let $\mu$ be feasible for \eqref{eq:disk-rate-lp} with $m=J(\mu)>0$.
Define $d\eta(z)=|z|^2\,d\mu(z)/m$, which gives greater relative weight to larger values of $|z|^2$.
Then $\eta$ is feasible for the same problem and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:disk-variance-improvement}
 J(\eta)-J(\mu)
 =\frac{\int |z|^4\,d\mu-m^2}{m}
 =\frac{\operatorname{Var}_\mu(|z|^2)}{m}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Since $|z|^2\geq0$ and $\int |z|^2\,d\mu=m$, $\eta$ is a probability measure on $D_s$.
For every cycle $\gamma$, the shift identity \eqref{eq:disk-cost-shift} and feasibility of $\mu$ give
\[
 \int\mathcal C_\gamma^s\,d\eta
 =\frac1m\int |z|^2\mathcal C_\gamma^s\,d\mu
 =\frac1m\int\mathcal C_{\gamma+1}^s\,d\mu\geq0.
\]
Also $J(\eta)=\int |z|^4\,d\mu/m$, which proves the identity.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Probability measures on the compact disk form a weakly compact set.
Every cycle cost is continuous, so the feasible set of \eqref{eq:disk-rate-lp} is closed.
It is nonempty: $\delta_{\alpha_s}$ has zero costs and objective $\alpha_s^2=q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)$.
Hence the feasible set is nonempty and compact under weak convergence.
Since $z\mapsto|z|^2$ is continuous and bounded on $D_s$, the objective $J$ is continuous under weak convergence.
The extreme value theorem, applied in this topology, therefore gives a maximum $m\geq q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)>0$.
If an optimal measure $\mu$ had $\operatorname{Var}_\mu(|z|^2)>0$, Lemma~\ref{lem:disk-radial-reweighting} would give a feasible measure with a strictly larger objective value.
Thus every optimal measure $\mu$ satisfies $\operatorname{Var}_\mu(|z|^2)=0$ and is supported on the circle $|z|^2=m$, with $0<m\leq\alpha_s<1$.

We now prove the fixed-factor equivalence. For any $q\in\mathcal Q(s)$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:closed-rate-problem} gives $q\leq\alpha_s$.
Choose an angular measure $\nu$ satisfying \eqref{eq:star-feasible}.
Replace each angle $\theta$ by the point $\sqrt q\,e^{i\theta}$ to obtain a probability measure $\mu$ on the corresponding circle in $D_s$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:stars}, $\nu$ satisfies all cycle inequalities.
The positive scaling in \eqref{eq:disk-cycle-cost} then makes $\mu$ feasible for \eqref{eq:disk-rate-lp}, with objective value $q$.
Conversely, let $\mu$ be feasible for \eqref{eq:disk-rate-lp} and supported on $|z|^2=q$ for some $q\in(0,1)$.
Map each point $z=\sqrt q\,e^{\pm i\theta}$ to $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ to obtain an angular probability measure $\nu$.
Conjugation preserves every cost, and division by the common positive factor $q^2/(s^2-1)$ gives all cycle inequalities for $\nu$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:stars} then gives a scalar $\varphi_0$ satisfying \eqref{eq:star-feasible}, so $q\in\mathcal Q(s)$.
This argument remains valid at $q=q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)$, even where $d_s(z)=0$, because it does not divide by $d_s(z)$.

Applying this equivalence to an optimizing disk measure gives
$m\in\mathcal Q(s)$.
Conversely, for every $q\in\mathcal Q(s)$, the construction above gives a feasible measure for \eqref{eq:disk-rate-lp} with objective value $q$, so $q\leq m$.
Thus $m=\max\mathcal Q(s)$, proving \eqref{eq:qcyc}.
The lower bound follows from $q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)\in\mathcal Q(s)$, and the upper bound
from Lemma~\ref{lem:closed-rate-problem}.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:disk-finite-dual}
Each $q_{\mathrm{rel},n}(s)$ is attained, and
\[
 \alpha_s=q_{\mathrm{rel},0}(s)\geq q_{\mathrm{rel},1}(s)\geq\cdots\geqq_\star(s),
 \qquad \lim_{n\to\infty}q_{\mathrm{rel},n}(s)=q_\star(s).
\]
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:disk-polynomial-dual}
 q_\star(s)=\inf\left\{b:
 \begin{array}{l}
  \Gamma_0\subset\Gamma\text{ finite},\quad\lambda_\gamma\geq0,\\
  b-|z|^2-\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_0}
       \lambda_\gamma\mathcal C_\gamma^s(z)\geq0,
       \quad z\in D_s
 \end{array}\right\}.
\end{equation}
The dual constraint requires a real polynomial in $\operatorname{Re}z$ and $\operatorname{Im}z$ to be nonnegative on $D_s$.
For every $b>q_\star(s)$ such a polynomial can be strictly positive on $D_s$.
No finite dual attainment at $b=q_\star(s)$ is asserted.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Each finite-index feasible set is closed in the compact probability space
and contains $\delta_{\alpha_s}$, so its maximum exists. The sets are
nested. A weak limit of optimizers along $n\to\infty$ satisfies every fixed
cycle constraint, since that constraint holds eventually. Continuity of
$J$ proves that the decreasing limit of $q_{\mathrm{rel},n}(s)$ equals $q_\star(s)$.

Integrating any polynomial inequality in \eqref{eq:disk-polynomial-dual}
against a feasible measure gives $J(\mu)\leq b$. Conversely, fix
$b>q_\star(s)$. Compactness supplies finitely many cycle constraints whose
intersection with $J(\mu)\geq b$ is already empty. Their compact convex
moment image is disjoint from
$\{t\in\mathbf R:t\geq b\}\times\mathbf R_+^{|\Gamma_0|}$.
Strict separation gives nonnegative coefficients
$(\lambda_0,(\eta_\gamma)_{\gamma\in\Gamma_0})$ with
\[
 \lambda_0|z|^2+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_0}\eta_\gamma
           \mathcal C_\gamma^s(z)<\lambda_0b,
 \quad z\in D_s.
\]
The coefficient $\lambda_0$ is positive: otherwise evaluating at
$z=\alpha_s$, where all cycle costs vanish, contradicts strictness.
Division by $\lambda_0$ proves the claim.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:strict-alternative}
Fix $s>1$ and $q\in(q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s),1)$.
For a finite cycle $\gamma=(i_0,i_1,\ldots,i_m=i_0)$, define the sum of the interpolation terms \eqref{eq:edge-cost} by
\[
 c_\gamma(\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}c_{i_j i_{j+1}}(\theta).
\]
Exactly one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Every finite list of cycles $\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_n$ admits angles $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_m\in[0,\pi]$ and weights $a_i\geq0$, with $1\leq m\leq n+1$, satisfying
 \[
  \sum_{i=1}^m a_i=1,
  \qquad
  \sum_{i=1}^m a_i c_{\gamma_j}(\theta_i)\geq0,
  \qquad j=1,\ldots,n.
 \]
 \item There are finitely many cycles $\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_n$
 and weights $w_j\geq0$ such that
 \[
  \sum_{j=1}^nw_j
  c_{\gamma_j}(\theta)<0,
  \qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi.
 \]
\end{enumerate}
The angles and weights in alternative~1 may depend on the selected cycles.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
For any fixed list $\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_n$, consider the cost vectors
\[
 (c_{\gamma_1}(\theta),\ldots,c_{\gamma_n}(\theta)),
 \qquad \theta\in[0,\pi].
\]
Their convex hull is compact because the cycle costs are continuous.
By Carath\'eodory's theorem, every point in this hull is a convex combination of at most $n+1$ cost vectors.
Thus the hull intersects $\mathbf R_+^n$ exactly when the inequalities in alternative~1 hold for the selected cycles.

If alternative~1 fails, choose a list for which the hull and $\mathbf R_+^n$ are disjoint.
The separating hyperplane theorem gives a vector $w$ such that
\[
 \max_{\theta\in[0,\pi]}\sum_{j=1}^n w_jc_{\gamma_j}(\theta)
 <\inf_{v\in\mathbf R_+^n}w^Tv.
\]
The vector $w$ must be nonnegative, since a negative component would make the infimum $-\infty$.
The infimum is therefore zero, which gives alternative~2.

If both alternatives held, choose angles and weights from alternative~1 for the cycles in alternative~2.
Then
\[
 0\leq\sum_{j=1}^n w_j\sum_{i=1}^m a_i c_{\gamma_j}(\theta_i)
 =\sum_{i=1}^m a_i\sum_{j=1}^n w_j c_{\gamma_j}(\theta_i)<0,
\]
a contradiction.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:finite-optimal-support}
Fix $s>1$ and suppose $q_\star(s)>q_{\mathrm{quad}}(s)$.
There are a probability measure $\nu_\star$ supported on finitely many angles in $[0,\pi]$ and a real scalar $\varphi_0^\star$ that satisfy \eqref{eq:star-feasible} at $q=q_\star(s)$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Write $q_\star=q_\star(s)$ and choose an optimal pair $(\nu_\star,\varphi_0^\star)$.
We construct a function that vanishes on the support of $\nu_\star$ and has only finitely many zeros.
Since $q$ varies in this proof, we write $c_{ij}(q,\theta)$ for the term in \eqref{eq:edge-cost}, evaluated at $z=\sqrt q\,e^{i\theta}$ with $s$ fixed.
For $t\geq1$, put
\[
 u_t(q,\theta)=\frac{c_{0t}(q,\theta)}{1-q^t},
 \qquad v_t(q,\theta)=\frac{c_{t0}(q,\theta)}{1-q^t},
 \qquad d(q,\theta)=\operatorname{Re}d_s(\sqrt q\,e^{i\theta}).
\]
Here $u_t$ and $v_t$ compare iterations $0$ and $t$ in the two possible orders.
At $q_\star$, the interpolation inequalities give
$\int u_t\,d\nu_\star\geq\varphi_0^\star$ and
$\int v_t\,d\nu_\star\geq-\varphi_0^\star$.

Choose $q_\star<q_+<1$ and $q_n\downarrow q_\star$ with $q_\star<q_n\leq q_+$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:strict-alternative} and the compactness argument above, there is a strict cycle separator at every $q_n$.
The interpolation term $c_{ij}(q,\theta)$ in \eqref{eq:edge-cost} compares iterations $i$ and $j$ and satisfies
\[
 c_{ij}(q,\theta)=
 \begin{cases}
  (q^i-q^j)u_{j-i}(q,\theta), & i<j,\\
  (q^j-q^i)v_{i-j}(q,\theta), & i>j,\\
  0, & i=j.
 \end{cases}
\]
For a cycle $\gamma=(i_0,\ldots,i_m=i_0)$, the differences telescope to $\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(q^{i_k}-q^{i_{k+1}})=q^{i_0}-q^{i_m}=0$.
Since $0<q<1$, the positive differences are the coefficients of the $u_t$ terms, and the absolute values of the negative differences are the coefficients of the $v_t$ terms.
Their totals therefore agree for each cycle, and hence for every nonnegative weighted sum of cycles.
For each $n$, choose one strict separator at $q_n$ and expand its cycle costs using the displayed formula for $c_{ij}$.
Collect the coefficients of each $u_t(q_n,\theta)$ and $v_t(q_n,\theta)$.
Their common total is positive, since a zero total would make the separator identically zero.
Define $a_{t,n}$ and $b_{t,n}$ as these respective coefficients divided by their common total.
They are nonnegative, only finitely many are nonzero for each $n$, and
\[
 \sum_{t\geq1}a_{t,n}=\sum_{t\geq1}b_{t,n}=1.
\]
These coefficients depend on the separator chosen at $q_n$ and remain fixed as $q$ varies.
For $q\in[q_\star,q_+]$ and $\theta\in[0,\pi]$, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lag-separator-limit}
 w_n(q,\theta)
 =\sum_{t\geq1}a_{t,n}u_t(q,\theta)
 +\sum_{t\geq1}b_{t,n}v_t(q,\theta).
\end{equation}
At $q=q_n$, this is the chosen separator divided by its positive normalizing constant, so $w_n(q_n,\theta)<0$ on $[0,\pi]$.
At $q=q_\star$, the interpolation inequalities and the two coefficient sums give $\int w_n(q_\star,\theta)\,d\nu_\star\geq\varphi_0^\star-\varphi_0^\star=0$.
Since $\sum_t a_{t,n}=1$, we can also write
\[
 w_n(q,\theta)=d(q,\theta)
 +\sum_{t\geq1}a_{t,n}\bigl(u_t(q,\theta)-d(q,\theta)\bigr)
 +\sum_{t\geq1}b_{t,n}v_t(q,\theta).
\]

We next bound $u_t-d$ and $v_t$ to justify taking a limit in $w_n$.
We also bound their derivatives with respect to $\theta$ to control the Taylor remainder of the limiting function later in the proof.
Throughout this calculation, $q$ is held fixed when differentiating.
With $z=\sqrt q\,e^{i\theta}$, the definitions and \eqref{eq:edge-cost} give
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 u_t(q,\theta)-d(q,\theta)
 &=\frac{q^t d(q,\theta)-\operatorname{Re}[\overline{d_s(z)}\bar z^t]}{1-q^t},\\
 v_t(q,\theta)
 &=\frac{q^t d(q,\theta)-\operatorname{Re}[\overline{d_s(z)}z^t]}{1-q^t}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
To identify the cosine terms in these expressions, write \eqref{eq:nag-transfer} as
\[
 d_s(z)=-(s^2-1)\left(1-\frac{2(1-1/s)}{z}
 +\frac{(1-1/s)^2}{z^2}\right).
\]
Since $\operatorname{Re}(e^{ik\theta})=\cos(k\theta)$ and $\cos(-k\theta)=\cos(k\theta)$, both $u_t-d$ and $v_t$ are finite sums of $\cos(k\theta)$ with integer indices $0\leq k\leq t+2$.
For any finite sum $h(\theta)=\sum_k p_k\cos(k\theta)$, the triangle inequality gives $|h(\theta)|\leq\sum_k|p_k|$ at every angle.
The combined sum of the absolute values of the cosine coefficients of $u_t-d$ and $v_t$ is at most
\[
 \frac{2(s^2-1)(q^t+q^{t/2})}{1-q^t}
 \left(1+\frac{1-1/s}{\sqrt q}\right)^2
 \leq c r_+^t,
 \qquad q\in[q_\star,q_+],
\]
where
\[
 r_+=\sqrt{q_+}<1,\qquad
 c=\frac{4(s^2-1)}{1-q_+}
 \left(1+\frac{1-1/s}{\sqrt{q_\star}}\right)^2.
\]
Each differentiation multiplies the absolute value of a sine or cosine term's coefficient by its index $k$.
After $j$ derivatives, the absolute value of each coefficient is therefore multiplied by at most $(t+2)^j$.
Applying the same triangle inequality gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lag-geometric-tails}
 \left|\frac{d^j}{d\theta^j}(u_t-d)\right|
 +\left|\frac{d^j}{d\theta^j}v_t\right|
 \leq c r_+^t(t+2)^j,
 \qquad j\geq0,\quad\theta\in\mathbf R.
\end{equation}
For $j=0$, this bounds the functions themselves and shows that $u_t-d$ and $v_t$ tend to zero uniformly in $q$ and $\theta$ on $[q_\star,q_+]\times[0,\pi]$.
The function $d$ is a linear combination of $1$, $\cos\theta$, and $\cos(2\theta)$, and satisfies $|\frac{d^j}{d\theta^j}d|\leq c2^j$.

The separator coefficients $a_{t,n}$ and $b_{t,n}$ lie in $[0,1]$.
Choose nested subsequences along which the coefficients converge for $t=1$, then for $t=2$, and so on.
Taking the $n$th element of the $n$th subsequence gives a single subsequence with $a_{t,n}\to a_t$ and $b_{t,n}\to b_t$ for every fixed $t$.
At the fixed factor $q_\star$, define the function of the angle
\[
 w_\star(\theta)=d(q_\star,\theta)
 +\sum_{t\geq1}a_t\bigl(u_t(q_\star,\theta)-d(q_\star,\theta)\bigr)
 +\sum_{t\geq1}b_tv_t(q_\star,\theta).
\]
Along the chosen subsequence, both $w_n(q_n,\theta)$ and $w_n(q_\star,\theta)$ converge to $w_\star(\theta)$ uniformly for $\theta\in[0,\pi]$.
Indeed, the bound with $j=0$ makes the sums over sufficiently large values of $t$ uniformly small, and each finite sum converges by coefficient convergence and continuity in $q$.
The limiting coefficient sums may be less than one because weights can move to larger and larger values of $t$ as $n$ increases.
The term $d$ outside the sums preserves their limiting contribution, since $u_t$ tends to $d$ and $v_t$ tends to zero as $t$ increases.
Passing to these limits in $w_n(q_n,\theta)<0$ and $\int w_n(q_\star,\theta)\,d\nu_\star\geq0$ gives
\[
 w_\star(\theta)\leq0\quad(0\leq\theta\leq\pi),
 \qquad \int w_\star(\theta)\,d\nu_\star\geq0.
\]
Since $w_\star$ is continuous and nonpositive, its nonnegative integral forces it to vanish on the support of $\nu_\star$.

At angle zero, writing $r=\sqrt q$ gives
\[
 u_t(q,0)=\frac{d_s(r)}{1+r^t},
 \qquad v_t(q,0)=-\frac{d_s(r)r^t}{1+r^t}.
\]
Since $r^t\leq r<1$ and $d_s(r)<0$, the two coefficient sums imply
$w_n(q,0)\leq d_s(r)(1-r)/(1+r)$.
Consequently,
\[
 w_\star(0)\leq d_s(\sqrt{q_\star})
 \frac{1-\sqrt{q_\star}}{1+\sqrt{q_\star}}<0.
\]

It remains to show that $w_\star$ has only finitely many zeros in $[0,\pi]$.
The same series defines $w_\star$ for every real $\theta$.
The derivative bound \eqref{eq:lag-geometric-tails} permits termwise differentiation of every order, uniformly on the real line.
Choose $b>0$ with $r_+e^b<1$.
Using $(t+2)^j\leq j!b^{-j}e^{b(t+2)}$ and summing the geometric series gives
\[
 \sup_{\theta\in\mathbf R}|w_\star^{(j)}(\theta)|
 \leq c_0j!b^{-j},
 \qquad c_0=\frac{ce^{2b}}{1-r_+e^b}.
\]
For any real expansion point $\theta_0$, Taylor's theorem therefore gives
\[
 \left|w_\star(\theta)
 -\sum_{j=0}^m\frac{w_\star^{(j)}(\theta_0)}{j!}(\theta-\theta_0)^j\right|
 \leq c_0\left(\frac{|\theta-\theta_0|}{b}\right)^{m+1}.
\]
When $|\theta-\theta_0|<b$, the right-hand side tends to zero as $m\to\infty$, so the Taylor series equals $w_\star$ on this neighborhood.
The same radius $b$ works at every real expansion point.

Suppose that $w_\star$ has infinitely many distinct zeros in $[0,\pi]$.
By compactness, we can choose a strictly monotone sequence of these zeros converging to some $\theta_0\in[0,\pi]$, possibly an endpoint.
Between consecutive zeros, Rolle's theorem gives a zero of $w_\star'$.
These zeros also converge to $\theta_0$, and the same argument applies successively to every derivative.
By continuity, $w_\star^{(j)}(\theta_0)=0$ for every $j\geq0$.
The Taylor expansion then gives $w_\star(\theta)=0$ whenever $|\theta-\theta_0|<b$.
At every point inside this interval all derivatives vanish, so a Taylor expansion there gives another interval of radius $b$ on which $w_\star$ is zero.
Repeating with expansion points spaced $b/2$ apart toward zero eventually gives $w_\star(0)=0$, contradicting the strict inequality above.
Thus $w_\star$ has only finitely many zeros in $[0,\pi]$, and the support of $\nu_\star$ is finite.

has norm at most $(1-a)^{-2}\|v\|_{\ell_2}$ by the triangle inequality.
It satisfies $v_t=u_t-2au_{t-1}+a^2u_{t-2}$ by cancellation of the coefficients.
The coefficient of $v_t$ is one, the coefficient of $v_{t-1}$ is $2a-2a=0$, and every later coefficient is $\bigl((j+1)-2j+(j-1)\bigr)a^j=0$ for $j\geq2$.
The same definition gives $g(v)_t-cv_t=-cu_t$.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cycle-convolution-coercivity}
 \sum_{t,j}k_j\langle g(v)_t-cv_t,v_{t+j}\rangle
 \leq-c_0\|v\|_{\ell_2}^2.
\end{equation}

Suppose temporarily that the sequence $\bar y$ generated by \eqref{eq:rescaled-recursion} is square summable for a given $e\in\ell_2^d$ and $\tau\in[0,1]$.
Substitute $\bar y=e+g(\bar\sigma)$ into \eqref{eq:summed-cycle-supply}.
The preceding inequality bounds the terms involving $g(\bar\sigma)-c\bar\sigma$.
Cauchy--Schwarz and the bound for the weighted shifts bound the remaining terms by $c_k\|e\|_{\ell_2}\|\bar\sigma\|_{\ell_2}$.
Thus
\[
 0\leq-c_0\|\bar\sigma\|_{\ell_2}^2
       +c_k\|e\|_{\ell_2}\|\bar\sigma\|_{\ell_2}.
\]
If $\|\bar\sigma\|_{\ell_2}>0$, divide by this norm and then use $\bar y=e+g(\bar\sigma)$.
If this norm is zero, the same bounds hold directly.
We obtain
\[
 \|\bar\sigma\|_{\ell_2}\leq\frac{c_k}{c_0}\|e\|_{\ell_2},
 \qquad
 \|\bar y\|_{\ell_2}\leq c_y\|e\|_{\ell_2},
 \qquad c_y=1+\frac{c_gc_k}{c_0}.
\]
Once the separator is fixed, these constants depend only on $s,q$.
In particular, they are independent of $\tau$, $e$, the objective, and the dimension.

We now remove this assumption by increasing $\tau$ from zero to one in finitely many steps.
Choose a positive integer $m>2c_yc_g(s^2-1)$ and set $\tau_j=j/m$ for $j=0,\ldots,m$.
We prove by induction on $j$ that, for every square-summable $e$ vanishing at negative indices, the sequence $\bar y$ generated by \eqref{eq:rescaled-recursion} at $\tau=\tau_j$ satisfies
\[
 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\|\bar y_t\|^2<\infty.
\]

\emph{Base case.}
At $\tau_0=0$, we have $\bar\sigma=0$ and $\bar y=e$, so the claim holds.

\emph{Induction step.}
Fix $1\leq j\leq m$ and assume the claim holds at $\tau_{j-1}$.
Fix any allowed $e$ and let $\bar y$ be the sequence at $\tau_j$.
We will bound $\sum_{t=0}^n\|\bar y_t\|^2$ independently of $n$.
For each integer $n\geq0$, define
\[
 u_t^{(n)}=
 \begin{cases}
  q^{-t/2}\nabla h(q^{t/2}\bar y_t),&0\leq t\leq n,\\
  0,&\text{otherwise},
 \end{cases}
 \qquad
 e^{(n)}=e+\frac1m g(u^{(n)}).
\]
Only finitely many terms of $u^{(n)}$ are nonzero, so the bound on $g$ makes $e^{(n)}$ square summable.
It also vanishes at negative indices.
The induction hypothesis therefore gives a square-summable sequence $\bar y^{(n)}$ at $\tau_{j-1}$ with $e^{(n)}$ in place of $e$.

The modification of $e$ makes the first $n+1$ terms of these two sequences agree.
Indeed, $g(u)_t$ uses only terms with indices less than $t$, so for $0\leq t\leq n$,
\[
 \bar y_t=e_t+\tau_jg(u^{(n)})_t
 =e_t^{(n)}+\tau_{j-1}g(u^{(n)})_t.
\]
The last equality uses $\tau_j=\tau_{j-1}+1/m$.
These are exactly the recurrence equations at $\tau_{j-1}$ with $e^{(n)}$ through time $n$.
They determine each term from the previous ones, so $\bar y_t^{(n)}=\bar y_t$ for $0\leq t\leq n$.

Since $\bar y^{(n)}$ is square summable, the preceding norm estimate applies to it.
Combining that estimate with the bound on $g$ and \eqref{eq:scaled-uncertainty} gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \left(\sum_{t=0}^n\|\bar y_t\|^2\right)^{1/2}
 &\leq\|\bar y^{(n)}\|_{\ell_2}
 \leq c_y\|e^{(n)}\|_{\ell_2}\\
 &\leq c_y\|e\|_{\ell_2}
 +\frac{c_yc_g(s^2-1)}m
  \left(\sum_{t=0}^n\|\bar y_t\|^2\right)^{1/2}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The coefficient multiplying the partial norm on the right is less than $1/2$.
Moving this term to the left gives, for every $n$,
\[
 \left(\sum_{t=0}^n\|\bar y_t\|^2\right)^{1/2}
 \leq2c_y\|e\|_{\ell_2}.
\]
Every partial sum of squared norms is therefore at most $4c_y^2\|e\|_{\ell_2}^2$.
These partial sums are nondecreasing and bounded, so their limit is finite.
This proves the claim at $\tau_j$ and completes the induction.

At $\tau_m=1$, the sequence $\bar y$ is square summable, and the gradient bound gives $\bar\sigma\in\ell_2^d$.
The earlier estimates for both sequences now apply and yield
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scaled-loop-gain}
 \|\bar y\|_{\ell_2}^2+\|\bar\sigma\|_{\ell_2}^2
 \leq\left(c_y^2+\frac{c_k^2}{c_0^2}\right)\|e\|_{\ell_2}^2.
\end{equation}

Return to the NAG initialization sequence $e$ derived above.
Since $a<1$, the squared norm $\|e\|_{\ell_2}^2$ is a finite multiple of $\|x_0\|^2$ depending only on $s,q$.
Finally, $s^2x_{t+1}=(s^2-1)y_t-\sigma_t$ gives
\[
 q^{-(t+1)}\|x_{t+1}\|^2
 \leq\frac{(s^2-1)^2+1}{s^4q}
 \left(\|\bar y\|_{\ell_2}^2+\|\bar\sigma\|_{\ell_2}^2\right).
\]
Combining this with \eqref{eq:scaled-loop-gain} gives a finite constant $b_1(s,q)$ such that
\[
 \|x_t\|^2\leq b_1(s,q)q^t\|x_0\|^2,
 \qquad t\geq1.
\]
Set $b(s,q)=\max\{1,b_1(s,q)\}$.
Since $b(s,q)\geq b_1(s,q)$, the bound still holds for every $t\geq1$.
At $t=0$, we have $q^0=1$ and $b(s,q)\geq1$, so the bound reduces to $\|x_0\|^2\leq b(s,q)\|x_0\|^2$, which also holds.

The proof used coordinates centered at the minimizer.
Thus the vectors denoted above by $x_t$ and $x_0$ are $x_t-x_\star$ and $x_0-x_\star$ in the original coordinates.
The bound in those coordinates is therefore
\[
 \|x_t-x_\star\|^2\leq b(s,q)q^t\|x_0-x_\star\|^2,
 \qquad t\geq0,
\]
as claimed.

\emph{Reading the modular factorizations.}
A polynomial is \emph{irreducible over $\mathbb Q$} if it cannot be factored
into two positive-degree polynomials with rational coefficients.
Rather than factor $F$ over $\mathbb Q$ directly, we reduce its integer
coefficients modulo a prime $p$ and factor using arithmetic modulo $p$.
Exact computation gives
\[
 \begin{array}{c|l}
 p&\text{degrees of the irreducible factors of }F\bmod p\\ \hline
 13&1,19\\
 107&1,1,2,3,13\\
 223&20
 \end{array}.
\]
The leading coefficients modulo these primes are $9$, $102$, and $124$,
respectively, so the degree does not drop. Each reduction is
\emph{squarefree}, meaning that no irreducible factor is repeated.

The \emph{Galois group} $\mathfrak{G}$ consists of the permutations of the complex roots
that preserve their algebraic relations over $\mathbb Q$.
Dedekind's theorem turns each row of the table into information about such
permutations: $\mathfrak{G}$ contains a permutation whose cycle lengths are precisely
the listed factor degrees~\citep[Theorem~4.28]{milne2022fields}.\footnote{The
cited theorem is stated for monic integer polynomials. With $a\doteq a_{20}$,
apply it to $a^{19}F(u/a)$, which is monic with integer coefficients and has
the same splitting field as $F$. At the three primes, $a$ is invertible,
so the change of variable and the scalar factor preserve factor degrees
and squarefreeness.}
Here cycles refer to permutations of polynomial roots, not to the
iteration-index comparisons used to construct $F$. A cycle of length $k$
sends each of $k$ roots to the next and the last back to the first, fixing
all other roots. The three rows give the following argument.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Irreducibility and transitivity.}
Modulo $223$, there is just one irreducible factor, of degree $20$.
Because the integer coefficients of $F$ have no common factor greater than
one and its leading coefficient is nonzero modulo $223$, this proves
irreducibility over $\mathbb Q$.
Consequently $\mathfrak{G}$ is \emph{transitive}: any root can be sent to any other
root by some permutation in $\mathfrak{G}$.
\item \emph{Moving pairs of roots.}
The row for $p=13$ supplies a $19$-cycle fixing the remaining root.
Its powers can send any of the other nineteen roots to any other while
keeping that root fixed. Transitivity gives the same property for any
choice of fixed root. Thus $\mathfrak{G}$ is \emph{$2$-transitive}: it can send any
ordered pair of distinct roots to any other such pair.
\item \emph{Swapping two roots.}
The row for $p=107$ supplies disjoint cycles of lengths $1,1,2,3,13$.
Applying that permutation $39=3\cdot13$ times makes the $3$- and $13$-cycles
return to their starting points, while the $2$-cycle still swaps its two
roots. The result is a \emph{transposition}, a permutation swapping just two
roots. By $2$-transitivity, relabelling this swap using elements of $\mathfrak{G}$
gives a swap of any chosen pair. Every permutation is a product of such
swaps, so $\mathfrak{G}=S_{20}$, the full symmetric group on the twenty roots.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Excluding radicals.}
The needed consequence of the Galois criterion for solvability by radicals
is this: an irreducible polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ whose Galois group is
$S_n$, with $n\geq5$, has no root expressible by
radicals~\citep[Theorem~3.28 and its following discussion; proof in Theorem~5.34]{milne2022fields}.
Irreducibility matters here: a radical expression for one root would give
such expressions for all the other roots as well.
Since $F$ is irreducible and its group is $S_{20}$,
this excludes a radical expression for $q_\dagger$.
Evaluating an everywhere-defined radical formula with rational coefficients
at the rational input $s_\dagger$ would contradict this conclusion, proving
Remark~\ref{rem:no-radical-formula}. This does not preclude specifying the
rate as the unique root of a polynomial in an explicitly given interval,
as we have done here.

The factor $q_\dagger$ is not expressible by radicals over $\mathbb Q(\tau)$

Let $L$ be the splitting field of $F$, so $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)=S_{20}$.
A transcendental $\tau$ remains transcendental over $L$. Clearing denominators
and comparing coefficients of powers of $\tau$ shows that a basis of $L$ over $\mathbb Q$
stays linearly independent over $\mathbb Q(\tau)$; likewise for
$\mathbb Q(q_\dagger)$. Thus adjoining $\tau$ preserves the irreducibility
and group $S_{20}$ of $F$, excluding radical expressions for its roots.

For a finite extension $E$, the splitting group over $E$ identifies with
$\mathfrak{G}_E=\operatorname{Gal}(L/(L\cap E))$, a subgroup of $S_{20}$ of index
$d=[L\cap E:\mathbb Q]<20$. Acting on the cosets maps $S_{20}$ to $S_d$.
Since $20!/2>d!$, the simple alternating group $A_{20}$ of even permutations
cannot inject into $S_d$. Its action is therefore trivial, so $\mathfrak{G}_E$
contains $A_{20}$. This makes $\mathfrak{G}_E$ transitive and nonsolvable: $F$ stays
irreducible over $E$, and the radical criterion again applies.

At the isolated common zero, outward-rounded evaluation gives

\emph{Galois group over $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$.}
At $\alpha_\dagger$, the roots are distinct and their group is $S_{20}$.
Each root lifts uniquely to a formal power-series root in
$\alpha-\alpha_\dagger$, with coefficients in the splitting field $L$ of $F$.
Acting coefficientwise, $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)$ permutes these
lifts faithfully because their constant terms are distinct. Thus the group
over $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ contains, and hence equals, $S_{20}$.

\emph{Allowing arbitrary complex constants.}
The discriminant
$\mathcal{D}(\alpha)\doteq\operatorname{Disc}_qH(\alpha,q)\in\mathbb Z[\alpha]$
detects collisions of roots in $q$. Exact computation gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:generic-radical-branch-certificate}
 \mathcal{D}(8)\equiv0,\qquad \mathcal{D}'(8)\equiv25\pmod{29}.
\end{equation}
These congruences imply that $\mathcal{D}$ has a simple complex zero: if every
characteristic-zero irreducible factor were repeated, reduction modulo $29$
could not produce a simple root. Since the leading $q$ coefficient is the
nonzero constant $16$, a simple discriminant zero is a simple collision of
two roots; continuation around it exchanges those roots and fixes the others.
This transposition lies in the subgroup fixing the algebraic constant field,
called the geometric group. It is normal in $S_{20}$, so contains all
conjugate transpositions and equals $S_{20}$.
Extending the algebraically closed constants from
$\overline{\mathbb Q}$ to $\mathbb C$ preserves this group.

A radical expression agreeing with a root on an open interval would give a
radical algebraic branch over that field, contradicting the radical
criterion. The rational change $\alpha=1-1/s$ proves the claim.
