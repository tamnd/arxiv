<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:complete}
For every positive integer $q$, every Dirichlet character $\chi\bmod q$,
and every integer $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complete}
 \left|\sum_{b\bmod q}^{*}g_q(b)\chi(b)\mathrm{e}(-nb/q)\right|
 \le2\sqrt{(q,n)}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
First, for an arbitrary character $\theta\bmod q$, put
\[
 T_q(\theta,n)=\sum_{b\bmod q}^{*}\theta(b)\mathrm{e}(-nb/q).
\]
Let $d=(q,n)$ and $Q_0=q/d$. Reduction of units modulo $q$ to units
modulo $Q_0$ is surjective. Each fibre has
$\varphi(q)/\varphi(Q_0)$ elements. The additive character is constant
on each fibre. If $\theta$ is nontrivial on the kernel of this
reduction, its sum on every fibre is zero. Otherwise $\theta$
is induced from a character $\theta_0\bmod Q_0$, and
\[
 T_q(\theta,n)=\frac{\varphi(q)}{\varphi(Q_0)}
                  T_{Q_0}(\theta_0,n/d).
\]
Since $(n/d,Q_0)=1$, the last sum has the same absolute value as
the ordinary Gauss sum of $\theta_0$. By \eqref{eq:induced} this
is at most $\sqrt{Q_0}$. The case $Q_0=1$ has the same bound
by the usual one-residue convention. Since
$\varphi(q)/\varphi(Q_0)\le d$, it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-gauss}
 |T_q(\theta,n)|\le d\sqrt{Q_0}=\sqrt{q(q,n)}.
\end{equation}

For odd $q$, the quadratic Gauss-sum evaluation gives
\[
 g_q(b)=\epsilon_q q^{-1/2}\left(\frac bq\right),
 \qquad |\epsilon_q|=1\quad((b,q)=1).
\]
This includes $q=1$ by convention. Thus \eqref{eq:general-gauss}
proves \eqref{eq:complete} with constant $1$ when $q$ is odd.
If $q\equiv2\pmod4$, the sum vanishes by \eqref{eq:gauss}.

It remains to handle $4\mid q$. For odd $b$, define
\[
 \chi_4(b)=(-1)^{(b-1)/2},\qquad
 \chi_8(b)=(-1)^{(b^2-1)/8},\qquad
 \chi_{-8}(b)=\chi_4(b)\chi_8(b).
\]
The explicit evaluations at powers of $2$ are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-adic}
 g_{2^v}(b)=2^{-v/2}
 \begin{cases}
 1+i\chi_4(b),&v\ge2\text{ even},\\
 \chi_8(b)+i\chi_{-8}(b),&v\ge3\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For example, the base cases follow by summing the squares modulo
$4$ and $8$. For $v\ge4$, splitting into even and odd arguments
and pairing the odd arguments gives
$G(b,2^v)=2G(b,2^{v-2})$, proving the formulas by induction.
The odd-modulus evaluation and these formulas are also recorded
in \cite[Lemmas 5.1 and 6.1]{MP}.

Write $q=2^v u$ with $u$ odd. The Chinese remainder theorem gives
\[
 G(b,q)=G(bu,2^v)G(b2^v,u).
\]
Combining the odd-modulus formula with \eqref{eq:two-adic} shows
that, on the units modulo $q$,
\[
 g_q(b)=q^{-1/2}\bigl(c_1\eta_1(b)+c_2\eta_2(b)\bigr),
 \qquad |c_1|=|c_2|=1,
\]
for two Dirichlet characters $\eta_1,\eta_2\bmod q$.
The constant factors resulting from the fixed unit arguments $u$
and $2^v$ have absolute value $1$.
Apply \eqref{eq:general-gauss} to $\chi\eta_1$ and $\chi\eta_2$
and divide by $\sqrt q$. This proves \eqref{eq:complete}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:divisor}
For every $\nu>0$, uniformly for integers $X<n\le2X$ and real
$1\le Y_0\le X$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:divisor}
 \sum_{s\le Y_0}\frac{\sqrt{(s,n)}}s\ll_\nu X^\nu.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The inequality $\sqrt{(s,n)}\le\sum_{d\mid(s,n)}\sqrt d$ gives
\[
 \sum_{s\le Y_0}\frac{\sqrt{(s,n)}}s
 \le\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\d\le Y_0}}\sqrt d
                    \sum_{\substack{s\le Y_0\\d\mid s}}\frac1s
 \le (1+\log Y_0)\sum_{d\mid n}d^{-1/2}.
\]
The last divisor sum is at most the number of divisors of $n$,
which is $\ll_\nu n^\nu$ with any prescribed positive loss.
Decrease that loss to absorb the logarithm and use $n\le2X$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:support}
Let $\psi$ be primitive of conductor $r$, and let $\chi\bmod q$
be the character it induces, where $r\mid q$. If
\[
 \tau(\overline\chi)
 \sum_{b\bmod q}^{*}g_q(b)\chi(b)\mathrm{e}(-nb/q)\ne0,
\]
then $\psi$ is admissible for $n$.
Consequently, writing $d$ for the odd part of $(r,n)$ and $t=r/d$,
one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forced-component}
 d\mid n,\quad d\text{ odd and squarefree},\quad(d,t)=1,
 \quad\psi=\lambda_d\psi_t,\qquad
 \lambda_d=(\cdot/d),
\end{equation}
where $\psi_t$ is primitive of conductor $t$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gcd-weight}
 \sqrt{\frac{(r,n)}r}\le\frac{\sqrt8}{\sqrt t}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By \eqref{eq:induced}, nonvanishing of $\tau(\overline\chi)$
requires $q=rs$ with $s$ squarefree and $(r,s)=1$.
If $q\equiv2\pmod4$, the complete square sum vanishes, so this
case cannot occur.

The proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:complete} expresses $g_q(b)$ as
$q^{-1/2}$ times a linear combination of at most two characters
$\eta_j\bmod q$. At an odd prime power $p^v\Vert q$, each $\eta_j$
has local component $(\cdot/p)^v$. Its local component at $2$
has conductor at most $8$. A nonzero complete residue sum requires
at least one of the sums $T_q(\chi\eta_j,n)$ to be nonzero.
The fibre argument in that lemma implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factor-condition}
 T_q(\theta,n)\ne0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \theta\text{ factors through modulus }q/(q,n).
\end{equation}

Suppose an odd prime $p$ divides both $r$ and $n$, and write
$p^v\Vert r$. Since $(r,s)=1$, also $p^v\Vert q$.
If $v\ge2$, the local component of $\psi$ is primitive modulo
$p^v$, whereas the twisting character has conductor at most $p$.
Their product is still primitive modulo $p^v$: if it factored
modulo $p^{v-1}$, multiplying by the inverse twist would show that
$\psi$ did too. This contradicts \eqref{eq:factor-condition}, since
$p\mid n$. Therefore $v=1$.
In this case \eqref{eq:factor-condition} forces the twisted local
character to be principal, so the local component of $\psi$ equals
$(\cdot/p)$. This proves the first admissibility condition.

If $n$ is even and $2^v\Vert r$ with $v\ge4$, the same argument
applies: the local component of $\psi$ has conductor $2^v$, while
the twist has conductor at most $8$, which divides $2^{v-1}$.
Their product cannot factor modulo $2^{v-1}$, again contradicting
\eqref{eq:factor-condition}. This proves the second condition.

The odd part $d$ of $(r,n)$ is therefore squarefree and coprime
to $t=r/d$. The components at its primes are fixed by the first
condition. Chinese remainder factorization of a primitive character
then gives \eqref{eq:forced-component}; the component $\psi_t$
is primitive. Here $\lambda_1$ denotes the character of conductor $1$.
Finally, the $2$-part of $(r,n)$ is at most $8$ (and equals $1$
if $n$ is odd). Thus $(r,n)\le8d$, proving \eqref{eq:gcd-weight}.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:restricted}
Fix a group $I$ of zeros, and abbreviate $a=a_I$.
For $1\le K\le P$ and every $\nu>0$, uniformly for integers
$X<n\le2X$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:restricted}
 \sum_{r\le K}\sqrt{\frac{(r,n)}r}
 \sum_{\substack{\psi\bmod r\ \mathrm{primitive}\\
                 \psi\ \mathrm{admissible\ for}\ n}}
 Z_{\psi,I}
 \ll_\nu X^\nu K^{3a/4}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
For each character in the sum, use the unique decomposition in
\eqref{eq:forced-component}. Fix $d$ and a dyadic block $T\le t<2T$.
By \eqref{eq:gcd-weight}, the contribution of this block is at most
$\sqrt8\,T^{-1/2}V_{d,T}$, where we may enlarge to
\[
 V_{d,T}=
 \sum_{\substack{T\le t<2T\\dt\le K\\(d,t)=1}}
 \sum_{\psi_t\bmod t}^{*}Z_{\lambda_d\psi_t,I}.
\]
This enlargement drops further admissibility conditions only after
the weight has been bounded for the original characters.

There are two independent bounds for $V_{d,T}$.
Since $Z_{\psi,I}\ll\log(r+2)$ and
$\sum_{T\le t<2T}\varphi(t)\ll T^2$, ordinary zero counting gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:restricted-trivial}
 V_{d,T}\ll T^2\log(K+2).
\end{equation}
On the other hand, for fixed $d$, the characters $\lambda_d\psi_t$
are distinct primitive characters of conductors $dt\le K$.
Primitivity follows from $(d,t)=1$ and primitivity of both factors;
the map $\psi_t\mapsto\lambda_d\psi_t$ is injective.
Thus \eqref{eq:band-density} gives, for every small $\xi>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:restricted-density}
 V_{d,T}\ll_\xi K^{a+\xi}.
\end{equation}
Combine \eqref{eq:restricted-trivial} and
\eqref{eq:restricted-density}. Absorbing logarithms and a suitably
small $\xi$ into $X^\nu$, a block contributes at most
\[
 \ll_\nu X^\nu\min\left(T^{3/2},K^aT^{-1/2}\right)
 \le X^\nu K^{3a/4}.
\]
The last inequality follows by splitting at $T=K^{a/2}$:
use the first bound below that threshold and the second above it.
There are $O(\log(2K))$ blocks and at most $\tau(n)$ possible
squarefree odd divisors $d$ of $n$. Since $\tau(n)\ll_\xi X^\xi$
for any $\xi>0$, choosing the auxiliary losses sufficiently small
proves \eqref{eq:restricted} with any prescribed $\nu>0$.
The conductor $1$ is included in the block $d=t=1$.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:twisted-density}
Let $K\ge2$, $1\le T\le K$, $d$ be odd and squarefree, and
$\sigma_0\le\sigma\le7/8$. Put $b=(1-\sigma)/(2-\sigma)$.
For every $\nu>0$, uniformly in $d,T,K$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twisted-density}
 \sum_{\chi\in\mathcal C_d(T,K)}Z_\sigma(\chi)
 \ll_\nu K^\nu\bigl((K+T^2)T^4\bigr)^b.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We may suppose the family is nonempty. Remove the character of
conductor $1$ during zero detection. Its weighted zero count is
$O(1)$, so it can be restored at the end.

For this family the large-sieve constant is $T^2+M$, with an
absolute change of constant for $t<2T$. Indeed, in
\eqref{eq:ordinary-LS} replace $c_m$ by $c_m\lambda_d(m)$
and then restrict to the allowed $t$. Since $|\lambda_d(m)|\le1$,
this does not increase the squared norm of the coefficients.
Consequently the hybrid constant at height $H\ge2$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twisted-hybrid}
 \Delta_H(M)\ll_\nu (KHM)^\nu(T^2H+M).
\end{equation}
The same observation applies to mollifier coefficients and to the
Dirichlet-polynomial coefficients used in zero detection. Their lengths
are bounded by a fixed power of $KH$, so the factor $(KHM)^\nu$
is absorbed into an arbitrarily small power of $KH$ below.

We next establish the fourth-moment bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twisted-fourth}
 \sum_{\chi\in\mathcal C_d(T,K),\ \chi\ne1}
 \int_{-H}^{H}|L(1/2+iu,\chi)|^4\,du
 \ll_\nu (KH)^\nu H(T^2+KH).
\end{equation}
Use the smooth approximate functional equation for
$L(1/2+iu,\chi)^2$. If $r$ is the conductor of $\chi$, the
analytic conductor of this square is $\asymp r^2(1+|u|)^2$;
both Dirichlet polynomials therefore have length
$\ll (KH)^{1+\xi}$, for arbitrarily small fixed $\xi>0$,
with a negligible tail. Their coefficients, before the smooth weights,
are
\[
 \tau(m)\lambda_d(m)\psi_t(m)m^{-1/2-iu},
 \qquad
 \tau(m)\lambda_d(m)\overline{\psi_t(m)}m^{-1/2+iu}.
\]
The factor multiplying the dual polynomial has absolute value $1$.
Separate the two parities and use Mellin inversion for the smooth
weights. On a line of real part $\xi>0$, the conductor-dependent
factors have size at most $(KH)^{O(\xi)}$; the remaining Mellin
integrals are absolutely convergent with uniform rapidly decreasing
kernels. Cauchy's inequality and \eqref{eq:ordinary-LS}, with the
common factor $\lambda_d$ absorbed into the coefficients, apply
to both polynomials. Conjugation preserves the varying character
family. Since
\[
 \sum_{m\le M}\frac{\tau(m)^2}{m}\ll\log^4(2M),
\]
their squared mean over the characters, for each fixed $|u|\le H$,
is $\ll_\nu(KH)^\nu(T^2+KH)$. Taking $\xi$ sufficiently small
and integrating over $u$ proves \eqref{eq:twisted-fourth}.
Only polynomial lengths and arbitrarily small losses are used here;
there is no assumed moment conjecture.

We give the zero-detection calculation to specify the dependence on
both parameters. It is the argument of \cite[Theorem 2.2 and its
proof]{CZ}, with \eqref{eq:twisted-hybrid} as the large-sieve input.
Put
\[
 D=T^2H,\qquad L_0=H(T^2+KH).
\]
For a mollifier length $U_0$ and a smoothing length $Y_0\ge U_0$,
both bounded by a fixed power of $KH$, that argument gives
\begin{align}
 \sum_{\chi\in\mathcal C_d(T,K),\ \chi\ne1}N(\sigma,H,\chi)
 \ll_\nu(KH)^\nu\bigg(&1+
 L_0^{1/3}(D+U_0)^{2/3}Y_0^{2(1-2\sigma)/3}\notag\\
 &+\sup_{u\ge U_0}(D+u)u^{1-2\sigma}e^{-2u/Y_0}\bigg).
 \label{eq:detector}
\end{align}
Here $U_0$ is the parameter denoted by $X$ in that reference;
the supremum harmlessly enlarges its possible dyadic block lengths.
The first nonconstant term controls zeros detected by the integral
containing $L(s,\chi)\sum_{m\le U_0}\mu(m)\chi(m)m^{-s}$;
H\"older's inequality uses the fourth moment of $L$ and the second
moment of this mollifier. The last term controls zeros detected by
the remaining Dirichlet polynomial. The separation of zeros for each
character, their multiplicities, and the enlargement of height by
$O(\log(KH))$ cost only $(KH)^\nu$. This also explains why using
$T$ in the large sieve and $K$ as the conductor bound is legitimate:
the zero-detection proof only needs the displayed uniform moment
and large-sieve inequalities.

Take
\[
 U_0=D,\qquad Y_0=(L_0D^2)^{1/(4-2\sigma)},
\]
with fixed constant factors if needed. Since $L_0\ge D$ and
$4-2\sigma<3$, we have $Y_0\ge D$. The supremum in
\eqref{eq:detector} is at most a constant times
$D^{2-2\sigma}+Y_0^{2-2\sigma}\ll Y_0^{2-2\sigma}$.
The other nonconstant term is also
\[
 L_0^{1/3}D^{2/3}Y_0^{2(1-2\sigma)/3}
       =Y_0^{2-2\sigma}=(L_0D^2)^b.
\]
It follows that the left side of \eqref{eq:detector} is
\[
 \ll_\nu (KH)^\nu
       \bigl(H^3T^4(T^2+KH)\bigr)^b
 \le (KH)^\nu H^{4b}\bigl((K+T^2)T^4\bigr)^b.
\]
Sum this over dyadic height blocks with the weight
$(1+|\gamma|)^{-10}$. The resulting height series converges,
since $4b<1$ in the stated range (and $\nu$ can be made small).
The first block is bounded using $H=2$.
Restore the conductor-$1$ contribution. This proves
\eqref{eq:twisted-density}.

\label{lem:restricted-high}
Let $I$ be an upper group, $\sigma=\sigma_I$, and
\[
 C(\sigma)=\frac{10-11\sigma}{3}.
\]
For $2\le K\le P$, uniformly for integers $X<n\le2X$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:restricted-high}
 \sum_{r\le K}\sqrt{\frac{(r,n)}r}
 \sum_{\substack{\psi\bmod r\ \mathrm{primitive}\\
                 \psi\ \mathrm{admissible\ for}\ n}}Z_{\psi,I}
 \ll_\nu X^\nu K^{C(\sigma)}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
As in Lemma~\ref{lem:restricted}, fix a squarefree odd $d\mid n$
and a dyadic block $T\le t<2T$. Its contribution is bounded by
$\sqrt8\,T^{-1/2}V_{d,T}$, with the same enlarged family as there.
Put $a=4(1-\sigma)$ and $b=(1-\sigma)/(2-\sigma)$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:twisted-density} and \eqref{eq:band-density} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-family-bounds}
 V_{d,T}\ll_\nu K^\nu
       \min\{((K+T^2)T^4)^b,K^a\}.
\end{equation}
If $T\ge K^{1/2}$, the weighted contribution is bounded by
\[
 \ll_\nu K^\nu\min\{T^{6b-1/2},K^aT^{-1/2}\}.
\]
The first expression increases with $T$ and the second decreases.
They meet at
\[
 T=K^{a/(6b)}=K^{2(2-\sigma)/3},
\]
whose exponent belongs to $[3/4,410/501]\subset[1/2,1]$.
Their common value is
\[
 K^{a-a/(12b)}=K^{(10-11\sigma)/3}=K^{C(\sigma)}.
\]
If $1\le T\le K^{1/2}$, the first bound in
\eqref{eq:two-family-bounds} gives instead
\[
 T^{-1/2}V_{d,T}\ll_\nu K^{b+\nu}T^{4b-1/2}
 \le K^{\max(b,\,3b-1/4)+\nu}.
\]
Both exponents here are less than $C(\sigma)$ throughout
$[\sigma_0,7/8]$. To check this explicitly, the functions
$C-b$ and $C-3b+1/4$ are decreasing, because
$b'(\sigma)=-(2-\sigma)^{-2}$. At $\sigma=7/8$ their values
are $1/72$ and $1/24$, respectively.
Sum over the $O(\log K)$ blocks and over at most $\tau(n)$ divisors
$d$. Absorb these factors into $X^\nu$ to obtain
\eqref{eq:restricted-high}.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:two-estimates}
For every $\nu>0$, uniformly for integers $X<n\le2X$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small-band}
 |B_{0,I}(n)|\ll_\nu X^{h-1/2+\nu}K^{3a/4}.
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:large-band}
 \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|B_{1,I}(n)|^2
 \ll_\nu X^{2h+\nu}
 \begin{cases}
 P^{a-1},&a\ge1,\\
 K^{a-1},&a<1.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
In the small-denominator term, substitute the character expansion
of $A_{q,I}$ before taking absolute values. For fixed $q$, the
integration interval and $E_{\chi^*,I}(\beta)$ do not depend on
the reduced residue $b$. We may therefore apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:complete} to the complete residue sum, retaining
its additive phase.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:support}, only characters induced by primitive
characters admissible for $n$ can contribute.

For $q=rs$, the induced Gauss sum vanishes unless $s$ is squarefree
and $(r,s)=1$. In the other cases its magnitude is $\sqrt r$.
Thus the coefficient of the primitive character is bounded by
\[
 \frac{2\sqrt r}{\varphi(rs)}\sqrt{(rs,n)}
 \ll_\nu X^\nu\sqrt{\frac{(r,n)}r}\frac{\sqrt{(s,n)}}s,
\]
where we used $(rs,n)=(r,n)(s,n)$ and
$1/\varphi(q)\ll_\nu X^\nu/q$ for $q\le P$.
The band estimate \eqref{eq:band-linear} and the integral bound
\[
 \int_\mathbb R |W(\beta)|(1+X|\beta|)^{10}\,d\beta\ll X^{-1/2}
\]
therefore give
\[
 |B_{0,I}(n)|\ll_\nu X^{h-1/2+\nu}
 \sum_{r\le K}\sqrt{\frac{(r,n)}r}
 \sum_{\substack{\psi\bmod r\ \mathrm{primitive}\\
                 \psi\ \mathrm{admissible\ for}\ n}}Z_{\psi,I}
               \sum_{s\le K/r}\frac{\sqrt{(s,n)}}s.
\]
Dropping the squarefree and coprimality restrictions on $s$
increases this upper bound. After Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor}, we have
the useful general bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small-reduction}
 |B_{0,I}(n)|\ll_\nu X^{h-1/2+\nu}
 \sum_{r\le K}\sqrt{\frac{(r,n)}r}
 \sum_{\substack{\psi\bmod r\ \mathrm{primitive}\\
                 \psi\ \mathrm{admissible\ for}\ n}}Z_{\psi,I}.
\end{equation}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:restricted} and rename the arbitrarily small
losses to prove \eqref{eq:small-band}. The bound is uniform for
every integer $X<n\le2X$; no further exceptional integers have
been excluded. For upper groups we will apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:restricted-high} to \eqref{eq:small-reduction} instead.

For the other estimate, disjointness of the arcs, Parseval,
\eqref{eq:gauss}, and \eqref{eq:orthog} give
\[
 \sum_n|B_{1,I}(n)|^2
 \ll\sum_{K<q\le P}\frac1{q\varphi(q)}
 \sum_{\chi\bmod q}|\tau(\chi)|^2
 \int_\mathbb R |W(\beta)|^2|E_{\chi^*,I}(\beta)|^2\,d\beta.
\]
For a fixed primitive conductor $r$, its coefficient is bounded by
\begin{align}
 \sum_{\substack{q=rs>K\\q\le P\\(r,s)=1}}
       \frac{r\mu^2(s)}{q\varphi(q)}
 &\le\frac1{\varphi(r)}\sum_{s>K/r}\frac1{s\varphi(s)}\notag\\
 &\ll\frac{\min(1,r/K)}{\varphi(r)}.\label{eq:tail-weight}
\end{align}
For completeness,
\[
 \frac{s}{\varphi(s)}=\sum_{d\mid s}\frac{\mu^2(d)}{\varphi(d)},
 \qquad \sum_{s\le y}\frac{s}{\varphi(s)}\ll y;
\]
partial summation gives the tail estimate in \eqref{eq:tail-weight}.
For $K/r<1$, convergence of the full series gives the other part
of the minimum.

Apply \eqref{eq:band-integral} and absorb logarithms and the
factor $r/\varphi(r)$ into $X^\nu$. This yields
\[
 \sum_n|B_{1,I}(n)|^2\ll_\nu X^{2h+\nu}
      \sum_{r\le P}\frac{\min(1,r/K)}r Z_{r,I}.
\]
On a dyadic block of size $R$, \eqref{eq:band-density} bounds
the last sum by $\ll_\nu X^\nu\min(1,R/K)R^{a-1}$.
Summing the blocks proves \eqref{eq:large-band}, after renaming
small losses. The conductor $r=1$ is included in the first block.
At $a=1$, absorb the additional logarithm into $X^\nu$.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:completion}
Under \eqref{hyp}, for every $\nu>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:completion}
 \sum_{\substack{X<n\le2X\\n\text{ nonsquare}}}
 |\mathfrak S(n)-\mathfrak S_P(n)|^2
 \ll_\nu X^\nu\left(X/P+\sqrt X\right).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let $L=X^A$ with $A$ a sufficiently large fixed constant.
Mellin inversion and a contour shift to $\operatorname{Re} z=-1/16$, using
\eqref{eq:F-growth} and the exponential decay of $\Gamma(z)$, give
\[
 \sum_{q\text{ odd}}\frac{\mu(q)\chi_{4n}(q)}{\varphi(q)}e^{-q/L}
 =\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(1)}F_n(z)\Gamma(z)L^z\,dz
 =\mathfrak S(n)+O(X^{-10}).
\]
The truncation at $q\le LX$ has exponentially small error.
Replacing $e^{-q/L}$ by $1$ for $q\le P$ costs
$\ll_\theta P^{1+\theta}/L$, which is also negligible after
increasing $A$. Hence, uniformly for the nonsquare $n$ in question,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-tail}
 \mathfrak S(n)-\mathfrak S_P(n)=
 \sum_{\substack{P<q\le LX\\q\text{ odd}}}
       \frac{\mu(q)e^{-q/L}}{\varphi(q)}\left(\frac nq\right)
       +O(X^{-10}).
\end{equation}

Consider a dyadic block $U<q\le2U$ of this finite sum.
Write uniquely
\[
 n=2^e d s^2,\qquad e\in\{0,1\},\quad d\text{ odd and squarefree},
 \quad s\ge1.
\]
Thus $s\le\sqrt{2X}$ and $d\le2X/(2^es^2)$.
For $c\in\{1,3\}$ restrict first to $d\equiv c\pmod4$.
Quadratic reciprocity, for odd squarefree $q$, gives
\[
 \left(\frac nq\right)=
 \left(\frac2q\right)^e
 (-1)^{((c-1)/2)((q-1)/2)}
 \left(\frac qd\right)\boldsymbol1_{(q,s)=1}.
\]
For fixed $e,c,s$, incorporate all factors other than $(q/d)$ into
\[
 b_q=\frac{\mu(q)e^{-q/L}}{\varphi(q)}
 \boldsymbol1_{U<q\le2U}\boldsymbol1_{q\le LX}
 \boldsymbol1_{(q,s)=1}
 \left(\frac2q\right)^e(-1)^{((c-1)/2)((q-1)/2)}.
\]
Then $\sum_q|b_q|^2\ll_\theta U^{-1+\theta}$.
Apply \eqref{eq:HB} with $M=2X/(2^es^2)$ and length $2U$,
enlarging the sum over $d$ to all positive odd squarefree $d\le M$.
Summing over the two values of $e$, the two residue classes $c$,
and all $s\le\sqrt{2X}$ bounds the block mean square by
\[
 \ll_\theta (XU)^\theta U^\theta
 \sum_{s\le\sqrt{2X}}\left(\frac{X}{s^2U}+1\right)
 \ll_\theta (XU)^\theta U^\theta
                 \left(\frac XU+\sqrt X\right).
\]
Enlargement may include square $n$, which is harmless for this
upper bound on the finite block. Since $U\le X^{A+1}$ with $A$
fixed, choose $\theta$ sufficiently small relative to $\nu$.
Cauchy's inequality over the $O_A(\log X)$ blocks in
\eqref{eq:finite-tail}, followed by summation of $X/U$, proves
\eqref{eq:completion}. This argument includes nonsquare integers
with arbitrarily large square factors.
