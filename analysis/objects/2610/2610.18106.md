<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
For every probability measure $\mu$ on $\Gamma$, every amenable subgroup $A$ in $\Gamma$ and every $g\in \Gamma$, $\|\lambda(\mu)\|\geq \mu(gA)$.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Replacing $\mu$ by $\delta_{g^{-1}}*\mu$ does not change the operator norm, so we may assume that $g = 1$. Let $\delta >0$ and $\nu := \frac{\mu_{|A}}{\mu(A)}$. We pick a non-zero $\xi\in L^2A\subseteq L^2\Gamma$ such that $\|\nu * \xi\|\geq( 1-\delta)\|\xi\|$. Write $\xi'(x)  =|\xi(x)|$ for every $x\in \Gamma$. Then $\|\xi'\| = \|\xi\|$ and $\|\nu*\xi'\|\geq \|\nu*\xi\|$, hence we may assume that $\xi\ge 0$. It follows that $\|\mu*\xi\|\geq \|\mu_{|A}*\xi\| \geq \mu(A)(1-\delta)\|\xi\|$. Hence upon letting $\delta\to 0$ we get $\|\lambda(\mu)\|\geq \mu(A)$ as desired.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- defin-1: body_md -->
\label{defin one percent}
		Say a discrete group $\Gamma$ has the \emph{one percent property} if and only if for every $\varepsilon >0$, there is $\delta>0$ such that the following holds:		
			 For every probability measure $\mu$ on $\Gamma$ with $\|\lambda(\mu)\| > \varepsilon$, there is an amenable subgroup $A$ of $\Gamma$ and $g\in \Gamma$, such that $\mu(gA)\geq \delta$.
<!-- end defin-1 -->
<!-- theo-1: body_md -->
\label{main theorem}
		Free groups have the one percent property.
<!-- end theo-1 -->
<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{frf}		Let $\Gamma$ be a discrete group with the one percent property. Assume that all amenable subgroups of $\Gamma$ are abelian.	If $L$ is fully residually $\Gamma$, then $L$ too has the one percent property (with the same modulus).
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- coro-1: body_md -->
\label{frfg}
		Fully residually free groups have the one percent property.
<!-- end coro-1 -->
<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{alternative} Suppose $\mathcal{C}$ is an irreducible algebraic subvariety of $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$. Let $s \ge 2$ be an integer. Then one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item there is an integer $k \ge 1$ and $v_1,v_2 \in \mathbb{C}^2\setminus\{0\}$ such that $x^kv_1 \in \mathbb{C} v_2$ for all $x \in \mathcal{C}$,
\item there are $x_1,\ldots,x_s \in \mathcal{C}$ that generate a nonabelian free subgroup of rank $s$ in $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$. 
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- prop-4: body_md -->
\label{uniform related ultrapower}
		Given $s,L\geq 1$, there is $c(s,L)\leq 2(2s)^{3L}L^{3}$ such that every $(s,L)$-related subset of $\mathbb{F}$ is contained in a union of at most $c(s,L)$ cosets of cyclic subgroups in $\mathbb{F}$.
<!-- end prop-4 -->
<!-- lemm-1: body_md -->
The free group $\mathbb{F}$ satisfies the two following conditions.
			
			$(i)$ Let $u\in \mathbb{F}$ be a non-trivial element. The centralizer of $u$, denoted by $C_\mathbb{F}(u)$, is cyclic.
			
			$(ii)$ For every $u\in\mathbb{F}$ such that $u\neq 1$ and all $g\in \mathbb{F}$, if $[u,g^{-1}ug] = 1$ then $[g,u] = 1$.
<!-- end lemm-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We refer to \cite[Chapter I, Theorem 2.19]{lyndon1977combinatorial} for $(i)$. 
			For $(ii)$ pick $u\in\mathbb{F}\backslash \{1\}$ and $g\in \mathbb{F}$ such that $[u,g^{-1}ug] = 1$. Denote by $v$ a generator of $C_\mathbb{F}(u)$, so that $g^{-1}ug = v^k$ for some $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $u = v^m$ for some $m\in\mathbb{Z}$. Thus we have $g^{-1}v^mg = v^k$. The cyclically reduced length $l(w)$ of a word $w\in\mathbb{F}$, defined by the smallest word length of a conjugate of $w$, is invariant by conjugation hence $|m|l(v) = l(v^m) = l(v^k) = |k|l(v)$ so $k = \pm m$ and $g^{-1}ug = u^{\pm 1}$. If $g^{-1}ug = u$ we are done. Otherwise, $g^{-2}ug^2= u$ hence $g^2$ commutes with $u$, so $u,g\in C_\mathbb{F}(g^2)$ and either $g^2 = 1$ so $g = 1$, or $[g,u] = 1$. In both cases $[g,u] = 1$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemm-2: body_md -->
\label{solvable abelian}
			
			Every solvable subgroup of $\mathbb{F}$ is cyclic.
<!-- end lemm-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $H$ be a solvable subgroup of $\mathbb{F}$ and pick $n\in\mathbb{N}$ minimal with $H^{(n)} =\{1\}$. Then $A = H^{(n-1)}$ is abelian non trivial. Pick $u\in A$ non-trivial. For every $g\in H$, $g^{-1}ug\in H^{(n-1)}$ hence by $(ii)$ above $[g,u] = 1$, so $H\subseteq C_{\mathbb{F}}(u)$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- defin-2: body_md -->
A discrete group $\Gamma$ is said to be uniformly exact if the following property holds.
		There is a function $k : \mathbb{N}\times (0,1)\to \mathbb{N}$ such that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ and every finite subset $F\subseteq \Gamma$, there is a finite subset $E\subseteq \Gamma$ with $|E|\leq k(|F|,\varepsilon)$ and a map $\eta : \Gamma\to \mbox{Prob\,} \Gamma$, $x \mapsto \eta^x$ with
		$$\bigcup_{x\in \Gamma}\text{supp}(\eta^x)\subseteq E \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sum_{s\in F}\sup_{x\in \Gamma} \|\eta^{sx}-s\eta^x\|_1\leq \varepsilon.$$
<!-- end defin-2 -->
<!-- theo-2: body_md -->
\label{oz}
		Fully residually free groups are uniformly exact.
<!-- end theo-2 -->
<!-- coro-2: body_md -->
\label{small relation if big norm}
		Let $\Gamma$ be a  uniformly exact discrete group, and let $s\geq 2$ be a positive integer and $b_s=2s^{-1}(s-1)^{1/2}$. If $\mu$ is the uniform measure on $x_1,\ldots,x_s \in \Gamma$, then either $\|\lambda(\mu)\|\leq 2b_s$, or there is a word $w\in\mathbb{F}_s\backslash\{1\}$ of length $\leq 2M_{1/2,s}$ such that $w(x_1,\cdots,x_s) = 1$.
<!-- end coro-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
If $\mu$ denotes the uniform probability measure on $s$ (not necessarily distinct) elements of $\Gamma$ and if these elements do not have any relation of length at most $2n$, then $\widetilde \mu^n (1)=\widetilde \nu^n(1)$, where $\nu$ is the uniform measure on the $s$ free generators of a free group of rank $s$. In particular $\widetilde \mu^n(1)\leq \|\lambda(\nu)\|^{2n} = b_s^{2n}$ by \eqref{freeradius}.
	
	On the other hand, by Theorem \ref{oz} and the remark above it, $\widetilde \mu^n (1)^{1/2n} \ge \|\lambda(\mu)\|/2$ as soon as $n \ge M_{1/2,s}$. So either $\|\lambda(\mu)\| \leq 2b_s$, or there is a relation of length at most $2M_{1/2,s}$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- lemm-3: body_md -->
\label{tits}
		Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, and $\mathcal{C}$ an irreducible algebraic variety in $\text{SL}_2(K)$. Assume that there is a non-trivial word in $s$ letters $w \in \mathbb{F}_s\backslash\{1\}$ such that $w(\mathcal{C}^s)$ lies in a coset of a Borel subgroup. Then there is an integer $k\geq 1$ such that $p_k(\mathcal{C}) := \{g^k \;|\; g\in \mathcal{C} \}$ lies in a coset of a Borel subgroup.
<!-- end lemm-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
By a standard base change argument, it suffices to prove the result over $\mathbb{C}$. For a vector $v \in \mathbb{C}^2$ write $\langle v \rangle$ for the line $\mathbb{C} v$. We prove the lemma by induction on $s\geq 1$. The case $s = 1$ is obvious. Therefore, it suffices to show that if there is a non-trivial word $w$ in $s\geq 2$ letters such that $w(\mathcal{C}^{s})$ is contained in a coset of a Borel subgroup, then the same holds for $s-1$. If $\mathcal{C}$ is zero dimensional (hence just a point) there is nothing to prove so we assume that $\dim(\mathcal{C})\geq 1$. We may label the letters such that $w = x_s^{n_1}w_1x_s^{n_2}\cdots x_s^{n_r}w_r$ where the $w_i$ are words in the letters $x_1,\cdots,x_{s-1}$ and such that $w_i$ is non-trivial for all $i\leq r-1$.
		
		We pick two non-zero vectors $v_1,v_2\in \mathbb{C}^2$ such that $w(\mathcal{C}^s)v_1\subseteq \mathbb{C} v_2$. Since $\mathcal{C}$ is unbounded, we pick a sequence $(g_n)_{n\geq 1}$ of elements of $\mathcal{C}$ such that $\|g_n\| \xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{} +\infty$. If $p_k(\mathcal{C})$ is zero dimensional for some $k\geq 1$ (as a constructible set, hence just a point because it is the image of an irreducible variety by a regular map) then the conclusion holds. So we assume it is of dimension at least $1$ for every $k$, hence unbounded. We can then arrange so that the sequence $\|g_n^{n_i}\|$ tends to $+\infty$ for all $i\in [\![1,r]\!]$. To see it, note that $\frac{1}{2}\mbox{Tr}(g^k) = T_k(\frac{1}{2}\mbox{Tr}(g))$ for all $g\in \text{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ where $T_k$ is the Chebychev polynomial of the first kind, so $g^k$ tends to infinity provided $\mbox{Tr}(g)$ does. This justifies the existence of $(g_n)$ when $\mbox{Tr}(g)$ is not constant on $\mathcal{C}$. If $\mbox{Tr}(g)$ is constant on $\mathcal{C}$ then by Cayley Hamilton we get $g^k = c_1g+c_2$ for all $g\in \mathcal{C}$, for constants $c_1,c_2$ depending on $k$ but not on $g\in\mathcal{C}$. If $c_1 = 0$, then $p_k(\mathcal{C})$ is a point, and otherwise $g^k$ tends to infinity with $g$ as desired.
		
		Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that $\frac{g_n^{n_i}}{\|g_n^{n_i}\|}$ converges to a rank one matrix $d_i$ for every $i\in[\![1,r]\!]$. Now for each $n\in \mathbb{N}$ and each $\underline{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{s-1}$ there is a scalar $\lambda(n,\overline{x})$ such that:
		\begin{equation}\label{firsteq}g_n^{n_1}w_1(\overline{x})g_n^{n_2}\cdots g_n^{n_r}w_r(\overline{x})v_1 = \lambda(n,\overline{x})v_2\end{equation}
		Dividing by the product of the $\|g_n^{n_i}\|$ and letting $n\to+\infty$, we get a scalar $\lambda(\overline{x})$ such that:
		\begin{equation}\label{secondeq}d_1w_1(\overline{x})d_2\cdots d_r w_r(\overline{x})v_1 = \lambda(\overline{x})v_2\end{equation}
		Note that $\lambda(\overline{x})$ is finite and is a regular function of $\overline{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{s-1}$ because the left hand side is. In particular, by irreducibility either $\lambda$ is identically zero or generically non-zero.
		
		Suppose first that $\lambda$ vanishes identically on $\mathcal{C}^{s-1}$. Then by \eqref{secondeq} above we see that for each $\overline{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{s-1}$ there is $i\in [\![1,r]\!]$ such that $w_i(\overline{x})\text{Im}(d_{i+1})\subseteq \ker(d_i)$ where by $\text{Im}(d_{r+1})$ we mean $\langle v_1\rangle$. By irreducibility one of those identities holds identically over $\mathcal{C}^{s-1}$, which concludes the induction step unless $w_r  = 1$, a case we postpone for now.
		
		Suppose now that $\lambda(\overline{x})$ does not vanish identically. Then $w_1(\overline{x})d_2w_2(\overline{x})\cdots d_rw_r(\overline{x})v_1$ does not vanish identically either. But it is the limit of $\frac{\|g_n^{n_1}\|\lambda(n,\overline{x})}{P_n}g_n^{-n_1}v_2$ where $P_n$ is the product of the $\|g_n^{n_i}\|$. Now we know that $\frac{\lambda(n,\overline{x})}{P_n}$ converges to $\lambda(\overline{x})$, therefore $\|g_n^{n_1}\|g_n^{-n_1}v_2$ has to converge to some non-zero vector $v_3\in\mathbb{C}^2$, and:
		$$w_1(\overline{x})d_2\cdots d_rw_r(\overline{x})v_1= \lambda(\overline{x})v_3 $$
		for generic $\overline{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{s-1}$. Hence $w_1(\mathcal{C}^{s-1})\text{Im}(d_2)\subset\langle v_3\rangle$. This concludes the induction step in this case.
		
		It remains to treat the case where $w_r = 1$, that is when the word starts and ends with a non-trivial power of $x_s$. Then instead of substituting $g_n$ in $x_s$, we substitute it into $x_{s-1}$ provided it appears in $w$ (if it does not then the induction step follows directly). This leads to:
		$$w_1'(\overline{x})g_n^{m_1}\cdots g_n^{m_{r'-1}}w'_{r'}(\overline{x})v_1 = \mu(n,\overline{x})v_2$$
		where the $m_i$ are all non-zero integers, the $w_i'$ are all non-trivial words and $\mu(n,\overline{x})$ is a scalar. Choosing $g_n$ so that the $\|g_n^{m_i}\|$ tend to $+\infty$ as before, dividing by the product of their norms and extracting as before, we get:
		$$w_1'(\overline{x})d_1'\cdots d_{r'-1}'w'_{r'}(\overline{x})v_1 = \mu(\overline{x})v_2$$
		where the $d_i'$ are rank one matrices and $\mu(\overline{x})$ is a regular function on $\mathcal{C}^{s-1}$. If it does not vanish identically then it is non-zero generically then $w_1'(\overline{x})\text{Im}(d_1 ')\subseteq \langle v_2\rangle$ as before, proving the induction step. If it is identically zero then for every $\overline{x}\in \mathcal{C}^{s-1}$ there is $i\in [\![2,r']\!]$ such that $w_{i}'(\overline{x})\text{Im}(d_i')\subseteq \ker(d_{i-1}')$, where by $\text{Im}(d_{r'}')$ we mean $\langle v_1\rangle$. Again by irreducibility one of these identities has to be true generically, proving the induction step.

\label{related subsets and pk}
		Let $\Omega$ be an $(s,L)$-related subset of $\mathbb{F}$. There is a decomposition $\Omega = \Omega_1\cup \cdots \cup\Omega_d$, with $d \leq d(s,L):=2(2s-1)^{3L}L^{3}$, such that for every $i\in [\![1,d]\!]$, there is $k_i \in [\![1,L]\!]$ such that $p_{k_i}(\Omega_i)$ is contained in a coset of an abelian subgroup of $\mathbb{F}$.

Consider $\mathbb{F}$ embedded into $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$. Pick $\Omega$ an $(s,L)$-related subset of $\mathbb{F}$, and $\mathcal{C}$ its Zariski closure. Note that $\mathcal{C}$ too is $(s,L)$-related. Write $\Omega_i = \Omega\cap \mathcal{C}_i$, where the $\mathcal{C}_i$ are the irreducible components of $\mathcal{C}$. By irreducibility of $\mathcal{C}_i^s$ there is a non-trivial word $w$ in $s$ letters with length at most $L$ such that $w(\mathcal{C}_i^s) = \{1\}$. Applying \Cref{alternative} there is $k_i \geq 1$ with $k_i \leq L$ such that $p_{k_i}(\Omega_i) \subset p_{k_i}(\mathcal{C}_i)$ is contained in a coset of a Borel subgroup of $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$. In particular the group generated by the $xy^{-1}$ for $x,y\in p_{k_i}(\Omega_i)$ is a solvable subgroup of $\mathbb{F}$, hence abelian by \Cref{solvable abelian}. So $p_{k_i}(\Omega_i)$ is contained in a coset of an abelian subgroup of $\mathbb{F}$, hence in a coset of a maximal abelian subgroup, i.e. a centralizer.
		
		We can bound $d$ as follows. Note that we may replace $\mathcal{C}$ by a maximal algebraic subvariety of $\text{SL}_2$ such that $\mathcal{C}^s \subset W$, where $W:=\cup_{\ell(w)\leq L} w^{-1}(1)$. Then $d$  is bounded by the number of irreducible components of $\mathcal{C}$ and hence by its degree (defined as the sum of the degrees of its components).  Each $w^{-1}$ is cut by polynomials in the matrix entries of degree at most $L$, so $W$ is cut by polynomials of degree at most $D:=(2s-1)^L L$. It is easy to check that the algebraic set $V$ cut out by the polynomials of degree at most $D$ that vanish on $\mathcal{C}$ will also satisfy $V^s \subset W$, so that $V=\mathcal{C}$ by maximality of $\mathcal{C}$.  It follows from the (relative) Bezout inequality (cf. \cite[Prop. 2.3]{heintz-schnorr}) that for closed subvarieties $X,Y$ of $\mathbb{C}^n$ we have $\deg(X \cap Y) \leq \deg(Y) D^{\dim Y}$ if $X$ is cut out by polynomials of degree at most $D$. Thus $d \leq \deg \mathcal{C} \leq \deg(\text{SL}_2) D^3 = 2D^3.$

\label{ninety} One may define an analogous \emph{ninety-nine percent property} of a group $\Gamma$, by asking that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that every probability measure $\mu$ on $\Gamma$ with $\|\lambda(\mu)\|\ge 1-\delta$ gives $\mu$-mass at least $1-\varepsilon$ to a coset of an amenable subgroup. It was shown in \cite[Theorem 7.6]{becker-breuillard} that $\text{GL}_d(\mathbb{C})$, viewed as a discrete group, has the ninety-nine percent property. It turns out that the one percent property implies the ninety-nine percent property in any group:
	
	\begin{prop} If $\Gamma$ is a discrete group with the one percent property (with modulus $\delta_1(\varepsilon)>0$), then it has the ninety-nine percent property (with modulus $\delta_{99}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon \delta_1(1/2)^4/2000$).
	\end{prop}
	
	
	\begin{proof}The following argument was suggested by ChatGPT 6 Astra in September 2026. Suppose that $\mu$ is a probability measure on $\Gamma$ such that $\|\lambda(\mu)\|\ge 1-\delta$. Then $\|\lambda(\widetilde{\mu})\|=\|\lambda(\mu)\|^2 \ge (1-\delta)^2\ge 1-2\delta$. Pick an integer $N$ such that $(1-2\delta)^N \ge 1/2$. Then $\|\lambda(\widetilde{\mu}^N)\| \ge 1/2$ and we may apply the one percent property of $\Gamma$ with $\varepsilon=1/2$ and conclude that there is a coset $gA$ of an amenable subgroup $A$ of $\Gamma$ such that $\widetilde{\mu}^N(gA)\ge \delta_0$, where $\delta_0=\delta_1(1/2)>0$ depends only on $\Gamma$. Without loss of generality we may assume that $\delta_0<1/2$. 
	
	Consider now the action of $\Gamma$ on the coset space $\Gamma/A$, and let $\nu$ be the push-forward of $\widetilde{\mu}^N$ under the map $\Gamma \to \Gamma/A$. Then $\nu$ has an atom of mass $\ge \delta_0$. List the $\nu$-mass of atoms in decreasing order $M_1 \geq M_2 \geq \ldots \geq M_n \geq\ldots$. Then $\sum M_i \leq 1$ and $M_1 \ge \delta_0$. It follows that there is $k\leq k_0=\lfloor 3/\delta_0 \rfloor$, such that $M_k-M_{k+1} > c_0:=\delta_0^2/5$. Indeed, if not we would have $1\ge M_1+\ldots +M_{k_0} \ge M_1 + M_1-c_0 + \ldots + M_1-(k_0-1)c_0 = k_0M_1-k_0(k_0-1)c_0/2$. Noting that $k_0\delta_0 \ge 2$ this would give $1 \ge 2-k_0^2c_0/2$, so $k_0^2c_0\ge 2$. However  $k_0^2c_0=(k_0\delta_0)^2/5\leq 9/5<2$. For this $k$ let
	$$E:=\{x\in \Gamma/A, \nu(x) \ge M_k\}$$
	and set $H=\{g \in \Gamma, gE=E\}$. Note that $E$ is finite, and that $H$ contains the pointwise stabiliser of $E$ (which is a subgroup of  a conjugate of $A$) as a subgroup of finite index. In particular $H$ is amenable. Moreover if $g \notin H$, then there is $x \in E$ such that $g^{-1}x \notin E$. In particular $\|\lambda_{\Gamma/A}(g) \nu - \nu\|_2 \ge |\lambda_{\Gamma/A}(g) \nu(x) - \nu(x)|=|\nu(g^{-1}x)-\nu(x)| \ge M_k-M_{k+1} \ge \delta_0^2/5$.
	
	It follows that $$\sum_{g \in \Gamma} \widetilde{\mu}(g)\|\lambda_{\Gamma/A}(g) \nu - \nu\|_2^2 \ge (1-\widetilde{\mu}(H))  \delta_0^4/25.$$
	
	On the other hand a simple spectral argument allows to bound the left hand side as follows: expanding the square we have
	 $$\sum_{g \in \Gamma} \widetilde{\mu}(g)\|\lambda_{\Gamma/A}(g) \nu - \nu\|_2^2 = 2\langle (1-\lambda_{\Gamma/A}(\widetilde \mu))\nu, \nu \rangle_{L^2(\Gamma/A)} = 2\langle P^{2N}(1-P) \delta_A,\delta_A\rangle$$
	 where $P=\lambda_{\Gamma/A}(\widetilde{\mu})$ is a non-negative self-adjoint operator. By the spectral theorem, $$\|P^{2N}(1-P)\|\leq \sup_{t \in [0,1]} t^{2N}(1-t)\leq 1/N.$$ Hence
	 $$\widetilde{\mu}(H) \ge 1- 50/N\delta_0^4.$$
	Choose $N$ maximal such that $(1-2\delta)^N \ge 1/2$. Assuming (as we may) that $\delta<1/8$, this yields $N \ge 1/(40\delta)$. Hence  $\max_{g \in \Gamma} \mu(Hg) \ge \widetilde{\mu}(H) \ge 1- 2000\delta \delta_0^{-4}$. This gives the ninety-nine percent property with modulus $\delta_{99}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon \delta_0^4/2000$. 
	\end{proof}

It follows $S$ must generate an amenable subgroup as soon as $N \sqrt{8M  \varepsilon}<\kappa_{F_2}$, where $\kappa_{F_2}$ is the Kazhdan constant on $L^2$ for the generators of the nonabelian free group $F_2$.  So  $E$ is contained in an amenable coset to which $\mu$ gives mass at least $1-1/M$.

\label{fgresfree} It is possible to convert the \Cref{resfree} into a finitely generated one. The following construction gives a $3$-generated group $G$ which contains $\Gamma=\bigoplus_{i\ge 1} \mathbb{F}^{(i)}$ and has the property that every pair $a,b \in G$ either commutes or generates a nonabelian free group. Hence $G$ fails the one-percent  but enjoys the ninety-nine percent property. Take $\{h_k, k\ge 1\}$ generators of $\Gamma$, consider new free variables  $u,v,x,y$ and form $s_0:=u$ and $s_k:=v^{-k}uh_kv^k$ for $k \ge 1$ and $t_k:=x^{k+1}y^{k+1}x^{k+1}$ for $k \ge 0$. Consider the free products $A:=\Gamma * F(u,v)$ and $B=F(x,y)$ and set $G$ to be the amalgamated product $G:=A *_{s_k=t_k, k \ge 0} B$. Then clearly $G=\langle x,y,v \rangle$. Moreover $\langle s_k, k\ge 0\rangle$ (resp. $\langle t_k, k\ge 0\rangle$) is malnormal in $A$ (resp. $B$); see Theorem 8 and the proof of its corollary in \cite{karrass-solitar}. This places us in the situation of \cite[Theorem 6]{karrass-solitar}, which asserts that every $2$-generated subgroup of such an amalgam $G$ is either nonabelian free, or conjugates into one of the factors. In the latter case, it is either free or conjugates into $\Gamma$, hence is either free or abelian as desired.
