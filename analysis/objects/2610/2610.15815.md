<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:informal}
For every fixed $0<R<1$, there exists $\varepsilon_R>0$ such that, for all sufficiently large $h$, the full-field RS code of dimension $\lfloor R2^h\rfloor$ over $\mathbb F_{2^h}$ has a received word with
\[
 2^{h^2/8-O_R(h)}
 =n^{\frac18\log_2 n-O_R(1)}
 =n^{\Omega_R(\log n)}
\]
codewords within relative distance $1-R-\varepsilon_R$, where $n=2^h$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:bijection}
The maps
\[
 c(X)\longmapsto p(X)
 \quad\text{where}\quad
 \frac{X^{2^h}-X}{c(X)}=p(X)^2+X,
\qquad
 p(X)\longmapsto
 c(X)=\frac{X^{2^h}-X}{p(X)^2+X}
\]
are mutually inverse bijections between $\mathcal B_K(D)$ and
$\mathcal L_K(D)$. In fact, every polynomial in $\mathcal L_K(D)$ has
degree exactly $D/2$ and agreement exactly $D$. Consequently,
\[
 A(2^h,D)=|\mathcal L_K(D)|.
\]
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Take $c\in\mathcal B_K(D)$. It vanishes at exactly $2^h-D$ elements of $K$, so the degree bound is tight: $\deg c=2^h-D$. Hence $c$ splits into distinct linear factors over $K$, all among the factors of
\[
 X^{2^h}-X=\prod_{\beta\in K}(X-\beta),
\]
and therefore $c\mid X^{2^h}-X$. Write
\[
 b(X)=\frac{X^{2^h}-X}{c(X)}.
\]
Then $\deg b=D$, and its roots are exactly the $D$ points where $c(\beta)=1$.

Differentiating
\[
 b(X)c(X)=X^{2^h}-X
\]
gives, in characteristic two,
\[
 b'(X)c(X)+b(X)c'(X)=1.
\]
At every root $\beta$ of $b$ we have $c(\beta)=1$, hence $b'(\beta)=1$. Since $b'(X)-1$ has degree at most $D-1$ and at least $D$ roots, $b'(X)=1$ identically.

Write $b(X)=\sum_j b_jX^j$. In characteristic two, differentiation kills the even powers, so $b'(X)=1$ says that the only odd-degree term of $b$ is $X$. Thus $b(X)-X$ contains only even powers. Because squaring is an automorphism of the finite field $K$, its coefficients have unique square roots, and there is a unique $p\in K[X]$ such that
\[
 b(X)=p(X)^2+X.
\]
Since $D$ is even, $\deg p=D/2$. At every root $\beta$ of $b$ we have $p(\beta)^2=\beta$, hence $p(\beta)=\beta^{2^{h-1}}$. Thus $p\in\mathcal L_K(D)$.

Conversely, let $p\in\mathcal L_K(D)$ and set $b=p^2+X$. Then $b'=1$, while $\deg b\le D$ and the agreement condition gives at least $D$ distinct roots in $K$. Hence $\deg b=D$ and these are exactly its roots. Therefore $b\mid X^{2^h}-X$, and
\[
 c(X)=\frac{X^{2^h}-X}{b(X)}
\]
has degree $2^h-D$. At a nonroot of $b$ we have $c(\beta)=0$; at a root, the differentiated identity above gives $c(\beta)=1$ because $b'(\beta)=1$. Hence $c\in\mathcal B_K(D)$. The two constructions are inverse by definition.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:binary-obstruction}
Since $c$ is $\mathbb F_2$-valued, it equals one
at every root of the complementary factor $b$.
Differentiating $bc=X^{2^h}-X$ therefore gives
$b'(\beta)=1$ at all its $D$ roots, and the
degree bound implies $b'\equiv1$. Over odd
characteristic, nonzero BCH symbols need not agree,
so the analogous derivative values
$-c(\beta)^{-1}$ need not be constant.
This is the obstruction to this particular
BCH--RS correspondence in odd characteristic.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:additive}
Let $V\le K$ be an additive subspace and put
$n_0:=|V|$ and $\nu_V:=\ell_V'\in K^*$. For even $2\le D<n_0$, there is a bijection
between
\[
 \left\{g\in K[X]:
   \deg g\le n_0-D,\ g(V)\subseteq\mathbb F_2,\ \operatorname{wt}_V(g)=D
 \right\}
\]
and the polynomials $p\in K[X]$ of degree at most $D/2$ satisfying
\[
 \operatorname{agr}_V(p,\beta\mapsto\beta^{2^{h-1}})\ge D.
\]
The maps are characterized by
\[
 \frac{\ell_V(X)}{g(X)}
   =\nu_V\bigl(p(X)^2+X\bigr),
 \qquad
 g(X)=\frac{\ell_V(X)}
 {\nu_V\bigl(p(X)^2+X\bigr)}.
\]
As in Proposition~\ref{prop:bijection}, the degree and agreement
inequalities are equalities.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
As in Proposition~\ref{prop:bijection}, the $n_0-D$
zeros of $g$ saturate the degree bound, so
$g\mid\ell_V$ and $b:=\ell_V/g$ has degree $D$,
with roots exactly where $g=1$. Differentiating
$bg=\ell_V$ at those roots gives $b'(\beta)=\nu_V$.
Because $\deg b'<D$, we have $b'\equiv\nu_V$,
and hence, in characteristic two, uniquely
\[
 b=\nu_V(p^2+X),\qquad \deg p=D/2.
\]
The $D$ roots give the required agreements with
$\beta^{2^{h-1}}$.

Conversely, such a polynomial $p$ yields
$b=\nu_V(p^2+X)$ of degree $D$ with $D$ distinct
roots in $V$ and derivative $\nu_V$. Thus
$b\mid\ell_V$; for $g=\ell_V/b$, the identity
$b'g+bg'=\nu_V$ shows that $g=1$ at those
roots and $g=0$ elsewhere on $V$.
This recovers the prescribed binary weight
and degree, and the constructions are inverse.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:anchor-family}
Fix an integer $c\ge0$. For each $h>c$, let
$V_h\le(\mathbb F_{2^h},+)$ be an additive subspace of
codimension $c$, and put $n_0:=|V_h|=2^{h-c}$.
A sequence $(\mathcal A_h)$ of nonempty sets
$\mathcal A_h\subseteq\mathbb F_{2^h}[X]$ is an
\emph{anchor family of codimension $c$}
(or \emph{density $\lambda=2^{-c}$}) if every
$p\in\mathcal A_h$ has degree $<n_0/4$ and
\[
 \min_{p\in\mathcal A_h}
 \frac{\operatorname{agr}_{V_h}(p,X^{2^{h-1}})}{n_0}
 \ge\tfrac12-o(1)\qquad(h\to\infty).
\]

\label{thm:anchor}
There is an anchor family $(\mathcal A_h)$ of codimension
$0$, with $V_h=\mathbb F_{2^h}$, such that for all sufficiently
large $h$,
\[
 |\mathcal A_h|\ge2^{h^2/8-O(h)}.
\]
Thus the full-field code $\operatorname{RS}_{\mathbb F_{2^h}}(2^{h-2})$

of degree $D_h^\star/2<2^{h-2}$, all agreeing with the received function
$\beta\mapsto\beta^{2^{h-1}}$ at exactly
$D_h^\star=(1/2-o(1))2^h$ points. Together they form
the claimed anchor family.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:asymptotic-padding}
Let $(\mathcal A_h)$ be an anchor family of fixed
codimension $c$ and density $\lambda=2^{-c}$,
and write $M_h=|\mathcal A_h|$.
For any fixed $0<R<1$ with $\lambda\le4R$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:padding-agreement}
 \alpha_\lambda(R):=
 \begin{cases}
 R+\dfrac{\lambda}{4},
    &R\le1-\dfrac{3\lambda}{4}
      \quad\text{(disjoint)},\\[4pt]
 \dfrac{1+R}{2}-\dfrac{\lambda}{8},
    &R\ge1-\dfrac{3\lambda}{4}
      \quad\text{(overlapping)}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The expressions agree at the switching rate.
For every fixed $0<\alpha<\alpha_\lambda(R)$,
all sufficiently large $h$ admit at least $M_h/n$
distinct padded messages of degree $<\lfloor Rn\rfloor$
with agreement fraction at least $\alpha$ against
a common received word on $K$.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By the anchor-family definition, we may take
$\eta_h\to0$ such that each $p\in\mathcal A_h$
agrees with $\rho$ at no fewer than
$(1/2-\eta_h)n_0$ points of $V$.
Let $k=\lfloor Rn\rfloor$ and $b=k-n_0/4$.
For each padding set $T$ of size $b$,
use $z_T\rho$ as the received word
and $z_Tp$ as the padded messages.
Their distinctness is preserved by multiplication
by the same nonzero polynomial $z_T$.

\emph{Disjoint padding.}
If $b\le n-n_0$, choose $T\subseteq K\setminus V$.
All original anchor agreements survive and the
$b$ roots give additional agreements. Every padded
message therefore agrees at least
\[
 b+(1/2-\eta_h)n_0
\]
times. Dividing by $n$ gives the limiting fraction
\[
 \left(R-\frac{\lambda}{4}\right)
       +\frac{\lambda}{2}
 =R+\frac{\lambda}{4}.
\]
All $M_h$ messages are retained.

\emph{Overlapping padding.}
If $b>n-n_0$, use every point outside $V$ as a
padding root and choose the remaining roots
uniformly from $V$. Equivalently, the $n-b$
unpadded coordinates form a uniformly random
subset of $V$. A uniformly selected unpadded
coordinate is an agreement for a fixed anchor
$p$ with probability
$\operatorname{agr}_V(p,\rho)/n_0\ge1/2-\eta_h$.
Including the $b$ automatic agreements,
the expected number of agreements for each
padded message is at least
\[
 n\mu_h,\qquad
 \mu_h:=\frac bn+
          \left(1-\frac bn\right)
          \left(\frac12-\eta_h\right).
\]
Since $b/n\to R-\lambda/4$, we obtain
\[
 \mu_h\longrightarrow
 \frac{1+R}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{8}.
\]

We choose the \emph{same} random padding set for
all anchors. Averaging shows that some set $T$
gives an average of at least $n\mu_h$ agreements
across the $M_h$ messages. Put
$A=\lfloor n\mu_h\rfloor$. If $G$ messages have at least
$A$ agreements, the rest have at most $A-1$, so
\[
 M_hn\mu_h\le(M_h-G)(A-1)+Gn,
 \qquad
 G\ge\frac{M_h(n\mu_h-A+1)}{n-A+1}
 \ge\frac{M_h}{n}.
\]

At the switching rate $R=1-3\lambda/4$, we have
$b=n-n_0$ exactly, so the disjoint construction
uses every point outside $V$.
The rounding loss $1/n$ in the overlapping case
vanishes, proving the assertion for every fixed
$\alpha<\alpha_\lambda(R)$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->

<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:W-invariant}
Let $W\le(K,+)$ be an additive subspace. A function
$f:K\to K$ is \emph{$W$-invariant} if
\[
 f(x+\omega)=f(x)\qquad
 \text{for every }x\in K\text{ and }\omega\in W.
\]
Equivalently, $f$ is constant on each coset of $W$.
This terminology also applies to $\mathbb F_2$-valued
functions on $K$.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:quadratic-polar}
A map $\varphi:K\to\mathbb F_2$ is a \emph{quadratic form} if
$\varphi(0)=0$ and
\[
 B_\varphi(x,y):=\varphi(x+y)+\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)
\]
is bilinear over $\mathbb F_2$. The bilinear form $B_\varphi$ is the
\emph{polar form} of $\varphi$. Since $B_\varphi(x,x)=0$ for every $x$,
it is alternating.
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\label{def:radical-rank}
The \emph{polar radical} of $\varphi$ is
\[
 \operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)
 :=\{\omega\in K:B_\varphi(x,\omega)=0
       \text{ for every }x\in K\}.
\]
By bilinearity, $\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$ is a linear subspace of $K$.
The \emph{polar rank} is
\[
 \operatorname{rank}(B_\varphi)
 :=h-\dim_{\mathbb F_2}\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi).
\]
The polar form is \emph{nondegenerate} when $\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)=\{0\}$:
then no nonzero direction has zero polar interaction with every other
direction.
<!-- end definition-4 -->
<!-- definition-5: body_md -->
\label{def:quadratic-rank}
The \emph{quadratic radical} of $\varphi$ is
\[
 \operatorname{rad}(\varphi)
 :=\{\omega\in\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi):\varphi(\omega)=0\}
 =\ker\bigl(\varphi|_{\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)}\bigr).
\]
The \emph{quadratic rank} is
\[
 \operatorname{rank}(\varphi)
 :=h-\dim_{\mathbb F_2}\operatorname{rad}(\varphi).
\]
<!-- end definition-5 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:binary-quadratic}
On $U=\mathbb F_2^3$, consider
\[
 \varphi_0(x_1,x_2,x_3)=x_1x_2,
 \qquad
 \varphi_1(x_1,x_2,x_3)=x_1x_2+x_3.
\]
They have the same polar form
$B(x,y)=x_1y_2+x_2y_1$ and hence the same polar radical
\[
 \operatorname{rad}(B_{\varphi_0})
 =\operatorname{rad}(B_{\varphi_1})
 =\langle(0,0,1)\rangle.
\]
Their quadratic radicals differ:
\[
 \operatorname{rad}(\varphi_0)=\langle(0,0,1)\rangle,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{rad}(\varphi_1)=\{0\}.
\]
Thus both have polar rank $2$, but their quadratic ranks are
$2$ and $3$, respectively; their Hamming weights are $2$ and $4$.
Translation by $(0,0,1)$ fixes $\varphi_0$ but flips
$\varphi_1$. Adding the linear function $x_3$ to $\varphi_1$
recovers $\varphi_0$ and restores invariance.
<!-- end example-1 -->

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:quadratic-character-sum}
Let $\varphi:K\to\mathbb F_2$ be a quadratic form of quadratic rank $r$.
Then
\[
 \operatorname{wt}_K(\varphi)=
 \begin{cases}
  2^{h-1}\pm2^{h-a-1},&r=2a,\\
  2^{h-1},&r=2a+1.
 \end{cases}
\]
For even $r$, the sign depends on the quadratic form,
not just its quadratic rank.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Put $S=\sum_{x\in K}(-1)^{\varphi(x)}$. Expanding $S^2$ and
using the bijection $(x,y)\mapsto(x,x+y)$ gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 S^2
 &=\sum_{x,y\in K}(-1)^{\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)}\\
 &=\sum_{x,z\in K}(-1)^{\varphi(x)+\varphi(x+z)}\\
 &=\sum_{z\in K}(-1)^{\varphi(z)}
     \sum_{x\in K}(-1)^{B_\varphi(x,z)}\\
 &=2^h\sum_{z\in \operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)}(-1)^{\varphi(z)}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The last equality evaluates the inner sum over $x$ in the third
line. For $z\notin \operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$, the map $x\mapsto B_\varphi(x,z)$
is a nonzero binary linear functional, so its character sum is
zero; for $z\in \operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$, that inner sum is $2^h$.

It remains to evaluate the sum over $z\in \operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$ in the last
line. As observed above,
$\varphi|_{\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)}$ is linear. If it is nonzero, this sum vanishes
and $S=0$. Otherwise it equals $|\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)|=2^{h-2a}$, since
the quadratic rank is then the polar rank $2a$. Hence
$S^2=2^{2h-2a}$. The identity
$\operatorname{wt}_K(\varphi)=(2^h-S)/2$ now gives the stated weights.
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:radical-shifts}
Let $\varphi:K\to\mathbb F_2$ be a quadratic form of polar rank $2a$. Among all affine shifts $L+b$, with $L:K\to\mathbb F_2$
linear and $b\in\mathbb F_2$, exactly $2^{2a}$ choices make
$f=\varphi+L+b$ $\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$-invariant and have weight $D_{h,a}$.
For each selected linear
part $L$, the quadratic radical $\operatorname{rad}(\varphi+L)$ equals $\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We first determine which linear parts $L$ make
$f=\varphi+L+b$ $\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$-invariant.
The translation identity shows that
\[
 L|_{\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)}=\varphi|_{\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)}
\]
is sufficient. It is also necessary: if $f$ is
$\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$-invariant, then for every $\omega\in \operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$,
\[
 0=f(\omega)+f(0)=\varphi(\omega)+L(\omega).
\]
As established above, $\varphi$ restricts to a linear functional on
its polar radical. This functional extends to $K$: extend a basis of $\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$ to a basis of $K$ and choose its
values arbitrarily on the added basis vectors. Any two extensions
differ by a linear functional that vanishes on $\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$, equivalently
by a linear functional on the quotient $K/\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$. Since this
quotient has dimension $2a$, there are exactly $2^{2a}$ extensions.

Fix one such extension $L$. The quadratic form $\varphi+L$ has the
same polar rank $2a$ and vanishes on $\operatorname{rad}(B_\varphi)$, so its quadratic
rank is $2a$. Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic-character-sum}
gives weight $2^{h-1}\pm2^{h-a-1}$. Adding the constant $b$
interchanges zeros and ones, so exactly one choice gives the
smaller weight $D_{h,a}$.

For each selected $L$, the quadratic form $\psi:=\varphi+L$

\label{lem:trace-degree}
Let $\varphi\in\mathcal F_{h,a}$ and let $f=\varphi+L+b$
for any affine shift $L+b$. If $c_f\in K[X]$ is the canonical

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
First consider a quadratic trace term. Expanding gives
\[
 \operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb F_2}(uX^{2^i+1})
 =\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}
   u^{2^j}X^{2^{i+j}+2^j},
\]
where exponents are reduced as polynomial functions on $K$. Thus each
resulting exponent has two binary $1$-positions, obtained by cyclically
shifting the positions $0$ and $i$ modulo $h$; their circular
separations are $i$ and $h-i$. For the indices in
$\mathcal F_{h,a}$, both separations are at least $a$. Hence, if the
larger position is $h-1$, the other is at most $h-a-1$, and every such
exponent is at most
\[
 2^{h-1}+2^{h-a-1}=2^{h-1}+\delta.
\]
When $h=2m$, the remaining term
$\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb F_{2^m}/\mathbb F_2}(u_m X^{2^m+1})$ is the trace of
$u_m$ times the relative norm $X^{2^m+1}$ from $K$ to
$\mathbb F_{2^m}$. Its expansion has exponents
$2^{m+j}+2^j$ for $0\le j<m$, whose binary positions are
separated by $m$, so the same bound applies.
Finally, every $\mathbb F_2$-linear functional on $K$ has the form
$\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb F_2}(vX)$ for some $v\in K$.\footnote{This is the trace description of $\operatorname{RM}(1,h)$~\cite{DGM1970}; alternatively, the $2^h$ distinct maps $x\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(vx)$ exhaust all $2^h$ binary linear functionals.}
Thus an affine shift $L+b$ has canonical degree at most
$2^{h-1}$ and does not increase the bound.

If $\operatorname{wt}_K(f)=D_{h,a}$, then $c_f$ has
$2^{h-1}+\delta$ zeros in $K$; the root bound forces equality
in the degree bound.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:subspace-descent}
Let $f:K\to\mathbb F_2$ be $W$-invariant, and let $c_f$
be its canonical polynomial of degree $<2^h$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Polynomial descent.}
There is a unique polynomial $f^{\downarrow}_W\in K[X]$ of degree $<n_0$,
taking values in $\mathbb F_2$ on $V$, such that $c_f=f^{\downarrow}_W\circ\pi$.
\item \emph{Degree scaling.}
For every $f$, $\deg c_f=w\deg f^{\downarrow}_W$, with the convention
$\deg0=-\infty$ and $w(-\infty)=-\infty$.
\item \emph{Weight scaling.}
The Hamming weights satisfy
$\operatorname{wt}_K(f)=w\,\operatorname{wt}_V(f^{\downarrow}_W)$, and the numbers of zero
positions have the same scaling factor $w$.
\item \emph{One-to-one correspondence.}
The map $f\mapsto f^{\downarrow}_W$ is a bijection between $\mathbb F_2$-valued
$W$-invariant functions $K\to\mathbb F_2$
and polynomials of degree $<n_0$ taking values in $\mathbb F_2$
on $V$. In particular, distinct $c_f$ yield distinct $f^{\downarrow}_W$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Polynomial descent and degree scaling here specialize
Berman--Shany--Tamo~\cite[Lemma~A.2]{BST}.
We give a direct proof that also establishes weight scaling
and the one-to-one correspondence.

\emph{(1) Polynomial descent.}
The subspace polynomial $\pi$ is $\mathbb F_2$-linear with kernel
$W$, and so induces an isomorphism $K/W\cong V$.
Both $f$ and $\pi$ are $W$-invariant, so each coset
$B=x+W$ determines a point
$(\pi(B),f(B))\in V\times\mathbb F_2$, where the two entries
denote their common values on $B$. The isomorphism
shows that the $n_0$ cosets have distinct
$\pi$-values, comprising all of $V$. Hence there is a unique
polynomial $f^{\downarrow}_W\in K[X]$ of degree $<n_0$ interpolating
these $n_0$ points. By construction,
$f(x)=f^{\downarrow}_W(\pi(x))$ for every $x\in K$.
As $\deg\pi=w$ and $wn_0=2^h$, the polynomial
$f^{\downarrow}_W\circ\pi$ has degree $<2^h$, as does $c_f$.
Since they agree throughout $K$, they coincide as
polynomials: $c_f=f^{\downarrow}_W\circ\pi$. The same interpolation
uniqueness shows that no other polynomial of degree $<n_0$
can satisfy this identity.

\emph{(2) Degree scaling.}
For $f^{\downarrow}_W\ne0$, polynomial composition gives
$\deg c_f=(\deg\pi)(\deg f^{\downarrow}_W)=w\deg f^{\downarrow}_W$,
including when $f^{\downarrow}_W$ is a nonzero constant.
If $f^{\downarrow}_W=0$, then $c_f=0$ by part~\textup{(1)},
so the identity holds
with the stated convention.

\emph{(3) Weight scaling.}
Each $\beta\in V$ has exactly $w$ preimages in $K$,
and $f$ takes the value $f^{\downarrow}_W(\beta)$ at all of them.
Counting ones and zeros gives the two scaling identities.

\emph{(4) One-to-one correspondence.}
Conversely, any polynomial $g$ of degree $<n_0$ that is
$\mathbb F_2$-valued on $V$ yields a $\mathbb F_2$-valued function
$g\circ\pi$ on $K$, which is $W$-invariant.
By uniqueness in part~\textup{(1)},
$(g\circ\pi)^{\downarrow}_W=g$. In the other direction,
part~\textup{(1)} gives $f=f^{\downarrow}_W\circ\pi$
as functions on $K$. These maps are inverse bijections,
and distinct canonical polynomials descend to distinct polynomials.
