 \nonscript\;
 \ifnum\currentgrouptype=16
  \;\middle|\;
 \else
  \;|\;
 \fi
 X_{[n]\smallsetminus\ensuremath{\left\{i,j\right\}}}$ for all non-adjacent vertices $i,j\in V$. One can identify this with the set of matrices
\[\mathcal M_{G} = \ensuremath{\left\{K\in \mathbb R^{n\times n}  
 \nonscript\;
 \ifnum\currentgrouptype=16
  \;\middle|\;
 \else
  \;|\;
 \fi
 K\succ 0, K=K^{\top}, K_{ij} = 0 ~\forall~ ij\notin E \text{ and } i\ne j\right\}}.\]

<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:chordalcomputation}
Consider the following graph, with its covariance matrix, with observed covariance data $a,b,c,d$ as indicated and $s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}$ on the diagonals.
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	{1\bullet} & {\bullet 2} \\
	{3\bullet} & {\bullet 4}
	\arrow["a", no head, from=1-1, to=1-2]
	\arrow["c"', no head, from=1-1, to=2-2]
	\arrow["b", no head, from=1-2, to=2-2]
	\arrow["d"', no head, from=2-1, to=2-2]
\end{tikzcd}, \qquad 
\Sigma(\alpha,\beta) = 
\begin{bmatrix}
s_{1} & a & \alpha & c\\
a& s_{2} & \beta & b\\
\alpha & \beta & s_{3} & d\\
c & b & d & s_{4}
\end{bmatrix}
\]
where $\alpha,\beta$ are unobserved. The constraints we need are $(\Sigma^{-1})_{13}=(\Sigma^{-1})_{23}=0$. Away from the zeros of $\det \Sigma(\alpha,\beta)$, the equations are $\operatorname{Cof}_{13} = \operatorname{Cof}_{23} = 0$. Note that both these cofactors are affine-linear in $\alpha,\beta$. Therefore, one can simultaneously solve these linear equations in $\alpha,\beta$ away from a vanishing condition which is polynomial in the observed data $\ensuremath{\left\{a,b,c,d,s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}\right\}}$.

\label{ex:4cycledegree}
Consider the cyclic graph $C_{4}$, with its covariance matrix, with observed covariance data $a,b,c,d$ as indicated and $s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}$ on the diagonals.
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	{1\bullet} & {\bullet 2} \\
	{4\bullet} & {\bullet 3}

	\arrow["c"', no head, from=2-1, to=2-2]

s_{1} & a & \alpha & d\\
a& s_{2} & b & \beta\\

\end{bmatrix}

where $\alpha,\beta$ are unobserved. The constraints we need are $(\Sigma^{-1})_{13}=(\Sigma^{-1})_{24} = 0$. The equations of the numerator vanishing are $\operatorname{Cof}_{13} = \operatorname{Cof}_{24} = 0$. Now,
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{Cof}_{13}(\alpha,\beta) &= \alpha\beta^{2} -\alpha s_{2}s_{4} - \beta(bd + ac) + abs_{4}+cds_{2},\\
\operatorname{Cof}_{24}(\alpha,\beta) &= \alpha^{2}\beta -\alpha (bd+ac) -\beta s_{1}s_{3} + ads_{3} + bcs_{1}.
\end{align*}

Homogenize the above polynomials to total degree three by the extra variable $\gamma$. Bezout's theorem gives nine solutions. But one must discount for the roots at infinity, namely those lying on $\gamma=0$, and those of the denominator. The corresponding equations are $\alpha\beta^{2}=\alpha^{2}\beta=0$, whence the roots at infinity are $[1:0:0], [0:1:0]$. But each of these occur with multiplicity two, so we discount a total of four roots. This gives five roots in the affine part $\ensuremath{\left\{\gamma\ne 0\right\}}$ of $\mathbb P^{2}$. For the latter, we claim that there is no common root of the cofactors and the denominator and leave it to the reader to verify this for generic entries.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:one-fill}
Let $H$ be a chordal graph, $uv \in E(H)$, and  $G=H\smallsetminus uv$. Fix a clique tree $T$ of $H$. Let $\mathcal C_{uv}(H)$ be the set of maximal cliques of $H$ containing both $u,v$, and let $\mathcal S_{uv}(H,T)$ be the set of distinct clique-tree separator bags containing both $u,v$. Then, independent of the chosen clique tree $T$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:off}\tag{one-fill formula}
 \operatorname{MLdeg}(G) = 2\left(\left|\mathcal C_{uv}(H)\right|+\left|\mathcal S_{uv}(H,T)\right|\right)-1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- example-3: body_md -->

Let $H$ be the resulting graph after joining $1,3$. Its maximal cliques are $\mathcal C(H)= \ensuremath{\left\{ \ensuremath{\left\{1,2,3\right\}} , \ensuremath{\left\{1,3,4\right\}} \right\}}$. The only clique tree of $H$ is the tree with these vertices and one edge between them. The running intersection property is trivial to verify. Therefore $\left|\mathcal C_{1,3}(H)\right|=2, \left|\mathcal S_{1,3}(H,T)\right| = 1$, which gives $\operatorname{MLdeg}(G) = 2(2+1)-1 = 5$ as we earlier checked.

<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:clique-tree}
Let $H$ be chordal. A {\color{cyan}clique tree} of $H$ is a tree $T$ with vertex set $\mathcal C(H)$ such that, for every $w\in V(H)$, the set $\ensuremath{\left\{C\in\mathcal C(H) 
 \nonscript\;

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:properties}
Let $H$ be a chordal graph and $T$ a clique tree of $H$. Recall that the vertex set of $T$ is the set $\mathcal C(H)$ of maximal cliques in $H$. The following are true.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\par\hangindent 40\p@ \label{path-property} For $C,C'\in \mathcal C(H)$ if $w\in C\cap C'$ then every clique on the unique path in $T$ between $C,C'$ contains $w$.
\par\hangindent 40\p@ \label{uv-subtree} For $f= uv \in E(H)$, the maximal cliques containing $u$ and $v$ induce a nonempty subtree $T_{f}$ of $T$. Its edges are exactly the edges $e = CC'\in E(T)$ for which $\ensuremath{\left\{u,v\right\}}\subseteq B_{e} = C\cap C'$.
\par\hangindent 40\p@ \label{sum-net-coefficients} For every $f\in E(H)$, $\sum_{B \in \mathcal B_{f}(H,T)} \lambda_{B}^{(f)}=1$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\par\hangindent 40\p@ By the running-intersection property, the maximal cliques containing $w$ induce a connected subgraph of the tree $T$. A connected subgraph of a tree that contains $C$ and $C'$ contains their unique connecting path.
\par\hangindent 40\p@ The maximal cliques containing $u$, and respectively $v$, each induce a subtree of $T$. Their intersection is nonempty because the edge $uv$ lies in a maximal clique. The intersection of two nonempty intersecting subtrees of a tree is connected, so its vertex set is that of a subtree $T_f$. An edge $e=CC'$ belongs to $T_f$ precisely when both $C$ and $C'$ contain $u$ and $v$, equivalently when $B_e=C\cap C'$ contains $\ensuremath{\left\{u,v\right\}}$.
\par\hangindent 40\p@ The sum, over $B$, of the first terms in \eqref{eq:net-coefficient} gives $\left|V(T_f)\right|$. Similarly the second terms give $\left|E(T_f)\right|$. Since $T_{f}$ is a connected tree and the above sum is the difference between the number of vertices and edges of $T_{f}$, the mentioned sum must be one.
\end{enumerate}

Let $\Sigma(s,x,z)$ be the symmetric matrix on $V(H)=V(G)$ with entries $s_{ij}$ for $ij\in \mathcal O_{G}$, $x_{f}$ for $f\in F$ and $z_{q}$ for $q\in Q$. Take $d=d(s,x,z)=\det \Sigma(s,x,z)$. By \eqref{eq:likelihoodequations}, $B\cong(\mathcal A_G)_{\Delta_H}$. Write $A=A_{H,F}$ for this proof. We first construct an explicit $\mathbb K_G$-algebra isomorphism $A \cong B$. Since $d\Delta_H$ is a unit in $B$, both $d$ and $\Delta_H$ are units in $B$. Thus the defining cofactor relations in $B$ are equivalent to the equations $(\Sigma^{-1})_{ij}=0 ~\forall~ ij\in F\cup Q=\mathcal E_G$.
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       

                                                                                                                                                                                                                           
Consider the morphisms $\phi,\psi$ given by \begin{align*}
\phi:B\to A, &\qquad x_{f} \mapsto x_{f}, \qquad  z_{q} \mapsto \Sigma^{H}(x)_{q};\\
\psi:A\to B, &\qquad x_{f}\mapsto x_{f};
\end{align*}
where $\Sigma^{H}(x) = K^{H}(x)^{-1}$. By \eqref{eq:chordal-formula}, $K^H(x)$ has entries in $R_{\Delta_{H}}=\mathbb K_{G}\left[\ensuremath{\left\{x_{f}:f\in F\right\}}, \Delta_{H}^{-1}\right]$. Every clique and separator determinant is a unit in $R_{\Delta_{H}}$, so \eqref{eq:chordal-det-factorization} implies $\det K^{H}(x)$ is also a unit. Consequently, $\Sigma^{H}(x)$ has entries in $R_{\Delta_{H}}$ and its determinant is also a unit in $R_{\Delta_{H}}$.

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           

To verify that these maps respect the corresponding relations, note that in $A$ the matrix $\Sigma^{H}(x) = K^{H}(x)^{-1}$ has the entries $y(x)$ on $\mathcal O_{H}$ (namely $s_{ij}$ and $x_{F}$) and $K^{H}(x)$ has zero entries on $Q$. Further, $\det \Sigma^{H}$ and all factors of $\Delta_{H}$ are units in $A$ by \eqref{eq:chordal-det-factorization}. Therefore $\phi$ respects all relations and localizations. Conversely in $B$, $\Sigma(s,x,z)$ is invertible, $\Sigma(s,x,z)^{-1}$ has zero entries on $Q$ and $\Sigma(s,x,z)$ agrees with $(s_{ij})_{\mathcal O_{G}}\cup (x_{f})_{F}$ on $\mathcal O_{H}$. $\Delta_{H}$ remains a unit. The unique completion to a concentration matrix, by the chordal formula, gives $\Sigma(s,x,z)^{-1} = K^{H}(x)$. The defining relations of $B$ (from \eqref{eq:fill-algebra}) gives $\Sigma(s,x,z)^{-1}_{f} = 0~\forall~ f\in F$. Therefore $\psi$ also respects all relations and localizations. Therefore both $\phi,\psi$ define morphisms.

We now verify that this is an isomorphism. $\phi\psi$ fixes every $x_f$. $\psi\phi$ fixes every $x_{f}$ and by the above discussion, maps $z_{q}$ to itself (because $\Sigma(s,x,z) = \Sigma^{H}(x)$ and chordal graphs have only one completion). This proves the isomorphism $A \cong B$.

Now by \eqref{eq:likelihoodequations}, $B \cong (\mathcal A_{G})_{\Delta_{H}}$. By \Cref{lem:Delta-unit}, the image of $\Delta_H$ is already a unit in $\mathcal A_{G}$, whence $A\cong B \cong (\mathcal A_{G})_{\Delta_{H}} \cong \mathcal A_{G}$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:local-schur}
If $D_B(x)\neq0$ then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-schur-entry}
\det\Sigma_B(x)=\det(T)D_B(x), \qquad (\Sigma_B(x)^{-1})_{uv}=\frac{c-x}{D_B(x)}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Using \eqref{eq:schur-terms}, the determinant formula for a Schur complement gives the first identity in \eqref{eq:local-schur-entry}. The upper-left block of $\Sigma_B(x)^{-1}$ is the inverse of this Schur complement, namely
\[
 \frac{1}{D_B(x)}
 \begin{bmatrix}\beta&c-x\\c-x&\alpha\end{bmatrix},
\]
which gives the second identity. For the reader, the latter is usually referred to as the inverse formula for Schur complements.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:schur-sum-degree}
Let $m=\left|\mathcal B\right|$ and assume that the $D_B$ are pairwise coprime, $\alpha_B\beta_B\neq0$, $\lambda_B\in\mathbb F^\times$, and $\sum_{B\in\mathcal B}\lambda_B=1$. Then
\[ \sum_{B\in\mathcal B}\lambda_B\frac{c_B-x}{D_B(x)}= \frac{N(x)}{D(x)}, \qquad \deg N=2m-1, \qquad \gcd(N,D)=1.\]
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:schur-quotient-length}
 \mathbb F[x,D^{-1}]/(N/D)\simeq\mathbb F[x]/(N)
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \dim_{\mathbb F}\mathbb F[x,D^{-1}]/(N/D)=2m-1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Since $D_{B}$'s are pairwise coprime, clearing denominators for the sum of the rational expressions gives $N/D$. Each $D_{B}$ is quadratic and $-D_{B}$ is monic. Therefore, the top coefficient (namely that of $x^{2m-1}$) of each summand in $N(x)$ has coefficient $\lambda_{B}\cdot (-1)\cdot (-1)^{m-1}$ where the first $-1$ comes from the term $c_{B}-x$ and the $(-1)^{m-1}$ comes from the term $\prod_{B'\in \mathcal B\smallsetminus \ensuremath{\left\{B\right\}}}D_{B'}(x)$. Therefore the coefficient of $x^{2m-1}$ in $N(x)$ is the sum of $(-1)^{m}\lambda_{B}$'s which is $(-1)^{m}$. Therefore $\deg N = 2m-1$.

To prove coprimality, work in an algebraic closure of $\mathbb F$ and let
$\rho$ be a root of $D_B$. Pairwise coprimality gives
$D_{B'}(\rho)\neq0$ for every $B'\neq B$. Moreover
$\rho\neq c_B$, because $D_B(c_B)=\alpha_B\beta_B\neq0$. At $\rho$, all
summands of $N$ except the $B$-summand vanish, and hence
\[
 N(\rho)=\lambda_B(c_B-\rho) \prod_{B'\neq B}D_{B'}(\rho)\neq0.
\]
No root of $D$ is a root of $N$, so $\gcd(N,D)=1$. Bezout's identity then makes $D$ a unit in $\mathbb F[x]/(N)$. Its vector-space dimension is $\deg N$, which proves \eqref{eq:schur-quotient-length}.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:generic-denominators}
Over $\mathbb K$, the numbers $\alpha_B\beta_B$ are nonzero and the quadratics $D_B(x)$, for $B\in\mathcal B_{uv}$, are pairwise coprime.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
On the open set where all $\det T_{B}\ne 0$, the $\alpha_{B}\beta_{B}$ and the pairwise resultants $\operatorname{Res}_x(D_B,D_{B'})$ are rational expressions of the observed entries. After clearing powers of the $\det T_{B}$'s, their simultaneous non-vanishing is a Zariski-open condition and thus it is enough to show that this open set is non-empty. Note that we have used here that $\gcd(D_{B},D_{B'})=1\iff \operatorname{Res}_x(D_B,D_{B'})\ne 0$. It is therefore enough to show that these conditions hold for some specialization of $s_{ij}$. 

Conveniently we choose them such that, referring to \eqref{eq:y-expression}, every $T_{B}$ is identity and every $s_{B}$ is the zero vector which would make \[\alpha_{B} = s_{uu} - \|r_{B}\|^{2},\qquad \beta_{B} = s_{vv}, \qquad c_{B} = 0,\qquad D_{B}(x) = (s_{uu}-\|r_{B}\|^{2})s_{vv}-x^{2}.\] It is enough to take $\|r_{B}\|^{2} \ne \|r_{B'}\|^{2}$ (and not equal to $s_{uu}$) for $B\ne B'\in \mathcal B_{uv}$ while ensuring $s_{vv}\ne 0$, in which case all the quadratics $D_{B}$ would have pairwise distinct unequal roots. Putting this together, we take the following specialization.

Order the vertices $V(H)\smallsetminus \ensuremath{\left\{u,v\right\}} = \ensuremath{\left\{w_{0}, \cdots, w_{t-1}\right\}}$ and take \[s_{uu} = 2^{t}, \qquad s_{vv} = 1, \qquad s_{u,w_{i}}=2^{i/2} ~\forall~ uw_{i}\in E(G), \qquad s_{w_{i}w_{i}}=1~\forall~ 0\le i<t\] and all other observed off-diagonal entries as zero. Then $\|r_{B}\|^{2} = \sum_{w_{i\in B\smallsetminus\ensuremath{\left\{u,v\right\}}}}2^{i} < 2^{t}$. These numbers, for distinct $B$, are all distinct because of the distinctness of the indices contained in $B$ and distinct sets of indices determine distinct binary numbers. Also $s_{uu} \in \mathbb C^{\times} \smallsetminus \ensuremath{\left\{\|r_{B}\|^{2}  
 \nonscript\;
 \ifnum\currentgrouptype=16
  \;\middle|\;
 \else
  \;|\;
 \fi
 B\in \mathcal B_{uv}\right\}}$. This makes all $\alpha_{B}\ne 0, \beta_{B}\ne 0$ and all $\ensuremath{\left\{D_{B}\right\}}_{B\in \mathcal B_{uv}}$ have no pairwise common root, that is, pairwise coprime.
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let \[D(x) = \prod_{B\in \mathcal B_{uv}}D_{B}(x).\] Observe by the first identity in \eqref{eq:local-schur-entry} of \Cref{lem:local-schur} and invertibility of $\det T_{B}$ by \Cref{lem:bag-expansion} that $\Delta_{H}$ and $D$ have the same factors $\ensuremath{\left\{D_{B}(x)\right\}}$, but $\Delta_{H}$ is a product of them with certain exponents, and $D$ is simply a product, up to units in $\mathbb K_{G}$. Consequently, $\mathbb K_{G}[x,\Delta_{H}^{-1}] = \mathbb K_{G}[x,D^{-1}]$. By this observation, \Cref{lem:one-fill-reduction}, we have $\operatorname{MLdeg}(G) = \dim_{\mathbb K}(\mathbb K[x,D^{-1}]/(K^{H}_{uv}(x)))$. Since the conditions of \Cref{lem:schur-sum-degree} are satisfied by \Cref{lem:bag-expansion,lem:generic-denominators} whence \eqref{eq:bag-term} gives $K^{H}_{uv} = N(x)/D(x)$ where $\gcd(N,D)=1, \deg N = 2\left|\mathcal B_{uv}\right|-1$ and $\dim_{\mathbb K}\mathbb K[x,D^{-1}]/(N/D) = \deg N$. Therefore \[\operatorname{MLdeg} (G) = 2\left|\mathcal B_{uv}\right|-1 = 2\left(\left|\mathcal C_{uv}(H)\right| + \left|\mathcal S_{uv}(H,T)\right|\right)-1.\]

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
If $H\smallsetminus uv$ is chordal, then $uv$ lies in only one maximal clique of $H$. Indeed, otherwise the subtree $T_{uv}$ has an edge $CC'$. Choose $a\in C\smallsetminus C'$ and $b\in C'\smallsetminus C$. These sets are nonempty because $C,C'$ are distinct maximal cliques. Moreover $ab\notin E(H)$: if a maximal clique $D$ contained $a,b$, then $D$ lies on one side of the edge $CC'$; applying the path property to $a$ or to $b$, according to the side, would force $a\in C'$ or $b\in C$. Hence $u,a,v,b,u$ is an induced four-cycle in $H\smallsetminus uv$, a contradiction. Thus $\left|\mathcal C_{uv}(H)\right|=1$ and $\mathcal S_{uv}(H,T)=\varnothing$, and the formula correctly gives ML degree $1$.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:component-factorization}
Every $A_{j}$ is nonzero and finite dimensional $\mathbb K_{G}-$algebra. Further, \[\operatorname{MLdeg}(H\smallsetminus F) = \prod_{j=1}^{s}\dim_{\mathbb K_{G}}A_{j}.\]
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
If $f\in F_{j}\cap F_{B}$ for some $j\in [s], B\in \mathcal B(H,T)$, then $F_{B}\subseteq F_{j}$. Indeed if $f\in F_{B}$ then for every $g\in F_{B}\smallsetminus \ensuremath{\left\{f\right\}}$, the vertices $f,g$ are adjacent in $\Gamma_{T}(F)$ by definition, so $g\in F_{j}$. It follows that $(\Sigma_{B}^{-1})_{f} \in \mathbb K_{G}[x_{F_{B}}, \det \Sigma_{B}^{-1}] \subseteq R_{j}$ whenever $f\in F_{j}$. By the clique separator formula \eqref{eq:chordal-formula} in \Cref{prop:chordal-formula} and observation \eqref{obs:padding}, we have 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:component-dependence}
K^{H}_{f} = \sum_{\substack{B\in\mathcal B(H,T)\\f\in F_B}} \lambda_{B}^{(f)}(\Sigma_B^{-1})_f \in R_{j} \text{ whenever }f\in F_{j}.
\end{equation}
The equations $K_{f}^{H}=0$ split similarly. If $F_{B}\neq\varnothing$, then all edges in $F_B$ belong to a unique component $F_j$. Every determinant associated with a bag $B$ satisfying $F_B=\varnothing$ is a nonzero element of $\mathbb K_{G}$. Hence, for some $u\in\mathbb K_{G}^\times$, we have $\Delta_{H}=u\prod_{j=1}^s\Delta_j$. Due to the separation of the global determinant for $F$ into component determinants, we get the decomposition $R\cong R_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb K_{G}}\cdots \otimes_{\mathbb K_{G}}R_{s}$ of the global localized algebra into componentwise localized algebras. Under this identification, we have  $J_{F} =\sum_{j\in[s]}J_{j}R$ due to the separation of the likelihood equations from \eqref{eq:component-dependence}. $J_{F}$ is precisely the kernel of the surjective homomorphism \[\bigotimes_{j=1}^{s}R_{j}\twoheadrightarrow \bigotimes_{j = 1}^{s} R_{j}/J_{j}, \qquad a_{1}\otimes \cdots\otimes a_{s} \mapsto (a_{1}+J_{1})\otimes \cdots\otimes (a_{s}+J_{s}).\] By the first isomorphism theorem, 
\[ 
R/J_F \cong \bigotimes_{j=1}^s R_j/J_j.
\]
By the discussion above with application of \Cref{lem:algebraic-fill-reduction}, $R/J_{F}$ is finite dimensional. Since each $A_{j} = R_{j}/J_{j}$ embeds into $R/J_{F}$, we must have $\dim_{\mathbb K_{G}}(R_{j}/J_{j}) < \infty$. Taking dimensions of the above isomorphism gives $\operatorname{MLdeg}(H\smallsetminus F) = \prod \dim_{\mathbb K_{G}}A_{j}$.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:bag-noninteracting}
Let $H$ be chordal, let $T$ be a clique tree of $H$, and let $F\subseteq E(H)$ be bag-noninteracting. Then
\[
 \operatorname{MLdeg}(H\smallsetminus F)=\prod_{f\in F}(2m_{f}-1).
\]
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Bag-noninteraction says exactly that $\Gamma_{T}(F)$ has no edge, so its components are the singletons $\ensuremath{\left\{f\right\}}$, for $f\in F$. Fix $f=uv$. A bag contributing to the sum \eqref{eq:component-dependence} in $K^{H}_{f}$ contains $f$ and hence, by bag-noninteraction, contains no other edge of $F$. Thus all entries of that bag except $x_{f}=\Sigma_{uv}$ lie in $\mathbb K_{G}$. \Cref{lem:schur-sum-degree,lem:bag-expansion} give
\[
 K^{H}_{f}(x_{f})= \sum_{B\in\mathcal B_f(H,T)} \lambda_{B}^{(f)}\left(-\frac{x_{f}-c_{B}}{D_{B}(x_{f})}\right), \qquad D_{B}(x_{f}) = \alpha_{B} \beta_{B}-(x_{f}-c_{B})^{2}.
\]
The coefficients satisfy $\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_{f}(H,T)}\lambda_{B}^{(f)}=1$. Moreover, the generic nonvanishing and coprimality conditions in \Cref{lem:generic-denominators} involve only entries of these relevant bags. Those entries, other than $x_{f}$, are all elements of $\mathbb K_{G}$. Hence the proof of \Cref{lem:generic-denominators} gives nonzero polynomials in the generic data on $G$. Since there are only finitely many $f$, the required conditions hold simultaneously for generic data.

By \Cref{lem:schur-sum-degree}, the reduced fraction for $K^{H}_{f}$ has a numerator which is coprime to its denominator and this numerator has degree $2m_{f}-1$. Consequently $\dim_{\mathbb K_{G}}A_{f}=2m_{f}-1$. Applying \Cref{lem:component-factorization} to the singleton components gives the claimed $\operatorname{MLdeg}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:double-blow-up-degree}
Let $D$ be the graph in \Cref{fig:counter-example-blow-up}. Then $\operatorname{MLdeg}(D)=9$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
See \Cref{sec:code} for a detailed proof. Consider the chordal graph $H=D+\ensuremath{\left\{15,25\right\}}$. Solving for these fill edges automatically completes the matrix variables for edges $36,46$. Let $x=\Sigma_{15}, y=\Sigma_{25}$. The unique clique tree on $H$ has vertices $C_{1}=\ensuremath{\left\{1,2,3,4,5\right\}}, C_{2}=\ensuremath{\left\{1,2,5,6\right\}}$ and edge label $S=\ensuremath{\left\{1,2,5\right\}}$. Chordal formula says $\partial_{z}\log \det \Sigma = \partial_{z}\log(q_{1}q_{2}/q_{0})$ where $q_{0}=\det \Sigma_{S}, q_{1}=\det \Sigma_{C_{1}}, q_{2}=\det \Sigma_{C_{2}}$ and $z\in \ensuremath{\left\{x,y\right\}}$. $\partial_{z}\log \det\Sigma$ is then a rational form with numerator a quintic polynomial for both $z=x,y$ and denominator $q_{0}q_{1}q_{2}$. The numerators form a system of two quintic equations whence they have $25$ solutions by Bezout. But one must also discount the roots of the denominator because the $\operatorname{MLdeg}$ is the number of roots away from the poles. One can show that $q_{0},q_{1}$ intersect at four distinct points. Same is true for $q_{1},q_{2}$. But $q_{0},q_{2}$ intersect at two distinct points with multiplicity two each. There is also no triple intersection. Therefore the number of roots of the numerators, which occur at the poles, is $4\cdot 1 + 4\cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 4 = 16$ whence $\operatorname{MLdeg}(D)=25-16=9$.
\end{proof}

\phi(A) = \begin{cases} 
(A\smallsetminus\ensuremath{\left\{w\right\}})\cup Q,&w\in A,\\ 
A,& w\notin A.
\end{cases}
\]

Indeed, any strict clique extension of $\phi(C)$ would, after replacing $Q$ by $w$, give a strict clique extension of $C$ in $H$. Conversely, replacing the vertices of $Q$ in any clique of $\tilde H$ by $w$ gives a clique of $H$. Any maximal clique of $\tilde H$ that meets $Q$ contains all of $Q$, because the vertices of $Q$ are pairwise adjacent true twins. Consequently every maximal clique of $\tilde H$ is $\phi(C)$ for a unique maximal clique $C$ of $H$. Uniqueness follows because membership in $Q$ records membership of $w$, after which the original subset is recovered. Thus $\mathcal C(\tilde H)=\ensuremath{\left\{\phi(C) 
 \nonscript\;
 \ifnum\currentgrouptype=16
  \;\middle|\;
 \else
  \;|\;
 \fi
 C\in\mathcal C(H)\right\}}$.

For each $z\in Q$, the nodes containing $z$ are precisely those whose original labels contained $w$, and these nodes also form a connected subtree. <!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:extension-of-scalars}
Let $\iota:K\to L$ be an embedding of fields. Suppose $h\in R\coloneqq K[(z_{i})_{i\in[n]}]\smallsetminus \ensuremath{\left\{0\right\}}$ and $\mathcal P = \ensuremath{\left\{P_{1},\cdots,P_{m}\right\}} \subseteq R[h^{-1}]$. Define $\mathcal A \coloneqq R[h^{-1}]/(\mathcal P), \mathcal A_{L} \coloneqq L[(z_{i})_{i\in[n]}, \iota(h)^{-1}]/(\iota(\mathcal P))$. Then there is a natural $L-$algebra isomorphism $\mathcal A_{L} \cong L\otimes_{K,\iota}\mathcal A$. If either algebra is finite dimensional over their respective fields, we have $\dim_{K}\mathcal A = \dim_{L}\mathcal A_{L}$.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
Extension of scalars commutes with forming polynomial rings and with localization. Consequently, $L\otimes_{K,\iota}R[h^{-1}] \cong L[(z_{i})_{i\in[n]},\iota(h)^{-1}]$. $\iota(h)\ne 0$ because $\iota$ is a monomorphism of fields. Passing to the quotient by the images of $P_1,\cdots,P_m$ gives
\[
L\otimes_{K,\iota}\mathcal A \cong \frac{L[(z_{i})_{i\in[n]},\iota(h)^{-1}]}{(\iota(P_{j}):j\in[m])} = \mathcal A_{L}.\]
Let $\mathcal E$ be a $K-$basis of $\mathcal A$. Note, we don't yet assume $\mathcal E$ is finite. As $L-$vector spaces, we have
\[
L\otimes_{K,\iota}\mathcal A \cong L\otimes_{K,\iota} \left(\bigoplus_{e\in\mathcal E}Ke\right) \cong \bigoplus_{e\in\mathcal E}L.
\]
Thus $\mathcal A_{L}$ is finite-dimensional precisely when $\mathcal E$ is finite, and its dimension is then $\left|\mathcal E\right|=\dim_{K}\mathcal A$.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:degree-two-blow-up}
If $w\in V(G)$ has $\deg w = 2$, then $\operatorname{MLdeg}(\operatorname{Bl}_w^q(G))=\operatorname{MLdeg}(G)~\forall~ q\geq1$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Let $G,w$ be as given with $S \coloneqq \mathcal N_{w,G}=\ensuremath{\left\{a,b\right\}}$. If $ab\in E(G)$ then $w$ is simplicial, and the result follows from \Cref{prop:simplicial-blow-up}. Henceforth assume $ab\notin E(G)$. 

Take $V_{0} = V(G) \smallsetminus \ensuremath{\left\{w\right\}}, G_{0} = G[V_{0}] = G\smallsetminus w$ and $G_{q}\coloneqq \operatorname{Bl}_{w}^{q}(G)$. Let $Q_{q}=K_{q}$ be the part that replaces $w$ in $G$. If $V_{0}=S$ then $G$ is a chordal graph whence $G_{q} = K_{Q_{q}\cup \ensuremath{\left\{a\right\}}} \cup_{Q_{q}} K_{Q_{q}\cup\ensuremath{\left\{b\right\}}}$ whence $\operatorname{MLdeg}(G_{q}) = 1 =\operatorname{MLdeg}(G)$. Therefore assume $\left|V_{0}\right|\ge 3$. We choose the chordal completion $H_{q} = K_{V_{0}} \cup_{S} K_{S\cup Q_{q}}$ of $G_{q}$. Then $F = {{V_{0}}\choose 2}\smallsetminus E(G_{0})$ determine the fill edges, that is, $H_{q}=G_{q}+F$. Clearly, $G_{0}+F = K_{V_{0}} \eqqcolon H_{0}$. In particular, $ab\in F$. This means that we have the same set of fill edges $F$ for every $q$. Clearly $\mathcal C(H_{q}) = \ensuremath{\left\{V_{0}, S\cup Q_{q}\right\}}$ and thus the only clique tree $T_{q}$ of $H_{q}$ has $\mathcal S(H_{q},T_{q}) = \ensuremath{\left\{\ensuremath{\left\{a,b\right\}}\right\}} = \ensuremath{\left\{S\right\}}$. By the chordal formula (and \Cref{lem:clique-gluing-identity}), \begin{equation}\label{eq:full-chordal}K^{H_{q}} = \left[ K^{H_{0}}\right]^{0} + \left[ \Sigma_{S\cup Q_{q}}^{-1}\right]^{0} - \left[\Sigma_{S}^{-1}\right]^{0}\end{equation} where the matrices have been embedded to the size of $V(G_{q})\times V(G_{q})$ padded with zeroes. We thus want to localize (for later) at $\Delta_{H_{q}} = \det (K^{H_{0}})^{-1}\det \Sigma_{S\cup Q_{q}} \det \Sigma_{S}$.

Introduce a variable $x_{f}$ for every $f\in F$ and write $x=x_{ab}$ for the special $ab\in F$. Let $\eta = (s_{ij})_{ij\in \mathcal O_{G_{0}}}$ and $k\coloneqq \mathbb C(\eta)$. $\eta$ comprises the observed entries in $G_{0}$. Let $\Sigma(x_{F})$ be the symmetric covariance matrix on $G_{0}$. Then \[\Sigma_{S}(x) = \begin{bmatrix}s_{aa} & x\\x&s_{bb}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \Sigma_{S\cup Q_{q}}(x) = \begin{bmatrix}
s_{aa} & x & r^{\top}\\
x & s_{bb} & u^{\top}\\
r & u & T
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The entries of $r,u,T$ are precisely the additional observed entries of $G_{q}$. The data field of $G_{q}$ is $L_{q}\coloneqq K_{G_{q}} = k\left(\ensuremath{\left\{r_{i}\right\}}_{i\in [q]} \cup \ensuremath{\left\{u_{i}\right\}}_{i\in [q]} \cup \ensuremath{\left\{t_{ij}\right\}}_{1\le i\le j \le q}\right)$. Consider the quantities \[\tilde c \coloneqq r^{\top}T^{-1}u \in L_{q}, \qquad \tilde D(x)\coloneqq (s_{aa}-r^{\top}T^{-1}r)(s_{bb}-u^{\top}T^{-1}u) - (x-\tilde c)^{2}\in L_{q}[x].\] By the Schur complement determinant formula discussed earlier, $\det \Sigma_{S\cup Q_{q}} = \det T\cdot \tilde D(x)$. The Schur complement inverse formula gives $(\Sigma_{S\cup Q_{q}}^{-1})_{ab} = -(x-\tilde c)/\tilde D(x)$. The quantities $\tilde c$ and $\tilde D(\tilde c)$ are algebraically independent over $k$. Indeed, specializing $T=I_q$, $r=(r_1,0,\ldots,0)^\top$, and $u=(u_1,0,\ldots,0)^\top$ gives $\tilde c = r_1u_1, \tilde D(\tilde c) = (s_{aa}-r_1^2)(s_{bb}-u_1^2)$.
Their Jacobian determinant with respect to $r_1,u_1$ is
$2(s_{bb}r_1^2-s_{aa}u_1^2)$, which is not identically zero.
Thus this specialization defines a dominant map to the two effective
parameters, proving the claimed algebraic independence for every
$q\ge1$.  Consequently there is a $k-$embedding $\iota_{q}: k(c,D) \hookrightarrow L_{q}$ given by $c\mapsto \tilde c, D\mapsto \tilde D(\tilde c)$.

The likelihood ideal for any $G_{q}$ is cut out by the following rational functions in $L_{q}(x_{F})$ for $f\in F$:
\[p_{f}(x_{F}) = \begin{cases}
(\Sigma_{H_{0}}(x_{F})^{-1})_{f} & \text{ if } f\ne ab\\
(\Sigma_{H_{0}}(x_{F})^{-1})_{ab} - (x-\tilde c)/\tilde D(x) + x/(s_{aa}s_{bb}-x^{2}) & \text{ if } f = ab
\end{cases}.
\] 
This directly comes from \eqref{eq:full-chordal} and the fact that $F\cap {{S\cup Q_{q}}\choose 2} = \ensuremath{\left\{ab\right\}}$. Therefore take $h = \det \Sigma_{H_{0}}(x_{F}) \cdot (D-(x-c)^{2})\cdot (s_{aa}s_{bb}-x^{2}) \in k(c,D)[x_{F}]$ and define \[
P_{f}(x_{F}) \coloneqq \begin{cases}
(\Sigma_{H_{0}}(x_{F})^{-1})_{f} & \text{ if } f\ne ab\\
(\Sigma_{H_{0}}(x_{F})^{-1})_{ab} - (x- c)/(D-(x-c)^{2}) + x/(s_{aa}s_{bb}-x^{2}) & \text{ if } f = ab
\end{cases} 
\]
in $k(c,D)[x_{F},h^{-1}]$. Take $\mathcal A = k(c,D)[x_{F},h^{-1}]/(P_{f}: f\in F)$. Note that $\Sigma_{H_{0}}(x_{F})$ has nonzero determinant in $k[x_{F}]$. Also $D-(x-c)^{2}$ and $s_{aa}s_{bb}-x^{2}$ are nonzero in $k(c,D)[x_{F}]$. Therefore $h\ne 0$. Therefore the localization, and hence the algebra $\mathcal A$, is well defined. Note that $p_{f} = \iota_{q}(P_{f})~\forall~ f\in F$ and $\iota_{q}(h) = \det \Sigma_{H_{0}}(x_{F}) \cdot \tilde D(x)\cdot (s_{aa}s_{bb}-x^{2})$ which is just $\Delta_{H_{q}}$ up to units in $L_{q}$. The generic likelihood algebra of $G_{q}$ is $\mathcal A_{q} = L_{q}[x_{F},\Delta_{H_{q}}^{-1}]/(p_{f}:f\in F)$. By \Cref{lem:extension-of-scalars}, \[
\dim_{k(c,D)}\mathcal A = \dim_{L_{q}}\mathcal A_{q} = \operatorname{MLdeg}(G_{q}).

<!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{lem:clique-gluing-identity}
Let $A,S,B$ be pairwise disjoint index sets. Let
\[\Sigma_1=\begin{bmatrix}P&U\\U^\top&R\end{bmatrix}_{A,S}, \qquad \Sigma_2=\begin{bmatrix}R&V\\V^\top&Q\end{bmatrix}_{S,B}\]
be invertible symmetric matrices with $R$ invertible. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glued-covariance}
\hat\Sigma=
\begin{bmatrix}
P&U&UR^{-1}V\\
U^\top&R&V\\
V^\top R^{-1}U^\top&V^\top&Q
\end{bmatrix}_{A,S,B}
\end{equation}
is invertible and satisfies
\begin{align}
\hat\Sigma^{-1} &=\left[\Sigma_1^{-1}\right]^{0}+\left[ \Sigma_2^{-1}\right]^{0}-\left[ R^{-1}\right]^{0}, \label{eq:glued-inverse}\\
\det\hat\Sigma &=\frac{\det\Sigma_1\det\Sigma_2}{\det R}. \label{eq:glued-determinant}
\end{align}
Moreover, \eqref{eq:glued-covariance} is the unique nonsingular symmetric matrix whose restrictions to $A\cup S$ and $S\cup B$ are $\Sigma_1$ and $\Sigma_2$ respectively, with zero entries in the $A\times B$ block of the inverse.
<!-- end lemma-12 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
We denote the Schur complement of a block $X$ in matrix $M$ as $S_{X}(M)$. First note that $\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}$ are invertible and the blocks $R$ within them are invertible. So  $S_{R}(\Sigma_{1}) = P-UR^{-1}U^{\top}, S_{R}(\Sigma_{2}) = Q-V^{\top}R^{-1}V$ are invertible. Now reorder the matrix $\hat\Sigma$ as $A,B,S$ and take the Schur complement of $R$ which is precisely
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diag-block}
S_{R}(\hat\Sigma) = \begin{bmatrix}
P-UR^{-1}U^\top&0\\
0&Q-V^\top R^{-1}V
\end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
S_{R}(\Sigma_{1})\\&S_{R}(\Sigma_{2})
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The determinant formula for a Schur complement says \[\det\hat\Sigma = \det R \cdot \det S_{R}(\hat\Sigma) = \det R\cdot \det S_{R}(\Sigma_{1}) \det S_{R}(\Sigma_{2}) = \frac{\det \Sigma_{1}\det\Sigma_{2}}{\det R}.\]

<!-- lemma-13: body_md -->
\label{lem:determinant-saturation-localization}
Let $\mathcal E$ be an index set, $K$ be a field of characteristic zero, $R=K[x_{ij}: ij\in \mathcal E],$ and $A$ be a symmetric matrix such that $A_{ij}=x_{ij}~\forall~ ij\in \mathcal E$ and $A_{ij}\in K$ otherwise. Write
\[p=\det(A),\qquad J=(\operatorname{Cof}_{ij}(A):(i,j)\in \mathcal E), \qquad I=J:p^\infty.\]
Then the natural $K$-algebra homomorphism $R/I\longrightarrow R[p^{-1}]/JR[p^{-1}]$ is an isomorphism.
<!-- end lemma-13 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
Write $C_{ij} = \operatorname{Cof}_{ij}(A)$. For a diagonal variable $x_{ii}$, Jacobi's cofactor formula gives $\partial p/\partial x_{ii}=C_{ii}$. For $i<j$, the variable $x_{ij}$ occurs in both the $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$-entries of $A$. Since $A$ and its cofactor matrix are
symmetric,
\[\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{ij}} = C_{ij}+C_{ji}=2C_{ij}.\]
Because $\operatorname{char}K=0$, it follows that $J= \left( \partial_{ij} p:ij\in \mathcal E\right)$. Thus $J$ is the Jacobian ideal of $p$ with respect to all the variables of $R$. By the  saturation-localization correspondence,
\[J:p^\infty=JR[p^{-1}]\cap R, \qquad JR[p^{-1}]=IR[p^{-1}]. \]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isomlocal}
R[p^{-1}]/JR[p^{-1}] \cong (R/I)[p^{-1}].
\end{equation}

We will be done if we show that the image of $p$ is a unit in $R/I$. By the definition of saturation, $I:p=I,$ so $p$ is a nonzerodivisor on $R/I$. Since $R/I$ is Noetherian, every minimal prime of $R/I$ is associated. Hence $p$ belongs to no minimal prime over $I$.

Let $\mathfrak q$ be a minimal prime over $I$ and put $F=\operatorname{Frac}(R/\mathfrak q)$. Since $J\subseteq\mathfrak q$,  
\[d\overline p = \sum_{ij\in \mathcal E}\frac{\partial\overline p}{\partial x_{ij}}d x_{ij} = 0\] 
in $\Omega_{F/K}$, the vector space of K\"ahler differentials. By \cite[Theorem~$87(i)$, p.~$267$]{MatsumuraCA}, for an extension $F/K$ of fields of characteristic zero, if $u\in F$ then one has $du=0$ in $\Omega_{F/K}$ iff $u$ is algebraic over $K$. Thus $\overline p$ is algebraic over $K$. Since $p\notin\mathfrak q$, it is nonzero in $F$, and hence it is a unit already in $R/\mathfrak q$. In fact, this inverse is a polynomial in $\overline p$ with coefficients in $K$ (constructed using the minimal polynomial).

Therefore $p$ is a unit modulo every minimal prime over $I$. Every maximal ideal containing $I$ contains a minimal prime over $I$, so no such maximal ideal can contain $p$. Hence $I+(p)=R$, meaning that $p$ is a unit in $R/I$. It follows that
\[(R/I)[p^{-1}]=R/I.\]
Combining this with \eqref{eq:isomlocal} proves the result.

<!-- lemma-14: body_md -->
\label{lem:Delta-unit}
Let $H$ be chordal, let $F\subseteq E(H)$, and put $G=H\setminus F$. Let $\mathcal A_{G}\coloneqq R_{G}/I_{G}$ be the generic likelihood algebra defined in \eqref{eq:likelihoodequations}. Identify the nonedge variables corresponding to $F$ with the variables $x_{f}$, and let
\[\Delta_{H}(x_{F})= \prod_{C\in\mathcal C(H)}\det y_{C}(x_{F}) \prod_{e\in E(T)}\det y_{B_{e}}(x_{F}).\]
Then the image of every factor $\det y_{C}(x_{F})$ and $\det y_{B_{e}}(x_{F})$ in $\mathcal A_G$ is a unit. In particular, the image of $\Delta_{H}(x_{F})$ is a unit in $\mathcal A_{G}$.
<!-- end lemma-14 -->
<!-- proof-24: body_md -->
Let
\[X=\mathcal M_{G}^\circ,\qquad Y=\mathbb A^{\mathcal O_{G}}, \]
and let $\pi_{G}:X\to Y$ be the coordinate projection. By \Cref{lem:likelihood-projection-generically-finite}, $\pi_{G}$ is dominant and generically finite. Matrix inversion identifies $X$ with a principal open subset of the affine space $L_G$. Consequently, $X$ is integral. Let $A_X=\mathbb C[X] \cong \mathbb C[k_{ij} : ij\in \mathcal \mathcal O_{G}][(\det K)^{-1}]$ and $A_Y=\mathbb C[Y] \cong \mathbb C[s_{ij}: ij\in \mathcal \mathcal O_{G}]$. Dominance of $\pi_G$ implies that the induced pullback homomorphism
\[\pi_{G}^{*}:A_{Y}\longrightarrow A_{X}, \qquad s_{ij}\mapsto (K^{-1})_{ij}\]
is injective. Recall that $\mathbb K_{G}=\operatorname{Frac}(A_{Y})$, so the coordinate algebra of the generic fiber is
\[A_{X}\otimes_{A_{Y}}\mathbb K_{G} = (A_{Y}\setminus\ensuremath{\left\{0\right\}})^{-1}A_{X}.\]
Since $A_{X}$ is a domain, this localization is also a domain. But $\pi_{G}$ is generically finite, whence $\operatorname{Frac}(A_{X})/\mathbb K_{G}$ is a finite extension. Consequently $(A_{Y}\setminus\ensuremath{\left\{0\right\}})^{-1}A_{X}$ is finite-dimensional over $\mathbb K_{G}$ (contained in $\operatorname{Frac}(A_{X})$). A finite-dimensional algebra over a field that is also a domain is a field. By \eqref{eq:likelihoodequations}, this generic-fiber algebra is naturally isomorphic to $\mathcal A_{G}$.

For each clique $C\in\mathcal C(H)$, consider the regular function $\det_C:X\to \mathbb C, \det_{C}(\Sigma) = \det(\Sigma_{C})$. Since $\det_{C}(I)=1$, the function $\det_{C}$ is a nonzero element in the domain $A_{X}$. Since localization of a domain is injective, the image of $\det_{C}$ in $(A_{Y}\smallsetminus\ensuremath{\left\{0\right\}})^{-1}A_{X} \cong \mathcal A_{G}$ is also nonzero and is therefore a unit. This image is precisely $\det y_{C}(x_{F})$.

The same argument applies to every separator $B_{e}$, because $\det(I_{B_{e}})=1$. Thus every clique and separator determinant is a unit in $\mathcal A_{G}$, and hence so is their product $\Delta_{H}(x_{F})$.
