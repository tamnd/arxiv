<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:ratirr}
The phase dependence above is the general situation for a \emph{rational}
normal. The periodic data restricted to the lower dimensional boundary   $\{x/\varepsilon : x\in{\partial\Omega},\ n(x)=n\}$ is periodic on that boundary,
and different sequences $\varepsilon_k\to0$ will see different parts of the original period cell.  For a smooth uniformly convex domain, or more generally a smooth domain
of finite type in the sense of~\cite{Zhuge18}, the boundary has no flat
parts and the set of boundary points with rational normal has surface
measure zero, so the normal is irrational at almost every boundary point.  For irrational
$n$ the restriction of $\{x/\varepsilon\}$ equidistributes over the full torus $\mathbb{T}^d$ as
$\varepsilon\to0$ (Weyl's theorem), the limit is \emph{unique},
and $d(n)$ is the ergodic average
\begin{equation}\label{eq:d-irrational}
  d(n) = \int_{\mathbb{T}^d} \chi_n(y)\,\rho(y,n)\,\mathrm{d} y ,
\end{equation}
with $\rho(\cdot,n)$ determined by the half-space problem in direction
$n$~\cite{GVM12,Prange13}, which we describe in the next section.
The rational normals then do not affect the data of $\theta^*$ in
$L^2({\partial\Omega})$.  Throughout the paper we assume that $\Omega$ is smooth and
uniformly convex or of finite type.  Then the limit $\theta^*$ of $\theta^\varepsilon$ is
unique and its boundary data is $d(n(x))\partial_n u^0$ with $d$ given
by~\eqref{eq:d-irrational}: the convergence $\theta^\varepsilon\to\theta^*$ is proved
in~\cite{GVM12} for uniformly convex domains, such as the disk used in
Section~\ref{sec:disk}, and in~\cite{Zhuge18} for domains of finite type.

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
The \emph{DtN operator} $N_n$ of~\eqref{eq:lifted} maps the boundary data
$f=\Psi(0,\cdot)$ to the conormal flux
\[
  N_n f \;:=\; -a\,(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f\big|_{t=0}
  \;=\; n\cdot a\nabla_y\psi_f\big|_{\partial H_n},
\]
where $\Psi_f$ is the bounded solution of~\eqref{eq:lifted} with data $f$ and
$\psi_f(y):=\Psi_f(-y\cdot n,y)$ is the corresponding $a$-harmonic function on
the half-space $H_n$; the second expression says that $N_n f$ is the usual
conormal derivative of $\psi_f$ on the hyperplane $\partial H_n$, written in the
cell variable.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:known}
Let $n$ be irrational.  For all smooth data $f$ the lifted
problem~\eqref{eq:lifted} has a unique solution $\Psi_f$ with
$(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f$ and $\nabla^\perp\Psi_f$ in $L^2((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^d)$;
it is smooth, and all derivatives of $(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f$ and
$\nabla^\perp\Psi_f$ are in $L^2((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^d)$ as
well~\cite[Prop.~2]{GVM11}.  The solution converges to a constant at depth: for $n$ satisfying a
Diophantine condition, $\Psi_f(t,\cdot)$ converges on $\mathbb{T}^d$ as $t\to\infty$,
faster than any power of $t$~\cite{GVM11}; for every irrational $n$, the
half-space solution $\psi_f(y)=\Psi_f(-y\cdot n,y)$ converges as
$y\cdot n\to-\infty$, locally uniformly in the tangential variable and
without a rate~\cite[Theorem~1.2]{Prange13}.  The constant
is~\cite{Prange13}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:representer}
   \lim_{y\cdot n\to-\infty}\psi_f(y)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}\rho(\theta,n)\,f(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta .
\end{equation}
In particular $\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}\rho=1$ and the boundary layer tail is
$d(n)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}\rho\,\chi_n$, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:d-twoterm}
  A_{nn}\,d(n)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}a\,\chi_n(1-\partial_n\chi_n)\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\langle\chi_n,N_n\chi_n\rangle .
\end{equation}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:energy}
Let $n$ be any unit vector and $f\in X$.  For every $T\in(0,\infty]$ the
problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:NT}
  \langle f,N_Tf\rangle:=\min\bigl\{\mathcal E_T(\Psi):\ \Psi(0,\cdot)=f\bigr\},
\end{equation}
over $\Psi$ with $(\partial_t-G)\Psi,\nabla^\perp\Psi\in L^2((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^d)$,
has a unique minimizer. 
The minimum is a quadratic form in $f$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:NT-bound}
  \langle f,N_Tf\rangle\le C\bigl(\|f\|^2+\|\nabla^\perp f\|^2\bigr),
\end{equation} where $C$
depends only on the bounds of $a$.  It is nondecreasing in $T$, and
$$\langle f,N_Tf\rangle\to\langle f,N_\infty f\rangle$$ as $T\to\infty$.  If $n$
is irrational and $f$ is smooth, the minimizer for $T=\infty$ is the
solution $\Psi_f$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:known}, so that $N_\infty=N_n$ on
$C^\infty(\mathbb{T}^d)$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Write $U(t,\theta):=\Psi(t,\theta-tn)$, the unknown of~\eqref{eq:gvm}.  Then
$\partial_tU$ and $\nabla^\perp U$ are $(\partial_t-G)\Psi$ and
$\nabla^\perp\Psi$ evaluated at $(t,\theta-tn)$, and since
$\theta\mapsto\theta-tn$ preserves the measure on $\mathbb{T}^d$,
\[
  \mathcal E_T(\Psi)=\int_0^T\!\!\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}a(\theta-tn)
  \bigl[|\partial_tU|^2+|\nabla^\perp U|^2\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d} t .
\]
Let $a\ge\lambda>0$ and $T<\infty$.  We minimize over the Hilbert space
\[
  \mathcal H_T:=\{U\in H^1(0,T;L^2(\mathbb{T}^d)):\ \nabla^\perp U\in L^2((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^d)\},
\]
with norm $\|U\|_{\mathcal H_T}^2:=\|U\|^2_{L^2}+\|\partial_tU\|^2_{L^2}+\|\nabla^\perp U\|^2_{L^2}$
(all on $(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^d$),
restricted to the closed affine set $\{U\in\mathcal H_T:\ U(0)=f\}$, where the
trace $U(0)$ makes sense because $U\in H^1$ in $t$.  On this set
$\mathcal E_T$ is continuous, and we show it is coercive as follows: writing $U(t):=U(t,\cdot)$
as an element of $L^2(\mathbb{T}^d)$,
$$U(t)=f+\int_0^t\partial_sU(s)\,\mathrm{d} s,$$ so
$$\|U\|^2_{L^2((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^d)}\le2T\|f\|^2+T^2\|\partial_tU\|^2_{L^2((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^d)},$$
and hence we have the coercivity inequality $$\|U\|_{\mathcal H_T}^2\le C_T\bigl(\|f\|^2+\mathcal E_T(U)/\lambda\bigr).$$
It is also strictly convex there, because $\mathcal E_T(W)=0$ with $W(0)=0$
forces $\partial_tW=0$ and hence $W=0$.  So it has a unique minimizer, and
the minimum is a quadratic form in $f$. Choosing the candidate
$U(t,\theta)=\varphi(t)f(\theta)$, with $\varphi$ smooth,
$\varphi(0)=1$ and $\varphi=0$ for $t\ge1$, gives
\[
  \langle f,N_Tf\rangle\le\mathcal E_T(U)
  \le\sup a\int_0^1\bigl(\varphi'(t)^2\|f\|^2+\varphi(t)^2\|\nabla^\perp f\|^2\bigr)\,\mathrm{d} t ,
\]
which is the bound~\eqref{eq:NT-bound}, uniformly in $T$.
Next we let $T\to\infty$.  Write $m_\infty$ for the infimum of
$\mathcal E_\infty$ over competitors with $U(0)=f$ (finite, by the candidate
above).  For $T<S\le\infty$ the restriction to $(0,T)$ of a competitor
on $(0,S)$ is a competitor on $(0,T)$ with no larger energy, so
$\langle f,N_Tf\rangle\le\langle f,N_Sf\rangle\le m_\infty$: the minima are
nondecreasing in $T$ and bounded, hence converge to a limit
$q\le m_\infty$.  We show that $q=m_\infty$ and that the infimum is attained.
Let $U_T$ be the minimizer for depth $T$ and fix $S<\infty$.  For $T\ge S$,
the coercivity estimate on $(0,S)$ applied to the restriction of $U_T$ gives
$\|U_T\|^2_{\mathcal H_S}\le C_S\bigl(\|f\|^2+\mathcal E_S(U_T)/\lambda\bigr)
\le C_S\bigl(\|f\|^2+q/\lambda\bigr)$, since
$\mathcal E_S(U_T)\le\mathcal E_T(U_T)=\langle f,N_Tf\rangle\le q$.  So the
$U_T$ are bounded in $\mathcal H_S$ for every $S$, and along a sequence
$T_k\to\infty$ (chosen by a diagonal argument over $S=1,2,\dots$) they converge
weakly in every $\mathcal H_S$ to a function $U_\infty$ on $(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^d$.  The
trace at $t=0$ is a bounded linear map on $\mathcal H_S$, so $U_\infty(0)=f$.
Since $\mathcal E_S$ is convex and continuous on $\mathcal H_S$, it is weakly
lower semicontinuous, and
\[
  \mathcal E_S(U_\infty)\le\liminf_k\mathcal E_S(U_{T_k})\le\liminf_k\mathcal E_{T_k}(U_{T_k})=q
  \qquad\text{for every }S .
\]
Letting $S\to\infty$ gives $\mathcal E_\infty(U_\infty)\le q\le m_\infty$,
while $\mathcal E_\infty(U_\infty)\ge m_\infty$ because $U_\infty$ is a
competitor.  So $U_\infty$ attains the infimum, $q=m_\infty=\langle f,N_\infty f\rangle$,
and the minimizer for $T=\infty$ is unique by the same strict convexity
argument as before ($\mathcal E_\infty(W)=0$ and $W(0)=0$ force $W=0$).

Finally let $n$ be irrational and $f$ smooth.  The solution $\Psi_f$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:known} has finite energy, so it is a competitor for
$T=\infty$, and we show that it is the minimizer.  Every competitor is
$\Psi_f+W$ with $W(0)=0$ and $\mathcal E_\infty(W)<\infty$, and expanding
the square,
\[
  \mathcal E_\infty(\Psi_f+W)=\mathcal E_\infty(\Psi_f)+2B_\infty(\Psi_f,W)+\mathcal E_\infty(W),
\]
where $B_T$ denotes the bilinear form of $\mathcal E_T$.  Since
$\mathcal E_\infty(W)\ge0$, it suffices to show that $B_\infty(\Psi_f,W)=0$.
Multiply~\eqref{eq:lifted} by $W$ and integrate over $(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^d$.
Integrating by parts in $t$, with $W(0)=0$, and using that $G$ is
antisymmetric on $L^2(\mathbb{T}^d)$, the left side becomes
\[
  \bigl\langle W(T),\,a(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f(T)\bigr\rangle
  -\int_0^T\!\bigl\langle a(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f,(\partial_t-G)W\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d} t ,
\]
and the right side becomes $\int_0^T\langle a\nabla^\perp\Psi_f,\nabla^\perp W\rangle\,\mathrm{d} t$
after integrating by parts in $\theta$.  Rearranging (first for smooth $W$,
then by density),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BT}
  B_T(\Psi_f,W)=\bigl\langle W(T),\,a(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f(T)\bigr\rangle .
\end{equation}
We now let $T\to\infty$ in~\eqref{eq:BT}.  The left side tends to
$B_\infty(\Psi_f,W)$, since both $\Psi_f$ and $W$ have finite energy on
$(0,\infty)$.  For the right side, set
$g(t):=\|a(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f(t)\|$, so that by Cauchy--Schwarz
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BT-bound}
  |B_T(\Psi_f,W)|\le\|W(T)\|\,g(T).
\end{equation}
Both factors are controlled by the energy.  First, $g$ is square
integrable on $(0,\infty)$, since
$\int_0^\infty g^2\,\mathrm{d} t\le(\sup a)\,\mathcal E_\infty(\Psi_f)<\infty$.
Second, writing $\widetilde W(t,\theta):=W(t,\theta-tn)$ as at the beginning
of the proof, $\widetilde W(0)=0$ and $\partial_t\widetilde W$ is
$(\partial_t-G)W$ evaluated at $(t,\theta-tn)$, so
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \|W(T)\|=\|\widetilde W(T)\|
  &\le\int_0^T\|\partial_t\widetilde W(t)\|\,\mathrm{d} t
  \le\sqrt T\,\|\partial_t\widetilde W\|_{L^2((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^d)}\\
  &\le\sqrt T\,(\mathcal E_\infty(W)/\lambda)^{1/2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Inserting both in~\eqref{eq:BT-bound},
\[
  |B_T(\Psi_f,W)|\le(\mathcal E_\infty(W)/\lambda)^{1/2}\,\sqrt T\,g(T).
\]
It remains to see that $\sqrt T\,g(T)$ is small for some arbitrarily large $T$.
If $T\,g(T)^2\ge c>0$ for all $T\ge T_0$, then $g(t)^2\ge c/t$ on
$(T_0,\infty)$, which is not integrable, a contradiction.  So there is a
sequence $T_j\to\infty$ with $\sqrt{T_j}\,g(T_j)\to0$, and along it the
right side of~\eqref{eq:BT} tends to zero.
Thus $B_\infty(\Psi_f,W)=0$, $\Psi_f$ is the minimizer for $T=\infty$, and
$\langle f,N_\infty f\rangle=\mathcal E_\infty(\Psi_f)=\langle f,N_n f\rangle$
by~\eqref{eq:energy}.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:riccati}
Let $G=n\cdot\nabla_\theta$ be the derivative along $n$ and
$L_\perp(n)=-\nabla^\perp\!\cdot(a\nabla^\perp\,\cdot)$ the tangential part of the
operator, with $\nabla^\perp=\nabla_\theta-n\,G$.  As operators on
$C^\infty(\mathbb{T}^d)$, the DtN operator $N_n$ satisfies the algebraic equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CR}
  N_n\,a^{-1}N_n \;+\; [\,G,N_n\,] \;=\; L_\perp(n),
  \qquad [G,N_n]:=GN_n-N_nG .
\end{equation}
Moreover the lifted solution satisfies the \emph{first-order} equation in the
depth variable
\begin{equation}\label{eq:semigroup}
  \partial_t\Psi_f=-K\,\Psi_f,\qquad K:=a^{-1}N_n-G ,\qquad \Psi_f(0,\cdot)=f,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ $\Psi_f(t,\cdot)=e^{-tK}f$: once $N_n$ is known, the boundary layer is
obtained by solving a linear ODE in $t$ whose unknown is a function on the cell.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The problem below depth $s$, with data $\Psi_f(s,\cdot)$, has the same
coefficient $a(\theta)$ as the problem below depth $0$ and its solution is
$\Psi_f(s+\cdot,\cdot)$.  Hence the DtN operator at every depth is the same
operator $N_n$, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:same-depth}
  -a\,(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f(t,\cdot)=N_n\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\qquad\text{for all }t\ge0,
\end{equation}
which is~\eqref{eq:semigroup} after dividing by $a$.  Substituting into~\eqref{eq:lifted} and using that
$N_n$ does not depend on $t$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
  L_\perp(n)\Psi_f=(\partial_t-G)\bigl(-N_n\Psi_f\bigr)
  &= -N_n(\partial_t-G)\Psi_f + [G,N_n]\Psi_f\\
  &= N_n a^{-1}N_n\Psi_f+[G,N_n]\Psi_f .
\end{aligned}
\]
Since the data $f$ are arbitrary, \eqref{eq:CR} follows.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:why}
The proof uses that in the form~\eqref{eq:lifted} the coefficient $a(\theta)$
does not depend on $t$, so the DtN operator is the same at every depth and the
equation is algebraic.  In the form~\eqref{eq:gvm} the coefficient moves with
$t$, and the DtN operator $M(t)$ of the problem below depth $t$ satisfies the
usual Riccati differential equation.  Since that problem is the problem below
depth $0$ with the cell shifted by $tn$, $M(t)$ is $N_n$ conjugated by the
shift $\tau_tf(\theta):=f(\theta-tn)$, and
\[
  \dot M=M\,a(\cdot-tn)^{-1}M-L_\perp(t),\qquad
  M(t)=\tau_tN_n\tau_t^{-1},\qquad
  \dot M=-[G,M] .
\]
Conjugating back gives~\eqref{eq:CR}: the derivative in depth has become the
commutator with $G$.  Without it the equation would be $N_n a^{-1}N_n=L_\perp(n)$,
whose solution is an operator square root (Section~\ref{sec:sqrt}).  For
coefficients periodic in depth (rational $n$), stationary Riccati equations of
this kind underlie the transparent boundary conditions of Joly, Li and
Fliss~\cite{JLF06,FJ09}; \eqref{eq:CR} extends this to quasi-periodic depth
dependence.

Multiplying~\eqref{eq:CR} by $-1$ and using $G^{T}=-G$ puts it in the
standard form of control theory~\cite{BIM12},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CARE}
  \mathcal A^{T}X+X\mathcal A-X\mathcal BX+\mathcal C=0,
  \qquad X:=N_n,\quad \mathcal A:=G,\quad \mathcal B:=a^{-1},\quad \mathcal C:=L_\perp(n) ,
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal B$ and $\mathcal C$ symmetric and non-negative.  Of its many solutions the relevant one
is the stabilizing solution, for which $e^{t(\mathcal A-\mathcal BX)}=e^{-tK}$
does not grow: the boundary layer does not grow into the half-space (in a weak
sense, because of the constant mode and, for rational $n$, the neutral modes
of Remark~\ref{rem:fd}).  Decay in depth plays the role of decay in time, so
numerical methods for such equations apply directly to~\eqref{eq:CR}
(Section~\ref{sec:marching}); they rely on the same depth invariance.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:fd}
On a finite-dimensional space of trigonometric polynomials, the setting of
Section~\ref{sec:computing}, the truncated lifted equation is a linear system
of ordinary differential equations in $t$ with constant coefficients, and
$N_n$ is the map from boundary values to fluxes on its bounded solutions.
For rational $n$ these include, besides the constant, the \emph{neutral}
modes $e^{2\pi ik\cdot(\theta+tn)}$ with $k\cdot n^\perp=0$: functions constant
along each line $\{\theta+zn^\perp\}$, on which the flux vanishes (the
phase-dependent tails of Remark~\ref{rem:rational}).  We do not address the
extent to which~\eqref{eq:CR} together with $N_n\ge0$ determines $N_n$; for
irrational $n$ in infinite dimensions the decay rates of the boundary layer
accumulate at $0$ (the small divisors).

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:density}
Let $\rho(\cdot,n)$ be given by \eqref{eq:rho-cell}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}\rho=1$, and for all boundary data $f$ for which
  $\Psi_f(t,\cdot)$ converges in $L^2(\mathbb{T}^d)$ to a constant as $t\to\infty$, the limit is
  $\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}\rho f$, i.e.\ \eqref{eq:representer} holds.
\item[(ii)] $\rho$ satisfies the linear equation
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
    N_n\!\left(\frac{\rho}{a}\right)+\partial_n\rho=0 ,
  \end{equation}
  that is, $K^{T}\rho=0$ with $K$ the operator of \eqref{eq:semigroup}.
  In other words, $\langle\rho,\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\rangle$ is a conserved quantity
  of the depth equation~\eqref{eq:semigroup}: it does not depend on $t$ for any
  solution.
\item[(iii)] If $n$ is such that $\Psi_f(t,\cdot)$ converges in $L^2(\mathbb{T}^d)$
  to a constant as $t\to\infty$ for all data $f$ (in particular every
  Diophantine $n$, by~\cite{GVM11}), then
  $\rho\ge0$, so that $\rho$ is a probability density on the cell, and $\rho$
  is the unique solution of~\eqref{eq:kernel} with $\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}\rho=1$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
(ii) Apply $N_n a^{-1}+G$ to $A_{nn}\rho$.  Using~\eqref{eq:CR} in the form
$N_n a^{-1}N_n=L_\perp(n)-[G,N_n]$,
\begin{align*}
 (N_n a^{-1}+G)\bigl[a(1-G\chi_n)\bigr]&=-N_nG\chi_n+\partial_na-\partial_n(a\partial_n\chi_n),\\
 (N_n a^{-1}+G)\bigl[N_n\chi_n\bigr]&=L_\perp(n)\chi_n-GN_n\chi_n+N_nG\chi_n+GN_n\chi_n ,
\end{align*}
and adding,
\[
  (N_n a^{-1}+G)(A_{nn}\rho)=L_\perp(n)\chi_n+\partial_na-\partial_n(a\,\partial_n\chi_n)
  =-\nabla\!\cdot\bigl(a(\nabla\chi_n-n)\bigr)=0
\]
by the cell equation~\eqref{eq:cell} (inner product with $n$).  Since $N_n$ is
symmetric and $G$ antisymmetric, $N_n a^{-1}+G=K^{T}$, and for any $\ell$
on $\mathbb{T}^d$,
$\frac{d}{dt}\langle\ell,\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\rangle=-\langle K^{T}\ell,\Psi_f\rangle$
by~\eqref{eq:semigroup}; so $K^{T}\ell=0$ is exactly the statement that
$\langle\ell,\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\rangle$ is conserved.

(i) By~\eqref{eq:rho-cell}, \eqref{eq:Ann}, the symmetry of $N_n$ and
$N_n1=0$,
\[
  A_{nn}\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}\rho=\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}a(1-\partial_n\chi_n)+\langleN_n1,\chi_n\rangle=A_{nn},
\]
so $\int\rho=1$.  If $\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\to c$ as $t\to\infty$, the conserved
quantity of~(ii) gives
\[
  \langle\rho,f\rangle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle\rho,\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\rangle=c\int_{\mathbb{T}^d}\rho=c,
\]
which is~\eqref{eq:representer}.

(iii) By the maximum principle $c\ge0$ in~(i) whenever $f\ge0$; since now
every $f$ has a limit, $f\mapsto\int\rho f$ is a positive functional and
$\rho\ge0$.  Let $K^{T}\ell=0$, so that $\langle\ell,\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\rangle$ is
independent of $t$.  Letting $t\to\infty$ and using~(i),
\[
  \langle\ell,f\rangle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle\ell,\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\rangle
  =\langle\ell,1\rangle\,\langle\rho,f\rangle
  \qquad\text{for every }f,
\]
i.e.\ $\ell=\langle\ell,1\rangle\,\rho$: the conserved linear functionals
are exactly the multiples of the far-field value, and the normalization
$\int\rho=1$ selects $\rho$.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rem:rational}
The proofs of Theorems~\ref{thm:riccati} and~\ref{thm:density} use only the
structure of the lifted equation, and for rational $n$ they apply with the
strip problems of~\cite{MV96} in place of Theorem~\ref{thm:known}.  For
rational $n$, where the lifted problem is the collection of the strip problems
for all phases (Section~\ref{sec:lifted}), their content must be read with
the phase in mind.  On each line $\{\theta+zn^\perp\}$, \eqref{eq:rho-cell} is the strip
density~\eqref{eq:rho} for that phase.  In the lifted variables the solutions do not converge as $t\to\infty$: on
each line the strip solution tends to its tail $d(n;s)$, but the lines move
with depth (the cell variable of $\Psi$ is the point itself,
see~\eqref{eq:lift-def}), so $\Psi_f(t,\cdot)$ approaches a traveling
profile, constant along each line and translated by $tn$, built from the
neutral modes of Remark~\ref{rem:fd}.  The density~\eqref{eq:rho-cell} is
still conserved by Theorem~\ref{thm:density}(ii), and
$\langle\rho,\Psi_f(t,\cdot)\rangle$ is the average over the phase of the
strip tails, which for $f=\chi_n$ is $\bar d(n)$.  The uniqueness argument of
Theorem~\ref{thm:density}(iii) uses convergence to a constant and does not
apply; we do not address uniqueness for~\eqref{eq:kernel} at rational $n$.
The density is still non-negative, since the maximum principle argument
of~(iii) applies to each strip.  For an actual
problem at a fixed $\varepsilon$ the phase is fixed by $\varepsilon$, as
in~\eqref{eq:phase}, and the relevant density is the restriction
of~\eqref{eq:rho-cell} to the corresponding line.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:contrast}
Let $a=a_0+\delta b$ with $a_0>0$ constant and $b$ smooth and periodic, let
$n$ be irrational, and let $N_n$, $N_0$ and $\chi_n$ be the Fourier-truncated
operators and corrector of Section~\ref{sec:marching}.  Then
$\langle\chi_n,N_n\chi_n\rangle-\langle\chi_n,N_0\chi_n\rangle=O(\delta^3)$,
with a constant that may depend on $K$ and on $n$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Since $\chi_n=0$ for constant $a$, $\chi_n=O(\delta)$.  For $\delta=0$ both
$N_n$ and $N_0$ equal the DtN operator $N^{(0)}$ of the constant coefficient
$a_0$, because $G$ and $N^{(0)}$ are both Fourier multipliers and
$[G,N^{(0)}]=0$.  Both depend analytically on $\delta$: at $\delta=0$ the
decaying modes have decay rates $|q_k|>0$ ($q_k=2\pi k\cdot n^\perp\neq0$ for
$k\neq0$, since $n$ is irrational), so they stay separated from the imaginary
axis for small $\delta$ and $N_n$, the flux map on these modes and the
constant, is analytic in $\delta$; and $N_0$ is analytic because the kernel of
$L_\perp(n)$ remains the constants.  Hence $N_n-N_0=O(\delta)$, and the difference of
the two quadratic forms is $O(\delta^3)$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:glue}
In the truncated problem, $N_T=P_T-Q_TR_T^{-1}Q_T^{*}$, and the blocks for
depth $2T$ are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glue}
\begin{aligned}
  P_{2T}&=P_T-Q_T(P_T+R_T)^{-1}Q_T^{*},\qquad
  Q_{2T}=-Q_T(P_T+R_T)^{-1}Q_T,\\
  R_{2T}&=R_T-Q_T^{*}(P_T+R_T)^{-1}Q_T .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
$E_T(u,v)=0$ forces $\Psi'=iW\Psi$, so $\Psi(t)=e^{itW}u$ and
$v=e^{iTW}u$; hence $E_T(u,0)>0$ for $u\neq0$ and $E_T(0,v)>0$ for $v\neq0$,
and $P_T$, $R_T$ and $P_T+R_T$ are positive definite.  Since nothing is
prescribed at $t=T$ in~\eqref{eq:NT},
$u^{*}N_Tu=\min_vE_T(u,v)$, and minimizing the quadratic form over $v$ gives
the first formula.  Since $a$ does not depend on $t$, the problem on $(T,2T)$
is the problem on $(0,T)$ translated in depth, so
$E_{2T}(u,v)=\min_w\bigl[E_T(u,w)+E_T(w,v)\bigr]$, the minimum over the value
$w$ at $t=T$; it is attained at $w=-(P_T+R_T)^{-1}(Q_T^{*}u+Q_Tv)$, which
gives~\eqref{eq:glue}.

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
\label{rem:dt}
As $T\to\infty$ with $dt$ fixed, the doubling converges to the DtN matrix
$\Lambda_\infty$ of an infinite stack of slabs of thickness $dt$, and the
claim is that $\Lambda_\infty=N$ exactly, for every $dt$; the step size
affects only the finite-$T$ iterates.  This is a known property of the
doubling algorithm, and we give the short direct check.  Both $\Lambda_\infty$
and $N$ are determined by their action on the decaying modes, each sending
the boundary value of a mode to its flux, so it suffices to check that the
two problems have the same decaying modes and give them the same flux.  A
solution on the infinite stack is a sequence of face values $u_0,u_1,\dots$ with finite
total energy $\sum_j\bigl(u_j^{*}Pu_j+2\,\mathrm{Re}\,u_j^{*}Qu_{j+1}+u_{j+1}^{*}Ru_{j+1}\bigr)$,
where $(P,Q,R)=(P_{dt},Q_{dt},R_{dt})$ are the blocks~\eqref{eq:PQR}.  Setting
the derivative with respect to each interior face to zero gives the
equilibrium conditions
\[
  Q^{*}u_{j-1}+(P+R)u_j+Qu_{j+1}=0,\qquad j\ge1,
\]
and the flux at the top face is $\Lambda_\infty u_0=Pu_0+Qu_1$.  Now try a
geometric sequence $u_j=z^j\varphi$ with $|z|<1$.  Substituting the
blocks~\eqref{eq:PQR}, a short computation gives
\[
  Q^{*}+z(P+R)+z^2Q=-\tfrac{dt}{4}(1+z)^2\bigl[(\mu+iW)A(\mu+iW)-Q_\perp AQ_\perp\bigr],
  \qquad z=\frac{1-\mu\,dt/2}{1+\mu\,dt/2},
\]
so $z^j\varphi$ satisfies the equilibrium conditions exactly when
$(\mu+iW)A(\mu+iW)\varphi=Q_\perp AQ_\perp\varphi$, that is, when
$e^{-\mu t}\varphi$ is a decaying mode of the truncated lifted problem
(Section~\ref{sec:dcurve}); the map $\mu\mapsto z$ sends
$\mathrm{Re}\,\mu>0$ onto $|z|<1$.  For such a mode the same substitution
gives for the flux
\[
  (P+zQ)\varphi=A(\mu+iW)\varphi=N\varphi ,
\]
the continuous flux of that mode.  So the stack and the truncated lifted
problem have the same decaying modes with the same fluxes, and
$\Lambda_\infty=N$.  (The neutral modes of Remark~\ref{rem:fd} have $\mu$
imaginary, hence $|z|=1$, and carry zero flux in both problems.)  In the
Riccati literature this is the statement that the doubling algorithm is
applied to the continuous-time equation after a Cayley transform with a free
parameter, here $2/dt$, and that the transformed equation has the same
solution for every value of the parameter, which affects only the speed of
convergence~\cite{CFL05}.  A one-sided difference in place of the midpoint
rule~\eqref{eq:slab} would not have this property, and its limit would differ
from $N$ at first order in $dt$.
