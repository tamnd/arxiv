<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $K$ be a number field of degree $n$ and absolute discriminant $D_K$.
Then there exists a polynomial
\[
 f(x)=a_0x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n\in\mathbb Z[x]
\]
of degree $n$ with a root $\alpha$ such that $K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ and
$H(f):=\max \{|a_i|\} \ll_n\sqrt{D_K}$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{boundlemma}
For any fractional ideal $I$ of $\mathcal O_K$ and any two elements
$\alpha,\beta\in I$, write
\[
 F_{I;\alpha,\beta}(x,y)
 =\frac{\operatorname{N}(\alpha x+\beta y)}{\operatorname{N}(I)}
 =\sum_{i=0}^n c_i x^iy^{n-i}.
\]
Then $F_{I;\alpha,\beta}$ is integral, and
\[
 |c_i|\leq\binom ni\frac{\|\alpha\|^i\|\beta\|^{n-i}}{\operatorname{N}(I)}.
\]
Moreover, its coefficient height satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:binary-bound}
 H(F_{I;\alpha,\beta})
 \leq\frac{2^n}{\operatorname{N}(I)}
       \prod_v\max\{|\sigma_v(\alpha)|,|\sigma_v(\beta)|\}^{d_v}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Multiplication by $\alpha x+\beta y$ defines a map from
$\mathcal O_K[x,y]$ to $I[x,y]$. With respect to integral bases of $\mathcal O_K$
and $I$, its matrix has entries in $\mathbb Z[x,y]$, and its determinant
is $F_{I;\alpha,\beta}$ up to sign. This yields integrality.

The norm is the product of $\sigma(\alpha)x+\sigma(\beta)y$ over
all $n$ embeddings $\sigma:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb C$. The coefficient
of $x^iy^{n-i}$ is therefore a sum of $\binom ni$ products,
each with $i$ conjugates of $\alpha$ and $n-i$ conjugates of
$\beta$. Every conjugate is bounded in absolute value by the
Euclidean length of the corresponding element. This proves
the first bound. Bounding each factor instead by
$\max\{|\sigma(\alpha)|,|\sigma(\beta)|\}$ gives
\eqref{eq:binary-bound}.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:ratios}
Let $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k\in I$, with $\alpha_1\ne0$ and
$k\leq n$, have normalized lengths at most~$R$. Suppose that
$\mathbb Q(\alpha_2/\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k/\alpha_1)=K$.
Then there is an integer linear combination $\beta=\sum_i a_i\alpha_i$,
with $0\leq a_i\leq M$, such that $\beta/\alpha_1$ generates $K$.
In particular, there is a defining polynomial $f$ for $K$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratios}
 H(f)\leq(2nMR)^n\sqrt D.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For each pair of distinct embeddings $\sigma,\tau:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb C$,
the equation
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^k a_i
 \bigl(\sigma(\alpha_i/\alpha_1)-\tau(\alpha_i/\alpha_1)\bigr)=0
\]
is a nonzero linear equation in the $a_i$; otherwise $\sigma$
and $\tau$ would agree on generators of $K$.
Each such equation contains at most $(M+1)^{k-1}$ points of
$\{0,\ldots,M\}^k$: after fixing all but a coordinate with nonzero
coefficient, there is at most one choice for that coordinate.
There are $M$ pairs of embeddings, so their union does not exhaust
this set.

Choose $(a_1,\ldots,a_k)$ outside the union and put
$\beta=\sum_i a_i\alpha_i$. Then all conjugates of
$\beta/\alpha_1$ are distinct, so this ratio generates $K$.
The normalized length of $\beta$ is at most $nMR$.
Taking $f=\Phi_{I;\alpha_1,\beta}$ in \eqref{eq:norm} proves
\eqref{eq:ratios}.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{thminmostcases}
Theorem~$\ref{thm:main}$ holds for every number field $K$ with no
index-two subfield.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $I$ be a fractional ideal of $\mathcal O_K$, and put $k=\lceil n/2\rceil$.
Since $k\leq n+1-k$, transference gives
\[
 \lambda_k(L(I,1))\lambda_k(L(I,1)^*)\leq T_n.
\]
Thus one of these lattices contains $k$ independent vectors of
length at most $\sqrt{T_n}$. By \eqref{eq:duality}, they correspond
to elements $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k$ of either $I$ or
$I^\vee=\mathfrak d_K^{-1}I^{-1}$, with bounded normalized lengths.

The field
\[
 E=\mathbb Q(\alpha_2/\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k/\alpha_1)
\]
contains the $k$ independent ratios $\alpha_i/\alpha_1$.
Hence $[E:\mathbb Q]\geq k$. Since $K$ has no index-two subfield,
every proper subfield has degree at most $n/3<k$.
It follows that $E=K$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:ratios} completes the proof.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:reduction}
There exists a constant $C_0(n)$ with the following property.
If $K$ has no defining polynomial of height at most $C_0(n)\sqrt D$,
then $n=2m$ and $K$ is a CM field with maximal totally real subfield
$F=K^+$. Moreover, $\mathcal O_K^\times=\mathcal O_F^\times$.
For each nonzero fractional ideal $I$, there is a one-dimensional
$F$-subspace $W_I$ of $K$ such that, for every scaling $u$ as in
\eqref{eq:lattice}, the lattice $I\cap W_I$ contains $m$ independent
elements of normalized length~$<\sqrt{2m}$.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Choose
$R>\max\{1,\sqrt{T_n},T_n/\sqrt n\}$, put $\rho=T_n/R$, and set
$C_0(n)=(2nMR)^n$. Suppose now that $K$ has no defining polynomial of
height at most $C_0(n)\sqrt D$. We claim that
\[
 \lambda_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}(L(I,u))>R
 \qquad\text{for every }I\text{ and }u.
\]
Indeed, if there were $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ independent elements of
normalized length at most $R$, their ratios to the first would remain
independent over $\mathbb Q$. The field generated by these ratios would
therefore have degree greater than $n/2$. Since every proper subfield
of $K$ has degree at most $n/2$, these ratios would generate $K$,
contrary to Lemma~\ref{lem:ratios}.

If $n$ were odd, put $j=(n+1)/2$, so that $j=n+1-j$.
The claim applied to both $L(I,u)$ and its dual would give
$\lambda_j(L(I,u))\lambda_{n+1-j}(L(I,u)^*)>R^2>T_n$,
contrary to \eqref{eq:transference}.
We must therefore have $n=2m$. Applying the claim to the dual and
using \eqref{eq:transference} again yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
 \lambda_m(L(I,u))<\rho<R<\lambda_{m+1}(L(I,u)),
 \qquad \rho<\sqrt n.
\end{equation}

Fix $I$ and consider the rational space
\[
 W(I,u)=\operatorname{span}_\mathbb Q\{\alpha\in I:\|t_Iu\sigma(\alpha)\|<R\}.
\]
By \eqref{eq:gap}, this space has dimension exactly $m$ for every $u$.
A basis chosen from its short elements remains short when $u$ is
varied sufficiently little. The dimension remains $m$, so the space
itself is locally constant. The set of allowed $u$ is connected,
as is clear after taking logarithms. Hence $W(I,u)$ is independent
of $u$; write it as $W_I$.

Choose a short basis $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m$ of $W_I$, and let
$E=\mathbb Q(\alpha_2/\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m/\alpha_1)$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:ratios} implies that $E\ne K$. On the other hand,
the $m$ independent ratios imply $[E:\mathbb Q]\geq m$. It follows that
$[E:\mathbb Q]=m$ and $W_I=\alpha_1E$.

For a unit $\varepsilon\in\mathcal O_K^\times$, the tuple
$(u_v|\sigma_v\varepsilon|)_v$ is another allowed scaling.
Multiplication by $\varepsilon$ carries the short elements for this
new scaling bijectively to the short elements for $u$. Since $W_I$
does not depend on the scaling, we have $\varepsilon W_I=W_I$.
The equality $W_I=\alpha_1E$ now implies $\varepsilon\in E$.
Thus $\mathcal O_K^\times=\mathcal O_E^\times$, and Dirichlet's unit theorem gives
\[
 m\leq r_1(K)+r_2(K)
   =r_1(E)+r_2(E)\leq [E:\mathbb Q]=m.
\]
Equality throughout forces $K$ to be totally complex and $E$ to be
totally real.

Finally, in any fixed embedding $K\subset\mathbb C$, a real element of $K$
outside $E$ would generate the quadratic extension $K/E$. This would
make $K$ a subfield of $\mathbb R$, a contradiction. Hence
$E=K\cap\mathbb R=K^+$, independently of $I$. Writing this field as $F$,
we obtain the asserted $F$-line $W_I$ and the equality
$\mathcal O_K^\times=\mathcal O_F^\times$. The bound on the lengths of $m$ independent
elements of $I\cap W_I$ follows from \eqref{eq:gap}.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:small-base}
Under the failure hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction},
the maximal totally real subfield $F=K^+$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small-base}
 D_F\ll_{m,\epsilon}D^\epsilon
 \qquad\text{for every }\epsilon>0.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $\mathfrak D_{K/F}$ be the relative discriminant ideal, and write
$d=\operatorname{N}(\mathfrak D_{K/F})$, so that $D=D_F^2d$.
Fix a nonzero fractional ideal $I$ of $K$, and let $W_I$ be the line
provided by Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}.
Choose $0\ne \alpha\in I\cap W_I$, and put $J=(\alpha)I^{-1}$.
Since $\alpha\in I$, the ideal $J$ is integral.

We remove from $J$ the largest ideal extended from $F$ that divides it.
More precisely, define an integral ideal $I_0$ of $F$ by
\[
 v_P(I_0)=\min_{P_1\mid P}
       \left\lfloor\frac{v_{P_1}(J)}{e(P_1/P)}\right\rfloor
\]
for each prime ideal $P$ of $F$, where $e(P_1/P)$ is the ramification
index. We may then write $J=I_0\mathcal O_K J_0$ with $J_0$ integral.
The fractional ideal $I_1=\alpha^{-1}I\cap F$ equals $I_0^{-1}$.
Indeed, $\alpha^{-1}I=J^{-1}$, and taking valuations gives
\[
 v_P(I_1)=\max_{P_1\mid P}
       \left\lceil-\frac{v_{P_1}(J)}{e(P_1/P)}\right\rceil
       =-v_P(I_0).
\]
Since $W_I=\alpha F$, it follows that $I\cap W_I=\alpha I_1$.

We compute the covolume $V$ of the rank-$m$ lattice
$t_Iu\sigma(\alpha I_1)$ in its real span. At each complex place of $K$,
the real line arising from $\alpha F$ has its length multiplied by
$t_Iu_v|\sigma_v(\alpha)|$. Consequently,
\[
 V=t_I^m
       \left(\prod_{v=1}^m u_v|\sigma_v(\alpha)|\right)
       \sqrt{D_F}\,\operatorname{N}(I_1).
\]
We have $\prod_vu_v=1$ because $K$ is totally complex.
Using also $\operatorname{N}(J)=\operatorname{N}(I_0)^2\operatorname{N}(J_0)$,
$I_1=I_0^{-1}$, $t_I^{2m}=2^m/(\sqrt D\,\operatorname{N}(I))$, and
$\sqrt D=D_F\sqrt d$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:volume}
 V^2=2^m\frac{D_F}{\sqrt D}
         \frac{|\operatorname{N}(\alpha)|}{\operatorname{N}(I)}\operatorname{N}(I_1)^2
     =2^m\frac{\operatorname{N}(J_0)}{\sqrt d}.
\end{equation}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}, this lattice contains $m$
independent vectors of length less than $\sqrt{2m}$.
Their span over $\mathbb Z$ is a sublattice of finite index, so its covolume
is an integer multiple of $V$. Hadamard's inequality therefore gives
$V\leq(2m)^{m/2}$. It follows from \eqref{eq:volume} that
$\operatorname{N}(J_0)\leq m^m\sqrt d$.

Modulo classes extended from $F$, the equality
$J=(\alpha)I^{-1}=I_0\mathcal O_K J_0$ gives $[J_0]=[I]^{-1}$.
As $I$ varies, we therefore obtain an integral representative of
norm at most $m^m\sqrt d$ for every class in
$\operatorname{Cl}(K)/\operatorname{im}\operatorname{Cl}(F)$.
For any number field of degree $n$, the number of integral ideals
of norm at most $x\geq1$ is at most $x(1+\log x)^{n-1}$.
To see this, bound the number of ideals of each norm by the
$n$-fold divisor function and sum over products of $n$ positive
integers at most $x$.
Writing $h_E$ for the class number of a field $E$, we conclude that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:class-count}
 \frac{h_K}{h_F}
 \leq\bigl|\operatorname{Cl}(K)/\operatorname{im}\operatorname{Cl}(F)\bigr|
 \ll_m\sqrt d\,(\log(2d))^{2m-1}.
\end{equation}
The first inequality uses only that the image of $\operatorname{Cl}(F)$ has order
at most $h_F$; injectivity of the extension map is not needed.

We now compare this estimate with the class-number formula.
Let $\kappa_E=\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\zeta_E(s)$, and let $w_E$ and $R_E$ denote
the number of roots of unity and the regulator of $E$, respectively.
We use the regulator obtained by deleting one coordinate from the
unit logarithms $(d_v\log|\sigma_v\varepsilon|)_v$, with regulator one
when the unit rank is zero.
The equality $\mathcal O_K^\times=\mathcal O_F^\times$ implies
$w_K=w_F=2$ and $R_K=2^{m-1}R_F$.
If $\eta=\eta_{K/F}$ is the quadratic Hecke character associated with
$K/F$, then $\zeta_K(s)=\zeta_F(s)L_F(s,\eta)$.
The two class-number formulas consequently give
\[
 \frac{h_K}{h_F}
 =\frac{2}{(2\pi)^m}\sqrt{D_Fd}\,L_F(1,\eta),
 \qquad L_F(1,\eta)=\frac{\kappa_K}{\kappa_F}.
\]

We use the following Brauer--Siegel-type estimate:
for fields $E$ of degree at most $N$ and every $\epsilon>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residue}
 D_E^{-\epsilon}\ll_{N,\epsilon}\kappa_E
       \ll_{N,\epsilon}D_E^\epsilon.
\end{equation}
The lower bound does not require $E$ to be normal over $\mathbb Q$.
Indeed, Stark's residue bound \cite[Lemma~4, p.~140]{Stark} gives
$\kappa_E\gg_N\min\{1-\beta,(\log D_E)^{-1}\}$, where the first
term is omitted if there is no exceptional real zero $\beta$.
If $1-\beta\geq(4N!\log D_E)^{-1}$, this already suffices.
Otherwise Stark's Lemma~8 \cite[p.~145]{Stark}, which applies to non-normal fields as
well, places the same zero in a quadratic subfield $E_0\subset E$.
Siegel's theorem gives $1-\beta\gg_\epsilon D_{E_0}^{-\epsilon}$
after an arbitrarily small adjustment of $\epsilon$.
Since $D_{E_0}\leq D_E$, this proves the required lower bound.
The upper bound follows from the standard estimate
$\kappa_E\ll_N(\log D_E)^{N-1}$. The field $E=\mathbb Q$ may be handled
separately, since its residue is one.

Applying \eqref{eq:residue} to $K$ and $F$, with sufficiently small
exponents, gives
$L_F(1,\eta)\gg_{m,\epsilon}D^{-\epsilon/4}$.
Comparison with \eqref{eq:class-count} then yields
\[
 \sqrt{D_F}
 \ll_{m,\epsilon}D^{\epsilon/4}(\log(2D))^{2m-1}
 \ll_{m,\epsilon}D^{\epsilon/2}.
\]
Squaring proves \eqref{eq:small-base}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:quadratic-form}
Let $I$ be a nonzero fractional ideal of $K$, and let $\alpha,\beta\in I$ satisfy
$\alpha\ne0$ and $\mathbb Q(\beta/\alpha)=K$. If both elements have length at most $B$
in $L(I,u)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relative-form}
 Q(x,y)=\operatorname{N}_{K/F}(\alpha x-\beta y)
 =\alpha\bar \alpha x^2-(\alpha\bar \beta+\bar \alpha \beta)xy+\beta\bar \beta y^2
\end{equation}
belongs to $F[x,y]$ and is positive definite at every real place.
The polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{eq:form-polynomial}
 f(x)=\frac{\operatorname{N}_{F/\mathbb Q}(Q(x,1))}{\operatorname{N}(I)}
\end{equation}
is integral, has degree $n$ and root $\beta/\alpha$, and satisfies
$H(f)\leq(2B^2)^m\sqrt D$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
The coefficients of $Q$ are fixed by conjugation and hence lie in $F$.
For a real place $v$ of $F$, choose an extension $\sigma_v$ to $K$.
Then $v(Q)$ is
$|\sigma_v(\alpha)x-\sigma_v(\beta)y|^2$, which is positive definite
because $\beta/\alpha$ generates $K$ and hence has no real conjugate.
Transitivity of the norm identifies $f$ with
$\Phi_{I;\alpha,\beta}$ of Section~\ref{sec:polynomial}, proving integrality,
degree, and the assertion about its root.

Put $z_v=t_Iu_v\sigma_v(\alpha)$ and $w_v=t_Iu_v\sigma_v(\beta)$.
Both have modulus at most $B$. The sum of the absolute values of
the coefficients of $Q_v(x,y)=|z_vx-w_vy|^2$ is therefore at most
$4B^2$. Since $\prod_vu_v^2=1$, we have
$f(x)=2^{-m}\sqrt D\prod_v Q_v(x,1)$, and the height bound follows.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:epstein-short}
Put
\[
 r_m=\left(1+\frac{(m+4)e^\pi}{4}\right)^{-1/m}.
\]
If $L\subset\mathbb R^{2m}$ has covolume one and
$\lambda_1(L)\leq r_m$, then $Z_L(1/2)\geq1$, where the
central value is defined by meromorphic continuation.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The approximate functional equation of Terras \cite{Terras},
obtained by Poisson summation, gives
\begin{align}
 Z_L(s)={}&-\frac1{ms}-\frac1{m(1-s)}\notag\\
 &+\int_1^\infty
 \bigl((\theta_L(t)-1)t^{ms}
       +(\theta_{L^*}(t)-1)t^{m(1-s)}\bigr)\,\frac{dt}{t};
 \label{eq:epstein-continuation}
\end{align}
see \cite[Theorem~2.3]{Khayutin}, with $L=\mathbb Z^{2m}g$,
parameter $2ms$, and $E^*(g,2ms)=Z_L(s)/2$.
The integral is entire in $s$, since the theta series minus one
decay exponentially. The formula gives both
$Z_L(s)=Z_{L^*}(1-s)$ and the reality of $Z_L(1/2)$.
If $v\in L\setminus\{0\}$ has length at most $r<1$, the terms
$v,-v$ contribute at least $2e^{-\pi}$ to $\theta_L(t)-1$
for $1\leq t\leq r^{-2}$. All remaining terms in the integral
at $s=1/2$ are nonnegative, so
\[
 Z_L(1/2)\geq-\frac4m+
       2e^{-\pi}\int_1^{r^{-2}}t^{m/2}\,\frac{dt}{t}
 =-\frac4m+\frac{4e^{-\pi}}m(r^{-m}-1).
\]
For $r=r_m$, the last expression equals $1$.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:hecke-average}
For every totally complex field $K$ of degree $2m$, Hecke's
formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hecke-period}
 \mathcal A_K(s)=\frac{w_K}{m\,h_KR_K}\Lambda_K(s),\qquad
 \Lambda_K(s)=D^{s/2}\bigl((2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\bigr)^m\zeta_K(s)
\end{equation}
for $s\ne0,1$, with meromorphic continuation understood.
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hecke-center}
 \mathcal A_K(1/2)
 =\frac{2^{m/2}\pi^m}{m}\,
       D^{-1/4}\frac{\zeta_K(1/2)}{\kappa_K},
 \qquad \kappa_K=\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\zeta_K(s).
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We apply \cite[Theorem~3.4]{Khayutin}, a form of Hecke's
formula \cite{Hecke}. The following observations identify its
normalizations with ours.
\begin{itemize}
\item By Definitions~2.1--2.2 and Lemma~3.5 of \cite{Khayutin},
the Euclidean structure is the same as ours, and
$E^*(g,2ms)=Z_L(s)/2$ when $L=\mathbb Z^{2m}g$ has covolume one.
\item The compact group
$[H]=\mathcal O_K^\times\backslash((\mathbb C^\times)^m/\mathbb R^\times)$ in
Definitions~3.1--3.2 maps onto $T/U_K$ by
$h\mapsto(|h_v|/(\prod_w|h_w|)^{1/m})_v$.
Its kernel consists of rotations modulo units.
This map carries Haar probability measure to $du$, and the
corresponding normalized ideal lattices are isometric.
\item The completed partial zeta function in Definition~3.3 is
\[
 \zeta_I^*(s)=D^{s/2}
       \bigl((2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\bigr)^m
       \zeta_K(s,[I]^{-1}),
\]
where $\zeta_K(s,c)$ sums $\operatorname{N}(J)^{-s}$ over the integral ideals
$J$ in the class $c$. Indeed, $\alpha\mapsto(\alpha)I^{-1}$
identifies nonzero elements of $I$ modulo units with these
ideals.\footnote{The sum in \cite[Definition~3.3]{Khayutin}
is understood modulo units, as in the proof of \cite[Theorem~3.4]{Khayutin}.}
\item The regulator uses $2\log|\sigma_v(\varepsilon)|$ at
complex places, as in the proof of
\cite[Proposition~4.1]{Khayutin}, and thus agrees with $R_K$.
\end{itemize}
The cited theorem therefore gives
\[
 \int_{T/U_K}Z_{L(I,u)}(s)\,du
 =2\frac{w_K}{2mR_K}\zeta_I^*(s).
\]
Averaging over the ideal classes proves \eqref{eq:hecke-period},
including its continuation to $s\ne0,1$.
The class-number formula
$\kappa_K=(2\pi)^m h_KR_K/(w_K\sqrt D)$ and
$\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi$ give \eqref{eq:hecke-center}.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:uniform-subconvexity}
There are $a\geq1/2$ and $0<\delta<1/4$, depending only on $m$,
such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta-subconvexity}
 |\zeta_K(1/2)|\ll_m D_F^a d^{1/4-\delta}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Apply Michel--Venkatesh \cite[Theorem~1.2]{MV} to the
Eisenstein representations
$\pi_1=1\boxplus\eta$ and
$\pi_2=|\cdot|^{iv}\boxplus|\cdot|^{-iv}$, with $v\ne0$
tending to zero. Here $\boxplus$ denotes the representation
induced from the displayed pair of Hecke characters; its
$L$-function is the product of their $L$-functions.
Both representations have unitary central character.
Since $\eta\ne1$, $\pi_1$ is nonsingular, and
\[
 L(\pi_1\otimes\pi_2,1/2)
   =\zeta_K(1/2+iv)\zeta_K(1/2-iv).
\]
The finite conductor of this product has norm $d^2$;
the archimedean parameters are bounded for $|v|\leq1$.
The second representation is unramified at every finite place.
The polynomial dependence stated after (1.1) of \cite{MV}
therefore gives, uniformly for such $v$,
\[
 |\zeta_K(1/2+iv)\zeta_K(1/2-iv)|
       \ll_m D_F^b d^{1/2-2\delta}
\]
for some $b\geq0$.\footnote{Immediately after (1.1), they write:
\textquotedblleft more precisely, the constant implied depends polynomially on the
discriminant of $F$ (for $F$ varying over fields of given degree)
and on $C(\pi_2)$.\textquotedblright\ Their
\S\S2.1.1 and~4.1.5 explain the convention for polynomial
implicit constants. Any base-discriminant factor included in
the analytic conductor is absorbed into $D_F^b$.}
Letting $v\to0$ and taking a square root proves
\eqref{eq:zeta-subconvexity}; we may enlarge $a$ to ensure $a\geq1/2$.
<!-- end proof-9 -->
<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:small-average}
There are $A\geq0$ and $\delta>0$, depending only on $m$, such
that for every $\epsilon>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small-average}
 |\mathcal A_K(1/2)|
       \ll_{m,\epsilon}D_F^A D^{-\delta+\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
The implied constant may be ineffective.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Combine \eqref{eq:hecke-center}, \eqref{eq:zeta-subconvexity},
and the residue bound \eqref{eq:residue} to obtain
\[
 |\mathcal A_K(1/2)|
 \ll_{m,\epsilon} D^{-1/4+\epsilon}D_F^a d^{1/4-\delta}
 =D_F^{a-1/2+2\delta}D^{-\delta+\epsilon}.
\]
This gives the assertion with $A=a-1/2+2\delta\geq0$.
