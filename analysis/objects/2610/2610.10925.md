\label{nolawinBf}
    The group $B_f$ defined above do not satisfy a non-trivial law.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
By \cite[Lemma 1.2]{MR2705763},\cite[Lemma 4.2]{Schleimer2000} if $B_f$ satisfies a non-trivial law in $k$ variables, it must also satisfy a non-trivial law in two variables. Suppose, therefore, that $B_f$ satisfies a non-trivial law $w(x,y) = 1$ for some $w \in \mathbb{F}_2 = \langle x,y \rangle$. Since every group is a quotient of a free group, there exists a surjective homomorphism
$$\phi: \mathbb{F}_2 \twoheadrightarrow B_f.$$
Since $w(x,y) = 1$ in $B_f$, there exists a non-trivial word $w' \in \mathbb{F}_2$ such that $\phi(w') = w(x,y) = 1$.
On the other hand, by Theorem \ref{FreegroupresisuallyAn}, $\mathbb{F}_2$ is residually $\text{Alt}(2n+1)$. Consequently, for the word $w'$ defined above, there exists some $M > 0$ such that for all $n \geq M$, there exists a homomorphism $\psi: \mathbb{F}_2 \to \text{Alt}(n)$ such that $\psi(w') \neq 1$.
This contradicts the fact that for sufficiently large $k$, we have the following composition of maps:
$$\mathbb{F}_2 \xrightarrow{\phi} B_f \xrightarrow{\pi_k} \text{Alt}(d(k)),$$
where $\pi_k(\phi(w')) = \pi_k(w(x,y)) = 1$. Since the image of $w'$ under this composition is trivial, while it remains non-trivial in $\text{Alt}(n)$ for $n \geq M$, we reach a contradiction.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{nikolovSegal}
For every $n\in\mathbb N$, there exists $M_n$ such that, for every $m\ge M_n$, there are $A_m,B_m\in\operatorname{Alt}(m)$ satisfying
$$w(A_m,B_m)\neq1$$
for every nonidentity $w\in F(x,y)$ of length at most $n$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $\mathcal W_n$ be the finite set of non-identity elements of $F(x,y)$ having length at most $n$. Since free groups are residually finite, for any $w\in\mathcal W_n$ there are a finite group $Q_w$ and a homomorphism $\varphi_w:F(x,y)\to Q_w$ such that $\varphi_w(w)\neq1$. The diagonal homomorphism
$$\varphi:F(x,y)\longrightarrow\prod_{w\in\mathcal W_n}Q_w$$
therefore satisfies $\varphi(w)\neq1$ for every $w\in\mathcal W_n$. Let $Q=\varphi(F(x,y))$, $a=\varphi(x)$, and $b=\varphi(y)$. Then $Q$ is finite and $w(a,b)\neq1$ for every $w\in\mathcal W_n$.
Put $q=|Q|$. The diagonal of two copies of the left regular representation gives an embedding
$$\rho:Q\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Alt}(2q).$$
Indeed, every permutation occurs twice, so its resulting sign is $1$. Thus $A=\rho(a)$ and $B=\rho(b)$ satisfy $w(A,B)\neq1$ for every $w\in\mathcal W_n$. Finally, extending these permutations by the identity embeds $\operatorname{Alt}(2q)$ into $\operatorname{Alt}(m)$ for every $m\ge2q$. The conclusion follows with $M_n=2q$

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{Bfhasinfinitegirth}
    $\operatorname{girth}(B_f)=\infty$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
\textit{Step 1: The family $\{\operatorname{Alt}(n)\}_{n\in \mathbb{N}}$ does not satisfy a common non-trivial law.}
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists a non-trivial word $w(x, y) \in \mathbb{F}_2$ which is a law for $\operatorname{Alt}(n)$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Then, for any homomorphism $\phi \colon \mathbb{F}_2 \to \operatorname{Alt}(n)$, it must hold that $\phi(w(x,y)) = 1$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

For any fixed $M > 0$, let $B(M)$ denote the set of all non-trivial freely reduced words in $\mathbb{F}_2 = \langle x, y \rangle$ of length at most $M$. By Proposition \ref{FreegroupresisuallyAn}, for every $w' \in B(M)$, there exists an odd integer $k$ and a homomorphism $\phi_{w'} \colon \mathbb{F}_2 \to \operatorname{Alt}(k)$ such that $\phi_{w'}(w') \neq 1$. Since $B(M)$ is a finite set, we may define
\[ N_M = \max \{ k \mid \exists w' \in B(M) \text{ and } \phi_{w'} \colon \mathbb{F}_2 \to \operatorname{Alt}(k) \text{ such that } \phi_{w'}(w') \neq 1 \}. \]
Since $\operatorname{Alt}(n)$ embeds into $\operatorname{Alt}(n+1)$, it follows that for all $t \geq N_M$, every $w' \in B(M)$ is non-trivial under some homomorphism $\phi \colon \mathbb{F}_2 \to \operatorname{Alt}(t)$. This is consistent with the result of G. A. Jones \cite{Jones_1974}, which establishes that no infinite family of non-isomorphic finite simple groups satisfies a non-trivial group law. 

By choosing $M$ sufficiently large such that $w(x, y) \in B(M)$, we obtain a contradiction to the assumption that $w$ is a law for all $\operatorname{Alt}(n)$.

\textit{Step 2: Construction of generating sets for infinite girth.}
Fix a positive integer $M > 0$. We seek a finite generating set $S^{(M)}$ such that $\text{girth}(B_f, S^{(M)}) > M$. Since $B_f$ is two-generated, we may assume that $S^{(M)}$ consists of two elements. Let $F(M)$ be the finite set of non-trivial reduced words of length at most $M$ in the generators freely generated by variables $S^{(M)}\cup (S^{(M)})^{-1}$ of $B_f$.
For any $w \in F(M)$, we consider its image under the projection map $\pi_k: B_f \twoheadrightarrow \text{Alt}(d(k))$. By the result in Step 1, for sufficiently large $k$ (and thus sufficiently large $d(k)$), we have $\pi_k(w) \neq 1$ for all $w \in F(M)$. Specifically, by Lemma \ref{nikolovSegal}, for $d(k)$ large enough, there exist elements $A, B \in \text{Alt}(d(k))$ such that no non-trivial word of length at most $M$ in $A$ and $B$ equals the identity.
As $\text{Alt}(d(k))$ is a subgroup of $B_f$ generated by $\alpha_{d(k)}$ and $\beta$, we can find elements $p, q \in B_f$ such that:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\pi_k(p) = s_k^{-1}A$ and $\pi_k(q) = \beta^{-1}B$,
\item $\pi_i(p) = 1$ and $\pi_i(q) = 1$ for all $i \neq k$.
\end{itemize}
(Note: $s_k$ and $\beta$ are the projections of the original generators $s$ and $t$ to the $k$-th coordinate).
We define the generating set $S_M$ as follows:
$$S_M = \{s \cdot p, t \cdot q\}.$$
We now verify that this choice of generators satisfies the required girth condition. For any word $w \in F(M)$ of length at most $M$, we have:
$$\pi_k(w(s \cdot p, t \cdot q)) = w(\pi_k(s \cdot p), \pi_k(t \cdot q)) = w(\pi_k(s)\pi_k(p), \pi_k(t)\pi_k(q))$$
$$= w(s_k s_k^{-1} A, \beta \beta^{-1} B) = w(A, B).$$
By our choice of $A$ and $B$, $w(A, B) \neq 1$. Since $\pi_k(w) \neq 1$, it follows that $w \neq 1$ in $B_f$. Since such a generating set $S_M$ exists for all $M > 0$, we conclude:
$$\text{girth}(B_f, S_M) > M \implies \text{girth}(B_f) = \sup_{S} \text{girth}(B_f, S) = \infty.$$

<!-- question-2: body_md -->
\label{pyberresidualfiniteness1}
    If a residually finite group $\Gamma$ possesses finite quotients with arbitrarily large girth, does it follow that $\Gamma$ itself has infinite girth?
<!-- end question-2 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
A family of group homomorphisms $T_k:G\to H$ is a separating family if \[\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\ker(T_k)=e.\]
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{counterexample} 
We show that $\mathbb{Z}^2$ possesses a separating family of finite quotients $\{Q_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ such that $\lim_{n \to \infty} \operatorname{girth}(Q_n) = \infty$, although $\operatorname{girth}(\mathbb{Z}^2) < \infty$.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          
    
Let $\{p_k\}_{k \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of primes such that $p_k > k^2$ for all $k$. For each $k$, define the homomorphism $\phi_k \colon \mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{Z}/p_k\mathbb{Z}$ by
    \begin{equation}\label{equationdefinition}
        \phi_k(m, n) = (m + kn) \pmod{p_k}.
    \end{equation}
    Each $\phi_k$ is surjective, as $\phi_k(1, 0) = 1$. To show that the family $\{\mathbb{Z}/p_k\mathbb{Z}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ is separating, suppose $(m, n) \in \bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} \ker(\phi_k)$. Then $p_k$ must divide $m + kn$ for all $k \geq 1$. Since $p_k > k^2$, the asymptotic behavior of the linear function $g(k) = m + kn$ relative to the quadratic growth of $p_k$ implies that
    \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{|m + kn|}{p_k} = 0. \]
    Thus, there exists $K \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $k \geq K$, we have $|m + kn| < p_k$. Given that $p_k$ divides $m + kn$, it must be that $m + kn = 0$ for all $k \geq K$. This forces $m = n = 0$, proving that $\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} \ker(\phi_k) = \{(0,0)\}$.

    Regarding the girth, the quotients $Q_k = \mathbb{Z}/p_k\mathbb{Z}$ are cyclic groups of order $p_k$. Since they satisfy the law $x^{p_k} = 1$, they possess finite girth; specifically, $\operatorname{girth}(\mathbb{Z}/p_k\mathbb{Z}) = p_k$ (attained by the standard generating set). It follows that $\lim_{k \to \infty} \operatorname{girth}(Q_k) = \infty$.
    
    On the other hand, $\operatorname{girth}(\mathbb{Z}^2) = 4$. Since any generating set $S$ of $\mathbb{Z}^2$ consists of commuting elements, any two generators $s_1, s_2 \in S$ satisfy the relation $s_1 s_2 s_1^{-1} s_2^{-1} = 1$, which is a word of length 4. Thus, $\operatorname{girth}(\mathbb{Z}^2) \leq 4$, and equality is realized by the standard basis $S = \{(1,0), (0,1)\}$.

<!-- example-2: body_md -->
\label{nonabelianKleinbottle}
    In the non-abelian setting, the answer to Question \ref{pyberresidualfiniteness1} also remains negative. The fundamental group of the Klein bottle, 
    \[ \pi_1(\mathbb{K}) = \langle a, b \mid aba^{-1} = b^{-1} \rangle, \]
    provides a counterexample. $\pi_1(\mathbb{K})$ is torsion-free (as both $a$ and $b$ have infinite order) and virtually abelian, containing the subgroup $\langle a^2, b \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ of index $2$. Note that $\pi_1(\mathbb{K})$ can be realized as the Baumslag--Solitar group $BS(1, -1)$, which is known to be residually finite \cite[Theorem C]{MR285589}. Since $\pi_1(\mathbb{K})$ is virtually abelian, it follows that $\operatorname{girth}(\pi_1(\mathbb{K})) < \infty$ \cite[Lemma 3.1]{Schleimer2000}. 

    However, one can easily construct a family of finite quotients of $\pi_1(\mathbb{K})$ with arbitrarily large girth. For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, define the homomorphism
    \[ \phi_n : \pi_1(\mathbb{K}) \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \langle c \mid c^n = 1 \rangle \]
    by setting $\phi_n(a) = c$ and $\phi_n(b) = 1$. Since $\phi_n(aba^{-1}) = c \cdot 1 \cdot c^{-1} = 1 = \phi_n(b^{-1})$, the map $\phi_n$ is well-defined; surjectivity follows immediately. Moreover, $\operatorname{girth}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, \{c\}) = n$, which implies that $\operatorname{girth}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \to \infty$ as $n \to \infty$.

The failure of the implication in Example \ref{counterexample} and \ref{nonabelianKleinbottle} arises because a generating set $S_k$ realizing large girth in a quotient $Q_k$ does not necessarily lift to a generating set of $\Gamma$ that preserves this girth.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{nolawforfinitesimple}
    Let $\Gamma$ be a finitely generated group. If $\Gamma$ admits infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic, non-abelian finite simple quotients $\{Q_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, then $\Gamma$ satisfies no non-trivial group law.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Suppose, toward a contradiction, that $\Gamma$ satisfies a non-trivial group law. That is, there exists a non-trivial word $w(x_1, \dots, x_k) \in \mathbb{F}_k$ such that $w(g_1, \dots, g_k) = 1$ for all $g_1, \dots, g_k \in \Gamma$.
    
    Let $\pi_n \colon \Gamma \twoheadrightarrow Q_n$ be the surjective homomorphisms onto the finite simple quotients. Since $w$ is a law in $\Gamma$, it must necessarily descend to a law in each quotient $Q_n$; that is, $w(q_1, \dots, q_k) = 1$ for all $q_1, \dots, q_k \in Q_n$ and for all $n\geq 1$.
    
    However, a result of G. A. Jones \cite{Jones_1974} establishes that for any non-trivial word $w$, there exists a constant $N_w$ such that $w$ is not a law for any finite simple group $G$ with $|G| > N_w$. Since the $\{Q_n\}$ are pairwise non-isomorphic finite simple groups, their orders $|Q_n|$ must tend to infinity as $n \to \infty$. Consequently, the word $w$ can be a law for only finitely many of the quotients $Q_n$. This contradicts the assumption that $w$ is a law for all $Q_n$, completing the proof.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{nonvirtuallysolvableofGamme}
    Any group $\Gamma$ satisfying the hypotheses of Proposition \ref{nolawforfinitesimple} cannot be virtually solvable. Indeed, if $\Gamma$ contains a solvable subgroup $\Gamma'$ of finite index $d=[\Gamma:\Gamma']$, the law $w$ satisfied by $\Gamma'$ would induce a non-trivial law $w(x_1^{d!}, \dots, x_n^{d!}) = 1$ for the entire group $\Gamma$. This contradicts the fact that $\Gamma$ satisfies no non-trivial group law.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{quoitientfinitenessgirth}
    Let $\Gamma$ be a finitely generated group. Suppose $\Gamma$ satisfies the condition $(\ast)$, then $\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma) = \infty$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
To the contrary, suppose $\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma)=M<\infty$. Then by definition of girth, for all finite generating set $S$ of $\Gamma$, we have \[\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma,S)\leq M.\] Taking $N>M$, by the hypothesis, there exists finite generating set $S_N$ of $\Gamma$ and surjective $\pi_N:\Gamma\to Q_N$ such that \[\operatorname{girth}(Q_N,\pi_N(S_N))>N,\]
 we claim that $\operatorname{girth}(Q_N,\pi_N(S_N))\leq \operatorname{girth}(\Gamma,S_N)$.

 Indeed, every relations in $\Gamma$ (with respect to $S_N$) remains a relation under $\pi_N$ in $Q_N$. More precisely, consider a relation in $\Gamma$ \[w(s_1,s_2,\dots,s_k)=1,\] applying $\pi_N,$ we have \[1=\pi_N(1)=\pi_N(w(s_1,s_2,\dots,s_k))=w(\pi_N(s_1)\pi_N(s_2)\dots\pi_N(s_k)),\] which is a relation in $Q_N$ with respect to the generating set $\pi_N(S_N)$. However,  finite quotient $Q_N$ could possibly have shorter relations which are not in the relations coming from $\Gamma$, therefore we must have \[N< \operatorname{girth}(Q_N,\pi_N(S_N))\leq \operatorname{girth}(\Gamma,S_N)\leq M\] which is a contradiction to the fact that $N>M$.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
Let us note that the statement in Theorem \ref{quoitientfinitenessgirth} would be false if it were formulated as: “If, for every $N\in\mathbb{N}$, there exists a finite quotient $Q_N$ such that $\operatorname{girth}(Q_N)>N$, then $\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma)=\infty$.” Indeed, by the definition of girth, there exists a generating set $T_N$ of $Q_N$ with arbitrarily large girth, but there need not exist a generating set $S_N$ of $\Gamma$ such that $T_N=\pi_N(S_N)$. This is precisely what happens for $\mathbb{Z}^2$ in Theorem \ref{counterexample}.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
We also note that Theorem \ref{quoitientfinitenessgirth} does not contradict the proof of \cite[Proposition 1.1]{akhmedov2005girth}, where it is shown that if $\operatorname{girth}(G)=m$, then $\operatorname{girth}(G/N)=Lm$ for some constant $L$ depending on $m$, for a normal subgroup $N\leq G$.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{markedfinitequotient}(Marked finite quotient criterion)
    Let $\Gamma$ be a finitely generated residually finite group. The following are equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma)=\infty$.
        \item  $\Gamma$ satisfies the condition $(\ast)$.
    \end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
$(2) \implies (1)$ follows from Theorem \ref{quoitientfinitenessgirth}.

    We will prove $(1) \implies (2)$,  Suppose \[\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma)=\infty.\]
    Then, for any every $N\in \mathbb{N}$, there exists a finite generating set $S_N$ of $\Gamma$, we have \[\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma,S_N)>N.\] Let $\mathcal{B}_N$ be the collection of all nontrivial freely reduced words with respect to $\{S_N,S_N^{-1}\}$ of length at most $N$. Since \[\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma,S_N)>N,\] $w(S_N)\neq 1$ for every $w\in \mathcal{B}_N$. By residual finiteness of $\Gamma$, for each $w\in B_N$, there exists a quotient $\phi_w:\Gamma\to Q_w$ such that $\phi_w(w(S_N))\neq 1$. Now, consider the product map, \[\Phi:\Gamma\to \prod_{w\in B_N} Q_w.\] Then, no non-trivial freely reduced word in $w\in \mathcal{B}_N$ evaluates to identity on $\Phi(S_N)$ in the image subgroup $\Phi_N(S_N)$. Letting $Q_N=\prod_{w\in B_N}Q_w$ we get \[\operatorname{girth}(Q_N.\Phi(S_N))>N,\] implying (2).
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
Theorem \ref{markedfinitequotient} shows that for the class of residually finite groups, it does not just give a sufficient condition but it gives a exact marked finite quotient reformulation (the images of some specific generating sets are remembered) of infinite girth.

A group isomorphism sends generators to generators and preserves all relations with respect to corresponding marked generating set, hence, $\operatorname{girth}(\Lambda)=\infty$ implies $\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma)=\infty.$ by Akhmedov quotient principle \cite[Proposition 1.1]{akhmedov2005girth}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{A/mfield}
    Let $A$ be a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra which is an integral domain. Let $a_1,a_2,\dots, a_s\in A$ be non-zero elements. Then there exists a maximal ideal $m\subset A$ such that \[a_i\not\in m\]for every $i=1,2,\dots, s$. Moreover, $A/m$ is a finite field.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $a=a_1a_2\dots a_s.$ Since $A$ is an integral domain and $a_i\neq 0$, we must have $a\neq 0$. It suffice to find a maximal ideal $\mathcal{M}$ such that $a\not \in \mathcal{M}.$ Indeed, if $a_i\in \mathcal{M}$ for some $1\leq i\leq s$, then by definition of ideal $a\in \mathcal{M}$. It follows from \cite[Theorem 3, Section 3.4]{bourbaki1998commutative} $A$ is a Jacobson ring (as $\mathbb{Z}$ is Jacobson). Because $A$ is a Jacobson ring, it follows from \cite[Proposition 3, section 3.4]{bourbaki1998commutative} that for any ideal $\mathcal{B}\in A$, \[\bigcap_{m\in \mathcal{M}, m \supset \mathcal{B}} m=\bigcap_{p\in\mathcal{P}, p\supset \mathcal{B}} p=0\] The last equality follows from the fact that $A$ is an integral domain. Since, $a\neq 0$, $a$ cannot lie in all maximal ideals of $A$. Hence, there exists an maximal ideal $m_1\in \mathcal{M}$ such that \[a\not\in m_1.\] Therefore, $a_i\not \in m_1$ for all $1\leq i\leq s$. $A/m$ is a finite field follows again from \cite[Corollary 1, Section 3.4]{bourbaki1998commutative}.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
(Congruence realization and rigidity of marked girth)
    Let $\Gamma \leq \operatorname{GL}_d(K)$ be a finitely generated linear group over a field $K$ of characteristic zero. Suppose $\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma) = \infty$. Then,  $\Gamma$ satisfies the condition $(\ast)$. More precisely, for any $N \in \mathbb{N}$, there exists a finite generating set $S_N$ of $\Gamma$, a finitely generated integral domain $A \subset K$ such that $\Gamma \leq \operatorname{GL}_d(A)$, and a maximal ideal $m_N$ of $A$ such that the congruence quotient $\psi_{m_N}(\Gamma) \leq \operatorname{GL}_d(A/m_N)$ satisfies:
    \[ \operatorname{girth}(\psi_{m_N}(\Gamma), \psi_{m_N}(S_N)) > N. \]
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
\textit{Step 1: Fixing a finitely generated coefficient ring.}

As $\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma)=\infty$, for any $N>0$, one can choose a finite generating set $S_N=\{u_{N1},u_{N2},\dots, u_{Nk}\}$ of $\Gamma$ such that \[\operatorname{girth}(\Gamma,S_N)>N.\] Let \[T=\{u_{N1}, u_{N2}, \dots, u_{Nk}, u_{N1}^{-1}, u_{N2}^{-1}, \dots, u_{Nk}^{-1}\}.\]   For each $t\in T$, which is a $d\times d$ with entries in $K$. Write down all the matrix entries for each $t\in T$, that is, \[t_1,t_2,\dots, t_r\] where $r\leq 2k.d^2$. Define $A$ to be the subring of $K$ generated by $\mathbb{Z}$ and $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_r$ that is 
\begin{equation}
    A=\mathbb{Z}[t_{1}, t_2, \dots, t_{r}]=\{f(t_{1}, t_{2}\dots, t_{r}): f\in \mathbb{Z}[x_{1}, x_2, \dots, x_{r}]\}\subset K
\end{equation}
which can be seen as the image of the evaluation map \[e_{\{t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\}}:\mathbb{Z}[x_{1}\dots,x_{r}]\to K\] defined as $e_{\{t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\}}(f)=f(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})$. Clearly, $A$ is a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra. Hence, $A$ is the coeffient ring we will be working with.
\medskip

$A$ is an integral domain follows from a basic fact from the ring theory, "every unital subring of an integral domain is an integral domain". Hence $A$ is an integral domain. We may now assume that $\Gamma\leq GL_d(A)$, as every element of $\Gamma$ has entries in $A$, so $\Gamma\leq GL_d(A)$.
\medskip

\textit{Step 2: Choosing a maximal ideal to obtain the finite residue field.}

Let \[\mathcal{W}_N=\{1\neq w\in F_k:|w|\leq N, w \hspace{0.1cm}\text{is freely reduced}\}.\]

As there are finitely many non-trivial freely reduced words $w$ of length at most $N$, the set $\mathcal{W}_N$ is finite. For $w\in \mathcal{W}_N$, we have \[w(S_N)\neq I_d.\]  Now, $w(S_N)-I_d$ has at least one non-zero entry. Choose one such entry and denote it by \[a_w\in A.\] Thus, $a_w\neq 0$. Now consider the product, \[a_N=\prod_{w\in \mathcal{W}_N}a_w\] As $A$ is an integral domain, $a_w\neq 0$ for all $w\in \mathcal{W}_N$, which implies $a_N\neq 0$. By Lemma \ref{A/mfield}, we have there exists a maximal ideal $m_N \subset A$ such that $a_N\not\in m_N$, implying each $a_w\not\in m_N$, for all $w\in \mathcal{W}_N$ (indeed, if $a_w\in m$ for some $w$, then $a_N\in m_N$, which is impossible).
\medskip

Again, by Lemma \ref{A/mfield}, $A/m_N$ is a finite field. So we have the following reduction mod $m_N$ homomorphism as $\Gamma\leq GL_d(A)$, \[\psi_{m_N}:\Gamma\to GL_d(A/m_N)\] Set $Q_N:=\psi_{m_N}(\Gamma)$ is a finite subgroup of $GL_d(A/m_N)$. Moreover $S_N'=\psi_{m_N}(S_N)=\{\psi(u_{N1}), \psi(u_{N2}),\dots, \psi(u_{Nk})\}$ is a finite generating set of $Q_N$ (because $S_N$ generates $\Gamma$). For $w\in \mathcal{W}_N$, suppose \[\psi_{m_N}(w(S_N))=I_d \implies \psi_{m_N}(w(S_N)-I_d)=0.\] This means any chosen entry $a_w$ of $w(S_N)$ would be $0$ modulo $m_N$, which further means $a_w\in m_N$, which is impossible by the choice of $m_N$. Hence, for any non-trivial $w\in \mathcal{W}_N$, we have \[w(S_N')=w(\psi_{m_N}(S_N))=\psi_{m_N}(w(S_N))\neq 1\] in the finite quotient $Q_N$, therefore \[\operatorname{girth}(Q_N,S_N')=\operatorname{girth}(\psi_{m_N}(\Gamma), \psi_{m_N}(S_N))>N.\]

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{Altfinite}
Let $X$ be a set, let $U\subseteq X$ with $|U|\ge 3$, and let $T\subseteq X$ be a three-element subset such that $U\cap T\neq\varnothing$. Let $\gamma_T\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ be a $3$-cycle with  $\operatorname{supp}(\gamma_T)=T$ (fixing $X\setminus T$ pointwise). Consider $\operatorname{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}(U)$ as a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ by extending every permutation of $U$ to fix $X\setminus U$ pointwise. Then
$$\left\langle\operatorname{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}(U),\gamma_T\right\rangle
=\operatorname{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}(U\cup \operatorname{supp}(\gamma_T))
=\operatorname{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}(U\cup T).$$
Here, if $T=\{a,b,c\}$, then $\gamma_T$ is either $(a\,b\,c)$ or its inverse $(a\,c\,b)$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
By hypothesis, $|U\cap T|=1,2$ or $3$. We will use the lemma in case-by-case basis.

 Case 1: $|U\cap T|=3$: As $|T|=3$, each element of $T$ intersects with $U$ meaning $T\subseteq U$. This immediately proves the assertion as $U\cup T=U$.
\medskip

 Case 2: $|U\cap T|=2$: 
 \begin{itemize}
     \item $|U|\geq 4$
     
     \noindent Write \[T=\{u_1,u_2,t\}, \qquad u_1,u_2\in U, \qquad t\in T\backslash U.\] We can assume $\gamma_T=(u_1 u_2 t)$ (replacing $\gamma_T$ by it's inverse if necessary). Clearly, $\operatorname{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}(U)$ is 2-transitive on the set $U$ when $|U|\geq 4$. Therefore, there exists $\sigma\in \operatorname{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}(U)$ such that \[\sigma(\{u_1,u_2\})=\{r,s\}, \qquad \text{for any distinct}\hspace{0.1cm}\{r,s\}\in U\backslash T.\] Assume $\sigma=(u_1 r)(u_2 s)$ . As $\sigma$ fixes $t$, \[\sigma^{-1}\gamma_T \sigma=\sigma^{-1}(u_1 u_2 t)\sigma=(rst)\] Thus, $\langle \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U), \gamma_T\rangle$ contains all 3-cycles $(rst)$ where $r\neq s$ and $r,s\in U\backslash T$ and $t\in T$. It already contains 3-cycles supported in $U$. Hence, \[\langle \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U), \gamma_T\rangle=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U\cup \{t\})= \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U\cup T) \]

     \item $|U|=3$ Write $T=\{u_1,u_2,t\}$ and $U=\{u_1,u_2,u_3\}$ Take $\gamma_T=(u_1u_2t)$ and $\sigma=(u_1u_2u_3)$. Then, \[\sigma^{-1}\gamma_T\sigma=(u_1 tu_3)\] and the argument follow as in the previous subcase $|U|\geq 4$.

     \noindent 
 \end{itemize}
\medskip
Case 3: $|U\cap T|=1$: Write \[T=\{u_1,s,t\}\qquad u_1\in U, \qquad s,t\in T\backslash U\] Like Case 2, assume $\gamma_T=(u_1st)$ (replacing $\gamma_T$ by $\gamma_T^{-1}$ if necessary). $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U)$ acts transitively on $U$. Indeed, for any $u,v\in U$, choose $\sigma_v=(uvw)$ for any $w\in U\backslash \{u_1,u_2\}$, hence $\sigma_v(u)=v$. Clearly, $\sigma_v\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U)$ being a 3-cycle and the choose of $w$ is guaranteed by $|U|\geq 3. $ Therefore, by transitivity of $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U)$, there exists $\sigma_v\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U)$ such that $\sigma_v(u_1)=v$, for $u_1$ as defined in $\gamma_T$ and any $v\in U$. Moreover, $\sigma_v$ fixes $s,t$ \[\sigma_v \gamma_T \sigma_v^{-1}=(stv)\implies \sigma_v(u_1st)\sigma_v^{-1}=(vst).\] Hence, all the 3-cycles of the form $(vst)\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U)$ for all $v\in U$. Moreover, for distinct $x,v\in U$, \[(vst)(xst)^{-1}=(xvs)\] Hence, $(xvs)\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U)$ for all $x\neq v$ and $x,v\in U$. Together with $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U)$, it generates 
\[\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U\cup \{s\}).\] Let's come back to the original cycle $\gamma_T=(u_1st)$. As $u_1,s\in U\cup \{s\}$, apply Case 2 replacing $U$ by $U\cup \{s\}$ \[\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U\cup \{s\}, \gamma_T)=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}{(U\cup \{s,t\})}=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U\cup T).\] This completes the proof of the lemma.

\label{altfin(X)asproductof3cycles}
    Every permutation $\sigma\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(X)$ is a product of 3-cycles.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{G=AltX}
    Let $X$ be a set, and let $\mathcal{C}$ be a collection of 3-cycles in $\operatorname{Sym}_\mathrm{fin}(X)$. If the subgroup $G=\langle \mathcal{C}\rangle$ acts transitively on $X$, then \[G=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(X).\]
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Clearly, as every element of $\mathcal{C}$ is an even permutation (every 3-cycle is a product of transpositions), it follows 
    \begin{equation}\label{inclusion}
        G\leq \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(X).
    \end{equation} We first show that, for every $G$-orbit $Y\subseteq X$ containing at least three elements, the group induced by $G$ on $Y$ is \[G|_Y=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(Y).\] Suppose $Y$ be a $G$-orbit containing at least three elements. We show that $G$ contains every 3-cycle supported in $Y$. By Lemma \ref{altfin(X)asproductof3cycles}, it follows that \[\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(Y)\leq G|_Y.\] To prove the other direction, let $\gamma_0\in \mathcal{C}$ be an element supported in $Y$, say \[Y_0=\operatorname{supp}(\gamma_0).\]Then if $|Y_0|=3$,
    \[\langle\gamma_0\rangle=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(Y_0)\leq G.\] Now, we prove the following elementary enlargement observation. If $U\subseteq Y, |U|\geq 3,$ and $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U)\leq G$, and suppose there exists $\gamma\in \mathcal{C}$ satisfying $\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)\cap U\neq \emptyset$, then \[\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U \cup \operatorname{supp}(\gamma))\leq G.\] Indeed, by Lemma \ref{Altfinite}, \begin{equation}\label{equationenlargement}
        \langle\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U),\gamma\rangle=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(U\cup \operatorname{supp}(\gamma)).
    \end{equation}For any finite $F\subseteq Y$, there exist finitely many $\gamma_1.\gamma_2,\dots, \gamma_m\in \mathcal{C}$ such that \[F\subseteq (Y_0 \cup \bigcup_{i=1}^m \operatorname{supp}(\gamma_i))\] reordering if necessary, for all $1\leq j\leq m$, \[\operatorname{supp}(\gamma_j)\cap (Y_o \cup\bigcup_{i\neq j} \operatorname{supp}(\gamma_i))\neq \emptyset \quad 1\leq i\neq j\leq m. \] Applying equation \ref{equationenlargement} repeatedly starting with $Y_0$, we get for \[\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(F)\leq G|_Y.\] As $F$ was arbitrary, $G|_Y$ contains every 3-cycles on $Y$. Hence, \[\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(Y)\leq G|_Y.\] The reverse inclusion follows from Equation \ref{inclusion}. Therefore, \[G|_Y=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(Y).\] Now, as $G$ acts transitively on $X$, there is just one $G$-orbit of $X$, hence \[G=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(X).\]

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{Altfinsatisfiesnolaw}
    The group $\operatorname{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbb Z)$ satisfies no non-trivial law. Consequently, the group $\langle c,t\rangle=\operatorname{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbb Z)\rtimes \langle t \rangle$ does not satisfy any non-trivial law, where $c=(0\;1\;2)$.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Let $w(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)$ be an arbitrary non-trivial reduced word in the free group $\mathbb{F}_n$. We will find elements $g_1,g_2\dots, g_n\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})$ such that \[w(g_1,g_2,\dots,g_n)\neq 1.\] Because the free group is residually finite, there exists a finite group $\mathcal{F}_w$ and a group homomorphism $\phi:\mathbb{F}_n\to \mathcal{F}_w$ such that \[\phi(w)\neq 1.\] Let $k_j=\phi(x_j)\in \mathcal{F}_w$. Then, \[w(k_1,k_2,\dots,k_n)=\phi(w(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n))\neq 1.\] Then we embed $\mathcal{F}_w$ into a finite alternating group. Let $\mathcal{F}_w$ act on $\mathcal{F}_w\times \{0,1\}$ by left multiplication\[f.(h,\epsilon)=(fh,\epsilon).\] This gives a faithful representation \[\Phi:\mathcal{F}_w\hookrightarrow \operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{F}_w\times \{0,1\})\] Since, every permutation $\Phi(f)$ consists of two identical copies of $\mathcal{F}_w$ that is \[\operatorname{sgn}(\Phi(f))=\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda_f.\lambda_f)=\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda_f)^2=1.\] Hence, $\Phi(\mathcal{F}_w)\leq \operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{F}_w\times \{0,1\})\cong\operatorname{Alt}_{2|\mathcal{F}_w|}$. Since, $\Phi$ is injective, we have,\begin{equation}\label{gi's}
\Phi(w(k_1,k_2,\dots,k_n))=w(\Phi(k_1),\Phi(k_2),\dots,\Phi(k_n))\neq 1.
    \end{equation} Substituting the values of $k_i$, implies \[\Phi(w(\phi(x_1),\phi(x_2),\dots,\phi(x_n))=\Phi(\phi(w))\neq 1\] Choose a subset $E\subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ with $|E|=2|\mathcal{F}_w|$, identify $\mathcal{F}_w\times \{0,1\}$ with $E$ and extend every permutation of $E$ to fix $\mathbb{Z}\backslash E$. This identifies $\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{F}_w\times\{0,1\})$ with a subgroup of $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})$. By equation (\ref{gi's}), there exists $g_1,g_2,\dots, g_n\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})$ such that \[w(g_1,g_2,\dots,g_n)\neq 1.\] Because, $w(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)$ was arbitrary, it shows that $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})$ contains a subgroup which do not satisfy a law. Applying, Lemma \ref{G=AltX} to the set $\mathcal{C}=\{t^{n}ct^{-n}=(n\; n+1\; n+2), n\in \mathbb{Z}\}$, we get $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})$ does not satisfies a law. Finally, if the semidirect product $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\leq \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\rtimes \langle t \rangle$, satisfies a law $w=1$ (say) then it must also be satisfied in $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})$, which is impossible. Hence, $\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\leq \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\rtimes \langle t \rangle$ does not satisfy a non-trivial law.

\label{Gamma0surjectsontoH}
Consider the sets as defined above
$$\Omega=\bigsqcup_{i\geq 1}\Omega_i,\qquad |\Omega_i|=n_i,$$

<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
Let $\mathbb{F}=\langle x,y \rangle$ be the free group of rank 2. Define \[\phi: \mathbb{F}\to \Gamma_0, \quad x\mapsto \pi, y\mapsto \tau\] 
    \[\psi: \mathbb{F}\to H, \quad x\mapsto c, y\mapsto t,\]
where $c=(0\; 1\; 2)$. 
\medskip

Claim 1: We want to prove that $\ker \phi \subseteq \ker \psi$.
\medskip
    
      Let $W(x,y)\in \ker \phi$. Then, \[\phi(W(x,y))=1\implies W(\phi(x),\phi(y))=W(\pi,\tau)=1\] Because the products defining $\tau$ and $\pi$ act coordinately on the block $\Omega_i$, we have,\begin{equation}\label{wordinpitau}
          W(\pi_i,\tau_i)=1\quad \text{for all}\; i.
      \end{equation} Suppose $R=|W(x,y)|$.
    \medskip
For $p\in \mathbb{Z}$ \[c_p=t^pct^{-p}=(p\; p+1\; p+2).\] Moving all powers of $t$ to the right, we can write 
\begin{equation}\label{movingtoright}
    W(c,t)=Pt^q, \quad \text{where}\; P=c_{p_1}^{\epsilon_1}c_{p_2}^{\epsilon_2}\dots c_{p_s}^{\epsilon_s},\; \quad \epsilon_j=\{\pm 1\}, |p_j|\leq R, |q|\leq R. 
\end{equation}
for all $1\leq j\leq s$. Moreover, it follows that \begin{equation}\label{supportP}
    \operatorname{supp}(P)\subseteq \bigcup_{j=1}^s \operatorname{supp}(c_{p_j}^{\epsilon_j})\subseteq \bigcup_{j=1}^s\{p_j, p_j+1, p_{j}+2\}\subseteq [-R,R+2]
\end{equation}
More precisely, $\operatorname{supp}(P)\subseteq I_R:=\{-R, -R+1, \dots -R+1,-R+2\}$.

Since $(n_i)$ is unbounded (by definition), we can choose $i$ such that 
\begin{equation}\label{inequality}
    n_i>2R+3.
\end{equation}Also, identify each block $\Omega_i \cong\mathbb{Z}/n_i\mathbb{Z}$ by sending $\omega_i^{r+1}$ to $r \mod n_i$. Under this identification, \[\pi_i=(0\; 1\; 2),\quad \tau_i(r)=r+1 \mod n_i.\]
Then, equation \ref{movingtoright} gives \[W=P_i\tau_i^q\] where $P_i=\prod_{j=1}^s \tau_i^{p_j} \pi_i^{\epsilon_j} \tau_i^{-p_j}$ and the factor $\tau_i^{p_j} \pi_i^{\epsilon_j} \tau_i^{-p_j}$ is the reduction modulo $n_i$ of $c_{p_j}^{\epsilon_j}$. Since, $n_i> 2R +3,$ the reduction modulo $n_i$ is injective on $I_R$. Consequently, $P_i$ is precisely the reduction of $P$ modulo $n_i$, with no identification between distinct points in the support of $P$.

By equation \ref{wordinpitau}, we have $P_i\tau_i^q=1$ which further implies, \begin{equation}\label{equalityofPandtaupowerq}
    P_i=\tau_i^{-q}.
\end{equation}

We will now prove that $q=0$. Suppose $q\neq 0$ then by \ref{movingtoright} and \ref{inequality} we have\[0<|q|<R<2R+3<n_i.\] Therefore, $\tau_i^q$ acts non-trivially with $|q|<n_i$ as translation \[r\mapsto r+q \mod{n_i}.\] Therefore, $\tau_i^q$ do not fix anything on $\Omega_i$. On the other hand, the left hand side of equation \ref{equalityofPandtaupowerq}, $P_i$ is supported on $I_R$ by equation \ref{supportP}. Since $|I_R|=2R+3<n_i$, there is a point outside this image and $P_i$ fixes that point, which contradicts equation \ref{equalityofPandtaupowerq}. Hence, $q=0$. So, equation \ref{equalityofPandtaupowerq} gives \[P_i=1.\] Since, each $P_i$ is supported on $I_R$ and the reduction modulo $n_i$ is injective on $I_R$, we have \[P=1\quad \text{in}\;\operatorname{Sym}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\] Combining equations \ref{wordinpitau}, \ref{equalityofPandtaupowerq}, we get \[W(c,t)=Pt^q=1\implies W\in \ker \psi.\] This completes the prove of claim 1. Therefore, $\psi$ induces a homomorphism \[\Phi: \mathbb{F}/\ker \phi\to H \] Therefore, by the first isomorphism theorem we get a surjection \[\Phi:\Gamma_0 \twoheadrightarrow H\]

<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{AltZsemiZ}
    Let \[H=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\rtimes \langle t \rangle,\quad t(k)=k+1.\]Then $\operatorname{girth}(H)=\infty$. More precisely, for every $N\geq 1$, there exists an odd cycle \[\sigma_N\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\] such that $H=\langle t,\sigma_N\rangle$ and no nonempty freely reduced word of length at most $N$ in $\{t^{\pm}, \sigma_N^{\pm}\}$ represents identity.
<!-- end theorem-8 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
Fix $N\geq 1$, and let \[\mathbb{F}_2=\langle x,y \rangle\]be the free group on two generator. Consider the ball of radius $N$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$ \[B_N=\{g\in \mathbb{F}_2:|g|_{\{x,y\}}\leq N\}.\]We will construct a finite cycle $\sigma_N$ such that the action of $t$ and $\sigma_N$ on a finite subset of $\mathbb{Z}$ generates $H$ with the desired result as in the statement of the theorem.
\medskip

    \noindent \textit{Step 1: Acting $x$ as a shift on $x$-cosets in $\mathbb{F}_2$}.
Consider the action of the cyclic group $\langle x\rangle$ on the set $B_N$ by left multiplication. This action partitions the $B_N$ into a finite collection of disjoint orbits. Since $B_N$ is finite, each orbit $\mathcal{O}$ is a finite subset of a right coset $\langle x\rangle g$. More precisely, for any $g\in B_N$, the orbit containing $g$ is the set \[\mathcal{O}_g=\{x^kg\in\mathbb{F}_2: x^kg\in B_N\}.\] Because, $\mathbb{F}_2$ is a free group (which has no relations), there exist unique integers $a_g\leq 0$ and $b_g\geq 0$ such that \[\mathcal{O}_g=\{x^kg:a_g\leq k\leq b_g\}.\] In some sense, each orbit represents an "interval of integers" of the form $x^{a_{g}}g,x^{a_{g}+1}g,\dots,$ $x^{b_g}g$.

Now, let $\{\mathcal{O}_1,\mathcal{O}_2,\dots, \mathcal{O}_m\}$ represents distinct orbits of $B_N$. Define $\beta: B_N\to \mathbb{Z}$ as follows \[\beta(x^kg_i)=S_i+k,\quad a_{g_i}\leq k\leq b_{g_i}\] where the offsets $S_i$ are chosen to ensure that the intervals of integers $[S_i+a_{g_i},S_i+b_{g_i}]$ are pairwise disjoint. By construction, $\beta$ is injective and satisfies the property:
\begin{equation}\label{translationofbeta}
    \beta(xg)=\beta(g)+1
\end{equation}whenever both $g$ and $xg$ belongs to $B_N$. Moreover, we can choose the offsets $S_i$ to be sufficiently large, we may ensure that the image $\beta(B_N)\subseteq \{2,3,\dots,M\}$ for some $M\in \mathbb{N}$.
\medskip

\noindent \textit{Step 2: Encoding the $y$-action as a partial permutation.} We now define a partial map $p:\beta(B_N)\to \beta(B_N)$ that mimics the action of the generator $y$. For any $\beta(g)\in \beta(B_N),$ define: 
\begin{equation}\label{definitionofp}
    p(\beta(g))=\beta(yg)
\end{equation} provided that $yg\in B_N$. This map $p$ is well-defined injective partial permutation of $\mathbb{Z}$ because $\beta$ is injective. To ensure that $p$ can be extended to a single finite cycle, we must verify that it possesses no periodic points. Suppose \[p^r(\beta(g))=\beta(g)\] for some $r\geq 1$, with all the intermediate iterates defined, then \[\beta(y^rg)=\beta(g)\] injectivity of $\beta$ gives $y^rg=g$, and $y^r=1$, which is impossible in $\mathbb{F}_2$. Hence the map $p$ has no non-trivial periodic points. 

Choose an integer $D>M$ sufficiently large that $[0,D]$ contains as many unused points as will be needed. Extend $p$ by prescribing \begin{equation}
    D\mapsto 0\mapsto 1.
\end{equation} These points are disjoint from $\beta(B_M)\subseteq \{2,3,\dots, M\}$. The resulting partial permutation still has no non-trivial periodic points.

By Lemma \ref{singleextension}, $p$ extends to a single cycle $\sigma_N$, whose support is contained in $[0,D]$. Inserting a few points if necessary, we may assume that $\sigma_N$ is odd and $>N$. Thus, \[\sigma_N\in \operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z}).\]Hence, by construction (equation \ref{definitionofp}), \begin{equation}\label{sigmaN}
\sigma_N(D)=0,\quad \sigma_N(0)=1, \quad \text{and}\quad\sigma_N(\beta(g))=\beta(yg),\end{equation} whenever $g,yg\in B_N$.
\medskip

\noindent\textit{Step 3: Absence of short relations.} 
We claim that \[w(t,\sigma_N)\neq 1\]for every non-trivial freely reduced word $w=w(x,y)$ of length atmost $N$. We use the usual convention that the products of the permutations are composed from right to left. Write \[w=s_1s_2\dots s_l,\quad s_i\in \{x^{\pm 1}, y^{\pm 1}\}, \quad 1\leq i\leq l,1\leq l\leq N.\]Each suffix $s_js_{j+1}\dots s_l$ is a reduced word of length atmost $N$, and therefore belongs to $B_N$. Equation \ref{translationofbeta} and \ref{sigmaN}, together with their inverse versions ($t(\beta(x^{-1}g))=\beta(xx^{-1}g)=\beta(g)\implies t^{-1}(\beta(g))=\beta(x^{-1}g)$ and similarly for $\sigma^{-1}$), give \begin{equation}
    t(\beta(g))=\beta(xg),\quad t^{-1}(\beta(g))=\beta(x^{-1}g)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\sigma_N(\beta(g))=\beta(yg),\quad \sigma_N^{-1}(\beta(g))=\beta(y^{-1}g)
\end{equation}
whenever the elements $g,x^{\pm 1}g, y^{\pm 1}g\in B_N$. Thus, \[w(t,\sigma_N)(\beta(1))=\beta(w).\]
Since $w\neq 1$ and $\beta$ is injective, \[\beta(w)\neq \beta(1).\]Therefore, $w(t,\sigma_N)\neq 1.$ Hence, this proves that for every non-trival reduced words $w$ of length atmost $N$,  $w(t,\sigma_N)\neq 1.$
\medskip

\noindent \textit{Step 4: $t^D\sigma_Nt^{-D}\cap\sigma_N=\{D\}.$}
Suppose $A=\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_N)$. As $A\subseteq [0,D]$, we have $A+D\subseteq [D,2D]$. Therefore, \[A\cap (A+D)=\{D\}.\] Because \[\operatorname{supp}(t^D\sigma_N t^{-D})=t^D(\operatorname{supp} (\sigma_N))\subseteq[D,2D].\] Hence, we proved that $t^D\sigma_Nt^{-D}\cap \sigma_N=\{D\}.$
\medskip

\noindent \textit{Step 5: $[\sigma_N,t^D\sigma_Nt^{-D}]=(D,\;0,\;D+1)$.} Suppose $a$ and $b$ are two cycles whose support intersects exactly at $q$. Then \[[a,b]=aba^{-1}b^{-1}=(q,\;a(q),\;b(q)).\]  This follow from the fact that two cycles sharing exactly one point has a commutator that is 3-cycle. In our case, take \[a=\sigma_N,\quad b=t^D\sigma_N t^{-D}, q=D\] This prescribed values gives us, \[a(D)=\sigma_N(D)=0, \quad b(D)=t^D\sigma_N t^{-D}(0)=D+\sigma_N(0)=D+1.\] Hence, \begin{equation}\label{commutator}
    [\sigma_N,t^D\sigma_N t^{-D}]=(D\; 0\; D+1)
\end{equation}
\medskip

\noindent \textit{Step 6: $\{\sigma_N, t\}$ generates $H$ and  $\operatorname{girth}(H)=\infty$.} Conjugating equation \ref{commutator} by $t^n$ gives 
\begin{equation}\label{gammaDn}
    \gamma_{D,n}:=t^n[\sigma_N,t^D\sigma_N t^{-D}]t^{-n}=(D+n,\; n,\; D+n+1)\in \langle \sigma_N, t\rangle. 
\end{equation} Plugging in $D=1$ gives the 3-cycles set \[\mathcal{C}=\{\gamma_{1,n}=(n+1,\; n,\; n+2): n\in \mathbb{Z}\}.\] $\langle\mathcal{C}\rangle$ acts transitively on $\mathbb{Z}$, this is because the $\operatorname{supp}(\gamma_{1,n})=\{n, n+1, n+2\}$ connect every integer to every other integer in a finitely many steps. Hence, Lemma \ref{G=AltX} gives 
\begin{equation}\label{gammaonen}
    \langle \gamma_{1,n}:n\in \mathbb{Z} \rangle=\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z}).
\end{equation} Equations \ref{gammaonen} and \ref{gammaDn} gives \[\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\leq \langle t, \sigma_N\rangle\] Since, $t\in \langle t,\sigma_N \rangle$, we obtain \[\operatorname{Alt}_\mathrm{fin}(\mathbb{Z})\rtimes \langle t\rangle=\langle t,\alpha_N\rangle=H.\] Thus, $\{t,\sigma_N\}$ generates $H$ and no reduced word of length at most $N$ represents identity, which implies \[\operatorname{girth}(H,\{t,\sigma_N\})>N\]As our choose of $N$ is arbitrary, this implies $\operatorname{girth}(H)=\infty$.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:laws-profinite}
Let $G$ and $K$ be residually finite groups. If their profinite completions are isomorphic, $\widehat G \cong \widehat K$, then $G$ and $K$ satisfy exactly the same group laws.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
Suppose that $w(x_1,\ldots,x_r)$ is a group law in $G$. By definition, this means that for every $r$-tuple of elements $(g_1, \ldots, g_r) \in G^r$, the word evaluates to the identity: $w(g_1, \ldots, g_r) = 1_G$.

We consider the word map $w \colon \widehat{G}^r \to \widehat{G}$, defined by extending the group operations to the profinite completion. Since the group operations in $\widehat{G}$ are continuous by construction, the map $w$ is a continuous function from the product space $\widehat{G}^r$ to $\widehat{G}$. 

Because $G$ is residually finite, it embeds densely into its profinite completion $\widehat{G}$. Consequently, the set $G^r$ is dense in $\widehat{G}^r$. Since $w$ is trivial on the dense subset $G^r$ (as $w$ is a law in $G$), and since the identity $\{1_{\widehat{G}}\}$ is a closed set in the profinite topology, the continuity of $w$ implies that $w$ must be trivial on the entire domain $\widehat{G}^r$. Thus, $w$ is a group law for $\widehat{G}$.

Now, let $\Phi \colon \widehat{G} \to \widehat{K}$ be the given isomorphism of profinite completions. Since $\Phi$ preserves the group structure, it maps the identity of $\widehat{G}$ to the identity of $\widehat{K}$. Therefore, the word $w$ is also a law in $\widehat{K}$. Restricting this result to the dense subgroup $K \leq \widehat{K}$, we conclude that $w(k_1, \ldots, k_r) = 1_K$ for all $k_i \in K$. Hence, $w$ is a law in $K$.

The reverse implication follows by symmetry, interchanging the roles of $G$ and $K$.

A $k$-generated group $G$ is said to satisfy an almost identity if for all $k\in \mathbb{N}$, there exists a non-trivial freely reduced word $w(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_k)\in \mathbb{F}_k$ such that if \[w(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_k)=1, \quad g_1,g_2,\dots,g_k\in G,\] then $\{g_1,g_2,\dots, g_k\}$ generates the group $G$.

While the first Grigorchuk group $\mathfrak{G}$ provides a positive example (by \cite[Corollary 6.13] {bartholdi2015ordering}) to Question \ref{intermediategrouphasinfinitegirth}, it remains an open problem whether intermediate growth generally obstructs the existence of almost-identities, \cite[Question 8.6]{bartholdi2015ordering}.
