<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:three}
For every $\varepsilon>0$,
\[
 D_3(X)=\kappa_3\sqrt X+O_\varepsilon\bigl(X^{2/5+\varepsilon}\bigr).
\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
There is a constant $c_*>0$ such that, for $k\in\{5,6\}$ and all sufficiently large $X$, at least $c_*X$ squarefree integers $n\in[X/2,X]$ satisfy $F(n)=k$. For $k=5$ this holds separately for even and for odd $n$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:growth}
As $X\to\infty$,
\[
\begin{gathered}
 D_2(X)=\left\lfloor \sqrt X\right\rfloor-1,\qquad D_3(X)\sim\kappa_3\sqrt X,\\
 D_4(X)\asymp D_5(X)\asymp D_6(X)\asymp X.
\end{gathered}
\]
For $k\ge7$, we have $D_k(X)=0$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The statements for $k=2$ and $k\ge7$ were recalled above; see also Section~\ref{subsec:main-term}. If $n=v^2u$ with $v\ge2$ and $u\ge2$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonsquarefree}
 n!\,(n-1)!\,u!\,(u-1)!=\bigl(vu\,(n-1)!\,(u-1)!\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
with four distinct arguments, so $F(n)\le4$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:three}, all but $O(\sqrt X)$ nonsquarefree $n\le X$ therefore lie in $\mathcal D_4$, which gives $D_4(X)\ge(1-6/\pi^2+o(1))X$. The remaining assertions are Theorems~\ref{thm:three} and~\ref{thm:main}.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:pell}
Fix $K>0$. For positive integers $e_1,e_2\le X^K$ and a nonzero integer $\Delta$ with $|\Delta|\le X^K$, the equation
\[
 e_1 u^2-e_2 v^2=\Delta
\]
has $X^{o(1)}$ solutions in positive integers $u\le X$ and $v$, uniformly in $e_1,e_2,\Delta$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:larger}
Let $A\subseteq[1,X]\cap\mathbb Z$ with $X>1$. Let $\mathcal P$ be a finite set of primes, and suppose that $A$ lies in at most $\rho(p)$ residue classes modulo $p$ for each $p\in\mathcal P$, where $1\le\rho(p)\le p$. If
\[
 \Delta=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\frac{\log p}{\rho(p)}-\log X>0,
\]
then
\[
 |A|\le\frac1\Delta\Bigl(\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\log p-\log X\Bigr).
\]
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We may assume $N=|A|\ge1$. For $p\in\mathcal P$ let $N_{p,r}$ be the number of elements of $A$ in the class $r$ modulo $p$. Since $\sum_rN_{p,r}=N$ and at most $\rho(p)$ of these counts are nonzero, Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\sum_rN_{p,r}^2\ge N^2/\rho(p)$. The quantity $\sum_rN_{p,r}^2-N$ counts ordered pairs of distinct elements of $A$ whose difference is divisible by $p$. For each such pair the primes $p\in\mathcal P$ dividing the difference have $\sum\log p\le\log X$. Hence
\[
 \sum_{p\in\mathcal P}\log p\Bigl(\frac{N^2}{\rho(p)}-N\Bigr)\le N(N-1)\log X,
\]
and dividing by $N$ gives the lemma.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:nonconsecutive}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ we have $E(X)\ll_\varepsilon X^{2/5+\varepsilon}$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Fix $0<\eta<1/100$ and let $X$ be large, so that $\ell,c\le X^\eta$ in every representation under consideration.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Case 1: $s(c!)\le X^{\ell/5}$.}\quad
Write $n-i=d_iu_i^2$ with $d_i=s(n-i)$ for $0\le i<\ell$. A prime $p>\ell$ divides at most one term of the block, so by~\eqref{eq:three-block} it divides one of the $d_i$ if and only if it divides $s(c!)$. A prime $p\le\ell$ divides at most $\ell/p+1$ terms. Hence
\[
 \log\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}d_i\le\log s(c!)+\sum_{p\le\ell}\Bigl(\frac\ell p+1\Bigr)\log p=\log s(c!)+\ell\log\ell+O(\ell).
\]
Choose $i<j$ so that $d_i,d_j$ are the two smallest squarefree parts. Then
\[
 d_id_j\le\Bigl(\prod_{k=0}^{\ell-1}d_k\Bigr)^{2/\ell}\ll\ell^2s(c!)^{2/\ell}\ll X^{2/5+2\eta}.
\]
Subtracting the corresponding terms gives
\[
 d_iu_i^2-d_ju_j^2=j-i\ne0.
\]
We count these Pell equations without fixing $c$ or $\ell$. There are $X^{2/5+2\eta+o(1)}$ pairs of positive integers $(d_i,d_j)$ with product $O(X^{2/5+2\eta})$, and at most $X^\eta$ values of $j-i$. For each choice, Lemma~\ref{lem:pell} gives $X^{o(1)}$ solutions $(u_i,u_j)$, and $n=d_iu_i^2+i$ is then determined up to at most $X^\eta$ choices of $i$. Case~1 therefore contributes at most $X^{2/5+4\eta+o(1)}$ values of $n$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Case 2: $s(c!)>X^{\ell/5}$.}\quad
Fix $c$ and $\ell$, and let $A_{c,\ell}$ be the set of possible integers $n\le X$ for this pair. We apply Lemma~\ref{lem:larger} with two sets of primes.

First let $\mathcal P_0$ be the set of primes $p>\ell$ dividing $s(c!)$. Such a prime occurs to an odd power in $c!$, so it must divide a term of the block; since $p>\ell$, this confines $n$ to the $\ell$ classes $0,1,\dots,\ell-1$ modulo $p$. These primes contribute
\[
 \sum_{p\in\mathcal P_0}\frac{\log p}{\ell}\ge\frac{\log s(c!)-\vartheta(\ell)}{\ell}\ge\frac{\log X}5-O(1).
\]

Next let $\mathcal P_1$ be the set of primes $q$ with $Y<q\le Q$, where
\[
 Y=X^{3\eta},\qquad Q=X^{2/5+4\eta}.
\]
These primes exceed $c$ and $\ell$; in particular $\mathcal P_1$ is disjoint from $\mathcal P_0\subseteq[1,c]$. For $q\in\mathcal P_1$, every $n\in A_{c,\ell}$ makes $f(n)$ a square modulo $q$, where $f(z)=c!\,(z)_\ell$ has the $\ell$ distinct roots $0,1,\dots,\ell-1$ and a nonzero leading coefficient modulo $q$. The number of residues $z$ for which $f(z)$ is a square or zero is
\[
 \rho(q)=\frac12\Bigl(q+\ell+\sum_{z\in\mathbb F_q}\chi_q(f(z))\Bigr)\le\frac q2+\frac{\ell+(\ell-1)\sqrt q}2
\]
by~\eqref{eq:weil}. As $\ell/\sqrt q\le X^{-\eta/2}$, this gives $1/\rho(q)\ge(2/q)(1-O(X^{-\eta/2}))$, and so
\[
 \sum_{q\in\mathcal P_1}\frac{\log q}{\rho(q)}\ge(2-o(1))\log\frac QY+O(1)=\Bigl(\frac45+2\eta-o(1)\Bigr)\log X.
\]

Adding the two contributions and subtracting $\log X$, the denominator $\Delta$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:larger} is at least $\eta\log X$ for large $X$. The numerator is at most $\vartheta(c)+\vartheta(Q)\ll Q$. Hence
\[
 |A_{c,\ell}|\ll_\eta\frac{X^{2/5+4\eta}}{\log X}.
\]
Summing over the at most $X^{2\eta}$ pairs $(c,\ell)$, Case~2 contributes $O_\eta(X^{2/5+6\eta})$ values of $n$.

Combining the two cases and taking $\eta$ small in terms of $\varepsilon$ proves the theorem.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The exponent $2/5$ comes from balancing the two cases. If the cases are separated at $s(c!)=X^{\sigma\ell}$, the first contributes $X^{2\sigma+o(1)}$, while in the second the primes dividing $s(c!)$ supply $\sigma\log X$ of the sieve denominator and the quadratic residue conditions must supply the rest, which leads to the bound $X^{(1-\sigma)/2+o(1)}$. The two exponents agree when $\sigma=1/5$. Without the first set of primes the larger sieve alone would give only $X^{1/2+o(1)}$.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For $a\in\mathcal S$ let $c(a)$ be the least $c\ge1$ with $s(c!)=a$. A consecutive representation $n!(n-1)!c!=\square$ exists if and only if $n=au^2$ for some $a\in\mathcal S$ with $c(a)<n-1$. The families $\{au^2\}$ are disjoint, since $a=s(n)$.

Every prime in $(c/2,c]$ divides $s(c!)$, so $s(c!)\ge e^{\delta c}$ for some $\delta>0$ and all large $c$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S-count}
 \#\{a\in\mathcal S:a\le Z\}\ll\log(2Z),\qquad\sum_{a\in\mathcal S}a^{-1/2}<\infty.
\end{equation}
The same estimate gives $c(a)\ll\log(2a)$, so $c(a)<a-1$ for all but finitely many $a$, and the condition $c(a)<n-1$ excludes only $O(1)$ integers $n$. The number of $n\le X$ of this form is therefore
\[
 \sum_{\substack{a\in\mathcal S\\ a\le X}}\left\lfloor \sqrt{X/a}\right\rfloor+O(1)=(\kappa_3+1)\sqrt X+O(\log X),
\]
since the rounding errors are $O(\log X)$ by~\eqref{eq:S-count}, and so is the tail:
\[
 \sqrt X\sum_{\substack{a\in\mathcal S\\ a>X}}a^{-1/2}\ll\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j/2}\log(2^{j+1}X)\ll\log X.
\]
Every other $n$ admitting a three-factor representation is counted by $E(X)$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:nonconsecutive}, the number of $n\le X$ admitting a three-factor representation is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:all-three}
 (\kappa_3+1)\sqrt X+O_\varepsilon(X^{2/5+\varepsilon}).
\end{equation}

It remains to remove the $n$ with $F(n)=2$. By the theorem of Erd\H{o}s and Selfridge~\cite[Theorem~1]{ES}, a product of two or more consecutive positive integers is never a perfect power. Hence $n!r!=\square$ with $r<n$ forces $r=n-1$ and $n=\square$; that is, $\mathcal D_2$ consists of the squares $n\ge4$. These squares were included in~\eqref{eq:all-three} (take $c=1$), and there are $\sqrt X+O(1)$ of them up to $X$. Subtracting them proves Theorem~\ref{thm:three}.

Every prime in $(c/2,c]$ occurs exactly once in $c!$ and therefore divides $(n)_{\ell_1}(m)_{\ell_2}$. Hence $\vartheta(c)-\vartheta(c/2)\le(\ell_1+\ell_2)\log n$, and the claim follows from the prime number theorem.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:coarse}
Every solution of~\eqref{eq:two-block} satisfies
\[
 \ell_1\ll n^{3/5},\qquad\ell_2\ll\ell_1\log n+n^{3/5}.
\]
In particular $L=\max(\ell_1,\ell_2)\le n^{2/3}$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
If $\ell_1\ge n/2$, a prime in $(n/2,n]$ (which exists by Bertrand's postulate) divides exactly one term of $B_1$ and nothing else in~\eqref{eq:two-block}, which is impossible. Hence $\ell_1<n/2$, and the same argument shows that $(n-\ell_1,n]$ contains no prime. By~\eqref{eq:huxley}, $\ell_1\ll n^{3/5}$.

For the lower block we may assume $\ell_2\ge m^{3/5}$ and $m$ large. Put $y=\min(\ell_2,m/2)$, so that $m^{3/5}\le y\le m/2$ and $\ell_2\le2y$. Each prime in $(m-y,m]$ divides exactly one term of $B_2$, does not divide $c!$, and therefore divides $(n)_{\ell_1}$. By~\eqref{eq:huxley},
\[
 \ell_2\le2y\ll\vartheta(m)-\vartheta(m-y)\le\log((n)_{\ell_1})\le\ell_1\log n.\qedhere
\]

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:transfer}
Let $n=ap$ with $p$ prime, $p>\sqrt n$ and $p>\max(\ell_1,\ell_2,c)$, and suppose that~\eqref{eq:two-block} holds with $\ell_1\ge2$. Then
\[
 P\bigl(s(n-1)\bigr)\le\max(\ell_1,c,a\ell_2).
\]
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The prime $p$ divides $n$ exactly once, divides no other term of $B_1$, and does not divide $c!$. It must therefore divide a term of $B_2$. Since $p>\ell_2$, that term is unique:
\[
 m-j=tp\qquad\text{for some }0\le j<\ell_2\text{ and }1\le t<a,
\]
where $t<a$ because $m<n$.

Now let $q>\max(\ell_1,c,a\ell_2)$ be a prime dividing $n-1$ to an odd power. It divides no other term of $B_1$ and does not divide $c!$. If it divided a term $m-i$ of $B_2$, it would divide
\[
 a(m-i)-t(n-1)=a(j-i)+t.
\]
This integer is nonzero since $0<t<a$, and has absolute value less than $a\ell_2<q$, which is a contradiction. So $q$ occurs to an odd power in~\eqref{eq:two-block}, which is impossible.

\label{lem:short}
Let $0<\alpha<\varepsilon\le10^{-3}$. If $n$ is sufficiently large, $P(n)>n^{1-\alpha}$, and~\eqref{eq:two-block} holds, then $\max(\ell_1,\ell_2)<n^\varepsilon$.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:prime-comparison}
Fix $\varepsilon>0$. Let $n$ be large, $n^\varepsilon\le L\le n^{2/3}$, $Q=L(\log n)^2$, $0<\theta<1$ and $M\in\{n,\theta n\}$. Let $W:\mathbb R^3\to[0,1]$ be smooth and $\mathbb Z^3$-periodic, with $\|\partial^\beta W\|_\infty\ll_\beta(\log n)^{C_W|\beta|}$ for some fixed $C_W>0$ and every multi-index $\beta$. Then, for every fixed $\Lambda>0$,
\[
 \sum_{Q<q\le2Q}W\Bigl(\frac nq,\frac{\theta n}q,\frac M{q^2}\Bigr)
 =\int_Q^{2Q}W\Bigl(\frac nv,\frac{\theta n}v,\frac M{v^2}\Bigr)\frac{dv}{\log v}
 +O\Bigl(\frac Q{(\log n)^\Lambda}\Bigr),
\]
where the implied constant depends on $\varepsilon$, $\Lambda$, $C_W$ and the implied constants in the derivative bounds.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Expand $W$ in a Fourier series. The derivative bounds allow us to truncate at frequencies bounded by a power of $\log n$, with uniform error $O((\log n)^{-B})$ for any prescribed $B>0$. The resulting error in either the prime sum or the integral is $O(Q(\log n)^{-B})$.

Each remaining Fourier mode contributes the phase
\[
 \frac{(k_1+k_2\theta)n}{q}+\frac{k_3M}{q^2}
\]
with integers $k_1,k_2,k_3$ of polylogarithmic size. Proposition~1.12(i) of~\cite{MRST}, with exponent $j=2$, compares the prime sum for each such mode with its integral, with error $O_B(Q(\log Q)^{-B})$ for every fixed $B>0$, provided both real phase coefficients are $O(\exp((\log Q)^{5/4}))$. Our coefficients are $O(n(\log n)^{O(1)})$, and $\log n=o((\log Q)^{5/4})$ since $Q\ge n^\varepsilon$. As there are only $(\log n)^{O(1)}$ modes, with total coefficient size $(\log n)^{O(1)}$, the lemma follows.
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Suppose that $L=\max(\ell_1,\ell_2)\ge n^\varepsilon$, and write $n=ap$ with $p=P(n)$, so that $a<n^\alpha$. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:smallest} and~\ref{lem:coarse}, $p>\max(\ell_1,\ell_2,c,\sqrt n)$ for large $n$. As in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer}, $p$ divides exactly one term $m-j$ of $B_2$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rational}
 m-j=\theta n,\qquad\theta=\frac ta\in(0,1),\qquad 1\le t<a.
\end{equation}
Thus $B_2$ consists of the integers $\theta n+e$ with $j-\ell_2<e\le j$, and in particular $|e|<L$. Write $\theta=A_0/A$ in lowest terms, so $2\le A\le a<n^\alpha$. Put
\[
 Q=L(\log n)^2,\qquad\delta=\frac LQ=(\log n)^{-2},\qquad J=\Bigl[\frac n{2Q},\frac nQ\Bigr].
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:smallest}, $c\ll L\log n$, so $Q>\max(\ell_1,\ell_2,c)$ for large $n$. Since $A<n^\alpha$, $n^\varepsilon\le L\le n^{2/3}$ and $\alpha<\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scales}
 \frac AQ=o(1),\qquad\frac{AQ}n\ll n^{-1/3+\alpha}(\log n)^2=o(1).
\end{equation}
The second estimate ensures that $J$ contains many periods of length $A$. The first will let us exclude square divisibility while retaining whole periods.

Every prime $q\in(Q,2Q]$ divides at most one term of each block and does not divide $c!$. We first construct a weight detecting primes that divide one block and avoid the other. We then modify it to exclude divisibility by $q^2$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Separating the blocks.}\quad
We seek a smooth $\mathbb Z^2$-periodic function $H:\mathbb R^2\to[0,1]$ such that $H(n/q,\theta n/q)>0$ implies that $q$ divides a term of exactly one block, and such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:period-mass}
 \int_I H(u,\theta u)\,du\gg A\delta
\end{equation}
for every interval $I=[u_0,u_0+A]$ with $u_0\in\mathbb Z$. We also choose $M\in\{n,\theta n\}$ so that every term of the selected block lies within $L$ of $M$.

For smooth $1$-periodic functions $f,g$, we have the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:period-average}
 \int_I f(u)g(\theta u)\,du
 =\int_0^1f(x)\sum_{k=0}^{A-1}g\Bigl(\frac kA+\theta x\Bigr)dx.
\end{equation}
Indeed, split $I$ into its $A$ unit intervals. On the interval beginning at $u_0+r$, substitute $u=u_0+r+x$; the residues $\theta(u_0+r)$ run through $k/A$ modulo $1$, since $A_0$ and $A$ are coprime. We will also use $\int_I f(\theta u)\,du=A\int_0^1f$, which follows directly from $\theta A=A_0\in\mathbb Z$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Case 1: $\ell_1\ge\ell_2/100$.}
Then $\ell_1\ge L/100$. We select the upper block and take $M=n$. Choose smooth $1$-periodic functions $f,g:\mathbb R\to[0,1]$ such that $f$ is supported in $(\ell_1/(16Q),3\ell_1/(8Q))$ modulo $1$ and equals $1$ on $[\ell_1/(8Q),\ell_1/(4Q)]$, and $g$ is supported in $(\delta,1-\delta)$ modulo $1$ and equals $1$ on $[2\delta,1-2\delta]$. Set $H(x,y)=f(x)g(y)$.

If $H(n/q,\theta n/q)>0$, then $0<q\{n/q\}<3\ell_1/4$. Since $q\{n/q\}$ is an integer, $q$ divides a term of $B_1$. Also $\{\theta n/q\}\in(\delta,1-\delta)$, so $\theta n$ is at distance more than $\delta q>L$ from every multiple of $q$. Thus $q$ divides no term of $B_2$.

At least $\left\lfloor A/2\right\rfloor\ge A/3$ of the points $k/A$, $0\le k<A$, lie in $[1/4,3/4]$. For each of them and every $x$ in the support of $f$ in $[0,1)$, we have $0\le\theta x\le3\delta/8$, hence $g(k/A+\theta x)=1$. By~\eqref{eq:period-average},
\[
 \int_I H(u,\theta u)\,du
 \ge\frac A3\int_0^1f\ge\frac{A\ell_1}{24Q}\ge\frac{A\delta}{2400},
\]
which proves~\eqref{eq:period-mass} in this case.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Case 2: $\ell_2>100\ell_1$.}
Now $L=\ell_2$. We select the lower block and take $M=\theta n$. Let $f$ be a smooth $1$-periodic function with values in $[0,1]$, supported strictly inside
\[
 \Bigl(-\frac j{2Q},\frac{\ell_2-j}{2Q}\Bigr)\pmod1
\]
and equal to $1$ on its middle subinterval of length $\delta/8$. The whole interval has length $\delta/2$, and $\int_0^1f\ge\delta/8$. Let $g$ be a smooth $1$-periodic function with values in $[0,1]$ which vanishes on $[0,\ell_1/Q]$ modulo $1$ and equals $1$ outside
\[
 E=\Bigl[0,\frac{\ell_1}Q+\frac\delta{1000}\Bigr]\cup\Bigl[1-\frac\delta{1000},1\Bigr],
\]
so that $|E|<0.012\,\delta$. Set $H(x,y)=g(x)f(y)$.

If $H(n/q,\theta n/q)>0$, let $\xi$ be the representative of $\theta n/q$ modulo $1$ in the support of $f$. Since $\theta n=tp$ is an integer, so is $q\xi$, and $-j<q\xi<\ell_2-j$ because $q\le2Q$. Hence $\theta n-q\xi$ is a term of $B_2$ divisible by $q$. Since $g(n/q)\ne0$, we have $\{n/q\}>\ell_1/Q>\ell_1/q$, so $q$ divides no term of $B_1$.

On every interval $I$ of length $A$ with integer endpoints,
\[
 \int_I H(u,\theta u)\,du
 \ge\int_I f(\theta u)\,du-\int_I(1-g(u))\,du
 \ge\frac{A\delta}{8}-A|E|\ge\frac{A\delta}{10}.
\]
This proves~\eqref{eq:period-mass} in the second case.

In both cases we construct $f$ and $g$ by translating and rescaling fixed smooth functions. In Case~1, their transition widths can be chosen at least a fixed multiple of $\delta$, since $\ell_1/Q\ge\delta/100$. In Case~2, the transition widths of $f$ are comparable to $\delta$, and those of $g$ are $\delta/1000$, independently of $\ell_1/Q$. Each differentiation costs the inverse of a transition width, so
\[
 \|f^{(r)}\|_\infty+\|g^{(r)}\|_\infty\ll_r\delta^{-r},
\]
uniformly in the parameters; changing $j$ in Case~2 only translates $f$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Excluding square divisibility.}\quad
The selected prime might still divide its term more than once. We now exclude this possibility while preserving~\eqref{eq:period-mass} on a fixed proportion of the complete periods in $J$.

Every term of the selected block lies within $L$ of $M$. Put $U=M/Q^2$. If $U<1/4$, then $M+L<Q^2$ for large $n$, so no term of the block is divisible by $q^2$ for $q>Q$, and we take $G=1$. Otherwise, fix a small absolute constant $\tau_0>0$ and a smooth $1$-periodic $G:\mathbb R\to[0,1]$ supported where $\tau_0<\{\zeta\}<1-\tau_0$ and equal to $1$ where $2\tau_0\le\{\zeta\}\le1-2\tau_0$. If $G(M/q^2)>0$, then $M$ is at distance more than $\tau_0q^2>L$ from every multiple of $q^2$, so again no term of the block is divisible by $q^2$.

Under the substitution $u=n/v$, the interval $[Q,2Q]$ becomes $J$, and $M/v^2$ becomes $\phi(u)=Mu^2/n^2$. Partition $J$ into intervals $I=[u_0,u_0+A]$ with $u_0\in\mathbb Z$, discarding two end pieces of total length at most $2A$. Since $0<\phi'(u)\le2/Q$ on $J$, the variation of $\phi$ on each period is at most $2A/Q<\tau_0$ for large $n$, by~\eqref{eq:scales}.

Suppose $U\ge1/4$. Then $\phi(J)=[U/4,U]$. The points in this interval within $3\tau_0$ of an integer have total length $O(\tau_0(U+1))$, hence relative measure $O(\tau_0)$, uniformly for $U\ge1/4$. Since $du/dz$ varies by at most a factor $2$ for $z=\phi(u)$ in this range, their preimage in $J$ also has relative measure $O(\tau_0)$. Any complete period containing a point outside this preimage has $G(\phi(u))=1$ throughout: the distance from an integer starts above $3\tau_0$ and changes by less than $\tau_0$. Call these periods \emph{good}. Their total length is at least
\[
 \Bigl(1-C\tau_0-\frac{4AQ}n\Bigr)|J|\ge\frac12|J|
\]
once $\tau_0$ is small and $n$ is large. When $G=1$, every complete period is good.

On each good period, multiplication by $G(\phi(u))$ leaves the integral in~\eqref{eq:period-mass} unchanged. Summing over these periods gives
\[
 \int_J H(u,\theta u)G(\phi(u))\,du\gg\delta|J|\asymp\frac{\delta n}{Q}.
\]

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Applying the prime comparison.}\quad
Set $W(x,y,\zeta)=H(x,y)G(\zeta)$. By construction, positivity of $W(n/q,\theta n/q,M/q^2)$ would make $q$ divide exactly one term of the two blocks, to the first power, and nothing in $c!$. The square condition therefore forces
\[
 S(W)=\sum_{Q<q\le2Q}W\Bigl(\frac nq,\frac{\theta n}q,\frac M{q^2}\Bigr)=0.
\]
The derivative bounds for $f,g$, together with the fixed derivative bounds for $G$, give
\[
 \|\partial^\beta W\|_\infty\ll_\beta\delta^{-|\beta|}=(\log n)^{2|\beta|},
\]
so Lemma~\ref{lem:prime-comparison} applies with $C_W=2$ and $\Lambda=5$.

On the other hand, $|dv|/\log v\asymp Q^2(n\log Q)^{-1}\,du$ under $u=n/v$, so the corresponding integral satisfies
\[
 I(W)\gg\frac{Q^2}{n\log Q}\cdot\frac{\delta n}{Q}
 \gg_\varepsilon\frac Q{(\log n)^3}.
\]
Thus Lemma~\ref{lem:prime-comparison} gives $S(W)=I(W)+O(Q(\log n)^{-5})>0$ for large $n$, a contradiction.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:count}
Let $0<\varepsilon\le10^{-3}$. The number of integers $n\le X$ that have a prime factor $p>X^{1-\varepsilon}$ and satisfy
\[
 n!\,(n-1)!\,m!\,(m-\ell_2)!\,c!=\square,\qquad n-1>m>m-\ell_2>c\ge0,\qquad 2\le\ell_2\le X^\varepsilon,
\]
for some $m,\ell_2,c$, is $O(X^{1/2+5\varepsilon}(\log X)^2)$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Write $n=ap$, so that $a<X^\varepsilon$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:smallest}, $c\ll\ell_2\log X\le X^{\varepsilon+o(1)}$, so $p>\max(\ell_2,c,\sqrt n)$ for large $X$. As in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer}, $m-j=tp$ for a unique $0\le j<\ell_2$ and some $1\le t<a$.

Fix $(a,t,\ell_2,j,c)$ and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:remaining}
 f(z)=at\,c!\prod_{\substack{0\le i<\ell_2\\ i\ne j}}(z-i).
\end{equation}
Removing $p^2$ from~\eqref{eq:two-block} (with $\ell_1=1$) shows that $f(m)$ is a square. Each possible $m\le X$ determines $n=a(m-j)/t$.

For every prime $\sqrt X/2<q\le\sqrt X$, we have $q>\max(a,t,c,\ell_2)$ for large $X$. Thus $f$ has nonzero leading coefficient and $\ell_2-1$ distinct roots modulo $q$. By~\eqref{eq:weil}, the number $\rho(q)$ of residues for which $f$ is a square or zero satisfies
\[
 \rho(q)\le\frac q2+\frac{\ell_2-1+(\ell_2-2)\sqrt q}{2}\le\frac{3q}{4},
\]
uniformly in the parameters, since $\ell_2/\sqrt q\ll X^{\varepsilon-1/4}=o(1)$. Apply~\eqref{eq:sieve} to the possible values of $m$, with $\mathcal P$ consisting of the primes in this interval. As $(q-\rho(q))/\rho(q)\ge1/3$, the number of possible $m$ is
\[
 \ll X\Biggl(\sum_{\sqrt X/2<q\le\sqrt X}\frac{q-\rho(q)}{\rho(q)}\Biggr)^{-1}
 \ll\sqrt X\log X.
\]
There are $O(X^{5\varepsilon}\log X)$ parameter tuples, since $a,t,\ell_2,j\le X^\varepsilon$ and $c\ll X^\varepsilon\log X$. Summing gives $O(X^{1/2+5\varepsilon}(\log X)^2)$ values of $n$, which proves the stated bound.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:unit}
Let $0<\varepsilon\le10^{-3}$. Apart from at most $X^{1/2+6\varepsilon+o(1)}$ exceptions, every squarefree $n\in[X/2,X]$ with
\[
 P(n)>X^{1-\varepsilon/2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P\bigl(s(n-1)\bigr)>X^{3\varepsilon}
\]
has no representation with fewer than five factorials, and each of its five-factor representations has the form
\[
 n!\,(n-1)!\,m!\,(m-1)!\,c!=\square,\qquad n-1>m>m-1>c\ge1.
\]
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Write $n=ap$ with $p=P(n)$, so $a<X^{\varepsilon/2}$. Consider a four- or five-factor representation, in the notation of~\eqref{eq:two-block}. Since $p>n^{1-\varepsilon/2}$, Lemma~\ref{lem:short} (with $\alpha=\varepsilon/2$) gives $\ell_1,\ell_2<X^\varepsilon$, and then Lemma~\ref{lem:smallest} gives $c\le X^{\varepsilon+o(1)}$. Thus $\max(\ell_1,c,a\ell_2)<X^{3\varepsilon}$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer} forces $\ell_1=1$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:count}, $\ell_2\ge2$ occurs for at most $X^{1/2+6\varepsilon+o(1)}$ values of $n$. Otherwise $\ell_1=\ell_2=1$; a four-factor representation would then give $nm=\square$ with $m<n$, which is impossible for squarefree $n$.

A two-factor representation would make $n$ a square (Section~\ref{subsec:main-term}), and by~\eqref{eq:all-three} only $O(\sqrt X)$ integers $n\le X$ have a three-factor representation.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:R}
The set $\mathcal R$ has positive lower density.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Let $B_0$ be large. The squarefree integers with no prime factor at most $B_0$ have density
\[
 \varrho_{B_0}=\frac6{\pi^2}\prod_{p\le B_0}\frac p{p+1}\asymp\frac1{\log B_0},
\]
and each of them satisfies $s(ac!)=a\,s(c!)\ge a$ for $c\le B_0$.

We now estimate how many of these integers fail the condition for some $c>B_0$. Put $s_c=s(c!)$. For squarefree $a$ we have $s(ac!)=as_c/\gcd(a,s_c)^2$, so $s(ac!)<a$ if and only if $\gcd(a,s_c)>\sqrt{s_c}$. In that case $a$ is divisible by a divisor $d>\sqrt{s_c}$ of $s_c$, so
\[
 \#\{a\le Z:a\ \text{squarefree},\ s(ac!)<a\}\le\sum_{\substack{d\mid s_c\\ d>\sqrt{s_c}}}\frac Zd\le Z\frac{\tau(s_c)}{\sqrt{s_c}}.
\]
The primes in $(c/2,c]$ show that $s_c\ge e^{(1/2+o(1))c}$, while $\tau(s_c)\le2^{\pi(c)}=e^{o(c)}$. Summing over $c>B_0$ gives a total loss of $O(Ze^{-\gamma B_0})$ from our initial set, for some absolute $\gamma>0$. Since $e^{-\gamma B_0}=o(\varrho_{B_0})$, the lower density of $\mathcal R$ is at least $\varrho_{B_0}/2$ for large $B_0$.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:smooth}
For all sufficiently small fixed $\varepsilon>0$, the number of $n\in[X/2,X]$ with $P(s(n-1))\le X^{3\varepsilon}$ is at most $CXe^{-1/(6\varepsilon)}+O(X^{3/4})$, where $C$ is absolute.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Write $n-1=s(n-1)u^2$. The number of $n$ with $u>X^{1/4}$ is at most $\sum_{u>X^{1/4}}X/u^2\ll X^{3/4}$. For the remaining $n$ we have $s(n-1)\ge X^{1/2}/3$ when $X$ is large.

Put $y=X^{3\varepsilon}$ and $\sigma=1/\log y$. Expanding into sums over squarefree divisors and using $\left\lfloor X/d\right\rfloor\le X/d$ gives
\[
 \frac1X\sum_{v\le X}\prod_{\substack{p\mid v\\p\le y}}p^\sigma
 \le\prod_{p\le y}\Bigl(1+\frac{p^\sigma-1}{p}\Bigr)\ll1,
\]
because $p^\sigma-1\ll\sigma\log p$ for $p\le y$ and $\sum_{p\le y}(\log p)/p\ll\log y$. If $s(n-1)$ is $y$-smooth, its squarefreeness gives
\[
 \prod_{\substack{p\mid n-1\\p\le y}}p^\sigma\ge s(n-1)^\sigma
 \ge(X^{1/2}/3)^{1/\log y}=e^{1/(6\varepsilon)+o(1)}.
\]
The lemma follows from Markov's inequality.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:candidates}
Let $\mathcal A$ be a set of squarefree positive integers such that $\#(\mathcal A\cap[1,Y])\ge\kappa Y$ for some $\kappa>0$ and all large $Y$. Let $\varepsilon>0$ be sufficiently small in terms of $\kappa$, and put $\alpha=\varepsilon/2$. Then for all large $X$ there are $\gg_\kappa\alpha X$ squarefree integers $n=ap\in[X/2,X]$ with $a\in\mathcal A$, $X^{\alpha/2}\le a\le X^\alpha/2$, $p=P(n)>X^{1-\alpha}$ and $P(s(n-1))>X^{3\varepsilon}$.

These integers have no representation with fewer than five factorials, and every five-factor representation has two consecutive pairs as in Corollary~\ref{cor:unit}. If $\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal R$, they have no five-factor representation either.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
For each $a\in\mathcal A$ with $X^{\alpha/2}\le a\le X^\alpha/2$, let $p$ run over the primes in $(X/(2a),X/a]$. Then $p>X^{1-\alpha}$ is larger than $a$ and than $\sqrt X$, so $n=ap$ is squarefree, $P(n)=p$, and distinct pairs $(a,p)$ give distinct $n$. By the prime number theorem, the number of such $n$ is
\[
 \gg\frac X{\log X}\sum_{\substack{a\in\mathcal A\\ X^{\alpha/2}\le a\le X^\alpha/2}}\frac1a.
\]
To bound this sum from below, we use the positive lower density of $\mathcal A$. Partial summation gives
\[
 \sum_{\substack{a\in\mathcal A\\ Y\le a\le Y^2/2}}\frac1a\ge\int_Y^{Y^2/2}\frac{\#(\mathcal A\cap[1,u])}{u^2}\,du-1\ge\kappa\log Y-O(1).
\]
Taking $Y=X^{\alpha/2}$, we obtain $\gg\kappa\alpha X$ candidates.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:smooth}, at most $CXe^{-1/(6\varepsilon)}+O(X^{3/4})$ of them have $P(s(n-1))\le X^{3\varepsilon}$. Since $e^{-1/(6\varepsilon)}=o(\varepsilon)$, we can make this less than half the count above by choosing $\varepsilon$ sufficiently small in terms of $\kappa$. Corollary~\ref{cor:unit} removes a further $o(X)$ integers and gives the first assertion. The second follows from the discussion of $\mathcal R$ above.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:affine}
For every integer $m\ge2$ there are an integer $N\ge1$ and distinct affine functions $a_i(t)=\alpha_it+\beta_i$, $1\le i\le N$, with integer coefficients $\alpha_i>0$ and $\beta_i\ge0$, such that for every sufficiently large integer $t$ the numbers $a_i(t)$ are distinct and at least $2$, and $\prod_{i=1}^Na_i(t)!$ is an $m$-th power. Moreover one can take $m\mid N$ and
\[
 N\le\begin{cases}2m(m-1)\lceil e^m\rceil&\text{if $m$ is a prime power},\\ 2c_m(c_m+1)\lceil e^{c_m+1}\rceil&\text{in general},\end{cases}
 \qquad c_m=\lceil m\log m\rceil.
\]
In particular $N\le\exp(O(m\log m))$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
We express the factorials as monomials in a fixed finite set of formal generators. The Davenport constant then supplies a nonempty subfamily whose exponent vectors sum to zero modulo $m$.

Let $R,K\ge1$ and $L_R=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,R)$. For $1\le\ell\le R$ and $0\le j\le\left\lfloor K/\ell\right\rfloor$ consider
\[
 F_{\ell,j}(t)=\Bigl(\frac{L_Rt}\ell+j\Bigr)!.
\]
Call the terms with a given $\ell$ the $\ell$-th row. For $j\ge1$, passing from $F_{\ell,j-1}$ to $F_{\ell,j}$ multiplies the factorial by $(L_Rt+\ell j)/\ell$. Since $\ell j\le K$, all rows use linear factors from the same list $L_Rt+1,\dots,L_Rt+K$. With the generators
\[
 \Phi_\ell(t)=\Bigl(\frac{L_Rt}\ell\Bigr)!\quad(1\le\ell\le R),\qquad Y_i(t)=L_Rt+i\quad(1\le i\le K),
\]
and the primes $p\le R$, we have
\[
 F_{\ell,j}(t)=\Phi_\ell(t)\,\ell^{-j}\prod_{h=1}^jY_{\ell h}(t),\qquad\ell^{-j}=\prod_{p\le R}p^{-jv_p(\ell)},
\]
where $v_p(\ell)$ is the exponent of $p$ in $\ell$ and $\ell h\le K$. Thus each $F_{\ell,j}$ is a monomial in $r=R+K+\pi(R)$ generators, with an exponent vector in $\mathbb Z^r$ (possibly with negative entries) that does not depend on $t$. Reducing these vectors modulo $m$ gives a sequence of
\[
 M_0=\sum_{\ell=1}^R\Bigl(\left\lfloor \frac K\ell\right\rfloor+1\Bigr)>K\sum_{\ell=1}^R\frac1\ell>K\log R,
\]
elements of $C_m^r$.

Suppose that $M_0\ge\mathsf D(C_m^r)$. Then some nonempty subsequence of at most $\mathsf D(C_m^r)$ terms has sum zero. The selection is independent of $t$. For the corresponding factorials, every generator occurs with total exponent divisible by $m$. Thus for every positive integer $t$ their product is the $m$-th power of a positive rational number. Since the product is an integer, this rational number is an integer too.

Let $N$ be the number of selected factorials. The generator $\Phi_\ell$ occurs exactly in the terms of the $\ell$-th row, with exponent $1$, so the number of selected terms in each row is divisible by $m$; in particular $m\mid N$.

Arguments in the same row are distinct. Arguments in different rows have different slopes $L_R/\ell$, whose differences are at least $1$, and shifts in $[0,K]$; so they are distinct for $t>K$. All arguments are at least $L_Rt/R\ge2$ for large $t$.

The number of available factorials exceeds $K\log R$, whereas the number of generators is $K+R+\pi(R)$. We choose $R$ first to make $\log R$ large enough, and then $K$ to ensure $M_0\ge\mathsf D(C_m^r)$. If $m$ is a prime power, let $R=\lceil e^m\rceil$, $R_0=R+\pi(R)$ and $K=(m-1)R_0$. Then $r=mR_0$ and $M_0>K\log R\ge m(m-1)R_0=r(m-1)$, so $M_0\ge\mathsf D(C_m^r)$ and $N\le\mathsf D(C_m^r)=1+m(m-1)R_0$. Since $m\mid N$, we get $N\le m(m-1)R_0\le2m(m-1)\lceil e^m\rceil$.

For general $m$, let $R=\lceil e^{c_m+1}\rceil$, $R_0=R+\pi(R)$ and $K=c_mR_0$. Then $r=(c_m+1)R_0$ and $M_0>K\log R\ge c_m(c_m+1)R_0=c_mr\ge\mathsf D(C_m^r)$, which gives $N\le c_m(c_m+1)R_0\le2c_m(c_m+1)\lceil e^{c_m+1}\rceil$.

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:ap}
For every $m\ge2$ there are $N\ge1$, $q_0\ge1$, a residue class $b$ modulo $q_0$ and $\lambda>0$ such that every sufficiently large $n\equiv b\pmod{q_0}$ admits a representation
\[
 a_1!\cdots a_N!=y^m,\qquad\lambda n\le a_1<\cdots<a_N=n,
\]
with $N$ bounded as in Theorem~\ref{thm:affine}.
<!-- end corollary-3 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
For large $t$ the order of the functions $a_i(t)$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:affine} is fixed. The largest has the form $q_0t+b$, and takes every sufficiently large value $n\equiv b\pmod{q_0}$, while the ratio of the smallest to the largest tends to $\min_i\alpha_i/\max_i\alpha_i>0$.
