<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm: main}
Let $(S,c)$ be a closed Riemann surface of genus $\mathtt g\ge 2$. There exists an open connected subset $\Omega\subset H^0(\mathcal K_c^2)\times H^0(\mathcal K_{\overline c}^2)$ with 
\[
\Omega\supset \Delta \ \ \cup\  \ H^0(\mathcal K_c^2)\times \{0\}\ \ \cup\  \ \{0\}\times H^0(\mathcal K_{\overline c}^2)\]
and invariant under the $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}^*$-action $\zeta\cdot (q_1,\overline{q_2})=(\zeta^2 q_1,\zeta^{-2}\overline{q_2})$, together with a unique local biholomorphism  $$\Phi: \Omega \to \chi^{an}(\pi_1(S),\mathrm{PSL}(2,\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}))$$ satisfying the following.  
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Restricted to $\Delta$, $\Phi(q,\overline{q})=\mathcal {W}(q)$, where $\mathcal W$ is Hitchin-Wolf's parametrization of Teichmuller space, the latter identified with $\mathrm{Fuch}(S)$.
        \item Restricted to $ H^0(\mathcal K_c^2)\times \{0\}$, $\Phi(q,0)=\mathcal S(-\frac 1 2q)$, where $\mathcal S$ is the Schwarzian parametrization, and similar on $\{0\}\times H^0(\mathcal K_{\overline c}^2).$
        \item $\Omega$ contains the image of $\mathsf{Uh}(S,c)$ under the map $q\mapsto (q,-\overline{q})$. On this image, $\Phi(q,-\overline{q})=\mathcal{U}(q)$, where $\mathcal{U}$ is Uhlenbeck's immersion.
    \end{enumerate}
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{cor: main cor}
There exists an open subset $\mathcal A\subset \mathcal{QF}(S)$ with 
  \[
  \mathcal A\supset \mathrm{Fuch}(S)\ \cup\ \mathcal T(S)\times\{[\overline c]\}\ \cup\ \{[c]\}\times \mathcal T(\overline S) \ \cup \ \mathrm{AF}(S,c),
  \] 
  and a unique holomorphic embedding 
    \[
    \Psi\colon \mathcal A\to H^0(\mathcal K_c^2)\times H^0(\mathcal K_{\overline c}^2). \] 
    such that, in the identification $\mathcal T(S)\times \mathcal T(\overline S)\cong \mathrm{QF}(S)$,
    for all $[c']\in \mathcal T(S)$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\Psi([c'],[\overline{c}'])=(\mathcal{W}^{-1}([c']),\overline{\mathcal{W}^{-1}([c'])})$,
        \item $\Psi([c'],[\overline c])=(0,-2 \mathcal{B}e([c']))$, i.e., the second component gives a rescaling of the Bers embedding relative to $c$, and similar for points $([ c],[\overline c'])$.
        \item $\Psi(\mathcal U(q))=(q, -\overline q)$, for all $q\in \mathsf {Uh}(S,c)$.
    \end{enumerate}

<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{prop: connection forms}
    Let $g$ be a complex metric on $\Sigma$, and let $\eta^1,\eta^2$ be local complex-valued 1-forms such that $g=\eta^1\cdot \eta^2$. Then, there exists a unique locally defined 1-form $\eta_c$ such that 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: isotropic LC forms}
    \begin{cases}
  d\eta^1=-\eta_c\wedge \eta^1  \\
  d\eta^2=\eta_c\wedge \eta^2,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
and, if $K_g$ is the sectional curvature, $$d\eta_c= \frac{K_g}{2}\eta^1\wedge \eta^2.$$
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
There are unique locally defined functions $A$ and $B$ such that $d\eta^1 = A\eta^1\wedge \eta^2$ and $d\eta^2 = B \eta^1\wedge \eta^2$. Define $$\eta_c=B\eta^1+A\eta^2.$$ Then, $d\eta^1=A\eta^1\wedge \eta^2= -A\eta^2\wedge \eta^1=-\eta_c\wedge \eta^1$, and similarly $d\eta^2= \eta_c\wedge \eta^2$, i.e., (\ref{eq: isotropic LC forms}) holds. Moreover, $\eta_c$ is the unique $1$-form satisfying \eqref{eq: isotropic LC forms}: for any other such $1$-form $\hat{\eta}_c$, $$(\eta_c-\hat{\eta}_c)\wedge \eta^1=(\eta_c-\hat{\eta}_c)\wedge \eta^2=0,$$ which implies $\eta_c-\hat{\eta}_c$ is parallel to both $\eta^1$ and $\eta^2$, and then linear independence of $\{\eta^1,\eta^2\}$ forces $\eta_c=\hat{\eta}_c$.

For the curvature formula, let $(E_1,E_2)$ be the dual frame of the complexified tangent bundle. This frame is isotropic for $g$, and $g(E_1,E_2)=\frac 12 $. The Levi-Civita connection $\nabla^g$ satisfies $\nabla^g E_1 = \eta_c \otimes E_1$ and $\nabla^g E_2 = -\eta_c\otimes E_2$. Indeed, it is routine to check that these expressions define a compatible and torsion free connection, which by uniqueness must agree with $\nabla^g$. Now, we calculate
\begin{align*}
R^g(X,Y)E_1
&=
\nabla^g_X\bigl(\eta_c(Y)E_1\bigr)
-\nabla^g_Y\bigl(\eta_c(X)E_1\bigr)
-\eta_c([X,Y])E_1 \\
&=
\bigl(
X(\eta_c(Y))-Y(\eta_c(X))-\eta_c([X,Y])
\bigr)E_1 
+\eta_c(Y)\eta_c(X)E_1
-\eta_c(X)\eta_c(Y)E_1 \\
&=
d\eta_c(X,Y)E_1.
\end{align*}
An analogous calculation returns $R^g(X,Y)E_2=-d\eta_c(X,Y)E_2.$ Hence, using $g(E_1,E_2)=\frac{1}{2}$ and the isotropy condition, $$K_g=\frac{g(R^g(E_1,E_2)E_2,E_1)}{-g(E_1,E_2)^2}=\frac{g(-d\eta_c(E_1,E_2)E_2,E_1)}{-g(E_1,E_2)^2}=\frac{-d\eta_c(E_1,E_2)g(E_1,E_2)}{-g(E_1,E_2)^2}=2d\eta_c(E_1,E_2).$$ We conclude that $d\eta_c=\frac{K_g}{2}\eta^1\wedge \eta^2.$

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rmk: equivalent harmonicity conditions}
It is proved in \cite[Section 4.1]{ES_Chmaps} that $d^f(\Pi_+\circ df) = -d^f(\Pi_-\circ df) $, and that the vanishing of either of these sections is equivalent to $\textrm{tr}_h((\nabla^{h^*}\otimes \nabla^f) (df))=0$, where $h$ is any positive complex metric inducing complex structures $(c_1,\overline{c_2})$, and $\nabla^{h^*}$ is the dual of the Levi-Civita connection on $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}} T^*\Sigma$. We can therefore define complex harmonicity as the vanishing of any of these three objects.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rmk: harmonicity in coords}
    In local holomorphic coordinates $z$ and $\overline{w}$ for $c_1$ and $\overline{c_2}$, for any $1$-form $\omega$, $$\Pi_+\omega = \frac{\omega(\partial_{w})}{\partial_{w}z}dz, \hspace{1mm} \Pi_-\omega = \frac{\omega(\partial_{\overline{z}})}{\partial_{\overline{z}}\overline{w}}d\overline{w}.$$
    By evaluating $d^f(\Pi_+ \circ df)$ with respect to the two induced local frames for $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}} T\Sigma$, $(\partial_{z}, \partial_{\overline {z}})$ and $(\partial_{w}, \partial_{\overline {w}})$, we get that 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq: harmonicity in coords 1}
    (d^f(\Pi_+ df)) (\partial_{z}, \partial_{\overline {z}})= - \nabla^f_{\partial_{\overline {z}}} ((\Pi_+ df)(\partial_{z})= -\nabla^f_{\partial_{\overline {z}}}\frac{df(\partial_{w})}{\partial_{w} z},
    \end{equation}
    and similarly, 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq: harmonicity in coords 2}
     (d^f(\Pi_- df)) (\partial_{w}, \partial_{\overline {w}})= - \nabla^f_{\partial_{ w}} ((\Pi_+ df)(\partial_{\overline {w}})= -\nabla^f_{\partial_{ w}}\frac{df(\partial_{\overline {z}})}{\partial_{\overline {z}} \overline {w}},
    \end{equation}
    where we used that $(\Pi_+ df)(\partial_{\overline {z}})= 0=(\Pi_- df)(\partial_{ {w}})$.
    Thus, complex harmonicity can be expressed in coordinates as $\nabla^f_{\partial_{\overline {z}}}(\frac 1 {\partial_{w} z}{df(\partial_{w})})=0$ or $\nabla^f_{\partial_{ w}}(\frac1 {\partial_{\overline {z}} \overline {w}}{df(\partial_{\overline {z}})})=0$.

\label{prop: hopf}
    If $f$ is complex harmonic, $q_1$ and $\overline {q_2}$ are holomorphic quadratic differentials for $c_1$ and $\overline{c_2}$ respectively. When $\mathbb{X}$ has complex dimension $2$ and $f$ is admissible in an open and dense subset of $\Sigma$, the converse holds.
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $z$ and $\overline w$ be local holomorphic coordinates for $c_1$ and $\overline{c_2}$ respectively.
    By metric compatibility and Equation \eqref{eq: harmonicity in coords 1},
    \begin{align*}
    \partial_{\overline z} (q_1(\partial_z, \partial_z)) &= \partial_{\overline z} (f^*\langle{(\Pi_+ df)(\partial_z), (\Pi_+ df)(\partial_z)}\rangle) = 2\langle{\nabla^f_{\partial_{\overline z}} ((\Pi_+ df)(\partial_z)), (\Pi_+ df)(\partial_z) }\rangle\\
    =& -2 \langle{(d^f(\Pi_+df))(\partial_z, \partial_{\overline z}), (\Pi_+df)(\partial_z)  }\rangle.
     \end{align*}
     Similarly, since $d^f(\Pi_+ df)=-d^f(\Pi_- df)$ (Remark \ref{rmk: equivalent harmonicity conditions}),
     \[
     \partial_w (\overline{q_2}(\partial_{\overline w}, \partial_{\overline w}))= -2 \langle{(d^f(\Pi_-df))(\partial_{\overline w}, \partial_{w}), (\Pi_-df)(\partial_{\overline w})  }\rangle= 2 \langle{(d^f(\Pi_+df))(\partial_{\overline w}, \partial_{w}), (\Pi_-df)(\partial_{\overline w})  }\rangle.
     \]
     Hence, if $d^f(\Pi_+ df)=0$, $q_1$ and $\overline{q_2}$ are holomorphic for $c_1$ and $\overline{c_2}$ respectively.
     
      For the converse, observe that if $f$ is admissible and $\mathbb{X}$ has complex dimension $2$, then for all $p\in \Sigma$, $df(\operatorname{\mathbb{C}} T_p \Sigma)=T_{f(p)}\mathbb{X}$, hence, with $z$ and $\overline{w}$ as above, $T_p\mathbb{X}$ is spanned by the vectors $((\Pi_+ df)(\partial_z)= \frac{1 }{\partial_w z} df(\partial_w)$ and $((\Pi_- df)(\partial_{\overline w})= \frac{1 }{\partial_{\overline z} {\overline w}} df(\partial_{\overline z})$. 
      Since $(d^f(\Pi_+df))(\partial_{\overline w}, \partial_{w})$ and  $(d^f(\Pi_+df))(\partial_z,\partial_{\overline z})$ are multiples, the computation above shows that, if $q_1$ and $\overline {q_2}$ are holomorphic differentials, then $f$ is complex harmonic. Finally, if $f$ is admissible in just an open and dense set, the result continues to hold due to continuity of second derivatives.

<!-- thm-3: body_md -->
\label{thm: Bochner equation and harmonicity}
Let $c_1$ and $\overline{c_2}$ be oppositely oriented complex structures on $\Sigma$ with Bers metric $h$. Let $q_1$ be a $c_1$-holomorphic quadratic differential and let $\overline{q_2}$ be a $\overline{c_2}$-holomorphic quadratic differential. Suppose there exists a function $U: \Sigma \to \operatorname{\mathbb{C}}^*$ such that     
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: Bochner}
\Delta_h \log U= 2U-2\frac{q_1\overline{q_2} }{h^2}\frac 1 U-2.
\end{equation} Then, there exists an equivariant complex harmonic and admissible immersion $f\colon (\widetilde \Sigma,c_1,\overline{c_2})\to \mathbb G$, unique up to isometries of $\mathbb{G}$, with pull-back metric 
\begin{equation}\label{eq: pullback, complex}
    g=q_1+ \left(U +\frac 1 U \frac{q_1\overline{q_2}}{ h^2}\right) h +\overline{q_2}.
    \end{equation}
Conversely, if we have an equivariant complex harmonic map with pullback metric (\ref{eq: pullback, complex}), then the function $U$ solves (\ref{eq: Bochner}).

<!-- lem-1: body_md -->
\label{lem: det}
    For a complex bilinear form expressed as $g=q_1+ \left(U +\frac 1 U \frac{q_1\overline{q_2}}{ h^2}\right) h +\overline{q_2}$, $g$ is non-degenerate if and only if $\left(U -\frac 1 U \frac{q_1\overline{q_2}}{ h^2}\right)$ never vanishes.
<!-- end lem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Local holomorphic coordinates $z$ and $\overline{w}$ for $c_1$ and $\overline{c_2}$ respectively determine the coframe $(dz,d\overline{w})$ in which the matrix of $g$ is expressed $$\begin{pmatrix}
        q_1 & \frac{1}{2}\left(U +\frac 1 U \frac{q_1\overline{q_2}}{ h^2}\right)h \\
        \frac{1}{2}\left(U +\frac 1 U \frac{q_1\overline{q_2}}{ h^2}\right)h & \overline{q_2}
    \end{pmatrix}.$$
    The determinant is $\left(U +\frac 1 U \frac{q_1\overline{q_2}}{ h^2}\right)^2h^2-4q_1\overline{q_2}=-\frac{h^2}{4}\left(U -\frac 1 U \frac{q_1\overline{q_2}}{ h^2}\right)^2.$
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By Propositions \ref{prop: metric to immersion}, Theorem \ref{thm: Bochner equation and harmonicity} is equivalent to the statement that $g$ has constant curvature $-1$ if and only if Equation \eqref{eq: Bochner} holds. 
    


In local coordinates $z$ and $\overline{w}$ for $c_1$ and $\overline{c_2}$, write $h=\lambda dzd\overline{w}$, $q_1=\varphi dz^2$, $\overline{q_2}=\overline{\psi}d\overline{w}^2$. We recall from \cite[Sections 3.3 and 3.4]{ES_Chmaps} the local coordinate expression $$\Delta_h = \frac 1 {\partial_{\overline {z}} \overline{w}}\partial_{\overline {z}}(\frac{1}{\partial_{w}z} \partial_{w})$$ and that $\Delta_h \log \lambda=2$.
In our coordinates, define the functions 
\begin{align*}
P&= \lambda U- \frac{\operatorname{\varphi} \overline \psi}{\lambda}\frac 1 U,\\
Q&=\frac{1}{\partial_{w}z} \partial_{w} \log (\lambda U).
\end{align*}
Since $\Delta_h \log \lambda=2$, Equation \eqref{eq: Bochner} can be rewritten as 
\[
\Delta_h \log(\lambda U)= \Delta_h \log\lambda + \Delta_h\log U=2+2\left(\frac 1 \lambda P-1\right)= 2\frac P \lambda.
\]
By our expression for $\Delta_h$, Equation \eqref{eq: Bochner} is thus equivalent to
\[
\frac 1 {\partial_{\overline {z}} \overline{w}}\partial_{\overline {z}} Q= \frac 1 2 P.
\]
We will show that this last equation is equivalent to $K_g=-1$. To this end, we find an expression involving the curvature of $g$ and our other objects. Preparing to apply Proposition \ref{prop: connection forms}, we write $g=\eta^1\cdot \eta^2$, where 
\begin{align*}
  \eta^1&= dz+\overline{\psi} \frac 1 {\lambda U} d\overline {w},\\
    \eta^2&= \operatorname{\varphi} dz+\lambda Ud\overline {w}.
\end{align*}
By a straightforward calculation, using 
Equation \eqref{eq: differential in dz dw bar} where needed, we get:
\begin{align*}
    \eta^1\wedge \eta^2&= P dz\wedge d\overline {w}\\
    d\eta^1&=d(\frac 1 {\lambda U})\wedge  \overline{\psi}d\overline {w} = - \frac {\overline{\psi}} {\lambda U} d (\log(\lambda U))\wedge  d\overline{w}= - \frac {\overline{\psi}} {\lambda U}Q dz\wedge d\overline{w}=- \overline \psi \frac{Q}{\lambda UP} \eta^1\wedge \eta^2\\
     d\eta^2&=d(\lambda U)\wedge d\overline {w}= \lambda U d(\log(\lambda U))\wedge d\overline {w}=  \lambda UQ dz\wedge d\overline{w}=\frac{\lambda UQ}P \eta^1\wedge \eta^2,
\end{align*}
where the division by $P$ is permitted thanks to Lemma \ref{lem: det}. Now let $\eta_c$ be the connection $1$-form associated with $\{\eta^1,\eta^2\}$ via Proposition \ref{prop: connection forms}. According to the definition of $\eta_c$,
\[
\eta_c= \frac{\lambda UQ}P \eta^1 - \overline \psi \frac{Q}{\lambda UP}\eta^2=
\frac{\lambda UQ} P dz +\overline \psi \frac Q P d\overline {w} - \frac{\operatorname{\varphi}\overline \psi Q}{\lambda U P}dz-  \overline \psi \frac Q P d\overline {w}=Q dz.
\]
{As well, by Equation \eqref{eq: differential in dz dw bar} again, 
$$d\eta_c = -\frac 1 {\partial_{\overline {z}} \overline{w}}\partial_{\overline {z}} Q dz\wedge d\overline w.$$
}
Hence, by Proposition \ref{prop: connection forms},
\[ \frac 12 K_g \cdot P dz\wedge d\overline {w}= \frac 12 K_g \eta^1\wedge \eta^2= d\eta_c= -{\frac 1 {\partial_{\overline {z}} \overline{w}}}\partial_{\overline {z}}Q dz\wedge d\overline {w} ,\]
which shows that Equation \eqref{eq: Bochner} is equivalent to $K_g=-1$.

\label{thm: A and B}
    Let $f=(f_1,\overline{f_2}):(\widetilde{\Sigma},c,\overline{c})\to \mathbb{G}$ be a complex harmonic map such that $g=f^*\operatorname{\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle}$ is positive. Let $z$ be a holomorphic local coordinate for $c$ and write $h=\lambda dz d\overline{z}$. Taking the second expression, we factor  
$$g^\zeta = U\lambda(dz+\zeta^{-2}\overline{\mu_2}d{z})(d\overline z+\zeta^2\mu_1d\overline{z}).$$ The expressions $dz+\zeta^{-2}\overline{\mu_2}d{z}$ and $d\overline z+\zeta^2\mu_1d\overline{z}$ are locally defined $1$-forms, which we denote by $\alpha^{\zeta}$ and $\beta^{\zeta}$ respectively. Their spans determine line sub-bundles of $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}} T^*\Sigma$, which we denote by just $\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\alpha^{\zeta}$ and $\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\beta^{\zeta}$. Let $\ell_1^\zeta$ be the complex line in $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}} T^*\Sigma$ spanned by $df_1^\zeta$ and let $\ell_2^\zeta$ be the complex line in $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}} T^*\Sigma$ spanned by $d\overline{f_2}^\zeta.$ Comparing expressions for the pullback metric, we have that over every point in our local coordinate patch, as unordered sets $$\{\ell_1^\zeta,\ell_2^\zeta\}=\{\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\alpha^{\zeta},\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\beta^{\zeta}\}.$$

If $\zeta\in A$, then $\alpha^\zeta$ has no real vector in its kernel, so $\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\alpha^\zeta$ never defines a point in $\mathbb{P}T^*S$. Note that for such $\zeta$, $\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\alpha^{\zeta}$ lies in the same component of  $\mathbb{PC}T^*S\backslash \mathbb{P}T^*S$ as $[dz]$. Thus, for $\zeta$ close to $1$, $\ell_1^\zeta=\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\alpha^{\zeta}$. By holomorphicity, we deduce that $\ell_1^\zeta=\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\alpha^{\zeta}$ for all $\zeta\in A$. Moreover, $df_1^\zeta$ has no real vector in its kernel and gives the same component as $[dz]$, so it is an orientation preserving local diffeomorphism.


The exact same argument applies to $\beta^\zeta$ to shows that for $\zeta \in B$, $\ell_2^\zeta=\textrm{Span}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}\beta^{\zeta}$, and $\overline{f_2}^\zeta$ is an orientation reversing local diffeomorphism.

\label{thm: immersion}
    Let $U\subset \mathcal H^*(S,c)$ be a connected open subset such that: \begin{itemize}
    \item $hol$ is a local biholomorphism on $U$;
        \item $\vb*g(q_1, \overline{q_2}, u)$ is a positive complex metric for all $(q_1, \overline{q_2}, u)\in U$;
    \item $U$ contains $\mathbf W(S,c)$. 
    \end{itemize}
    Then $\mathbb C^*\cdot U$ is contained in $\mathcal H^*(S,c)$ and $\mathrm{hol}$ is a local biholomorphism on $\mathbb C^*\cdot U$.

<!-- lem-4: body_md -->
\label{lem: symplectic action}
    On the (pre-)symplectic manifold $(\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}^*\cdot U,\mathrm{hol}^*\omega^{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}})$, the $\mathbb C^*$-action is (pre-)symplectic.
<!-- end lem-4 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}^*$-action restricts to an $S^1$-action on the real submanifold of real harmonic maps $\mathbf W(S,c)$. Using the above identification between $\mathbf W(S,c)$ and $H^0(\mathcal{K}_c^2)$, it follows from \cite[pp. 92 and Proposition 11.17]{Hitchin:1986vp} that on $\mathbf W(S,c)$, the $S^1$-action is symplectic for $\omega$. Indeed, in \cite{Hitchin:1986vp}, Hitchin's section identifies $H^0(\mathcal{K}_c^2)$ with the Hitchin component of the moduli space of $\textrm{SL}(2,\mathbb R)$-Higgs bundles, on which the pullback of the Goldman form is identified with his symplectic form $``\omega_1$" (Proposition 11.17), which he shows to be invariant under the $S^1$-action (pp. 92). The result now follows by analytic continuation: the action $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}^*\times \operatorname{\mathbb{C}}^*\cdot U\to \operatorname{\mathbb{C}}^*\cdot U$ holomorphically extends the action $S^1\times \mathbf W(S,c)\to \mathbf W(S,c)$.
<!-- end proof-9 -->
<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
We omit the argument since we don't need the result, but Lemma \ref{lem: symplectic action} holds even if we drop the condition that $U$ contains $\mathbf{W}(S,c)$, thus forbidding an analytic continuation argument. It follows from a routine calculation, using the explicit formula for $\omega^{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}}$ in terms of complex metrics from \cite{ElE} (i.e., the extension of Fischer-Tromba's formula for the Weil-Petersson metric) and the expression (\ref{eq: pullback, complex}). Restricting to the real locus, this also gives an alternative argument for the $S^1$-invariance.

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
The main goal is to find an open connected subset $\Omega\subset H^0(\mathcal K_c^2)\times H^0(\mathcal K_{\overline c}^2) $ that is $\mathbb C^*$-invariant, such that $\mathrm{hol}|_\Omega$ is a local biholomorphism, and on which $\mathrm{hol}|_\Omega$ admits an inverse $s\colon \Omega\to \mathcal H^*(S,c)$ of $\pi$. Then, the desired mapping is just $\Phi:=\mathrm{hol}\circ s$. As commented after the theorem statement, uniqueness is automatic.

    First of all, we prove that for every point of $H^0(\mathcal K_c^2)\times H^0(\mathcal K_{\overline c}^2)$, every neighborhood contains a pre-compact neighborhood such that the intersection with every $\mathbb C^*$-orbit is either empty or connected; we will call such neighborhoods ``$\mathbb C^*$-good." This is trivial at the point $(0,0)\in \mathcal H(S,c)$ by taking neighborhoods of the form 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq: nbhd (0,0)}
        \{(q_1, \overline{q_2})\ |\ \|q_1\|<\operatorname{\varepsilon}_1, \|q_2\|<\operatorname{\varepsilon}_2\},
    \end{equation}
    where $\|\cdot \|$ denotes just any real norm on the spaces of holomorphic differentials. On the other hand, $\mathbb C^*$ acts freely (and with closed embedded orbits) on $H^0(\mathcal K_c^2)\times H^0(\mathcal K_{\overline c}^2)\setminus\{(0,0)\}$. Therefore, the infinitesimal orbit distribution is integrable (with orbits as integral curves), and by the Frobenius Theorem the foliation by $\mathbb C^*$-orbits is locally trivializable. In trivializations, it isn now immediate to construct neighborhoods such that the intersection with every orbit is connected. 

Now, observe that if $s\colon U\to \mathcal H^*(S,c)$ is an inverse of $\pi$, $U$ is $\mathbb C^*$-good, and both $(q_1,\overline{q_2})$ and $\zeta\cdot (q_1,\overline{q_2})$ lie in $U$, then $s(\zeta\cdot (q_1,\overline{q_2}))=\zeta\cdot s((q_1,\overline{q_2}))$. Indeed, for $\zeta$ sufficiently close to $1$,  $\zeta \cdot(q_1,\overline{q_2})= \pi (\zeta\cdot s((q_1,\overline{q_2}))) $ lies in $U$, and hence $s(\zeta\cdot (q_1,\overline{q_2}))=\zeta\cdot s((q_1,\overline{q_2}))$ is obtained by applying $s$ to both terms. This implies that the subset $$\{\zeta\in \mathbb C^* \ | \ \zeta\cdot (q_1,\overline{q_2})\in U \text{ and } s(\zeta\cdot (q_1,\overline{q_2}))=\zeta\cdot s((q_1,\overline{q_2})) \}$$
is open and closed in $\{\zeta\in \mathbb C^* \ | \ \zeta\cdot (q_1,\overline{q_2})\in U\}$, and hence it's the whole thing by the fact that $U$ is $\mathbb C^*$-good. As a consequence, if $s\colon U\to \mathcal H^*(S,c)$ is an inverse defined on a $\mathbb C^*$-good subset $U$, and if $\mathbb C^*\cdot U\subset \mathcal H^*(S,c)$, then $s$ extends uniquely to a biholomorphic inverse of $\pi$ on $\mathbb C^*\cdot U$. Indeed, take $s(\zeta\cdot (q_1,\overline{q_2}) ) := \zeta \cdot s((q_1,\overline{q_2}) )$ for all $ (q_1,\overline{q_2}) )\in U$.


Let $Z_1=\mathbf W(S,c)$ and $Z_2=\mathbf {U}(S,c)$.
By the Generalized Inverse Function Theorem (see Exercise 1.8.14 in \cite{GuillPoll}), there exists a global inverse $s\colon \Omega_0\to \mathcal H(S,c)$ with $s(\Omega_0)$ containing both $Z_1$ and $Z_2$. As discussed in Section \ref{subsec: key loci}, $\mathrm{hol}$ is a local biholomorphism at every point of $Z_1\cup Z_2$, hence we can assume that $\mathrm{hol}$ restricts to a local biholomorphism on $s(\Omega_0)$ and that $\vb*g(q_1, \overline{q_2}, u)$ is a positive metric for all $(q_1, \overline{q_2}, u)\in s(\Omega_0)$. 
We claim that, after possibly shrinking $\Omega_0$ further, $s$ extends $\mathbb C^*$-equivariantly to $\mathbb C^*\cdot \Omega_0$. Granting this, on $\mathbb C^*\cdot \Omega_0$, $s$ determines a global biholomorphic inverse of $\pi$. By Theorem \ref{thm: immersion}, $\mathrm{hol}$ is a local biholomorphism on $s(\Omega_0)$, hence $\Omega=\mathbb C^*\cdot\Omega_0$ and $\Phi=\mathrm{hol}\circ s$ give the theorem statement. 


To realize the claim, we first extend $s$ to a $\mathbb C^*$-invariant neighborhood of $Z_1\cup Z_2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$. One can easily observe that the $\mathbb C^*$-action restricts to 
\[
Q=(H^0(S, \mathcal K_c^2)\setminus \{0\}) \times (H^0( \mathcal K_{\overline c}^2)\setminus \{0\})
\]
and that the action is free and proper over it: $Q/\mathbb C^*$ is therefore a complex manifold, and it contains $\pi(Z_1)/S^1$ and $\pi(Z_2)/S^1$ as disjoint closed embedded submanifolds. Now, cover each of these submanifolds with images of $\mathbb C^*$-good open subsets of $\Omega_0$ around points in $\pi(Z_k)$; refine these coverings with geodesically convex open subsets for some Riemannian metric on $Q/\mathbb C^*$: in this way, any two of them that intersect have convex intersection that intersects $\pi(Z_k)/S^1$. As a result, we obtain a covering of each $Z_k$ with a collection of $\mathbb C^*$-invariant open subsets over which $s$ can be extended to be $\mathbb C^*$-equivariant. Moreover, if any two of these open subsets intersect, then their intersection would be connected, and they would intersect on an open connected subset of $Z_k$ over which the inverses match. They therefore coincide on the whole intersection. We have hence constructed a $\mathbb C^*$-equivariant inverse $s$ on a $\mathbb C^*$-invariant neighborhood $\Omega_1$ of $\pi(Z_1)\cup\pi( Z_2)\setminus\{(0,0)\}$.
Up to shrinking the initial $\Omega_0$ to the union of some suitable $\mathbb C^*$-good open neighborhoods of the points of $\pi(Z_1)\cup\pi(Z_2)$, we can assume that these two inverses coincide on $\Omega_0\cap \Omega_1$. 

Finally, let $U_{0,0}$ be a $\mathbb C^*$-good neighborhood of $(0,0)$ of the form \eqref{eq: nbhd (0,0)} contained in $\Omega_0$. Observe that $s$ clearly glues to $U_{0,0}\cup \Omega_1$. We are left to show that $s$ extends $\mathbb C^*$-equivariantly to $\Omega\coloneqq\mathbb C^*\cdot U_{0,0}\cup \Omega_1$. This is simple to check: if $w\in \mathbb C^*\cdot U_{0,0}\cup \Omega_1$, then there exists $\zeta\in \mathbb C^*$ such that $\zeta\cdot w\in  U_{0,0}\cup \Omega_1$, thus
\[
s|_{\mathbb C^* \cdot U_{0,0}} (w)= \zeta^{-1}\cdot s(\zeta \cdot  w)= s|_{\Omega_1} (w).
\]
This completes the proof of the claim, and hence we're done.

<!-- lem-5: body_md -->
\label{lem: global inj of hol}
    In the identification $\mathrm{QF}(S)\cong\mathcal T(S)\times\mathcal T(\overline S)$, for $(q, -\overline q, u)\in \mathbf U(S,c)$, denote  $\mathrm{hol}(q, -\overline q, u)=:([c^q_1],[ \overline c^q_2])$. Then,
    $$
    [c]=[c^q_1]\  \Longleftrightarrow\ [\overline c]=[\overline c^q_2] \ \Longleftrightarrow \ [c^q_1]=[ c^q_2]\ \Longleftrightarrow q=0.
    $$
<!-- end lem-5 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Clearly, if $q=0$, then all the other statements follow. If $[c^q_1]=[c^q_2]$, then the holonomy is Fuchsian, so the unique almost-Fuchsian immersion with that holonomy is the totally geodesic one, i.e., $q=0$.

Now, let $\mathtt I$, $\mathtt {II}$, and $\mathtt B$ be the fundamental forms and the shape operator of the almost-Fuchsian immersion corresponding to $(q, -\overline q, u)$. By the work of Krasnov-Schlenker \cite{KrasnovSchlenker2008}, $c^{q}_1$ and $c^q_2$ give conformal structures (up to isotopy) for the Riemannian metrics $\mathtt I_+^* = \mathtt I((\mathrm{Id}+ \mathtt B)\cdot, (\mathrm{Id}+ \mathtt B)\cdot)$ and $\mathtt I_-^*=\mathtt I((\mathrm{Id}- \mathtt B)\cdot, (\mathrm{Id}- \mathtt B)\cdot)$ respectively. Observe that $\mathtt I+\mathtt I(\mathtt B\cdot, \mathtt B\cdot)$ is conformal to $\mathtt I$, and that
 \[
 \mathtt I_+^*= \mathtt I+\mathtt I(\mathtt B\cdot, \mathtt B\cdot) + q+\overline q \qquad \qquad \mathtt I_-^*= \mathtt I+\mathtt I(\mathtt B\cdot, \mathtt B\cdot) - q-\overline q.
 \]
 By holomorphicity of the Hopf differentials, the identity maps $id\colon (S, \mathtt I)\to (S, \mathtt I_+^*)$ and $id\colon (S, \mathtt I)\to (S, \mathtt I_-^*)$ are harmonic. 

  Assuming that $c$ is conformal to $c^q_1$, we prove that $q=0$. The proof will follow in the analogous way for $c^{q}_2$. If $c=\phi^*(c^q_1)$ for $\phi$ isotopic to the identity, then $\phi\colon (S,c)\to (S, \mathtt I^*_+)$ is harmonic and conformal (in fact, $\phi\colon (S,\phi^*(\mathtt I^*_+)) \to (S, \mathtt I^*_+) $ is an isometry). By construction, the curvature of $\mathtt I_+^*$ is $-1+ \frac 2 {det(\mathtt B)}<0$, hence the harmonic map $(S,c)\to (S, \mathtt I^*_+)$ isotopic to the identity is unique. Thus, $\phi=id$ and $id$ is conformal, hence $q=0$.

<!-- prop-5: body_md -->
\label{prop: global inj of hol}
    The restriction of $\Phi$ on $$\pi(\mathbf W(S,c))\cup \pi(\mathbf U(S,c))\cup  \pi(\mathbf{Be}(S,c)) \cup \pi(\overline{\mathbf{Be}}(S,c) ) $$ is injective.
<!-- end prop-5 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
By construction $\Phi$ is injective when restricted individually to each of the terms of the union. 
    By Lemma \ref{lem: global inj of hol}, their images through $\Phi$ pairwise meet only at $\Phi(0,0)=([c],[\overline c])\in \mathcal T(S)\times \mathcal T(\overline S)\cong \mathrm{QF}(S)$, hence the injectivity holds.
<!-- end proof-12 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
As mentioned before, to prove the statement, it is sufficient to prove that $\Phi$ is injective on an open subset $\Omega_3\subset H^0(\mathcal K_c^2)\times H^9(\mathcal K_{\overline c}^2)$ with $$ \Omega_3\supset  Z:= \pi(\mathbf W(S,c))\cup \pi(\mathbf {Be}(S,c))\cup\pi( \overline{\mathbf {Be}}(S,c))\cup\pi( \mathbf U(S,c)).$$  By Theorem \ref{thm: main}, $\Phi$ is a local biholomorphism at every point of $Z$ and, by Proposition \ref{prop: global inj of hol}, $\Phi$ is injective on $Z$.

By the Generalized Implicit Function Theorem (see \cite{GuillPoll}), $\Phi$ restricts to open embeddings around some closed neighbourhoods 
   \[
   \overline {\Omega^1_3}\supset \pi( \mathbf W(S,c)),\quad 
  \overline {\Omega_3^2}\supset \pi(\mathbf {Be}(S,c)),\quad
   \overline {\Omega_3^3}\supset \pi(\overline{\mathbf {Be}}(S, c)),\quad
  \overline {\Omega_3^4}\supset \pi( \mathbf U(S,c)).  
   \]
By defining 
\[
\mathcal A=int \left( \left\{\rho\in \bigcup_j \Phi(\Omega_3^j)\ |\ \text{if $\rho\in \Phi( \Omega_3^j) \cap  \Phi(\Omega_3^k)$, then } (\Phi|_{\Omega_3^j})^{-1}(\rho)= (\Phi|_{\Omega_3^k})^{-1}(\rho) \right\} \right) 
\]
we get that $\Phi$ restricts to a biholomorphism $\Phi\colon \Omega_3'\to \mathcal A$, where $\Omega_3'$ is an open subset of $\mathcal H^*(S,c)$ containing ${Z}$ (see \cite[Section 6.6]{ES_Chmaps} for a detailed argument of this type in a more complicated framework). By denoting $\Psi= (\Phi|_{\Omega'_3})^{-1}$, we get the statement of the corollary.

<!-- defn-5: body_md -->
A \textbf{complex harmonic bundle} over $(\Sigma,c_1,\overline{c_2})$ is a tuple $(E,Q,\nabla, \phi_1,\overline{\phi_2})$, where 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $E$ is a complex vector bundle,
        \item $Q$ is a non-degenerate bilinear form on $E$,
        \item $\nabla$ is $Q$-compatible connection on $E$,
        \item and $\phi_1\in \Omega_{c_1}^{1,0}(\textrm{End}_0(E))$ and $\overline{\phi_2}\in \Omega_{c_2}^{0,1}(\textrm{End}_0(E))$ are $Q$-symmetric sections satisfying $\nabla\phi_1=\nabla\overline{\phi_2}=0,$
    \end{enumerate}
  and such that the connection $$D:=\nabla + \phi_1 +\overline{\phi_2}$$ is flat.
<!-- end defn-5 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
One essentially just has to repeat the proof of Theorem \ref{thm: A and B}. Recalling the discussion at the beginning of Section \ref{subsec: Bochner}, for any harmonic immersion $f$ to $\mathbb{H}^2$ we can write the pullback metric as 
   $$ \left(U +\frac 1 U \frac{|q|^2}{ h^2}\right) h +q+\overline{q}.$$ If $f$ is just a branched immersion, then the formula extends over the branch points (we get zero identically). If $f$ parametrizes a geodesic, the same formula holds with $U=\frac{|q|}{h}$. Thus, when we apply the $\operatorname{\mathbb{C}}^*$-action, the associated complex harmonic map $f^\zeta=(f_1^\zeta,\overline{f}_z^\zeta)$ has pullback metric 
   \begin{equation}\label{eq: pullback, C star}
       \left(U +\frac 1 U \frac{|q|^2}{ h^2}\right) h +\zeta^2q+\zeta^{-2}\overline{q}.
   \end{equation}
   When we integrate the data of our complex bilinear form using the flat connection, there is an ambiguity not just up to composition with elements of $\textrm{PSL}(2,\operatorname{\mathbb{C}})$, but also the ordering of $f_1^\zeta$ and $\overline{f_2}^\zeta$ is not pre-determined. In the branched immersion case, we specify the initial maps so that $f_1$ is orientation preserving and $\overline{f_2}$ is orientation reversing. In the geodesic case, recall we exclude $\zeta=1$ from the theorem, so instead we specify that $\overline{f_1}^\zeta$ is orientation preserving and $\overline{f_2}^\zeta$ is orientation reversing for some fixed $\zeta$ of norm larger than $1$ and for some fixed $\zeta$ of norm smaller than $1$. From here, Theorem \ref{thm: A and B} goes through verbatim to show that on $A_f$, $f_1^\zeta$ is a branched immersion, and on $B_f$, $\overline{f}_2^\zeta$ is a branched immersion. The only difference is that at zeros of \eqref{eq: pullback, C star}, the complex lines from the proof of Theorem \ref{thm: A and B} degenerate, and hence the maps have branch points.

   Since $D_\zeta$ is the flat connection associated with the $f^\zeta$, the usual correspondence between (branched) projective structures and (branched) opers (see \cite[Section 3]{silva2024conformal}) allows us to say that $D_\zeta$ is a (branched) oper over the relevant Riemann surface.

Holomorphicity of \eqref{eq: pullback, C star} in $\zeta$ is clear. The holomorphicity of the maps $A_f\to \mathcal{CP}(\Sigma)$ and $B_f\to \mathcal{CP}(\overline{\Sigma})$ thus follows from Lemma \ref{lem: holo dependence Bonsante} and the standard fact that the holonomy map on complex projective structures is a local biholomorphism.
