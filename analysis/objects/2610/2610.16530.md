<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:127}
Let $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{127})$. For every integer $j\geq1$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\alpha_j=[\overline{z_j}]$ belongs to $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{127})$ and uses only
the digits $1,2,3,4$;
\item its primitive period has length $92j+2$;
\item its multiplier order is $\mathcal O_{f_j}$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fj}
 f_j=\frac{y_{4j}}4
     =\frac{\varepsilon^{4j}-\varepsilon^{-4j}}{8\sqrt{127}},
 \qquad
 \Delta(\alpha_j)=\frac{(\varepsilon^{4j}-\varepsilon^{-4j})^2}{16};
\end{equation}
\item the index $[\mathcal O_K^{\times,+}:\mathcal O_{f_j}^{\times,+}]$ is $4j$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:indexfour}
 [\mathcal O_{f_j}:\mathbb Z[\varepsilon^{4j}]]=4.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
In particular the family consists of infinitely many distinct periods, up to cyclic rotation, and supplies infinitely many elements of $\mathcal B_4(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{127}))$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:order}
Let $A_0X^2+B_0X+C_0$ be a primitive polynomial for a reduced quadratic
irrational $\alpha$, with $A_0>0$. The multiplier ring of
$\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha$ is
\[
 \mathbb Z+A_0\alpha\mathbb Z
   =\mathbb Z+\frac{-B_0+\sqrt{\Delta(\alpha)}}2\mathbb Z.
\]
In particular it is $\mathcal O_f$ if $\Delta(\alpha)=D_Kf^2$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
A multiplier belongs to $\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\alpha$, since it acts on $1$, so write it
$r+s\alpha$ with $r,s\in\mathbb Z$. Multiplication by $\alpha$ preserves the lattice
precisely when $A_0\mid sB_0$ and $A_0\mid sC_0$. Primitivity implies
$A_0\mid s$, prime by prime. The displayed formula and the order discriminant
follow. This is the usual form--order correspondence \cite{Buell89}.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:primitiveindex}
Suppose a word of even length with positive integer digits $w$ is primitive and $P_w$ has expanding
eigenvalue $\varepsilon_K^k$. Its fixed point has unit group index $k$.
Conversely, if $K$ has no units of norm $-1$ and the fixed point has unit group index $k$, then any word matrix with expanding eigenvalue $\varepsilon_K^k$ is primitive.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For the first assertion let $h=k(f)$. Then $h\mid k$, and
\eqref{eq:unitmatrix} gives a continued fraction matrix $V$ for $\varepsilon_K^h$.
Both matrices have the same two eigenlines, so $P_w=V^{k/h}$.
Freeness of this monoid implies that $w$ is a word power if $h<k$.
For the converse, a proper word root would yield an algebraic-integer unit
in the multiplier order with smaller expanding eigenvalue. Its norm cannot
be $-1$ under the stated hypothesis, so it would have an index strictly
between $0$ and $k$, a contradiction.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:contentfour}
For every $n\geq0$, the matrix in \eqref{eq:W127} satisfies $g(W_n)=4$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Write $w=(h,k)^t$. Directly from \eqref{eq:W127},
\begin{align}\label{eq:Wentries}
 (W_n)_{12}&=9hk+7k^2+9,\notag\\
 (W_n)_{21}&=5h^2+4hk-4,\\
 (W_n)_{11}-(W_n)_{22}&=9h^2+2hk-4k^2-12.\notag
\end{align}
The residues
\[
 A\equiv\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\3&3\end{pmatrix}\pmod4,\qquad u\equiv\binom03\pmod4
\]
show that $4\mid h$ and $k$ is odd for every $n$.
All three expressions in \eqref{eq:Wentries} are divisible by $4$, while
$(W_n)_{21}\equiv-4\pmod{16}$. Thus the $2$-part of $g(W_n)$ is exactly $4$.

Suppose an odd prime $p$ divides $g(W_n)$. Then $W_n$ is scalar modulo $p$,
say $sI$. Its product description gives
$ww^t-J=sB^{-1}$. Comparing the differences of the two off-diagonal entries gives $s=-1$.
With $z=(2,-3)^t$ one has $B^{-1}=zz^t+J$, whence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oddcontent}
 ww^t=-zz^t\pmod p.
\end{equation}
In particular $k^2=-9\pmod p$ and, since $v^tz=0$,
$v^tw=0\pmod p$.

An invariant integral form restricts the possible primes. Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qform}
 Q_0=\frac{JA-A^tJ}{4930}
  =\begin{pmatrix}-11792979999&14646902784\\14646902784&9764601856\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
One has $A^tQ_0A=Q_0$, either by direct multiplication or by
$A^tJA=J$. Therefore $w^tQ_0w=u^tQ_0u$.
Taking the trace after multiplying \eqref{eq:oddcontent} by $Q_0$ shows that
$p$ divides the following fixed integer:
\begin{align}\label{eq:Hfactor}
 H&=u^tQ_0u+z^tQ_0z=-125288734844\notag\\
  &=-2^2\cdot7^2\cdot13\cdot43\cdot557\cdot2053.
\end{align}
The primes $7$ and $43$ are excluded by $k^2=-9$, since they are $3$ modulo $4$.
For the three remaining primes, direct exponentiation gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oddorders}
 \begin{aligned}
 A^3&=-I\pmod{13},\qquad A^{139}=-I\pmod{557},\\
 A^{513}&=-I\pmod{2053}.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus the order of $A$ modulo each such prime divides twice an odd integer.
But $v^tw=\operatorname{tr} A^{n+1}=0\pmod p$ would imply
$(A^{n+1})^2=-I$ by Cayley--Hamilton. The element $A^{n+1}$ would have order
$4$, which is impossible. No odd prime divides $g(W_n)$.
Each equality in \eqref{eq:oddorders} can be checked by repeated squaring.
For convenience, the residues of $A$ are listed in Table~\ref{tab:modular}.

The words have lengths $|a|=46$, $|b|=4$, $|c|=90$, so $|z_j|=92j+2$.
By \eqref{eq:trace127}, the trace discriminant is
$508y_{4j}^2$. Lemma~\ref{lem:contentfour}, \eqref{eq:primitiveD}, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:order} give conductor $f_j=y_{4j}/4$ and
\eqref{eq:fj}. Integrality also follows from $4\mid y_4$ and the formula for
powers of $\varepsilon^4$.

The coefficient sequence satisfies
\[
 y_0=0,\quad y_1=419775,\quad
 y_{r+1}=9461248\,y_r-y_{r-1}.
\]
It is strictly increasing, and for $r\geq2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ygap}
 y_r>(9461248-1)y_{r-1}>4y_{r-1}.
\end{equation}
Hence $f_j=y_{4j}/4>y_{4j-1}$. No positive $y_r$ with $r<4j$ can be a
multiple of $f_j$, whereas $f_j\mid y_{4j}$. The unit group index is therefore
exactly $4j$. Since the field has no units of norm $-1$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:primitiveindex} proves that $z_j$ is primitive. This gives the
claimed least period, not merely a displayed period.

Finally $\mathbb Z[\varepsilon^{4j}]=\mathbb Z+y_{4j}\sqrt{127}\mathbb Z=\mathcal O_{y_{4j}}$.
The ratio of conductors gives \eqref{eq:indexfour}. The conductors increase
strictly with $j$, so the cycles are distinct.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Primitivity can also be checked combinatorially. The word has $2j$ occurrences
of $4$ and length $92j+2$, whose greatest common divisor is $2$.
Only a square could be a proper word power. For $j\geq3$ the overlap of its
two long blocks would force $\operatorname{rev}(a)$ to be the left cyclic shift of $a$ by
$|a|-3$. Palindromicity of $P$ makes it the shift by $|a|-1$, forcing period
$2$ in $a$, which contains four distinct digits. For $j=1,2$, the digit at
zero-based index $1$ differs from that at the corresponding index $48,94$
in the other half. This is an independent elementary check.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:pal}
Let $m\geq2$ and let $s$ be any palindrome on $\{1,\ldots,m\}$, including the
empty word. Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generalwords}
 a=s11211,\quad b=2111,\quad c=s121111s,\quad
 w_n=b\,a^nc\,\operatorname{rev}(a)^n,\quad L=|s|+5.
\end{equation}
Let $A=P_a$ and let $\lambda>1$ be its expanding eigenvalue. Put
\[
 K=\mathbb Q\bigl(\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A)^2-4\det A}\bigr).
\]
Then every $[\overline{w_n}]$ belongs to
$\mathcal B_m(K)$. For every $n\geq5$, the word $w_n$ is primitive of length
$2(n+1)L$. If $\lambda^2=\varepsilon_K^q$, then $q\geq1$ is an integer and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generalcounts}
 A_{K,m}(qj)\geq2jL\quad(j\geq6),\qquad
 R_{K,m}(T_{qr})\geq2L\sum_{\substack{j\mid r\\j\geq6}}j\quad(r\geq1).
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Write $S=P_s=S^t$ and $\sigma=\det S\in\{1,-1\}$.
The fixed matrices are
\[
 R=P_{11211}=\begin{pmatrix}12&7\\7&4\end{pmatrix},\quad
 B=P_{2111}=\begin{pmatrix}8&5\\3&2\end{pmatrix},\quad
 D=P_{121111}=\begin{pmatrix}18&11\\13&8\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus $A=SR$, $C=SDS$, and $P_{w_n}=BA^nC(A^t)^n$.
With $v=(2,1)^t$, $u=(3,2)^t$ and $J$ as above,
\[
 B=2vv^t-J,\quad D=2uu^t+J,\quad R=2uv^t-J.
\]
For any matrix $X$, symmetric--skew cancellation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generaltrace1}
 \operatorname{tr}(BXSDSX^t)=4(v^tXSu)^2+2\sigma\det X.
\end{equation}
Indeed $SJS=\sigma J$ and $XJX^t=\det(X)J$.
The matrix $A^nS$ is symmetric, since it is the alternating product
$SRS\cdots RS$. Consequently
$\operatorname{tr} A^{n+1}=\operatorname{tr}(A^nSR)=2v^tA^nSu$.
Since $\det A=-\sigma$, \eqref{eq:generaltrace1} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generaltrace2}
 \det P_{w_n}=1,\qquad
 \operatorname{tr} P_{w_n}=(\operatorname{tr} A^{n+1})^2-2\det(A^{n+1})=\operatorname{tr} A^{2n+2}.
\end{equation}
The expanding eigenvalue is $\lambda^{2n+2}$, so the quadratic field is $K$.
Also $\lambda^2$ is a totally positive algebraic-integer unit, establishing
$\lambda^2=\varepsilon_K^q$ for an integer $q\geq1$.

We prove primitivity explicitly. Let $p$ be the least period of the infinite
repetition of $w_n$; it divides $N=2(n+1)L$.
If $p\leq(n-1)L$, the block $a^n$ has periods $p,L$ and length at least
$p+L$. The Fine--Wilf theorem \cite{FineWilf65}, in the standard word
form \cite{Lothaire02}, gives period $\gcd(p,L)$. Since this divides $L$,
the infinite repetition of $a$ also has period $p$.
Shift each position of the initial block $b$ forward by an appropriate
multiple of $p$ into $a^n$. Periodicity forces $b$ to be the four digits
immediately preceding $a$ in its periodic repetition. But the last four digits
of $a$ are $1211$, whereas $b=2111$. Therefore $p>(n-1)L$.

For $n\geq5$, a proper repetition of exponent at least three would give
$p\leq N/3\leq(n-1)L$. Only a square remains.
If $p=N/2=(n+1)L$, shift the first long block by this half-length. Its
overlap with the reversed long block has length $(n-1)L+4\geq L$.
It forces $\operatorname{rev}(a)$ to be the left cyclic shift of $a$ by $L-4$.
But $s$ and $11211$ are palindromes, so $\operatorname{rev}(a)=11211s$ is its shift
by $L-5$. The shifts differ by one, making $a$ constant, contrary to the
digits $1,2$ in its terminal block. This proves primitivity.

The $2jL$ cyclic rotations give distinct roots. Their expanding eigenvalue is
$\varepsilon_K^{qj}$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:primitiveindex} gives unit group index $qj$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:divisoridentity} proves the second inequality.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Use the sum of matrix entries as norm. Hensley's theorem
\cite{Hensley89}, also stated in \cite[Theorem 1.7]{Kont22}, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hensleycount}
 \#\{P_v:\ v\text{ on }\{1,\ldots,m\},\ \|P_v\|\leq Y\}
 \asymp_m Y^{2\delta_m}.
\end{equation}
An equivalent matrix norm only changes constants. Passing between even word
lengths and all word lengths also affects only constants, by appending one
fixed digit when necessary.

The reductions of $B_1,B_2$ generate all six elements of
$\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb F_2)$, represented by
$\varnothing,1,11,2,12,21$. Append to each word a suffix from this list
so that its matrix $V$ satisfies
\[
 V\equiv L_0:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\pmod2.
\]
The suffix has norm at most $7$, so $\|V\|\leq7Y$.
Any output has at most six inputs: for each chosen suffix, right inversion
recovers the input. We retain $\gg_m Y^{2\delta_m}$ distinct matrices $V$.
If $v$ is the word of $V$, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:countmatrices}
 s=v1\operatorname{rev}(v),\qquad S=VB_1V^t,\qquad A=SR,
 \qquad R=P_{11211}.
\end{equation}
Freeness of the monoid makes the palindromes $s$, hence the matrices $S$,
distinct. They satisfy
$\det S=-1$ and $S\equiv I\pmod2$, since $L_0B_1L_0^t=I$.
The matrices $A$ therefore have determinant one.
Their traces $T$ are multiples of four, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tracebound}
 T\leq\|S\|\|R\|\leq(7Y)^2\cdot3\cdot30=4410Y^2.
\end{equation}
Thus there are $\gg_m N^{\delta_m}$ distinct matrices of trace at most $N$.

We bound collisions at a fixed trace. Write $S=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}$;
then $ac-b^2=-1$ and $T=12a+14b+4c$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:UV}
 U=T-3a-2b>0,\qquad V_1=T+4a+2b>0.
\end{equation}
Direct expansion gives
\[
 UV_1=T^2+4(ac-b^2)=T^2-4.
\]
Conversely a divisor $U$ of $T^2-4$, and hence $V_1=(T^2-4)/U$, determines
the entire matrix $S$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recover}
 a=U+V_1-2T,\quad b=\frac{7T-4U-3V_1}{2},\quad
 c=\frac{16U+9V_1-24T}{4}.
\end{equation}
The set of matrices $A$ of a given trace has size at most $\tau(T^2-4)$.
The elementary divisor bound gives, for every $\rho>0$, a uniform upper
bound $O_\rho(N^\rho)$ for these sets when $T\leq N$.
Hence these matrices give $\gg_{m,\rho}N^{\delta_m-\rho}$ distinct traces.

Since $4\mid T$, the number $(T/2)^2-1$ is odd and is $3$ modulo $4$.
Write it $d u^2$ with $d$ squarefree. Then $u$ is odd,
$d\equiv3\pmod4$, and $d<T^2/4$.
Within any one quadratic field, each expanding eigenvalue is a totally positive
unit $\varepsilon_K^r$. Such a unit has integral trace at least $3$, so uniformly
\[
 \varepsilon_K\geq(3+\sqrt5)/2.
\]
The condition $\operatorname{tr}(\varepsilon_K^r)\leq N$ permits at most $O(\log N)$ indices,
uniformly in $K$. Passing from distinct traces to distinct fields loses only
this logarithmic factor.
Every field obtained in this construction satisfies the infinitude conclusion of
Theorem~\ref{thm:pal}. Taking $N$ of order $\sqrt X$ and absorbing
$N^\rho\log N$ into $X^\eta$ proves \eqref{eq:fieldcount}.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The congruence restriction is substantive: a squarefree integer $3$ modulo
$4$ has a prime factor $3$ modulo $4$, so its field has no units of norm $-1$. The construction therefore supplies quantitative binary infinitude
even within this class. It applies unchanged to $m=3$ and every larger
fixed alphabet, but does not prescribe an individual radicand.
