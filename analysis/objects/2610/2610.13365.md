<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
The recurrence \eqref{eq:integer} is well defined for every
$k\geq0$, and all divisions are exact. If $p_n$ denotes the
$n$-th prime, with $p_1=2$, then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:valuation}
\nu_2(T_k)=p_{k+2}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:invariant}
For each $k\geq0$, put
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wheel-state}
\widetilde T_k
=\sum_{\substack{2\leq m\leq L_k+1\\\gcd(m,L_k)=1}}2^m.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:self}
\nu_2(\widetilde T_k)=p_{k+2},\qquad
\left\lfloor \log_2\widetilde T_k\right\rfloor-1=L_k.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The least integer greater than $1$ and coprime to $L_k$ is
$p_{k+2}$. Every smaller integer greater than $1$ has a prime
divisor among $p_1,\ldots,p_{k+1}$, whereas $p_{k+2}$ does not.
Since $L_k+1$ is itself coprime to $L_k$, this least integer
lies in the summation range in \eqref{eq:wheel-state}.

The lowest and highest occupied positions are therefore
$p_{k+2}$ and $L_k+1$, respectively. Each coefficient is
$0$ or $1$, so the sum is a binary expansion without carries.
The two identities follow.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We prove by induction that the recurrence is well defined and
$T_k=\widetilde T_k$. For $k=0$, the only integer in $[2,3]$
coprime to $2$ is $3$, so $\widetilde T_0=2^3=8=T_0$.

Assume $T_k=\widetilde T_k$. For this induction step, write
$p=\nu_2(T_k)$. By \cref{lem:invariant},
\[
p=p_{k+2},\qquad L=L_k,
\]
and hence $M=2^{Lp}-1$. In particular, $L\geq2$ and $p\geq3$,
so the denominators in \eqref{eq:parameters} are nonzero.
The geometric identity
\[
\frac{2^{Lp}-1}{2^L-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}2^{jL}
\]
shows that multiplication by this quotient places translated
copies of the current binary pattern in the disjoint intervals
$[jL+2,(j+1)L+1]$, for $0\leq j<p$. Translation by $jL$
preserves coprimality to $L$. Consequently no carries occur, and
\[
U
=T_k\frac{2^{Lp}-1}{2^L-1}
=\sum_{\substack{2\leq m\leq Lp+1\\\gcd(m,L)=1}}2^m.
\]
Similarly,
\[
V=\frac{2^{Lp}-1}{2^p-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}2^{jp}
\]
has set bits at the multiples of $p$ below $Lp$. The only
multiple of $p$ in $[2,Lp+1]$ not covered by this mask is $Lp$,
which is already absent from $U$ because $\gcd(Lp,L)=L>1$.
Thus $U\mathbin{\&} V$ consists exactly of the set bits of $U$
whose positions are divisible by $p$.

Every set bit of $U\mathbin{\&} V$ is also a set bit of $U$, so
the subtraction in \eqref{eq:parameters_integer} clears those
positions without borrowing. Therefore
\[
T_{k+1}
=\sum_{\substack{2\leq m\leq Lp+1\\\gcd(m,Lp)=1}}2^m
=\widetilde T_{k+1},
\]
because $Lp=L_{k+1}$. The geometric identities establish the
exactness of both divisions, and the bit at position $Lp+1$
ensures that the new state is positive. This completes the
induction. The prime-output identity follows from
\cref{lem:invariant}.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:state-period}
For every $k\geq0$,
\[
\left\lfloor \log_2 T_k\right\rfloor-1=\prod_{j=1}^{k+1}p_j.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\left\lfloor \log_2 T_{k+1}\right\rfloor-1
=\bigl(\left\lfloor \log_2 T_k\right\rfloor-1\bigr)\nu_2(T_k).
\]
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The first identity is \eqref{eq:self}, since
$L_k=\prod_{j=1}^{k+1}p_j$. The second follows from
$L_{k+1}=L_kp_{k+2}$ and $p_{k+2}=\nu_2(T_k)$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:state-gap}
For every $k\geq1$, the two lowest set bits of $T_k$ occur
at positions $p_{k+2}$ and $p_{k+3}$. In particular,
\[
\nu_2\!\left(T_k-2^{\nu_2(T_k)}\right)=p_{k+3},
\]
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:state-gap}
p_{k+3}-p_{k+2}
=\nu_2\!\left(\frac{T_k}{2^{\nu_2(T_k)}}-1\right).
\end{equation}
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The set bits of $T_k$ occur precisely at the positions in
$[2,L_k+1]$ that are coprime to $L_k$.
Both $p_{k+2}$ and $p_{k+3}$ lie in this interval.
For $k=1$, this follows directly from $T_1=2^5+2^7$.
For $k\geq2$, we have $L_k\geq6p_{k+1}$, so Bertrand's
postulate gives
\[
p_{k+3}<2p_{k+2}<4p_{k+1}\leq L_k.
\]
Every composite integer coprime to $L_k$ has all its prime
factors at least $p_{k+2}$ and is therefore at least
$p_{k+2}^2$. Since
$p_{k+3}<2p_{k+2}<p_{k+2}^2$, no composite position can
intervene between the two stated prime positions.
They are therefore the two lowest set bits.

Subtracting $2^{\nu_2(T_k)}$ removes the lowest set bit,
giving the first identity. Dividing the remaining integer
by $2^{\nu_2(T_k)}$ gives \eqref{eq:state-gap}.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The initial state $T_0=8$ has only one set bit. A uniform
version of \eqref{eq:state-gap}, valid for every $k\geq0$, is
\[
p_{k+3}-p_{k+2}
=\nu_2\!\left(
\frac{T_k}{2^{\nu_2(T_k)}}-1
+2^{\left\lfloor \log_2 T_k\right\rfloor-1}
\right).
\]
For $k\geq1$, the preceding proof gives $p_{k+3}\leq L_k+1$,
and hence
\[
p_{k+3}-p_{k+2}\leq L_k+1-p_{k+2}<L_k.
\]
The added power of $2$ has exponent $L_k$, so it does not
change the valuation. For $k=0$, the expression inside the
valuation is $4$, giving the gap $5-3=2$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:pi-recurrence}
Set $Z_2=34$. For each integer $x\geq2$, recover
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pi-recurrence-decode}
c_x=\nu_2(Z_x),
\qquad
W_x=\frac{Z_x/2^{c_x}-1}{2},
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pi-recurrence-indicator}
\varepsilon_x
=\left\lfloor \frac{1}{(\nu_2(W_x)-x-1)^2+1}\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
Put
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pi-recurrence-wheel}
W_x^+=W_x+\varepsilon_x\bigl(\Phi(W_x)-W_x\bigr)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pi-recurrence-state}
Z_{x+1}=2^{c_x+\varepsilon_x}(2W_x^++1).
\end{equation}
Then the recurrence is well defined, all divisions outside
the floor are exact, and, for every integer $x\geq2$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pi-recurrence-invariant}
\nu_2(Z_x)=\pi(x),
\qquad
W_x=T_{\pi(x)-1}.
\end{equation}
Thus the prime-counting function is the $2$-adic valuation
of a one-integer recurrence.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Since $Z_2=34=2(2T_0+1)$, decoding gives $c_2=1=\pi(2)$
and $W_2=T_0$, so the assertion holds at $x=2$.

Assume \eqref{eq:pi-recurrence-invariant} holds at $x$.
By \cref{thm:main},
\[
\nu_2(W_x)=\nu_2(T_{\pi(x)-1})=p_{\pi(x)+1},
\]
the least prime strictly greater than $x$. The reciprocal
in \eqref{eq:pi-recurrence-indicator} is $1$ when
$x+1=\nu_2(W_x)$ and lies strictly between $0$ and $1$
otherwise. Hence $\varepsilon_x=1$ exactly when $x+1$ is
prime, and $\varepsilon_x=0$ otherwise. It follows that
\[
\pi(x+1)=\pi(x)+\varepsilon_x.
\]
The update $\Phi(W_x)$ is well defined by \cref{thm:main}.
If $\varepsilon_x=0$, then $W_x^+=W_x$; if
$\varepsilon_x=1$, then $W_x^+=\Phi(W_x)=T_{\pi(x)}$.
In either case,
\[
W_x^+=T_{\pi(x+1)-1}.
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:pi-recurrence-state} therefore gives
\[
Z_{x+1}
=2^{\pi(x+1)}\bigl(2T_{\pi(x+1)-1}+1\bigr).
\]
The factor in parentheses is positive and odd, so
$\nu_2(Z_{x+1})=\pi(x+1)$. Applying
\eqref{eq:pi-recurrence-decode} then recovers
$W_{x+1}=T_{\pi(x+1)-1}$, with both divisions exact.
The divisions within $\Phi$ are exact by \cref{thm:main}.
This completes the induction.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The recurrence in \cref{thm:pi-recurrence} uses one stored
integer, but its update depends explicitly on the index $x$.
The recurrence of \cref{thm:main} is autonomous: its update
depends only on its state. In the prime-counting recurrence,
the wheel state advances exactly when the next integer is
prime, while the exponent of $2$ records the current prime
count, including the initial prime $2$. Substituting
\cref{thm:and-term} into $\Phi$ removes bitwise AND as a
primitive operation.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:twin-output}
For every $k\geq1$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:twin-output}
p_{k+3}-p_{k+2}=2
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{T_k}{2^{\nu_2(T_k)}}\equiv5\pmod8.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
By \cref{cor:state-gap},
\[
p_{k+3}-p_{k+2}
=\nu_2\!\left(\frac{T_k}{2^{\nu_2(T_k)}}-1\right).
\]
The positive integer inside the valuation has valuation $2$ precisely
when it is congruent to $4$ modulo $8$. This is equivalent to the
congruence in \eqref{eq:twin-output}.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:twin-candidates}
For every $k\geq1$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:twin-mask-invariant}
A_k=
\sum_{\substack{2\leq m\leq L_k-1\\
\gcd(m(m+2),L_k)=1}}2^m.
\end{equation}
In particular, $A_k>0$, and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:twin-candidate-count}
\operatorname{HW}(A_k)=\prod_{j=2}^{k+1}(p_j-2).
\end{equation}
The counts therefore satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:twin-candidate-growth}
\operatorname{HW}(A_{k+1})
=\bigl(\nu_2(T_k)-2\bigr)\operatorname{HW}(A_k).
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The description of the set bits of $A_k$, together with
\eqref{eq:twin-state-invariant}, gives
\eqref{eq:twin-mask-invariant}. The position $L_k-1$ occurs in this
sum, since both $L_k-1$ and $L_k+1$ are coprime to $L_k$.
Hence $A_k>0$.

Because $k\geq1$, the integer $L_k$ is divisible by $6$.
Among the residue classes modulo $L_k$, the class $0$ fails the
coprimality condition, and the class $1$ fails it modulo $3$.
Thus the interval $[2,L_k-1]$ contains exactly one representative
of every class satisfying $\gcd(m(m+2),L_k)=1$.

Modulo $2$, precisely one class is allowed. Modulo each odd prime
$p_j\mid L_k$, precisely the two distinct classes $0$ and $-2$
are forbidden, leaving $p_j-2$ allowed classes. Since $L_k$ is
squarefree, the Chinese remainder theorem gives
\eqref{eq:twin-candidate-count}. Adjoining the next prime
$q_k=\nu_2(T_k)$ to the product gives
\eqref{eq:twin-candidate-growth}.

Equivalently, each admissible class modulo $L_k$ has $q_k$ lifts
modulo $L_kq_k$. Exactly one lift has its first member divisible
by $q_k$, and exactly one has its second member divisible by $q_k$.
These lifts are distinct because $q_k\nmid2$, so exactly $q_k-2$
survive.
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:twin-square}
Let $k\geq1$. If the bit in position $m$ of $A_k$ is set and
$m+2<q_k^2$, then $m$ and $m+2$ are twin primes. In particular,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:twin-square-criterion}
\nu_2(A_k)+2<\nu_2(T_k)^2
\end{equation}
implies that $\nu_2(A_k)$ and $\nu_2(A_k)+2$ are twin primes.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Every integer greater than $1$ and coprime to $L_k$ has all its
prime factors at least $q_k$. If such an integer is composite,
it is therefore at least $q_k^2$. By
\eqref{eq:twin-mask-invariant}, both $m$ and $m+2$ are coprime
to $L_k$ and greater than $1$. The stated upper bound makes
both prime. The final assertion follows by taking
$m=\nu_2(A_k)$, which is defined because $A_k>0$.

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:twin-infinitude}
The following statements are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item There are infinitely many twin-prime pairs.
\item The congruence
\[
\frac{T_k}{2^{\nu_2(T_k)}}\equiv5\pmod8
\]
holds for infinitely many $k\geq1$.
\item The inequality
\[
\nu_2\!\left(T_k\mathbin{\&}\left\lfloor \frac{T_k}{4}\right\rfloor\right)+2
<\nu_2(T_k)^2
\]
holds for infinitely many $k\geq1$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end corollary-3 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The equivalence of the first two statements follows from
\cref{prop:twin-output}, since the values $p_{k+2}$ with $k\geq1$
run through all primes at least $5$.

Suppose the second statement holds at an index $k$. Then
$q_k$ and $q_k+2$ are the two lowest occupied positions of $T_k$,
so $\nu_2(A_k)=q_k$. Since $q_k\geq5$,
\[
\nu_2(A_k)+2=q_k+2<q_k^2.
\]
The third statement therefore follows from the second.

Conversely, each index satisfying the third statement gives a
twin-prime pair by \cref{prop:twin-square}. Every occupied
position of $A_k$ is also occupied in $T_k$, so
\[
\nu_2(A_k)\geq\nu_2(T_k)=p_{k+2}.
\]
Along any infinite set of indices these lower bounds tend to
infinity. The resulting twin-prime pairs are therefore unbounded,
which proves the first statement.
<!-- end proof-9 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
Equation~\eqref{eq:twin-candidate-growth} counts surviving pairs
over a complete period of length $L_k$. The inequality
\eqref{eq:twin-square-criterion} concerns the position of the
first such pair. Establishing that inequality for infinitely
many indices requires control of these positions beyond the
total count; the counting formula alone does not establish
twin-prime infinitude.

For every $n\geq 1$, all quantities in
\eqref{eq:nth-parameters-a}--\eqref{eq:nth-answer} are defined, every

\label{lem:nth-bounds}
The parameters above satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nth-bounds}
p_n\leq C_n<(n+1)^2,
\qquad C_n<H_n,
\qquad C_nF_n<D_n,
\qquad F_n<D_n<B_n<A_n.
\end{equation}
In particular $D_n\geq 8$, $B_n>4H_n$, and $\nu_2(A_n)\geq n+1$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
For $n>1$, Ruiz and Sondow prove $p_n<2n\log n+2$
\cite[Lemma~1]{RuizSondow}. The function
\[
x+1-2\log x-\frac2x
\]
is positive at $x=2$ and has positive derivative $1-2/x+2/x^2$ for
$x\geq 2$. Hence $p_n<n(n+1)=C_n$ for $n\geq 2$, while $p_1=C_1=2$. The
inequality $C_n<(n+1)^2$ is immediate.

The inequality $2^{n+1}>n(n+1)$ holds for $n=1,2$ and propagates by
induction, so $C_n<H_n$. Also $n+1\leq 2^n$ gives
\[
C_nF_n=n(n+1)(n+1)!
\leq(n+1)^{n+3}
\leq 2^{n(n+3)}
<2^{(n+2)^2}=D_n.
\]
The remaining inequalities follow from the definitions. Since
$B_n=2^{h_n+n+1}$,
\[
\nu_2(A_n)=(h_n+n+1)(C_n+1)\geq n+1.
\]

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:reciprocal-packing}
Let $m\geq 1$, let $F_0$ be a positive common multiple of $1,\ldots,m+1$,
and let $a=2^b$ with $b\geq m+1$ and $a>F_0$. Put $e=a^{m+1}$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reciprocal-packing}
\frac{
e^2+1-\bigl((a-1)^{eF_0}\bmod e^2\bigr)
}{e}
=
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{F_0}{j}a^j.
\end{equation}
The sum describes the packed result; the left side computes it with one
exponentiation and one remainder.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Write $\rho=eF_0$ and $\ell=b(m+1)=\nu_2(e)$. For $1\leq j\leq m$,
\[
\frac1e\binom{\rho}{j}
=
\frac{F_0}{j}
\prod_{u=1}^{j-1}\left(\frac{eF_0}{u}-1\right)
\equiv
(-1)^{j-1}\frac{F_0}{j}
\pmod e,
\]
because every $u\leq j$ divides $F_0$. Consequently the $j$-th nonconstant
term of $(1-a)^\rho$ is congruent to
\[
-e\frac{F_0}{j}a^j\pmod{e^2}.
\]

For every $1\leq j\leq\rho$, the identity
$j\binom{\rho}{j}=\rho\binom{\rho-1}{j-1}$ gives
\[
\nu_2\!\left(\binom{\rho}{j}a^j\right)
\geq
\ell+\nu_2(F_0)-\nu_2(j)+bj.
\]
At $j=m+1$ the right side is at least $2\ell$, because $m+1$ divides $F_0$.
If $j=m+1+t$ with $t\geq 1$, then
\[
\nu_2(j)\leq j-1=m+t\leq(m+1)t\leq bt,
\]
so the same lower bound $2\ell$ follows. Every term with $j\geq m+1$
therefore vanishes modulo $e^2$. Since $\rho$ is even,
\[
(a-1)^\rho=(1-a)^\rho
\equiv
1-e\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{F_0}{j}a^j
\pmod{e^2}.
\]
Set
\[
c=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{F_0}{j}a^j.
\]
Because $0<F_0/j<a$, this is a base-$a$ integer whose largest occupied
position is $m$, so $0<c<e=a^{m+1}$. Therefore
\[
(a-1)^\rho\bmod e^2=e^2+1-ec,
\]
and solving for $c$ proves \eqref{eq:reciprocal-packing}.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:nth-sieve}
The quantities in \eqref{eq:nth-Q}--\eqref{eq:nth-S} satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nth-Q-meaning}
\mathcal Q_n
=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{C_n}
\left\lfloor \frac ij\right\rfloor B_n^iA_n^j.
\end{equation}
If
\[
t_i=\#\{j:2\leq j\leq n,\ j<i,\ j\mid i\},
\qquad t_0=t_1=0,
\]
then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nth-T-meaning}
\Delta_n=\sum_{i=0}^{C_n}t_iB_n^i,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nth-PS-meaning}
\mathcal P_n=\sum_{\substack{2\leq i\leq C_n\\ i\ \mathrm{prime}}}B_n^i,
\qquad
\mathcal S_n=\sum_{i=0}^{C_n}\pi(i)B_n^i.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
In $\mathcal R_n G_1(B_n,C_n+1)$, formula \eqref{eq:nth-C-meaning} shows that
the coefficient in the field indexed by $i+(C_n+1)j$ is
\[
d=\frac{iF_n}{j}.
\]
These fields are distinct. Write $\delta=\kappa_n-D_n/F_n$, so
$0\leq\delta<1$. For $0\leq i\leq C_n$ and $2\leq j\leq n$,
\[
\frac{d\kappa_n}{D_n}
=
\frac ij+\frac{iF_n\delta}{jD_n},
\qquad
0\leq\frac{iF_n\delta}{jD_n}<\frac1j.
\]
The fractional part of $i/j$ is an integral multiple of $1/j$, hence
\[
\left\lfloor \frac{d\kappa_n}{D_n}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor \frac ij\right\rfloor.
\]
Furthermore,
\[
0\leq d\kappa_n<C_n(D_n+F_n)<D_n(C_n+1)\leq D_nH_n=B_n.
\]
Thus there are no carries between base-$B_n$ fields. Since $D_n$ and $H_n$
are powers of two, the mask $D_n(H_n-1)$ sends a field value
$0\leq x<B_n=D_nH_n$ to $D_n\left\lfloor x/D_n\right\rfloor$. The repunit in \eqref{eq:nth-Q}
covers every field, and division by $D_n$ yields \eqref{eq:nth-Q-meaning}.

Reducing \eqref{eq:nth-Q-meaning} modulo $A_n-1$ replaces every $A_n^j$ by
$1$. Hence the $i$-th base-$B_n$ digit of $\mathcal W_n$ is
\[
w_i=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left\lfloor \frac ij\right\rfloor.
\]
The bound $w_i\leq nC_n<C_nF_n<D_n<B_n-1$ prevents carries. For $i\geq 1$,
the difference $w_i-w_{i-1}$ counts divisors of $i$ between $2$ and $n$.
Multiplication by $1-B_n$ and reduction modulo $A_n$ forms these first
differences. The nonnegative multiplier $A_n-B_n+1$ is congruent to $1-B_n$
modulo $A_n$, and subtracting $B_n^2G_0(B_n,n-1)$ removes the self-divisor

$A_n=B_n^{C_n+1}$ creates no carry between fields. This proves the formula
for $\mathcal S_n$.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rem:nth-size}
The arithmetic operation counts are not bit-complexity bounds. The
largest modulus in the reciprocal-packing step is $E_n^2$, whose bit length
is
\[
2(h_n+n+1)(C_n+1)(n+1)+1.
\]
With $h_n=(n+2)^2$ this is $O(n^5)$. If one permits the more compact
evaluation choice
\[
h_n=1+\left\lfloor \log_2(C_nF_n)\right\rfloor,
\]
which still gives $2^{h_n}>C_nF_n$, the same packed construction has an
$E_n^2$ modulus of $O(n^4\log n)$ bits. These bounds concern an optimized
evaluation of the packed construction, not literal materialization of the
cited arithmetic-term expansions of the standard operations or of
\cref{thm:and-term}.

For the dedicated $\pi(n)$ construction of \cref{thm:pi-packed}, the
largest reciprocal-packing modulus is $(E_n^{(\pi)})^2$, with bit length
\[
2(h_n+n+1)(n+1)^2+1.
\]
Thus the fully arithmetic choice $h_n=(n+2)^2$ gives $O(n^4)$ bits.  With
the compact choice
\[
h_n=1+\left\lfloor \log_2(nF_n)\right\rfloor,
\]
the corresponding bound is $O(n^3\log n)$ bits.  This reduction reflects
the fact that prime counting needs candidates only through $n$ and requires
no rank-selection stage.

Literal substitution of \cref{thm:and-term} is far larger still. Its
exponent $m=2^a$ gives the modulus $X^m-2$ a bit length of order $\ell4^a$,
which is exponential in the value of the argument $a$ and hence doubly
exponential in its bit length. This applies to both AND operations in the
packed constructions and to the update $U-\operatorname{AND}(U,V)$ of the recurrence.
Since AND is symmetric, one may take $a$ to be the smaller argument, but the
growth remains of this type. The program length is unaffected. The log-free AND program in
Appendix~\ref{sec:slp-aux} uses a larger admissible parameter to reduce
its operation count; it also has exponential intermediate bit lengths
in the numerical values of its inputs.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:and-term}
For integers $a,b\geq 0$, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:and-parameters}
r=1+\left\lfloor \log_2(a+b+1)\right\rfloor,\qquad
\ell=2^r-1,\qquad
m=2^a,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:and-XY}
X=2^{\ell m+a+2},\qquad
Y=(X+1)^\ell+1,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:and-J}
J=
\left(
\left\lfloor \frac{(1+XY)^a(1+Y)^{\ell-a}}{Y^b}\right\rfloor
\bmod Y
\right).
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:and-term}
\operatorname{AND}(a,b)=
\nu_2\!\left(
\bigl(J^m\bmod(X^m-2)\bigr)\bmod(2m)
\right)
\end{equation}
satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:and-identity}
\operatorname{AND}(a,b)=a\mathbin{\&} b.
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
By construction $\ell=2^r-1\geq a+b$, so both $a$ and $b$ fit in $r$ binary
digits. Here we observe that $r = a + b + 1$ would also work, with less arithmetic operations but more digits to handle. Now consider
\[
F(Z,T)=(1+ZT)^a(1+T)^{\ell-a}
      =\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}F_j(Z)T^j.
\]
All coefficients of the $F_j$ are nonnegative, and
\[
F_j(X)\leq F(X,1)=(1+X)^a2^{\ell-a}
\leq(1+X)^\ell<Y.
\]
Thus the numbers $F_j(X)$ are valid base-$Y$ digits. Dividing $F(X,Y)$ by
$Y^b$, taking the floor, and reducing modulo $Y$ extracts the $b$-th digit:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:J-coefficient}
J=F_b(X).
\end{equation}

Write
\[
a=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a_j2^j,
\qquad
b=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}b_j2^j,
\qquad a_j,b_j\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Because $\ell$ has $r$ binary digits all equal to $1$, the integer $\ell-a$
is the $r$-bit complement of $a$. Over $\mathbb{F}_2[Z,T]$, Frobenius gives
\[
F(Z,T)\equiv
\prod_{a_j=1}\left(1+Z^{2^j}T^{2^j}\right)
\prod_{a_j=0}\left(1+T^{2^j}\right)
\pmod2.
\]
To obtain the coefficient of $T^b$, each factor is forced by the bit $b_j$.
Hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Fb-parity}
F_b(Z)\equiv Z^d\pmod2,
\qquad
d=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a_jb_j2^j=a\mathbin{\&} b.
\end{equation}
Since the coefficients of $F_b$ are nonnegative, there exists
$H\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0}[Z]$ such that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Fb-G}
F_b(Z)=Z^d+2H(Z).
\end{equation}

Now $m=2^a$. For $1\leq s<m$,
\[
\nu_2\!\binom{m}{s}=a-\nu_2(s),
\]
because
\(
\binom{m}{s}=(m/s)\binom{m-1}{s-1}
\)
and every binomial coefficient in row $m-1=2^a-1$ is odd. Therefore
\[
\nu_2\!\left(2^s\binom{m}{s}\right)
=s+a-\nu_2(s)\geq a+1.
\]
The final term $(2H)^m$ is also divisible by $2^{a+1}=2m$, since
$m=2^a\geq a+1$. Expanding \eqref{eq:Fb-G} to the $m$-th power gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Fbm}
F_b(Z)^m=Z^{dm}+2mK(Z)
\end{equation}
for some $K\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0}[Z]$.

Reduce this polynomial modulo $Z^m-2$. Each monomial $Z^e$ becomes
\[
2^{\left\lfloor e/m\right\rfloor}Z^{\,e\bmod m}.
\]
Hence the polynomial remainder has the form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:poly-rem}
R(Z)=2^d+2mK_0(Z),\qquad \deg R<m,
\end{equation}
with nonnegative coefficients. We next verify that evaluating this
polynomial remainder at $X$ also gives the ordinary integer remainder in
\eqref{eq:and-term}. By Vandermonde's identity,
\[
F_b(1)=\binom{\ell}{b}\leq 2^\ell,
\]
so the sum of the coefficients of $F_b(Z)^m$ is at most $2^{\ell m}$. Since
$\deg F_b^m\leq am$, reduction modulo $Z^m-2$ multiplies the coefficient of
any monomial by at most $2^a$. Thus the sum of the coefficients of $R$ is
at most
\[
2^{\ell m+a}=\frac X4.
\]
Because $\deg R<m$ and $X\geq 8$,
\[
0\leq R(X)\leq \frac X4X^{m-1}
=\frac{X^m}{4}<X^m-2.
\]
It follows that
\[
J^m\bmod(X^m-2)=R(X).
\]
Finally, \eqref{eq:poly-rem} and $0\leq d\leq a$ give
\[
\bigl(J^m\bmod(X^m-2)\bigr)\bmod(2m)
=R(X)\bmod(2m)=2^d,
\]
since $2^d<2^{a+1}=2m$. Taking the $2$-adic valuation yields
$\operatorname{AND}(a,b)=d=a\mathbin{\&} b$.

To justify the cosine bound, use
$1-\cos(2\pi/m)=2\sin^2(\pi/m)$ and concavity of $\sin$ on
$[0,\pi/2]$:
\[
\frac{\sin(\pi/m)}{\pi/m}
\geq
\frac{\sin(\pi/3)}{\pi/3}
=
\frac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi}.
\]
Also $n\geq2^{m-1}$ implies
$\rho\geq2^{(m-1)/m}>3/2$, hence $(1+\rho)^2\leq3\rho^2$.  Finally,
$\log(1-u)\leq-u$ gives
\[
\eta^s
\leq
\exp\left(
-\frac{s\rho(1-\cos(2\pi/m))}{(1+\rho)^2}
\right).
\]
The remaining scalar inequality is
\[
\frac{9n^{1-1/m}}m
\geq
\log\bigl(8m(m-1)n^2\bigr).
\]
The difference between the two sides is increasing in $n$ for
$n\geq2^{m-1}$, because its derivative has the sign of
\[
9(m-1)n^{1-1/m}-2m^2>0.
\]
At the endpoint it is positive for $m=3,4$ by direct substitution.  For
$m\geq5$, the left side is greater than $9\cdot2^{m-2}/m$, while the right
side is less than $2.6m$; the inequality follows from
$2^{m-2}>(2.6/9)m^2$, first at $m=5$ and then by induction.  This proves
\eqref{eq:root-spectral-app}.

Hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:root-uniformity-app}
\left|P_j(t)-\frac1m\right|
\leq
\frac Em
\qquad(t=s,s+1).
\end{equation}
Let
\[
C=c_{m-1}(s),
\qquad
r=\frac{c_{m-2}(s)}{c_{m-1}(s)}.
\]
From \eqref{eq:root-uniformity-app},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:root-leading-ratio-app}
r
=
\rho\frac{P_{m-2}(s)}{P_{m-1}(s)},
\qquad
|r-\rho|
\leq
\frac{2\rho E}{1-E}
<
\frac{\rho}{3mn^2}.
\end{equation}
Multiplication by $x+1$ in the quotient ring shows that the leading
coefficient of $F_{s+1}$ is $C(1+r)$.

The lower base-$b$ digits cause a smaller error.  Equation
\eqref{eq:root-uniformity-app} implies, for $0\leq j<m-1$ and
$t\in\{s,s+1\}$,
\[
\frac{c_j(t)}{c_{m-1}(t)}
=
\rho^{m-1-j}\frac{P_j(t)}{P_{m-1}(t)}
<2n.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\delta_t
:=
\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}
\frac{c_j(t)}{c_{m-1}(t)b^{m-1-j}}
<
2n\sum_{r=1}^{m-1}b^{-r}
<
\frac{2n}{b-1}
\leq
\frac{4n}{b}.
\]
Thus
\[
F_s(b)=Cb^{m-1}(1+\delta_s),
\]
\[
F_{s+1}(b)=C(1+r)b^{m-1}(1+\delta_{s+1}),
\]
where
\[
0\leq\delta_s,\delta_{s+1}<\frac{4n}{b}.
\]
Moreover, \eqref{eq:root-leading-ratio-app} gives $1+r<3n$.  Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|
\frac{F_{s+1}(b)}{F_s(b)}-(1+\rho)
\right|
&\leq
|r-\rho|
+(1+r)\frac{|\delta_{s+1}-\delta_s|}{1+\delta_s}\\
&<
\frac{\rho}{3mn^2}+\frac{24n^2}{b}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $s\geq12$, $b=n^s\geq n^{12}$, and $m\leq n$,
\[
\frac{24n^2}{b}<\frac{\rho}{3mn^2}.
\]
It follows that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:root-ratio-error-app}
\left|
\frac{F_{s+1}(b)}{F_s(b)}-(1+\rho)
\right|
<
\frac{2\rho}{3mn^2}.
\end{equation}

Let $q=\left\lfloor \rho\right\rfloor$.  Since $n$ is not a perfect $m$-th power,
\[
1\leq n-q^m
=(\rho-q)\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\rho^{m-1-j}q^j
\leq m\rho^{m-1}(\rho-q),
\]
so $\rho-q\geq\rho/(mn)$.  Similarly,
\[
1\leq(q+1)^m-n
=(q+1-\rho)\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(q+1)^{m-1-j}\rho^j.
\]
Since $q+1\leq2\rho$ and $2^{m-1}\leq n$, the final sum is at most
$m2^{m-1}\rho^{m-1}\leq mn^2/\rho$.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:root-gap-app}
\min(\rho-q,q+1-\rho)
\geq
\frac{\rho}{mn^2}.
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:root-encoding-app}, \eqref{eq:root-ratio-error-app}, and
\eqref{eq:root-gap-app} gives
\[
q
<
\frac{(b+1)^{s+1}\bmod D}{(b+1)^s\bmod D}-1
<
q+1.
\]
Hence
\[
\left\lfloor 
\frac{(b+1)^{s+1}\bmod D}{(b+1)^s\bmod D}
\right\rfloor
=q+1,
\]
and \eqref{eq:integer-root-term} follows.

Write
\[
a=\left\lfloor \log_2 n\right\rfloor,
\qquad
x=\log_2 n,
\qquad
\alpha=n^{1/k},
\qquad
\beta=2^{1/k},
\]
and set
\[
R=\frac{\alpha-1}{\beta-1}.
\]
Since $\alpha=\beta^x$ and $t\mapsto\beta^t$ is convex,
\[
R\geq x.
\]
Let $y=(\log n)/k$ and $z=(\log2)/k$.  Using
$e^y-1\leq ye^y$ and $e^z-1\geq z$,
\[
0\leq R-x
\leq
x(e^y-1)
\leq
\frac{(\log n)^2e^y}{k\log2}
<
\frac{2}{3n\log2}.
\]
Here $(\log n)^2<n$ for $n\geq2$, and $y<\log2$.

Because $2^{a+1}$ and $n$ are distinct integers,
$2^{a+1}\geq n+1$, so
\[
a+1-x
=
\frac{\log(2^{a+1}/n)}{\log2}
\geq
\frac{\log(1+1/n)}{\log2}
\geq
\frac{1}{(n+1)\log2}.
\]
For $n\geq3$ it follows that
\[
a+1-R
>
\frac{n-2}{3n(n+1)\log2}
\geq
\frac{\log2}{3n^2}
=
\frac{\log2}{k}.
\]
The last inequality follows from $(\log2)^2<1/2$.

For the lower boundary, if $n$ is not a power of $2$, then
\[
x-a
\geq
\frac{\log(1+1/n)}{\log2}
>
\frac{\log2}{k}.
\]
If $n=2^a$ and $a\geq2$, then
\[
R=1+\beta+\cdots+\beta^{a-1},
\]
so
\[
R-a\geq\beta-1\geq\frac{\log2}{k}.
\]
Thus for every $n\geq3$, the number $R$ lies in the interval
$[a,a+1)$ and is at distance at least
\[
\delta=\frac{\log2}{k}
\]
from either endpoint.  For $n=2$, one has $R=1$ exactly.

Now put
\[
\widetilde\alpha=\frac uQ,
\qquad
\widetilde\beta=\frac vQ,
\qquad
\widetilde R
=
\frac{u-Q}{v-Q}.
\]
The definitions of $u$ and $v$ give
\[
0\leq\alpha-\widetilde\alpha<\frac1Q,
\qquad
0\leq\beta-\widetilde\beta<\frac1Q.
\]
Since $\beta-1\geq(\log2)/k$ and the chosen $Q$ satisfies
$Q(\beta-1)>2$, we have
\[
\widetilde\beta-1\geq\frac{\beta-1}{2}>0.
\]
For $n\geq3$, using $R<a+1$ and $a+2\leq n+1$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\widetilde R-R|
&<
\frac{R+1}{Q(\widetilde\beta-1)}\\
&\leq
\frac{2k(n+1)}{Q\log2}
=
\frac{1}{8k\log2}
<
\frac{\log2}{k}
=\delta,
\end{aligned}
\]
where the last inequality uses $\log2>1/2$.  Hence
$\left\lfloor \widetilde R\right\rfloor=a$.  For $n=2$, the two radicands are equal, so
$u=v$ and $\widetilde R=1$.  This proves \eqref{eq:binary-log-root}.

It remains only to justify the arithmetic-term claim.  Since $Q\geq2$,
\[
k\leq\left\lfloor \log_2(nQ^k)\right\rfloor+1,
\qquad
k\leq\left\lfloor \log_2(2Q^k)\right\rfloor+1.
\]
Neither $nQ^k$ nor $2Q^k$ is a perfect $k$-th power.  Indeed, a rational
$k$-th root of the integer factor $n$ would have to be an integer, while
$k>\log_2 n$ rules out $n=d^k$ with $d\geq2$; the same argument applies to
$2$.  Thus \cref{thm:integer-root} applies to both $u$ and $v$, and
substitution into \eqref{eq:binary-log-root} gives the asserted arithmetic
term.
