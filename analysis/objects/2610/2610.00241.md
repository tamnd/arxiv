<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For every \(L>0\), there is a datum
\(u_0\in C^\infty(\mathbb T_L^2)\) whose solution of
\eqref{eq:nls} belongs to
\[
 C^\infty(\mathbb R\times\mathbb T_L^2)
 \cap\bigcap_{k\ge0}C(\mathbb R;H^k(\mathbb T_L^2))
\]
and has \(M(u)=m\), \(E(u)=0\).
The datum and the solution are invariant under the two coordinate
reflections and the exchange of the coordinates.
There are smooth functions \(\lambda(t)>0\) and \(\gamma(t)\),
defined for all sufficiently large positive times, such that
\begin{equation}\label{intro:scale}
 \frac{L}{\lambda(t)}
 =2\log t+\frac{11}{2}\log\log t+O(1)
                  \qquad(t\to+\infty)
\end{equation}
and, for every fixed integer \(k\ge0\),
\begin{equation}\label{intro:profile}
 \|u(t)-e^{i\gamma(t)}\mathcal Q_{\lambda(t)}\|_{H^k}
                         \longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for every fixed real \(s>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{intro:growth}
 \|u(t)\|_{H^s(\mathbb T_L^2)}
 \sim \|Q\|_{\dot H^s(\mathbb R^2)}
               \left(\frac{2\log t}{L}\right)^s .
\end{equation}

\label{thm:uniqueness}
Let \(u_1,u_2\) be smooth solutions of \eqref{eq:nls}, even in
each coordinate, with mass \(m\) and energy zero. Suppose that,
on positive-time tails, both admit
\eqref{intro:class-decomposition}--\eqref{intro:class-constraints}
about the same centre, with \(D(t)\to\infty\),
\[
 b/B_0(D)=1+O(D^{-1}),\qquad
 \|\varepsilon(t)\|_{H^k(\mathbb T_{D(t)}^2)}
       \le C_k B_0(D(t))/D(t)
\]
for every fixed integer \(k\ge0\).
Then there are constants \(\theta,\tau\in\mathbb R\) such that
\[
 u_1(t)=e^{i\theta}u_2(t+\tau)
\]
on their common translated lifespan.

\label{prof:overlap}
Define
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 \Phi_D(y)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z^2}Q(y-Dk),\\
 A_D=\sum_{k\ne0}\int Q(y)Q(y-Dk)\,dy,\qquad
 C_D=\sum_{k\ne0}\int Q^3(y)Q(y-Dk)\,dy.
 \end{gathered}
\]
For \(j=0,1,2\),
\begin{equation}\label{prof:overlap-asymptotic}
 \begin{split}
 \partial_D^j A_D
 &=(-1)^j2qD^{1/2}e^{-D}(1+O(D^{-1})),\\
 \partial_D^j C_D
 &=(-1)^j4qD^{-1/2}e^{-D}(1+O(D^{-1})).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{prof:unfolding}
 \int_{\mathbb T_D^2}\Phi_D^2=m+A_D,\quad
 \int_{\mathbb T_D^2}|\nabla\Phi_D|^2=m+C_D-A_D,\quad
 \int_{\mathbb T_D^2}\Phi_D^4=2m+4C_D+\mathcal R_D,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal R_D\ge0\) and, for every \(\eta>0\),
\[
 |\partial_D^j\mathcal R_D|\le C_{\eta,j}e^{-(2-\eta)D},
 \qquad j=0,1,2.
\]

Here \(f*g\) denotes convolution on \(\mathbb R^2\),
\((f*g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}f(x-y)g(y)\,dy\), and \(e_1=(1,0)\).
We use \(K_\nu\) for the modified Bessel function of the second kind
of order \(\nu\).
Put \(F=Q^3\) and \(P_1=F*F\).  The kernels of
\((-\Delta+1)^{-1}\) and \((-\Delta+1)^{-2}\) on \(\mathbb R^2\) are
\[
 G(r)=\frac{K_0(r)}{2\pi},\qquad
 H(r)=\frac{rK_1(r)}{4\pi}.
\]
For the second formula, differentiate the first resolvent kernel with
respect to the coefficient of its zeroth-order term.  Since \(Q=G*F\)
and \(F\) is even,
\[
 \int Q(y)Q(y-re_1)\,dy=(H*P_1)(re_1),\qquad
 \int Q^3(y)Q(y-re_1)\,dy=(G*P_1)(re_1).
\]
The required asymptotics follow from the heat-kernel formulas
\[
 G(r)=\frac1{4\pi}\int_0^\infty t^{-1}e^{-t-r^2/(4t)}\,dt,
 \qquad
 H(r)=\frac1{4\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-t-r^2/(4t)}\,dt,
 \qquad r>0.
\]
Indeed, these are obtained by integrating the planar heat kernel
against \(e^{-t}\) and \(te^{-t}\), respectively.  Set
\(t=(r/2)e^v\), combine the contributions from \(v\) and \(-v\),
and then put \(z=2\sqrt r\sinh(v/2)\).  This gives
\[
 \begin{split}
 G(r)&=\frac{e^{-r}r^{-1/2}}{2\pi}
       \int_0^\infty e^{-z^2/2}a_0(z^2/r)\,dz,\\
 H(r)&=\frac{e^{-r}r^{1/2}}{4\pi}
       \int_0^\infty e^{-z^2/2}a_1(z^2/r)\,dz,
 \end{split}
\]
where
\[
 a_0(w)=(1+w/4)^{-1/2},\qquad
 a_1(w)=(1+w/2)(1+w/4)^{-1/2},\qquad w\ge0.
\]
Both amplitudes equal one at zero and have bounded first and second
derivatives on \([0,\infty)\).  Thus, after multiplication by
\(\sqrt{2/\pi}\), each Gaussian integral equals \(1+E_j(r)\), where
\[
 |\partial_r^k E_j(r)|\le C_k r^{-1-k},
 \qquad r\ge1,\quad j=0,1,\quad k=0,1,2.
\]
For \(k=0\), use \(|a_j(w)-1|\le Cw\); for \(k=1,2\),
differentiate under the integral and use the finite Gaussian moments.
Since \(\int_0^\infty e^{-z^2/2}\,dz=\sqrt{\pi/2}\), we obtain
\[
 G(r)=\frac{r^{-1/2}e^{-r}}{\sqrt{8\pi}}(1+O(r^{-1})),
 \qquad
 H(r)=\frac{r^{1/2}e^{-r}}{4\sqrt{2\pi}}(1+O(r^{-1})).
\]
These expansions can be differentiated twice.  Convolution with \(F\)
in the first formula shows that \(c_Q=I_Q/\sqrt{8\pi}\), while
\(\int P_1(y)e^{y_1}\,dy=I_Q^2\).  It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{prof:single-overlap}
 \begin{split}
 \partial_r^j\int Q(y)Q(y-re_1)\,dy
 &=(-1)^j\frac q2 r^{1/2}e^{-r}(1+O(r^{-1})),\\
 \partial_r^j\int Q^3(y)Q(y-re_1)\,dy
 &=(-1)^jq r^{-1/2}e^{-r}(1+O(r^{-1})),
 \qquad j\le2.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Here differentiation of the error is justified at the convolution level.
The number \(\epsilon>0\) used to split the integral is fixed
and sufficiently small.
Indeed, \(F,P_1\), and their fixed derivatives decay like
\(e^{-(3-\eta)|y|}\).  On \(|y|\le\epsilon r\), expansion of
\(|re_1-y|\) leaves an integrable error bounded by \(r^{-1}\) times a
polynomial in \(|y|\) and its exponential moment.  The complement is
exponentially smaller.  Near the kernel singularity one first inserts a
smooth cutoff and moves derivatives to \(P_1\).  Its derivatives there
are exponentially small, and the kernels themselves are locally
integrable.  This argument applies to each of the two derivatives in
\eqref{prof:single-overlap}.

Radiality reduces the sums to separations \(D|k|\).  The four terms
\(|k|=1\) give \eqref{prof:overlap-asymptotic}; all other terms have
\(|k|\ge\sqrt2\).  Their differentiated series converge absolutely and
are exponentially smaller.

The mass identity in \eqref{prof:unfolding} follows by unfolding one
periodic cell.  For a nonzero shift \(z\), the equation for \(Q\) gives
\[
 \int\nabla Q(y)\cdot\nabla Q(y-z)\,dy
 =\int Q^3(y)Q(y-z)\,dy-\int Q(y)Q(y-z)\,dy,
\]
which proves the gradient identity.  In the quartic expansion, terms
with all four centers equal contribute \(2m\); the terms with three
equal centers contribute \(4C_D\).  The remaining terms are positive.
Their four centers can be paired into two pairs of distinct centers,
so the sum of the distances from any integration point to those
centers is at least \(2D\).

After unfolding at one center, this lower bound gives the remainder
estimate.  More explicitly, each derivative in \(D\) inserts
derivatives of \(Q\) and polynomial weights in the lattice indices.
These weights can be absorbed into an arbitrarily small fraction of
the exponential decay.  A further small fraction is retained for
integration and summation, using
\[
 \sup_{D\ge1,\ y\in\mathbb R^2}
 \sum_{k\in\mathbb Z^2}e^{-c|y-Dk|}<\infty\qquad(c>0).
\]
The remaining exponential is bounded by \(e^{-(2-\eta)D}\).
This also supplies a common integrable majorant for the differentiated
series.

\label{prof:products}
For \(f_a=Q+a\rho\), set
\[
 Z_{D,b}f(y)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z^2}
 e^{-ib|y-Dk|^2/4}f(y-Dk),\qquad P_{D,b,a}=Z_{D,b}f_a.
\]
Suppose that \(|a|\le C D^{1/2}e^{-D}\) and
\(|b|\le C B_0(D)\).  For every fixed integer \(k\ge0\),
\begin{equation}\label{prof:cubic-products}
 \left\||P_{D,b,a}|^2P_{D,b,a}
 -\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb Z^2}
 e^{-ib|y-D\ell|^2/4}f_a(y-D\ell)^3
 \right\|_{H^k(\mathbb T_D^2)}
 \le C_k R(D).
\end{equation}
Fixed spatial and parameter derivatives of a product involving
distinct image centers are bounded by \(C D^p e^{-D}\), in the
corresponding fixed Sobolev norm or unfolded scalar integral.

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Every mixed cubic term has at least two distinct centers.  For its
largest contribution, two factors are near one center and the third
is near a center at distance \(r\ge D\).  The estimate reduces to
\[
 \|Q(\cdot)^2Q(\cdot-re)\|_{H^k}\le C_k r^{-1/2}e^{-r},
 \qquad |e|=1.
\]
On \(|y|\le r/2\), the distant factor is bounded by
\(C r^{-1/2}e^{-r+|y|}\), and the square of the other two
factors times \(e^{2|y|}\) is integrable.  Near the other center,
or away from both centers, the repeated factors yield an additional
exponential gain.  The same proof applies to derivatives, since
fixed derivatives of \(Q\) have the same exponential tail.
Terms with three distinct centers have a larger total separation.
Summing the resulting bounds over the lattice gives
\eqref{prof:cubic-products}.  Replacing a factor by \(a\rho\)
introduces only a polynomial tail and the exponentially small factor
\(a\), so its contribution is smaller.

A derivative of a phase or of a center inserts a polynomial in the
local coordinates and the separation.  Integrating along the segment
between two centers produces at most another polynomial factor.
Thus fixed parameter derivatives satisfy the weaker, but sufficient,
bound \(C D^p e^{-D}\).  Absolute convergence of the lattice series
justifies taking these derivatives before integration.  Finally,
\(bD^2\to0\), and fixed spatial derivatives of the chirps preserve
all these estimates.

\label{prof:mass}
There is a fixed \(\delta>0\) such that, for \(D\) large and
\(|b|\le\delta\), there is a unique small real smooth function
\(a=a(D,b)\), even in \(b\), such that
\begin{equation}\label{prof:exact-mass}
 \int_{\mathbb T_D^2}|P_{D,b,a(D,b)}|^2=m.
\end{equation}
On this fixed parameter neighborhood,
\(|a(D,b)|\le C D^{1/2}e^{-D}\), and every fixed parameter
derivative is bounded by a polynomial in \(D\) times \(e^{-D}\).
In the narrower region \(|b|\le C_*B_0(D)\), for any fixed
\(C_*>0\), it satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{prof:a-asymptotic}
 a(D,b)=-\frac{A_D}{2c_0}+O(D^p e^{-2D})
 =-\alpha D^{1/2}e^{-D}(1+O(D^{-1})).
\end{equation}
For any fixed finite collection of derivatives,
\[
 |\partial_D^j a|\le C_jD^{p_j}e^{-D},\qquad
 |\partial_ba|\le C|b|D^p e^{-D},\qquad
 |\partial_D^j\partial_b^\ell a|\le C_{j,\ell}D^{p_{j,\ell}}e^{-D}
 \quad(\ell\ge2).
\]
The first two \(D\)-derivatives of the first equality in
\eqref{prof:a-asymptotic} have the corresponding differentiated error
bounds.

Unfolding the mass gives the diagonal term
\(m+2c_0a+a^2\|\rho\|_2^2\).  Each off-diagonal term contains
the cosine of a phase difference, after pairing it with its complex
conjugate.  The absolute values of the off-diagonal terms are bounded
by the corresponding unchirped overlaps.  Thus for \(|b|\le\delta\)
their sum is \(O(D^{1/2}e^{-D}+|a|D^p e^{-D})\), and the
mass derivative in \(a\) remains bounded away from zero, uniformly on a
fixed sufficiently small \(a\)-interval.  The implicit function
theorem gives the stated extension and the bound
\(|a|\le C D^{1/2}e^{-D}\).  Fixed derivatives of these
off-diagonal terms insert only polynomial weights in the overlap
integrals, even when \(b\) lies in the fixed interval.

At \(a=b=0\), the off-diagonal sum is \(A_D\).  Taylor's formula,
the weighted-overlap proof of Lemma~\ref{prof:products}, and
\eqref{prof:rho-mass} therefore give
\[
 \int |P_{D,b,a}|^2
 =m+A_D+2c_0a+
 O\bigl(a^2+|a|D^p e^{-D}+b^2D^p e^{-D}\bigr).
\]
Conjugation gives evenness in \(b\).  In the narrower region
\(|b|\le C_*B_0(D)\), substituting the bound for \(a\) into this
expansion yields the first equality in \eqref{prof:a-asymptotic}.
The second follows from Lemma~\ref{prof:overlap}.

All derivatives claimed in the statement follow by differentiating
this exact mass equation.  Its \(a\)-derivative remains bounded away
from zero, whereas fixed derivatives of the off-diagonal sums are
bounded by the weighted estimates of Lemma~\ref{prof:products}.
In particular their first \(b\)-derivative vanishes at \(b=0\),
and their second \(b\)-derivative is \(O(D^p e^{-D})\).
This gives the extra factor \(|b|\) in \(\partial_ba\).
Repeated implicit differentiation proves the remaining bounds;
no differentiation of an unspecified asymptotic error is required.

\label{prof:energy-branch}
For all sufficiently large \(D\), the equation
\[
 E_D(P_{D,b})=0
\]
has a unique solution \(b=B(D)\) in
\([B_0(D)/2,2B_0(D)]\).  It is smooth, positive, and
\begin{equation}\label{prof:B-asymptotic}
 B(D)=B_0(D)(1+O(D^{-1})),\qquad
 \frac{B'(D)}{B(D)}=-\frac12+O(D^{-1}),\qquad
 |\partial_D^jB(D)|\le C_jD^{p_j}B_0(D)
\end{equation}
for each fixed \(j\).  Uniformly for \(b\in[B_0(D)/4,4B_0(D)]\),
\begin{equation}\label{prof:energy-derivative}
 \partial_b E_D(P_{D,b})
 =2c_0b+O(|b|D^p e^{-D})\ge cB_0(D).
\end{equation}
In particular \(\lambda^{-1}P_{D,B(D)}(x/\lambda)\), with
\(\lambda=L/D\), has mass \(m\) and energy zero on the physical torus.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
First consider the real profile.  The normalized periodic sum
\(\varphi_D=(m/(m+A_D))^{1/2}\Phi_D\) has mass \(m\).
The identities \eqref{prof:unfolding} give the exact formula
\[
 E_D(\varphi_D)
 =-\frac{2mA_D^2+2m(m-A_D)C_D+m^2\mathcal R_D}
         {4(m+A_D)^2}
 =-\frac12C_D+O(e^{-3D/2}).
\]
The larger term \(A_D\) cancels at first order.  The error and its
first two \(D\)-derivatives satisfy the displayed bound, by
Lemma~\ref{prof:overlap}.

The difference \(h_D=P_{D,0}-\varphi_D\) is
\(O(D^p e^{-D})\) in every fixed Sobolev norm.  Since both functions
have mass \(m\), expansion of their actions yields
\[
 E_D(P_{D,0})-E_D(\varphi_D)
 =\langle S_D'(\varphi_D),h_D\rangle+O(\|h_D\|_{H^1}^2+
 \|h_D\|_{H^1}^4).
\]
The mixed-product estimates and the mass normalization give
\(\|S_D'(\varphi_D)\|_2\le C D^p e^{-D}\).
Consequently this difference, and its fixed derivatives when
required, is \(O(D^p e^{-2D})\).

The energy is even in \(b\).  Its diagonal phase contribution is
\[
 \frac{b^2}{8}\int_{\mathbb R^2}|y|^2(Q+a\rho)^2
 =c_0b^2+O(|a|b^2).
\]
Every other \(b\)-dependent term has second derivative bounded by
\(C D^p e^{-D}\), including the dependence through \(a(D,b)\).
This follows directly by differentiating the unfolded cross terms
and the exact mass equation.  We have proved
\begin{equation}\label{prof:energy-expansion}
 E_D(P_{D,b})
 =c_0b^2-\frac12C_D+O(e^{-3D/2}+b^2D^p e^{-D}),
\end{equation}
as well as \eqref{prof:energy-derivative}.  The same expansion holds
after the \(D\)-derivatives used below.

At \(b=B_0/2\) the leading energy is negative, whereas at \(b=2B_0\)
it is positive.  The intermediate value theorem and
\eqref{prof:energy-derivative} give the unique root.
The identity \(c_0B_0^2=2qR\), together with
\eqref{prof:overlap-asymptotic}, proves the first assertion in
\eqref{prof:B-asymptotic}.  Implicit differentiation of the exact
energy equation gives
\[
 2c_0BB'=\frac12 C_D'+O(D^p e^{-3D/2}),
\]
and hence \(B'/B=-1/2+O(D^{-1})\).  Higher derivatives are obtained
from the same equation and the differentiated image sums.
They are bounded by a polynomial times \(B_0\).
Finally, critical scaling preserves mass and multiplies energy by
\(\lambda^{-2}\), so the physical assertions follow.

 \ge c\|\varepsilon\|_{H^1}^2
 -C\big(&|\langle\varepsilon,Z_{D,b}Q\rangle|^2

       &+|\langle\varepsilon,T_2\rangle|^2\big).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}

The Euclidean ground-state coercivity estimate is
\[
 \begin{split}
 \langle L_+\operatorname{Re}z,\operatorname{Re}z\rangle
 +\langle L_-\operatorname{Im}z,\operatorname{Im}z\rangle
 \ge c\|z\|_{H^1}^2-C\big(&|\langle z,Q\rangle|^2
 +|\langle z,|y|^2Q\rangle|^2\\
 &+|\langle z,i\rho\rangle|^2\big)
 \end{split}
\]
for reflection-even \(z\); the usual additional translation
pairings vanish by symmetry.  This is the standard constrained
ground-state estimate; see \cite[(1.24)]{MartelRaphael18}.

We transfer it to the torus by localization.  Choose a large fixed
radius \(r\), then \(D>8r\), and an even quadratic partition of
unity \(\chi_r^2+\zeta_r^2=1\), with \(\chi_r=1\) on the ball
of radius \(r\), \(\chi_r=0\) outside the ball of radius \(2r\),
and gradients bounded by \(C/r\).  Apply the Euclidean estimate
to \(\chi_r\varepsilon\), extended by zero.  Its pairings differ
from the periodized pairings by at most
\(o_{r\to\infty}(1)\|\varepsilon\|_2\), uniformly for \(D>8r\).
In the core, replacing \(\Phi_D\) by \(Q\) gives an exponentially
small error in \(D\).  On the support of \(\zeta_r\), the
periodic potential is \(O(e^{-cr})\), so the free quadratic form
is positive.  The localization identity loses at most
\(Cr^{-2}\|\varepsilon\|_2^2\).  First choose \(r\) large and then
\(D\) large to absorb these errors.

The estimates

 \|Z_{D,b}f-Z_{D,0}f\|_2\le C_f|b|,\qquad

\]
follow from \(|e^{-ib|y|^2/4}-1|\le |b||y|^2/4\) and the

pairings by a quantity tending to zero.  This proves the general

 \langle\varepsilon,Z_{D,b}Q\rangle
 =-\frac12\|\varepsilon\|_2^2

\eqref{mod:mass-identity} equals
\(-a\langle z,Z_{D,b}\rho\rangle\), which is absorbed in the

On the physical torus, put
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 J=-i,\qquad g_0=V_{D,b},\qquad g_j=I_DT_j\quad(1\le j\le3),\\
 W=\{z:\langle z,g_j\rangle=0,\ 0\le j\le3\},\qquad
 S=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{Jg_j:0\le j\le3\}.
 \end{gathered}
\]
For \(D\) large these spaces give a direct sum \(W\oplus S\).
Let \(\Pi\) be the projection onto \(W\) along \(S\).
In operator expressions, \(I\) is the identity and \(\Pi^*\)
is the adjoint with respect to the real \(L^2\) pairing.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{mod:projection-identities}
 \Pi^2=\Pi,\qquad \Pi J=J\Pi^*,\qquad
 (\Pi J)^*=-\Pi J,\qquad \Pi\Pi_s\Pi=0.
\end{equation}
The last identity holds along any differentiable parameter curve.
The projection is uniformly bounded in every fixed scaled Sobolev
space, and
\begin{equation}\label{mod:projection-derivative}
 \|\Pi_s z\|_{k,\lambda}
 \le C_k\bigl(|\lambda_s/\lambda|+|b_s|\bigr)\|z\|_2.
\end{equation}
If \(w\) satisfies the mass and coordinate conditions above and
\(y=\Pi w\), \(n=(I-\Pi)w\), then
\begin{equation}\label{mod:normal-part}
 \|n\|_{k,\lambda}\le C_k\|w\|_2^2,\qquad
 \|w\|_{k,\lambda}\le C_k\|y\|_{k,\lambda}
\end{equation}
when \(\|w\|_2\) is small.

In \(DN(V)\), \(N\) denotes the cubic map \(z\mapsto |z|^2z\),
also written \(\mathcal N\) below, and \(D\) denotes its real
Fr\'echet derivative.

For
\begin{equation}\label{mod:physical-hessian}
 \mathcal L=-\lambda^2\Delta+1-\lambda^2 DN(V),\qquad
 DN(V)z=2|V|^2z+V^2\overline z,
\end{equation}
one has
\[
 c\|z\|_{1,\lambda}^2\le\langle\mathcal Lz,z\rangle
 \le C\|z\|_{1,\lambda}^2\qquad(z\in W).

 \|\Pi J\mathcal L n\|_{k,\lambda}
 \le C_k B_0(D)\|n\|_2\qquad(n\in S).

Subtracting the profile equation from NLS, on the fixed torus,
gives
\begin{equation}\label{mod:w-equation}
 iw_s=\mathcal Lw+\omega w-f-I_D\mathcal N_2(P,\varepsilon).
\end{equation}
Now differentiate \(\langle w,t_j\rangle=0\), use the real
self-adjointness of the Hessian, and only then rescale the
integrals.  The result is the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{mod:projection-equation}
 \langle\mathcal E,iT_j\rangle
 =\langle\varepsilon,\mathcal L_P(iT_j)+K_j+\omega iT_j\rangle
                 -\langle\mathcal N_2(P,\varepsilon),iT_j\rangle.
\end{equation}
All integrations by parts here are on the fixed periodic torus.

We spell out the cancellation in its rows.  The defect-independent
part of \(K_j\) is
\[
 K_j^0=bZ_{D,b}\Lambda\psi_j
       -i(a+b^2)Z_{D,b}(|y|^2\psi_j/4).
\]
For a single image and any localized complex \(z\),
\[
 \mathcal L_{e^{-ib|y|^2/4}f_a}
       (e^{-ib|y|^2/4}z)
 =e^{-ib|y|^2/4}\left[
 (-\Delta+1-2f_a^2)z-f_a^2\overline z
 +ib\Lambda z+\frac{b^2|y|^2}{4}z\right].
\]
Taking \(z=i\psi_j\), the term \(ib\Lambda(i\psi_j)\)
cancels the first term of \(K_j^0\).  The two \(b^2\) terms
also cancel.  Therefore, with
\[
 C_1=-4T_3,\qquad C_2=-T_1/4,\qquad C_3=2Z_{D,b}Q,
\]
the actual periodic row errors satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{mod:row-cancellation}
 W_j:=\mathcal L_P(iT_j)+K_j^0-C_j,\qquad
 \|W_j\|_2+\|\partial_bW_j\|_2\le C D^p e^{-D}.
\end{equation}
For completeness, after the cancellation the single-image
expression is
\[
 Z_{D,b}\left[
 (-\Delta+1-2f_a^2)(i\psi_j)
 -f_a^2\overline{i\psi_j}
 -\frac{ia|y|^2}{4}\psi_j\right].
\]
At \(a=0\), \eqref{prof:kernel} and \eqref{prof:rho}
give \(C_j\) exactly.  Replacing \(Q\) by \(f_a\) costs
\(O(a)\); the remaining periodic terms are products with
distinct centers and are controlled by
Lemma~\ref{prof:products}.  Their fixed \(b\)-derivatives have
the same polynomial-exponential bound.  This proves
\eqref{mod:row-cancellation}.

We can now solve the finite system explicitly.  In
\(K_j+\omega iT_j=K_j^0+\mu K_j^\mu+
\omega K_j^\omega+\nu K_j^\nu\), the coefficients are
\[
 K_j^\mu=-Z_{D,b}\Lambda\psi_j,\quad
 K_j^\omega=iT_j,\quad
 K_j^\nu=-iZ_{D,b}(|y|^2\psi_j/4).
\]
Set
\begin{equation}\label{mod:finite-matrix}
 A_{j\ell}(D,b,\varepsilon)
 =\langle R_\ell,iT_j\rangle
       -\langle\varepsilon,K_j^\ell\rangle,
 \qquad \ell\in\{\mu,\omega,\nu\}.
\end{equation}
At \(\varepsilon=0\), this matrix converges to
\eqref{mod:coordinate-matrix}, so its inverse is uniformly
bounded for small \(N\).  The exact right-hand side is
\begin{equation}\label{mod:finite-numerator}
 \begin{split}
 F_j(D,b,\varepsilon)={}&
 \langle\varepsilon,W_j\rangle
 -\langle\mathcal N_2(P,\varepsilon),iT_j\rangle
 -\langle\mathcal E_0,iT_j\rangle\\
 &-\mathbf1_{j=3}
 \left(\|\varepsilon\|_2^2+
                   2a\langle\varepsilon,Z_{D,b}\rho\rangle\right).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf1_{j=3}\) is one when \(j=3\) and zero otherwise.
We write \(F=(F_1,F_2,F_3)^{\mathsf T}\) for the right-hand side vector.
The final term is \eqref{mod:mass-identity}; it is essential
because \(C_3=2Z_{D,b}Q\) is not one of the imposed tests.
The terms \(C_1,C_2\) vanish by orthogonality.
Thus \eqref{mod:projection-equation} is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{mod:smooth-map}
 A(D,b,\varepsilon)(\mu,\omega,\nu)^{\mathsf T}
       =(F_1,F_2,F_3)^{\mathsf T}.
\end{equation}

Uniform \(H^1\)-to-\(L^4,L^6\) embeddings give
\[
 |\langle\mathcal N_2(P,\varepsilon),iT_j\rangle|
 \le C(N^2+N^3).
\]
Equations \eqref{mod:E0}, \eqref{mod:row-cancellation}, and
\eqref{mod:finite-numerator} actually give the stronger bound
\[
 |\mu|+|\omega|+|\nu|
 \le C\bigl(R(D)+D^p e^{-D}N+N^2\bigr).
\]
In particular \eqref{mod:estimate} follows.  Substitution in
\eqref{mod:affine-residual} proves
\eqref{mod:full-residual}.

Finally, \eqref{mod:finite-matrix} and
\eqref{mod:finite-numerator} define smooth maps on an \(H^1\)
neighborhood, even without imposing the constraints.  Their
restriction to the constraint surface is the vector obtained
from the PDE.  At \(\varepsilon=0\), the numerator consists
only of the pairings of \(-\mathcal E_0\) with \(iT_j\).
Differentiating these unfolded scalar pairings and the uniformly
invertible matrix gives the stated bounds for \(\mathbf m^0\).
The derivative estimates of Lemmas~\ref{prof:mass} and
\ref{prof:products} justify every such fixed differentiation.

Substitution of \(u=e^{i\gamma}(V+w)\) into the equation gives

\[
 D\ge D_0,\qquad \tfrac12B(D)<b<2B(D),\qquad
 X_1<K_1B(D)/D.

\[
 |\mu|+|\nu|+|\omega|\le CR,\qquad

the contributions \(b\|w\|_{1,\lambda}/R\) and

\(CK_1/D\) and \(CK_1^2/D^2\), respectively.

\(\kappa+|\omega|\le CB\) and

 |(X_1)_D|\le \frac CD X_1+
        \frac C{DB}X_1^2+C\frac BD,\qquad X_1(D_n)=0.

Backward Gronwall therefore gives

 X_1(D)\le C\int_D^{D_n}

 \int_D^\infty(r/D)^a B(r)r^{-p}\,dr
       \le C_{a,p,D_0}B(D)D^{-p},\qquad D\ge D_0.
\end{equation}
This follows directly from
\(B(r)\asymp r^{-1/4}e^{-r/2}\).

\[
 X_1(D)\le C_*\left(1+\frac{K_1^2}{D_0^2}\right)\frac{B(D)}D .

First take \(K_1>4C_*\), and then increase \(D_0\). This improves

 |b-B(D)|\le

Increasing \(D_0\) again keeps \(b\) strictly inside the factor-two

criterion rules out a finite endpoint before the solution reaches

compact scale interval. The backwards crossing occurs in finite time.
Absorbing the graph-norm equivalence into \(K\) proves the proposition.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{glob:continuation}
Every \(H^3(\mathbb T_L^2)\) datum with \(0<M\le m\) and
\(E\le0\) has a solution global in both time directions.
If the datum is smooth, the solution is smooth at every finite time.

<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
First, boundedness of \(H^1\) on a finite time interval prevents
\(H^3\) breakdown. Indeed, the periodic version of the logarithmic Sobolev
inequality \cite[Lemma~2]{BrezisGallouet80} and the tame cubic estimate give
\[
 \|u\|_\infty^2
 \le C_K\bigl(1+\log(2+\|u\|_{H^3})\bigr),\qquad
 \left|\frac{d}{dt}\|u\|_{H^3}\right|
       \le C\|u\|_\infty^2\|u\|_{H^3}
\]
whenever \(\|u\|_{H^1}\le K\). Equivalently,
\(\log(2+\|u\|_{H^3})\) satisfies a linear Gronwall inequality.
It cannot diverge in finite time. These estimates follow first
for smooth solutions and extend to \(H^3\) by approximation.

Suppose that a maximal \(H^3\) solution had a finite endpoint \(T\).
The preceding observation provides times \(t_n\to T\) with
\(\|\nabla u(t_n)\|_2\to\infty\).
Lemma~\ref{glob:concentration} excludes this if \(M<m\).
If \(M=m\), it gives a point \(p\) and a subsequence for which
\(V_p(t_n)\to0\). This contradicts Lemma~\ref{glob:moment}.
The same proof applies to either endpoint of the lifespan.

On each compact time interval the \(H^3\) norm is finite.
The tame estimates then propagate every higher Sobolev order of
the datum. The equation supplies the time derivatives, giving
joint smoothness in time and space.

Parameter differentiation of the absolutely convergent image sums gives,
at every fixed order,
\begin{equation}\label{uniq:profile-derivatives}
 \|\partial_b^rP\|_{H^k}
 +\sum_{j=1}^3\|\partial_b^rT_j\|_{H^k}\le C_{k,r},\qquad
 |a_b|\le C|b|D^pe^{-D}.
\end{equation}
Further fixed derivatives of \(a\) are bounded by polynomial multiples
of \(e^{-D}\).  Each differentiation of an image phase inserts a
polynomial weight in the distance to its own centre.  The diagonal
integrals are uniformly bounded, and the differentiated cross integrals
retain their exponential decay.  We abbreviate the stationary remainder \(r_{D,b}\) by \(r_b\).
Its subscript records the parameter; differentiation is written
as \(\partial_b r_b\).
Consequently the remainder
\begin{equation}\label{uniq:static-remainder}
 r_b=\mathcal S(P)-\frac{a+b^2}{4}T_1-bT_3
\end{equation}
satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{uniq:r-derivatives}
 \|r_b\|_{H^k}\le C_kR,\qquad
 \|\partial_b r_b\|_{H^k}\le C_kD^{p_k}e^{-D}.
\end{equation}
For instance differentiation of the central term \(ab\) costs \(a\),
which has this latter size.  These estimates follow by differentiating
the profile identities themselves.

Let \(E_D\) denote the dimensionless energy, and let
\(\mathfrak H_P(\varepsilon)\) be its relative action,
\[
 \mathfrak H_P(\varepsilon)
 =\frac12\int(|\nabla\varepsilon|^2+|\varepsilon|^2)
 -\frac14\int\bigl(|P+\varepsilon|^4-|P|^4
                  -4\operatorname{Re}(|P|^2P\overline\varepsilon)\bigr).
\]
Equal mass and \eqref{uniq:constraints} give the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{uniq:energy-identity}
 0=E_D(P)+\mathfrak H_P(\varepsilon)
                 +\langle r_b,\varepsilon\rangle.
\end{equation}
In particular the two leading terms removed in
\eqref{uniq:static-remainder} have zero pairing with the remainder.

The profile energy is even in \(b\).  Its diagonal chirp contribution is
\(b^2\int |y|^2(Q+a\rho)^2/8\); every other second \(b\) derivative
has exponentially small overlap coefficients.  Hence, with
\(F=C D^pe^{-D}\),
\begin{equation}\label{uniq:energy-b-derivative}
 \partial_bE_D(P)=2c_0b+O(|b|F)\ge cB.
\end{equation}
If \(X=\|\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2\|_{H^1}\), expansion of the
quartic relative action and the uniform embeddings on the expanding

\[

       -\mathfrak H_{P_2}(\varepsilon_2)|
 \le C N_*X+C N_*^2|b_1-b_2|,
\]

\[
 |\langle r_{b_1},\varepsilon_1\rangle
       -\langle r_{b_2},\varepsilon_2\rangle|

\]
Subtract \eqref{uniq:energy-identity}, use
\eqref{uniq:energy-b-derivative}, and absorb
\((CN_*^2+FN_*)|b_1-b_2|\) into \(cB|b_1-b_2|\).

points of the nonlinear constraint surface.

invertible.  We give \(X_D\cap H^1(\mathbb T_D^2)\) the \(H^1\) norm.
Derivatives in \(\eta\) are real Fr\'echet derivatives on this
space. In \(D_\eta\varepsilon-I\), \(I\) is its inclusion into
\(H^1\); the unlabelled norms of scalar \(\eta\)-derivatives below
are the corresponding operator norms.
For \(\eta\in X_D\), write
\[
 \varepsilon(b,\eta)=\eta+\sum_{\ell=0}^3c_\ell(b,\eta)h_\ell
\]
and impose \eqref{uniq:constraints}.  The finite-dimensional implicit
function theorem gives
\[
 \varepsilon(b,0)=0,\quad
 \|\varepsilon\|_{H^1}\asymp\|\eta\|_{H^1},\quad
 \|\partial_b\varepsilon\|_{H^1}\le C\|\eta\|_{H^1},
\]
and
\[
 \|D_\eta\varepsilon-I\|_{H^1\to H^1}
 \le C(|b-B|+\|\eta\|_{H^1}).
\]
The corrections have uniformly smooth finite-rank profiles.  On this

\(\mathcal G_D(b,\eta)\), satisfies
\[
 \partial_b\mathcal G_D
 =2c_0b+O(|b|F+N_*^2+FN_*),\qquad
 \|D_\eta\mathcal G_D\|\le C(N_*+R).
\]
It therefore selects a unique smooth branch with
\[
 |b(D,\eta)-B(D)|\le C\frac{N_*^2+RN_*}{B},\qquad
 \|D_\eta b(D,\eta)\|\le C\frac{N_*+R}{B}.
\]
Existence follows by changing the sign of \(\mathcal G_D\) across a
sufficiently large multiple of the first displayed interval; its
positive \(b\) derivative gives uniqueness.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{uniq:full-sequence}
Normalize the finite-terminal solutions of
Section~\ref{sec:construction} by assigning physical time zero to their
common outgoing scale section \(D_0\), and by making their modulation
phase zero at that section.  The entire resulting sequence of initial

<!-- proof-25: body_md -->
If the original member has phase \(\gamma_n(D_0)\), the phase
normalization means replacing it by
\(e^{-i\gamma_n(D_0)}u_n\).  This exact symmetry preserves its
equation, invariants, and all estimates.
The uniform bounds at every order proved in
Section~\ref{sec:construction} make the normalized initial data
precompact in each fixed Sobolev space.  Every subsequential limit has
the same centre, reflections, mass, energy, and outgoing bounds
\eqref{uniq:class}.  Theorem~\ref{thm:uniqueness} identifies any two
such limits up to a time shift and constant phase.

Both constants vanish under the stated normalization.  The scale
is strictly increasing on the common constructed tube.  If the time
shift were nonzero, exchange the two solutions if necessary so that
it is positive.  Cauchy uniqueness extends the identity back to time
zero, where the first representation has scale \(D_0\) and the second
has scale \(D_2>D_0\).

To apply local coordinate uniqueness to these two representations,
first compare their gradient norms.  Uniformly throughout the
constructed tube,
\[
 \|\nabla u\|_2
 =\frac{D}{L}\bigl(\|\nabla Q\|_2+o_{D_0\to\infty}(1)\bigr).
\]
The profile estimates and the uniform \(B/D\) remainder bound give
this formula.  Equality of the two fields implies
\(D_2/D_0=1+o_{D_0\to\infty}(1)\).  Their \(L^2\) profile overlap
also makes their phases close modulo \(2\pi\).  Thus, for the fixed
large starting section used in the construction, both representations
belong to the same uniform implicit-function neighborhood.  Local
coordinate uniqueness forces \(D_2=D_0\), a contradiction.  The time
shift is zero.  The common phase value zero at that section then fixes
the constant phase modulo \(2\pi\), which is the same phase factor.

Finally, if convergence of the full sequence failed in a fixed \(H^k\),
a subsequence would stay a positive distance from the unique limiting
datum in that norm.  Its uniform \(H^{k+1}\) bound would yield a
convergent subsubsequence, whose limit must be that same datum.  This
contradiction proves the assertion at every fixed order.

\label{rate:corollary}
Choose the common section \(D_0\) sufficiently large, once and for all.
For \(N>D_0\), let \(u_N\) be the solution whose terminal profile at
scale \(N\) is \(V_{N,B(N)}\), normalized by
\[
 t_N(D_0)=0,\qquad \gamma_N(D_0)=0.
\]
Let \(u\) be the limiting solution with the same normalization.
There are constants \(C,P\), independent of \(N\), such that
\begin{equation}\label{rate:H1}
 \|u_N(0)-u(0)\|_{H^1(\mathbb T_L^2)}
                 \le C N^P e^{-N}.
\end{equation}
For every fixed integer \(k\ge0\), every \(0<\eta<1\), and every
compact interval \(I\subset\mathbb R\), there is a constant
\(C_{I,k,\eta}\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{rate:compact}
 \|u_N-u\|_{C(I;H^k(\mathbb T_L^2))}
                 \le C_{I,k,\eta}e^{-\eta N}
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large \(N\).  The constants in these estimates
are independent of the terminal scale.

<!-- proof-26: body_md -->
Propositions~\ref{con:terminal-family} and \ref{con:all-orders}
apply to every sufficiently large terminal scale, with the same
constants at each fixed order.  The normalization is by exact
symmetries and does not change those estimates.  Each \(u_N\)
also extends globally by Proposition~\ref{glob:continuation},
since its mass is \(m\) and its energy is zero.

For the finite solution, a subscript \(N\) on an operator
means evaluation at
\((D,b_N(D))\). In particular, \(g_N^0(D)=g^0(D,b_N(D))\),
and \(\mathcal A_N=\Pi_NJ\mathcal L_N\Pi_N\).
The current scale is \(\lambda=L/D\); at the terminal section
we write \(\lambda_N=L/N\).

We first need a finite-interval version of
Proposition~\ref{uniq:refined}.  On \(D_0\le D\le N\), put
\[
 y_N=\Pi_N w_N,\qquad
 r_N^0=-\mathcal A_N^{-1}g_N^0,\qquad
 z_N^{\mathrm{err}}=y_N-r_N^0.
\]
The graph energy of this corrected remainder is
\[
 X_q(D)^2=\sum_{a=0}^{(q-1)/2}
 \big\langle\mathcal L_N\mathcal A_N^a z_N^{\mathrm{err}},
                  \mathcal A_N^a z_N^{\mathrm{err}}\big\rangle,
 \qquad q\ge3\ \hbox{odd}.
\]
Its dependence on \(N\) is suppressed in the notation.
All identities used to derive
\eqref{uniq:quasistatic-gronwall} are local identities.
The old bounds \eqref{con:uniform-high}, with their constants
independent of \(N\), therefore give for the graph-energy square
root \(X_q\) of \(z_N^{\mathrm{err}}\), at any fixed odd order
\(q\ge3\),
\begin{equation}\label{rate:finite-quasistatic}
 |(X_q)_D|\le \frac{C_q}{D}X_q+
                         C_qD^{p_q}B(D)^2.
\end{equation}
The inverse and coefficient estimates are uniform on this interval.
There is a nonzero terminal boundary term:
\[
 w_N(N)=0,\qquad
 z_N^{\mathrm{err}}(N)=-r_N^0(N),\qquad
 X_q(N)\le C_qN^{p_q}B(N)^2.
\]
In particular, the corrected error is not assigned a zero terminal
value.  Integrating \eqref{rate:finite-quasistatic} backwards gives
\begin{equation}\label{rate:finite-tail}
 \begin{split}
 X_q(D)\le{}&
 C_q(N/D)^{C_q}N^{p_q}B(N)^2\\
 &+C_q\int_D^N(r/D)^{C_q}r^{p_q}B(r)^2\,dr.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Both terms are bounded by \(C_qD^{P_q}B(D)^2\), uniformly in
\(N\ge D\).  Indeed,
\[
 \frac{B(r)^2}{B(D)^2}
 \le C(r/D)^{-1/2}e^{-(r-D)}\qquad(r\ge D\ge D_0).
\]
Writing \(r=D+x\), the remaining factors are bounded by a fixed
power of \(D\) times \((1+x/D)^a e^{-x}\).  The latter function
has a uniform supremum and integral for each fixed \(a\).
This estimates both the endpoint and the integral in
\eqref{rate:finite-tail}.
Adding \(r_N^0\) and then using the quadratic normal estimate
\eqref{mod:normal-part}, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{rate:finite-refined}
 \|w_N(D)\|_{q,\lambda}
       \le C_qD^{P_q}B(D)^2,\qquad D_0\le D\le N,
\end{equation}
with constants independent of \(N\).
Even and lower orders follow from the next odd order.

We now compare \(u_N\) and \(u\) at the same scale, with their
own physical clocks and modulation phases.  Denote their
phase-removed full fields by
\[
 Z_N(D)=V_{D,b_N}+w_N,\qquad
 Z(D)=V_{D,b}+w,
\]
and let
\[
 \xi_N=\Pi_N(Z_N-Z),\qquad
 \mathfrak D_N(D)=\langle\mathcal L_N\xi_N,\xi_N\rangle.
\]
The proof of \eqref{uniq:homogeneous-energy} uses the local
mass and energy constraints, the low-order modulation estimates,
and the improved remainder bounds.  It uses the infinite interval
only in its final passage of the boundary term to zero.
Here \eqref{rate:finite-refined} supplies the improved bounds
for the finite solution, with constants independent of \(N\).
Thus every term in \eqref{uniq:all-difference-terms}, including
the different clocks and phases, obeys the same estimate on
\([D_0,N]\).  Enlarging the fixed \(D_0\) for this finite
collection of low-order estimates, we have
\begin{equation}\label{rate:finite-difference}
 \mathfrak D_N\asymp\|Z_N-Z\|_{1,\lambda}^2,\qquad
 |(\mathfrak D_N)_D|\le\frac{C_*}{D}\mathfrak D_N,
\end{equation}
where \(C_*\) is independent of \(N\).
No assertion that \(u_N\) belongs to the infinite outgoing
class is needed.

At \(D=N\), the finite solution has \(b_N=B(N)\) and \(w_N=0\).
The infinite solution has
\(\|w(N)\|_{1,\lambda_N}\le CN^pB(N)^2\).
Applying Lemma~\ref{uniq:energy-map} to these two exactly
zero-energy fields gives
\[
 |B(N)-b(N)|\le CN^pB(N)^3.
\]
The smooth dependence of \(V_{N,b}\) on \(b\) consequently gives
\[
 \mathfrak D_N(N)^{1/2}\le CN^pB(N)^2.
\]
Integrating \eqref{rate:finite-difference} without discarding
this terminal error yields
\[
 \mathfrak D_N(D_0)^{1/2}
 \le (N/D_0)^{C_*/2}\mathfrak D_N(N)^{1/2}
 \le C N^P e^{-N}.
\]
At \(D_0\), both physical times and both modulation phases are
zero.  Moreover, \(\lambda_0=L/D_0\) is fixed.  Therefore
\(Z_N(D_0)-Z(D_0)=u_N(0)-u(0)\), and the scaled norm is
equivalent to the physical \(H^1\) norm.  This proves
\eqref{rate:H1}.

For every fixed integer \(K\), the common-section estimates give
\[
 \sup_{N\text{ large}}\|u_N(0)\|_{H^K}+\|u(0)\|_{H^K}<\infty.
\]
If \(1<k<K\), interpolation between \(H^1\) and \(H^K\) gives
\[
 \|u_N(0)-u(0)\|_{H^k}
 \le C_K\bigl(N^Pe^{-N}\bigr)^{(K-k)/(K-1)}.
\]
Given \(\eta<1\), choose \(K\) so that
\((K-k)/(K-1)>\eta\); the polynomial factor is then absorbed
by the remaining exponential margin.
The cases \(k=0,1\) follow directly from \eqref{rate:H1}.
Hence the initial-data estimate in every fixed order is
\(C_{k,\eta}e^{-\eta N}\).

Finally, choose an integer \(j\ge\max\{k,2\}\) and a compact
interval \(J\) containing both \(I\) and zero.  The limiting
solution is bounded in \(H^j\) on \(J\).  The algebra property
of \(H^j(\mathbb T_L^2)\) and Duhamel's formula give, on a
bounded \(H^j\) neighborhood of this solution,
\[
 \|u_N(t)-u(t)\|_{H^j}
 \le \|u_N(0)-u(0)\|_{H^j}
 +C_J\left|\int_0^t\|u_N(r)-u(r)\|_{H^j}\,dr\right|.
\]
A bootstrap and Gronwall keep \(u_N\) in that neighborhood on
\(J\), for all sufficiently large \(N\), and preserve the
initial exponential rate.  The argument is applied in both
time directions from zero.  Restricting to \(I\) and using the
embedding into \(H^k\) proves \eqref{rate:compact}.
