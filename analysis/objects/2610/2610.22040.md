\label{P:NS}
Let $\mathcal A:=A[z_1, z_2, \ldots]$ be the polynomial ring in countably many variables. The infinite symmetric group $\Sg_\infty$ acts on $\mathcal A$ by
permuting the variables. Let $f\in\mathcal A\setminus(0)$ and let 
\[I_f = \sum\limits_{\sigma\in\Sg_\infty}\mathcal A\sigma(f).\]
 Then there exist $n\ge0$ and a nonzero  $c \in  A $ that is a coefficient of $f$, such that $c\FPol_n(z_1, \ldots, z_n)\in I_f$.

    The  base case $i=0$ (that is, $f\in A$) is trivial. We also provide a separate argument for the case $i=1$. The general argument works only from step 2 onward.

    Case $i=1$. In this case $f\in A[z_1]$.

    We
   claim that $f_d \FPol_{d+1}(z_1, \dots, z_{d+1}) \in I_f$.
   To see this, let

         z_1&z_2&\ldots&z_{d+1}\\
         z_1^2&z_2^2&\ldots&z_{d+1}^2\\

    $$\mathcal A \cong A[z_1]\otimes_{A} A[z_2, z_3, \ldots].$$

    and let 
    $I'_f = \sum_{\sigma \in \Sg'_\infty} \mathcal A \sigma(f)$.
    By induction, there is $c \in A[z_1] \setminus \{0\}$ and $n \geq 0$ so that
    \[ C := c \FPol_n(z_2, \dots, z_{n+1}) \in I'_f.\]
Further, $c$ is a coefficient of $f$ when we think of $f$ as a polynomial in the variables $z_2, \dots, z_{i+1}$ with coefficients in $A[z_1]$.
Note that $I'_f \subseteq I_f$ and that the $A$-coefficients of  $c$ are  a subset of the $A$-coefficients of $f$, up to sign.

\[c'' \FPol_n(z_2, \dots, z_{n+1})\FPol_m(z_1, z_{n+2}, \dots, z_{n+m}) \in I_f,\]
we see that $c'' \FPol_{n+m}(z_1, \dots, z_{n+m}) \in I_f$.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:nonnoeth}
There is an ideal $J$ of $\Ua(\Lg)$ that satisfies the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item  $J^n$ is not finitely generated as a left, right, or two-sided ideal of $\Ua(\Lg)$ for any $n\geq 1$;
\item  $\gr (J^n)$ is not finitely generated as a Poisson ideal of $\Sa(\Lg)$ for any $n\geq 1$;
\item  $(\gr J)^n$ is not finitely generated as a Poisson ideal of $\Sa(\Lg)$ for any $n\geq 1$;
\item  $((\gr J)^n)_{2n}$ is not a finitely generated subrepresentation of $\Sa^{2n}(\Lg)$ for any $n\geq 1$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let ${\rm U} = \Ua(\mathfrak g)$, with augmentation ideal ${\rm U}_+$, and let $R = \im \Phi_1 \subseteq {\rm U}[t,t^{-1}]$.
Let ${\rm U}_+[t,t^{-1}]$ denote the ideal of ${\rm U}(\mathfrak g)\otimes {\Bbbk}[t,t^{-1}]$ generated by ${\rm U}_+$.
Since $\mathfrak g[t, t^{-1}] \subseteq R$ it is not hard to see that $R= {\Bbbk} \oplus {\rm U}_+  [t, t^{-1}]$.

Let $Z = Z({\rm U}) $ be the centre of ${\rm U}$ and let  $\ell$ be the rank of $\mathfrak g$: that is, $\ell$ is the dimension of a Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak g$.
Then (see for example \cite[Theorem~7.3.8(ii)]{Dix}) there are algebraically independent $\Omega_1, \dots, \Omega_\ell$, which we can choose to lie in ${\rm U}_+$,   so that
$Z = {\Bbbk}[\Omega_1, \dots, \Omega_\ell]$.
Since the $\Omega_i$ lie in ${\rm U}_+$, note that $Z_+:= {\rm U}_+\cap Z$ is the ideal of $Z$ generated by $\Omega_1, \dots, \Omega_{\ell}$ and is contained in $R$.  
Furthermore,
\begin{equation}\label{RZ} R\cap Z = ({\Bbbk} \oplus \Ua_+ [t,t^{-1}]) \cap Z = {\Bbbk}  \oplus Z_+ [t, t^{-1}]. \end{equation}
Let $K = Z_+ {\rm U}[t, t^{-1}]$, and let $J = \Phi_1^{-1}(K)$.

$(1)$, $(2)$.  Let $n \geq 1$.  
We have $\Phi_1(J^n) = K^n = (Z_+)^n{\rm U}[t, t^{-1}]$.
For $m \geq 0$, let 
\[K(n, m) = \sum_{i = -m}^m  (Z_+)^n t^i R,\]
so $K^n = \bigcup K(n, m)$.
We can write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K} K(n,m) = (Z_+)^{n-1}\bigl( \sum_{i=-m}^m \sum_{j = 1}^\ell \Omega_j t^i R\bigr).\end{equation}
Since the $\Omega_j t^i$ are (central) in $R$, thus $K(n,m)$ is an ideal of $R$.
Further, since $t \not\in R$, no $\Omega_k^n t^{m+1}$ is in $K(n,m) $. 
In more detail, $\Ua$ is free over  $Z$  by  Kostant's theorem \cite[Theorem~8.2.4]{Dix}, and in particular $Z$ is a $Z$-summand of $\Ua$.
If $\Omega_k^n t^{m+1} \in K(n,m)$, projecting this to $Z$ by taking the $\mathfrak g$-trivial component of \eqref{eq:K}, we find
\begin{align*}
\Omega_k^n t^{m+1} & \in \sum_{i=-m}^m (Z_+)^n t^i (R\cap Z) \\
 & = \sum_{i=-m}^m (Z_+)^n t^i \oplus (Z_+)^{n+1}[t,t^{-1}] \quad \mbox{ by \eqref{RZ}.}
\end{align*}
This is clearly false.


Thus $\{K(n,m) \}_m$ is an infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals
whose union is $K^n$, showing that $K^n$ is not finitely generated as a two-sided ideal of $R$.
Thus $J^n$ is not finitely generated either.
It follows by standard associated graded arguments that $\gr (J^n)$ is not finitely generated as a Poisson ideal of $\Sa(\Lg)$.

We next prove $(4)$ for $n=1$.
Without loss of generality, we may let  $\Omega := \Omega_1$ be the (unique) {\em quadratic Casimir element} of ${\rm U}$:  that is, if 
$\{ g^1, \dots, g^d\}$ is a basis of $\mathfrak g$,
with dual basis $\{ g_1, \dots, g_d\}$ (with respect to the Killing form)
then
\begin{equation}\label{casimir}
\Omega = \sum_{i =1}^d  g^i g_i.
\end{equation}
Let $\omega = \gr \Omega \in \Sa^2(\mathfrak g)$.
The formula for $\omega$ is the same as \eqref{casimir}, but notice that $\Omega \in {\rm U}$ and so $g^i , g_i$ may not commute in the formula for $\Omega$.



Now, $\Phi_1$ respects canonical filtrations: that is, $\Phi_1(\Ua_{\leq i}(\Lg)) \subseteq {\rm U}_{\leq i}[t,t^{-1}]$ for all $i$ and so $\Phi_1$ induces a graded Poisson homomorphism $
\underline{\Phi}: \Sa(\Lg) \to \Sa(\mathfrak g)[t, t^{-1}]$, where $\underline{\Phi}(g s^i) = g t^i$ for all $g \in \mathfrak g$.
(Here the grading on $\Sa(\mathfrak g)[t, t^{-1}]$ is by giving elements of $\mathfrak g$ degree 1 and giving $t$ degree 0.)
The kernel of $\underline{\Phi}$ is $\gr(\ker \Phi_1)$ and so is contained in the Poisson ideal $\gr J$ of $\Sa(\Lg)$.

Since the elements $\Omega t^i$ are in $R$, we have $\omega t^i \in \underline{\Phi}((\gr J)_2)$ for all $i \in \mathbb{Z}$.
These elements are Poisson central and linearly independent, so 
the $\mathfrak g$-trivial component of $\underline{\Phi}((\gr J)_2)$, considered as a representation of $\Lg$ under the adjoint action, is an infinite  direct sum of one-dimensional  representations.
As a result, $\underline{\Phi}((\gr J)_2)$  is not finitely generated.

Now let $n \geq 2$ and consider $((\gr J)^n)_{2n}$.
It is easy to see that $\underline{\Phi}(((\gr J)^n)_{2n}) \supseteq \bigoplus_{i \in \mathbb{Z}} {\Bbbk} \omega^n t^i$, which again is not finitely generated.  
Thus $((\gr J)^n)_{2n}$ is not finitely generated either.

Finally, for $(3)$, if $(\gr J)^n$ were a finitely generated Poisson ideal, its lowest-degree piece $((\gr J)^n)_{2n}$ would be a finitely generated subrepresentation of $\Sa^{2n}(\Lg)$, contradicting $(4)$.

\label{not:E}
Let   $\mathfrak b$ be a Borel subalgebra of $\mathfrak g$ and let $\mathfrak h$ be a Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak b$.
Let $e\in\mathfrak b$ be a  highest weight vector  for the action of $\mathfrak h$ on $\mathfrak b$,
and let $E:= e\otimes{\Bbbk}[s, s^{-1}] \subseteq \Lg$.
Since $E$ is an abelian Lie subalgebra of $\Lg$, we can consider $\Sa(E) \cong \Ua(E)$ as a subalgebra both of $\Sa(\Lg)$ and of $\Ua(\Lg)$.
Let $H := \mathrm{L} \mathfrak h = \mathfrak h \otimes {\Bbbk}[s, s^{-1}]$.
Since $E$ is stable under the adjoint action of $H$, thus $H$ acts on each $E^{\otimes k}$ and $\Sa^k(E)$, and thus also on $\Sa(E)$.
We say that an ideal $I$ of $\Sa(E)$ that is preserved under the adjoint action of $H$ is an {\em $H$-ideal} of $\Sa(E)$, and write 
\[ I \vartriangleleft_H \Sa(E)\]
to denote this. 

The action of $\mathfrak h$ on $e$ has a kernel of codimension 1 and thus it reduces to the action of a single element $h\in \mathfrak h$ with $[h, e]=e$. 
We set ${\mathrm L} h= h\otimes {\Bbbk}[s, s^{-1}]$. 
It is clear that an ideal $I$ of $\Sa(E)$ is $H$-stable if and only if it is ${\mathrm L} h$-stable. Set $h_{i}:=h\otimes s^i$ and $e_j : = e \otimes s^j$, so $[h_i, e_j] = e_{i+j}$.
Note that $\Sa(E)$ and the ${\mathrm L} h$-action on it are the same for any choice of a finite-dimensional  simple Lie algebra $\mathfrak g$; in particular, $H$-ideals of $\Sa(E)$ are independent of $\mathfrak g$.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:annihilator2}
     The ideal of $\Ua(\mathrm{L}\mathfrak g)$ generated by $\mathfrak k$ contains $\Ker \Phi_{k}$.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
It is enough to check that the natural map $\pi: \Ua(\mathrm{L}\mathfrak g)\to \Ua(\bar{\mathfrak k})$ sends $\Ker \Phi_{k}$ to 0. 
Fix a basis $\{b_1,\dots, b_d\}$ of $\mathfrak g$ and consider the $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d$-grading on $\Ua(\Lg)$ from Notation~\ref{not:grade}.
Recall from \eqref{qgradeB} that
$$\Ker \Phi_{k}=\bigoplus_{q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^d}(\Ker \Phi_{k}\cap\Ua(\mathrm{L}\mathfrak g)_q)$$
and also recall the definitions of the   linear bijections
$$  \Theta_{\Sg;\star}^{b^q}: \Ua(\mathrm{L}\mathfrak g)_q  \leftrightarrow  \mathcal Z_q^{\Sg_q}: \Theta^{\Sg;\star}_{b^q}.$$
We will suppress some notation and simply refer to the bijections above as $\Theta$-maps.
Likewise,  there is a decomposition
\[ \Ua(\bar{\mathfrak k}) = \bigoplus_q \Ua(\bar{\mathfrak k})_q,\]
where $\Ua(\bar{\mathfrak k})_q = \pi(\Ua(\Lg)_q)$.
There are also natural bijections, which we again refer to as $\Theta$-maps, between 
\[ \Ua(\bar{\mathfrak k})_q \leftrightarrow \Bigl( \mathcal Z_q/\langle p(z_1), \dots, p(z_{|q|})\rangle \Bigr)^{\Sg_q}.\]
(Here we use $\langle p(z_1), \dots, p(z_{|q|})\rangle$ to denote the ideal of $\mathcal Z_q$ generated by $p(z_1), \dots, p(z_{|q|})$.)
Since $\pi$ respects these decompositions,  it suffices to prove that $(\ker \Phi_k)_q \subseteq \ker \pi$ for all $q$.


Fix $q\in \mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d$, and set $I_{k}$ to be the $\Sg_{|q|}$-ideal of $\mathcal Z_q$ generated by $\FPol_{k+1}$, where recall that
\[ \FPol_{k+1}=\prod_{1\le i<j\le k+1}(z_i-z_j).\]
Thanks to Proposition~\ref{prop:kernel} and Theorem~\ref{thm:Lov}, 
 $\Ker \Phi_{k}\cap\Ua(\mathrm{L}\mathfrak g)_q$ is identified under the $\Theta$-maps with  $\mathcal Z_{q}^{\Sg_q}\cap I_{k}$. 
Further, 
 the  $\Theta$-maps identify
\[ \ker \pi \cap \Ua(\Lg)_q \leftrightarrow  \langle p(z_1), \dots, p(z_{|q|})\rangle \cap \mathcal Z_q^{\Sg_q}.\]
To prove that $\pi$ sends $(\ker \Phi_k)_q$ to $0$, it thus suffices to show that $I_k \subseteq \langle p(z_1), \dots, p(z_{|q|})\rangle$.

To prove this,  we consider a formal polynomial
$$\tilde p(u):=u^{k}+p_{k-1}u^{k-1}+\dots+p_1u+p_0$$
where $p_0, p_1, \dots$ are free parameters. 
We set 
$T:={\Bbbk}[p_0, p_1, \dots, p_{k-1}]$ and let $ \tilde{{\Bbbk}}:={\Bbbk}(p_0, p_1, \dots, p_{k-1})$ be  the quotient field of $T$. 

 As the next step we need the following.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:formalfoo}
The ideal $I_{k}\otimes T\subseteq\mathcal Z_{q}\otimes T$ is contained in the ideal $\langle \tilde p(z_1),\dots, \tilde p(z_{|q|})\rangle$ of $\mathcal Z_{q}\otimes T$. \end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Note that both $\mathcal Z_{q}\otimes T$ and $(\mathcal Z_{q}\otimes T)/\langle \tilde p(z_1), \dots, \tilde p(z_{|q|})\rangle$ are flat  $T$-modules: the first  is free  and the second one is an injective limit of a sequence of free $T$-modules. 
In particular both are $T$-torsionfree.
Thus the needed inclusion is equivalent to showing that $I_{k}\otimes \tilde{{\Bbbk}}\subseteq\langle \tilde p(z_1),\dots, \tilde p(z_{|q|})\rangle\otimes_T \tilde{{\Bbbk}}$. 

Set $\hat {\Bbbk}$ to be the algebraic closure of $\tilde{{\Bbbk}}$. 
It is clear that it is enough to prove the analogue of the desired statement over $\hat{{\Bbbk}}$.

To see that the statement holds over $\hat{{\Bbbk}}$, note we have $$\tilde p(u)=(u-u_1)(u-u_2)\dots(u-u_{k})$$ for some pairwise distinct $u_1,  \dots u_k \in\hat{{\Bbbk}}$ and moreover 
$$(\tilde{{\Bbbk}}[u]/(\tilde p(u)))\otimes_{\tilde{\Bbbk}}\hat{{\Bbbk}}\cong \hat{{\Bbbk}}[u]/(\tilde p(u))\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{ k} \hat{{\Bbbk}}[u]/(u-u_i)\cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{ k} \hat{{\Bbbk}}.$$ 
Thus we have
\begin{equation}\label{formal}
\frac{\mathcal  Z_q\otimes\hat{{\Bbbk}}}{\langle \tilde p(z_1),\dots, \tilde p(z_{|q|})\rangle} \cong
\bigoplus \limits_{i_1, i_2, \dots, i_{|q|}\in\{1, 2, \dots, k\}} \frac{\mathcal Z_{q}\otimes\hat{{\Bbbk}}}{\langle z_1-u_{i_1}, z_2-u_{i_2}, \dots, z_{|q|}-u_{i_{|q|}}\rangle}.
\end{equation}
But in every component of the right-hand side of \eqref{formal}, the image of any polynomial in $I_k\otimes \hat{{\Bbbk}}$ 
must vanish, as $z_1, \dots, z_{|q|}$ can certainly take on no more than $k$ distinct values.
This proves the claim.
\end{proof}

We recall that $p(u)$ is a monic polynomial of degree $k$ and thus $p(u)=u^k+\alpha_{k-1}u^{k-1}+\dots+\alpha_0$ for some $\alpha_i\in{\Bbbk}$. We denote by $\mathfrak m$ the ideal of $T$ generated by $p_i-\alpha_i$ with $0\le i\le k-1$. 
This provides a specialisation map 
$$T\to T/\mathfrak m \cong {\Bbbk}, \hspace{10pt}p_i\mapsto \alpha_i.$$

By right exactness of $\otimes$, 
\[ \frac{\mathcal Z_{q}\otimes T}{\langle\tilde p(z_1), \dots, \tilde p(z_{|q|})\rangle} \otimes_T T/\mathfrak m \cong \mathcal Z_{q}/\langle p(z_1), \dots,  p(z_{|q|})\rangle.\]
Thus applying $-\otimes_T T/\mathfrak m$ to the composition
\begin{equation}\label{zeromap}
I_k \otimes T \subseteq \mathcal Z_{q} \otimes T \to \frac{\mathcal Z_{q}\otimes T}{\langle\tilde p(z_1), \dots, \tilde p(z_{|q|})\rangle}
\end{equation}
and identifying  $I_k \otimes T \otimes_T T/\mathfrak m$  with $I_k$,
we obtain the natural map
\begin{equation}\label{zeromap2}
I_k \to  \mathcal Z_{q}/\langle p(z_1), \dots,  p(z_{|q|})\rangle.
\end{equation}
Since by Lemma~\ref{lem:formalfoo} the composition \eqref{zeromap} is zero, the map \eqref{zeromap2} is also zero, as needed.

By analogy with (\ref{Theta}) and~(\ref{Theta2}), for $q\in \mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d$ we identify $\Sa(\mathrm{L}\mathfrak g)_q$

\begin{equation} \label{Theta2+P}

        Then $\lambda(f)=0$ implies $f_0=0$. 
        Set
        $$\ord f:=\min\{i\mid f_i\ne0\}, \hspace{10pt}\ord I:=\min\{\ord f\mid f\in I\setminus (0)\}.$$

    $(3)$ clearly follows from $(2)$.

\[\lambda(e_{k+2})-2\lambda(e_{k+1})+\lambda(e_k)=\lambda(e_{k+2}-2e_{k+1}+e_k)=0 \quad \mbox{for all $k$}\]
and it is well known that this is a  linear homogeneous recursive relation of minimal degree for the sequence $\lambda(e_i), i\in\mathbb Z$. 
This implies that $p_\lambda=(z_1-1)^2$ and $ I_H(\lambda)=\Sa(E)\Theta^{\Sg;\star}_{e}(\mathcal Z_1(z_1-1)^2)$.
We have $(z_1-1)^2\in\Theta_\star(I_H(\lambda))$ and hence $z_1-1\in\sqrt{\Theta_\star(I_H(\lambda))}$. 
Thus $A_\star\subseteq\sqrt{\Theta_\star(I_H(\lambda))}$ by Example~\ref{Ex1}. 
Since $A_\star$ is a maximal ideal of $\mathcal Z_\infty$,  hence $\sqrt{\Theta_\star(I_H(\lambda))}=A_\star$.


To summarise, $A^\star$ and $I_H(\lambda)$ are distinct $H$-primitive  ideals of $\Sa(E)$ such that $$\sqrt{\Theta_\star(A^\star)}=\sqrt{\Theta_\star(I_H(\lambda))}.$$

\label{Exm1} We continue to use the notation of Example~\ref{Ex1}.
Consider the homomorphism
\[\gamma: \mathcal Z_{\infty}\to{\Bbbk}, \hspace{10pt}z_1\mapsto -1, \quad z_i\mapsto 1 \mbox{ if $i \geq 2$.}\]
Let 
\[C_\star = \bigcap_{\sigma \in \Sg_\infty} \sigma\cdot(\ker\gamma)\]
be the maximal $\Sg_\infty$-ideal contained in $\ker\gamma$. 
Since $\ker \gamma$ is a maximal ideal of $\mathcal Z_\infty$, thus
 $C_\star$ is radical. 

For all $k$, by construction,  $\mathcal Z_k\cap C_\star$ is the intersection of $k+1$ distinct maximal ideals corresponding to the $k+1$ points $(\underbrace{1, 1, \dots, 1}_{k~\mathrm{times}})$ and the same point with a single $1$ replaced by $-1$.
In particular, $\mathcal Z_k \cap C_\star \subsetneqq \mathcal Z_k \cap A_\star$ for all $k$ and thus $C_\star \subsetneqq A_\star$.

We have $\mathcal Z_1\cap C_\star=\mathcal Z_1(z_1^2-1) \subsetneqq \mathcal Z_1 \cap A_\star = \mathcal Z_1(z_1-1)$. 
This implies that  $A^\star = \Theta^\star(A_\star)\supsetneqq\Theta^\star(C_\star)$. 

Recall that $e_1 - e_0 \in A^\star$.  
Since $(z_1-1)(z_2-1) \in C_\star \cap \mathcal Z_2^{\Sg_2}$, we have
\[ \Theta^{\Sg; \star}_{e^2}((z_1-1)(z_2-1) )= (e_1-e_0)^2 \in \Theta^\star(C_\star).\]

We claim that $A^\star = \sqrt{\Theta^\star(C_\star)}.$
By the previous paragraph and the fact that $A^\star$ is generated as an  $H$-ideal by $e_1-e_0$, we have $A^\star \subseteq \sqrt{\Theta^\star(C_\star)}$.
For all $k$, further, $\gr_k(A^\star)$ has codimension 1 in $\Sa^k(E)$.
Thus either $\sqrt{\Theta^\star(C_\star)} = A_\star$ or $\sqrt{\Theta^\star(C_\star)} \supseteq \Sa^{\geq 1}(E)$.
If $e_1\in \sqrt{\Theta^\star(C_\star)}$, then some $e_1^N\in\Theta^\star(C_\star)$ and so  $z_1z_2\dots z_N\in C_\star$ and
$\gamma(z_1 z_2 \dots z_N) = 0$.
This is not true and hence $\sqrt{\Theta^\star(C_\star)}=A_\star$. 

To summarise,   $A_\star$ and $C_\star$ are distinct radical $\Sg_\infty$-ideals of $\mathcal Z_\infty$ with $\Theta^\star(A_\star) = \sqrt{\Theta^\star(A_\star)}=\sqrt{\Theta^\star(C_\star)}$.
Since $\Theta^\star(A_\star) \neq \Theta^\star(C_\star)$, thus $\Theta^\star(C_\star)$ is not radical.
