<!-- defn-1: body_md -->
\label{defn:markov_sol}
 A \emph{Markovian solution} of \eqref{eq:BSDE} is a pair of measurable functions
\begin{equation*}
v \colon [0,T]\times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R},
\qquad
w \colon [0,T]\times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d,
\end{equation*}
such that for every $\left(s,x\right)\in \left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d$,
$Y_t^{s,x}=v\left(t,X_t^{s,x}\right)$ for all $t\in[s,T]$, while
\begin{equation*}
Z_t^{s,x}=w\left(t,X_t^{s,x}\right)
\qquad \text{for }\mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb P\text{-almost every }(t,\omega)\in[s,T]\times\Omega.
\end{equation*}
With this convention, \eqref{eq:BSDE} holds with
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}\left(\int_s^T \left\lvert f(r,X_r^{s,x},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}) \right\rvert\mathrm{d} r + \int_s^T \left\lvert Z_r^{s,x} \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} r\right) < \infty.
\end{equation*}
<!-- end defn-1 -->
<!-- assumption-1: body_md -->
\label{ass:flow}
The coefficients $b \colon \left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ and
$\sigma \colon \left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ are
continuous in $t$ and continuously differentiable in $x$, with
$\nabla_x b$ and $\nabla_x \sigma$ bounded on
$\left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d$.
<!-- end assumption-1 -->
<!-- rem-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:flow_consequences}
Assumption~\ref{ass:flow} already implies that $b$ and $\sigma$ are
globally Lipschitz in $x$, uniformly with respect to $t$, and of at
most linear growth: the Lipschitz bound follows from the boundedness
of the spatial gradients via the integral form of the mean value
theorem, and the growth bound then follows because
$t\mapsto \bigl(b(t,0),\sigma(t,0)\bigr)$ is continuous on the compact
interval $\left[0,T\right]$, hence bounded.
<!-- end rem-1 -->
<!-- lem-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:jacobian_flow}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:flow}, for every $0\le s\le t\le T$ the map
$x\mapsto X^{s,x}_t$ is differentiable, and its Jacobian
$J_{s,t}:=\nabla_x X^{s,x}_t$ is a continuous, a.s.\ invertible
$\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$-valued process solving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobian_sde}
\mathrm{d} J_{s,t}
= \nabla_x b(t,X^{s,x}_t)\,J_{s,t}\,\mathrm{d} t
+ \sum_{\ell=1}^d \nabla_x \sigma_\ell(t,X^{s,x}_t)\,J_{s,t}\,\mathrm{d} W_t^\ell,
\qquad J_{s,s}=I_d,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\ell$ is the $\ell$-th column of $\sigma$ and $I_d$ the identity
matrix. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flow_moments}
\mathbb{E}\left( \sup_{t\in [s,T]}\bigl( \left\lVert J_{s,t} \right\rVert^q + \left\lVert J_{s,t}^{-1} \right\rVert^q \bigr) \right)
< \infty
\qquad \text{for every } q\ge 1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
By Assumption \ref{ass:flow} the coefficients $b$ and $\sigma$ are continuously
differentiable in $x$ with bounded derivatives, and by Remark \ref{rem:flow_consequences} of at most
linear growth. The hypotheses of \cite[\S2.8, Theorem~6]{Krylov} therefore
hold with $i=1$, which gives the differentiability of $x\mapsto X^{s,x}_t$
together with $\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in [s,T]}\left\lVert J_{s,t} \right\rVert^q\right)<\infty$ for every
$q\ge 1$. Differentiating \eqref{eq:SDE} in $x$ and interchanging the
derivative with the integrals yields \eqref{eq:jacobian_sde}.
 
To prove invertibility, let $K_{s,t}$ be the unique
  strong solution of the matrix-valued linear SDE
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse_jacobian_sde}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{d} K_{s,t}
&= -K_{s,t}\,\nabla_x b(t,X^{s,x}_t)\,\mathrm{d} t \\
&\quad - \sum_{\ell=1}^d K_{s,t}\,\nabla_x \sigma_\ell(t,X^{s,x}_t)\,\mathrm{d} W_t^\ell \\
&\quad + \sum_{\ell=1}^d K_{s,t}
\bigl(\nabla_x\sigma_\ell(t,X^{s,x}_t)\bigr)^2\,\mathrm{d} t, \\
K_{s,s}&=I_d.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}
The coefficients in \eqref{eq:inverse_jacobian_sde} are bounded by
Assumption~\ref{ass:flow}. Standard Burkholder--Davis--Gundy and
Gronwall estimates for linear SDEs give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K_moments}
\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[s,T]}\left\lVert K_{s,t} \right\rVert^q\right)<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }q\ge1.
\end{equation}
Applying It\^o's product rule to $K_{s,t}J_{s,t}$ and using
\eqref{eq:jacobian_sde} and \eqref{eq:inverse_jacobian_sde}, the drift
terms and the stochastic terms cancel identically, including the quadratic
covariation term, and therefore
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{d}\bigl(K_{s,t}J_{s,t}\bigr)=0,
\qquad
K_{s,s}J_{s,s}=I_d.
\end{equation*}
Thus $K_{s,t}J_{s,t}=I_d$ for every $t\in[s,T]$, almost surely. Since
$J_{s,t}$ and $K_{s,t}$ are square matrices, the existence of this left
inverse implies that $J_{s,t}$ is invertible and
$K_{s,t}=J_{s,t}^{-1}$. Combining this identity with
\eqref{eq:K_moments} gives the inverse-moment bound in
\eqref{eq:flow_moments}.

\label{rem:forward_roles}
The distinction between Assumptions \ref{ass:flow} and
\ref{ass:parabolic} will be used repeatedly below. Assumption
\ref{ass:flow} is the stochastic-flow hypothesis: it yields
well-posedness of the forward SDE, moment bounds, differentiability of
$X^{s,x}$ with respect to the initial condition, and Malliavin
differentiability of the forward process. Assumption
\ref{ass:parabolic}, by contrast, is tailored to the analytic part of
the proof: it provides classical local regularity for the decoupling
field, Aronson-type absolute continuity of the law of $X_t$ at positive
times, and places the differentiated equation within the scope of the
unique continuation and backward uniqueness results of
Section~\ref{sec:rigidity}.

For later reference, we record the form in which Assumption
\ref{ass:parabolic} enters these arguments. Define the diffusion matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A_def}
A := \tfrac12\,\sigma\sigma^{\mathsf{T}},
\end{equation}
a notation in force throughout the paper. Under Assumption
\ref{ass:parabolic}, the matrix $A$ is bounded, with the two-sided
ellipticity bounds
\begin{equation*}
\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\left\lvert \xi \right\rvert^{2}
\;\le\;
\xi^{\mathsf{T}}A(t,x)\,\xi
\;\le\;
\tfrac12\,\left\lVert \sigma \right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\,\left\lvert \xi \right\rvert^{2},
\qquad
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad (t,x)\in\left[0,T\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.
\end{equation*}
The gradient $\nabla_{x}A$ is bounded, by \ref{it:P1} and the
boundedness of $\nabla_{x}\sigma$ in Assumption~\ref{ass:flow}, so
that $A$ is globally Lipschitz in $x$, uniformly in $t$. Combined
with the Lipschitz bound in $t$, uniformly in $x$, supplied by
\ref{it:P1} and \ref{it:P2}, it follows that $A$ is globally
Lipschitz in $(t,x)$ on $\left[0,T\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$. In particular, $A$ is uniformly continuous and belongs to
$C^{\alpha/2,\alpha}$ on bounded cylinders. Moreover, since
$t\mapsto A(t,x)$ is Lipschitz uniformly in $x$, the derivative
$\partial_{t}A(\cdot,x)$ exists for almost every $t\in\left[0,T\right]$,
with $\left\lvert \partial_{t}A \right\rvert$ bounded almost everywhere by the Lipschitz
constant. This is the form in which the time regularity of $A$ is used
in the backward uniqueness argument of Section~\ref{sec:rigidity},
where $\partial_{t}A$ enters only through integral identities and
almost-everywhere bounds.

\label{ass:backward}
The BSDE \eqref{eq:BSDE} admits a Markovian solution. We fix one such pair
$(v,w)$ for the remainder of the paper and define
\begin{equation*}
C_Y := \sup_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^d}\left\lvert v \right\rvert\in[0,+\infty],
\qquad
C_Z := \sup_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^d}\left\lvert w \right\rvert\in[0,+\infty],
\end{equation*}
together with the associated solution strip
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{S} := [0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^d\times\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\left\lvert y \right\rvert\le C_Y\}
       \times\{z\in\mathbb{R}^d:\left\lvert z \right\rvert\le C_Z\},
\end{equation*}

$C_Y=+\infty$ (resp.\ $C_Z=+\infty$). Assume, moreover, that there exist a
constant $K_f>0$ and, for every $R>0$, a constant $K_{f,R}>0$, such that the
following hold.

\renewcommand{\textup{(\alph{enumi})}}{\textup{(B\arabic{enumi})}}
\renewcommand{\alph{enumi}}{B\arabic{enumi}}

differentiable and of at most polynomial growth.
\item\label{it:B2} The generator
$f\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^d\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuously
differentiable in $(x,y,z)$. On $\mathcal{S}$, it is H\"older continuous in $t$ and
$x$, locally uniformly in $(y,z)$: for all $t,s\in[0,T]$, $x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and

\left\lvert f(t,x,y,z)-f(s,\xi,y,z) \right\rvert

\label{eq:holder_f_tx}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\left\lvert \partial_y f(t,x,y,z) \right\rvert+\left\lvert \nabla_z f(t,x,y,z) \right\rvert\le K_f.
\label{eq:lip_fyz}
\end{equation}

\begin{align}
\nabla_x f(t,x,y,0)&=0,\label{eq:struct_fx_zero}\\
\left\lvert \nabla_x f(t,x,y,z)-\nabla_x f(t,x,y,\zeta) \right\rvert

\end{align}
\end{enumerate}

\label{rem:CYCZ}
The quantities \(C_Y\) and \(C_Z\) are determined by the Markovian solution
itself and are not fixed in advance. They are the possibly infinite supremum
norms of the decoupling fields \(v\) and \(w\). By
Definition~\ref{defn:markov_sol},
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert Y_t^{s,x} \right\rvert\le C_Y
\qquad\text{for every }t\in[s,T],\quad\text{a.s.},
\end{equation*}
while
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert Z_t^{s,x} \right\rvert\le C_Z
\qquad\text{for }\mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb P\text{-almost every }(t,\omega)\in[s,T]\times\Omega.
\end{equation*}
Thus \(C_Y\) and \(C_Z\) localize the assumptions on the generator to the
region of \((y,z)\)-space visited by the solution, with the natural
\(\mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb P\)-almost-everywhere interpretation for the
\(Z\)-component. When both constants are finite, \ref{it:B2} and
\ref{it:B3} require control of the coefficients only on the strip \(\mathcal{S}\).
This localization is used through the truncation maps \(\rho_Y\) and
\(\rho_Z\) introduced in Step~1 of the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}. If \(C_Y\) or \(C_Z\) is infinite, the
corresponding restriction disappears and the relevant bounds become global in
that variable.

The existence of a Markovian solution is itself a hypothesis, but sufficient
conditions are classical: if the conditions of \ref{it:B2} and \ref{it:B3}
hold with the strip \(\mathcal{S}\) replaced by all of
\([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^d\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^d\), and \(g\) is continuous and of at most
polynomial growth, then the generator is globally Lipschitz in \((y,z)\) and
of linear growth, and the nonlinear Feynman--Kac construction provides a pair
\((v,w)\) as in Definition~\ref{defn:markov_sol} -- see \cite{PP2} and
\cite[Section~4]{KPQ}.

\label{rem:struct_fx_necessary}
The structural condition imposed on \(\nabla_x f\) is dictated by the PDE mechanism underlying the proof. The decoupling field \(v\) solves a semilinear parabolic equation of the form
\begin{equation*}
\partial_t v + \mathcal{L} v + f\left(t,x,v,(\nabla_x v)\sigma\right)=0,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}A_{ij}\,\partial_{ij}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\,\partial_{i},
\end{equation*}
where \(\mathcal{L}\) is the generator of the forward diffusion, cf.~\eqref{eq:A_def}. Differentiating in space and setting $u=\nabla_x v$ produces a linear parabolic system for \(u\) with the external forcing term
\begin{equation*}
\nabla_x f\left(t,x,v,\left(\nabla_x v\right)\sigma\right).
\end{equation*}
For the unique continuation and backward uniqueness argument to apply, this forcing must vanish whenever \(u\) does. This is precisely the role of the condition
\begin{equation*}
\nabla_x f(t,x,y,0)=0.
\end{equation*}
Combined with the Lipschitz control of \(\nabla_x f\) in the \(z\)-variable, it yields
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert \nabla_x f\left(t,x,v,(\nabla_x v)\sigma\right) \right\rvert\leq C\left\lvert u \right\rvert,
\end{equation*}
and thereby reduces the differentiated equation to a differential inequality of the form
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert \mathcal{P} u \right\rvert\leq C\left(\left\lvert u \right\rvert+\left\lvert \nabla_xu \right\rvert\right),
\end{equation*}
which is exactly the homogeneous structure required by the unique continuation and backward uniqueness results proved later.
\\

The assumption should therefore be understood as a compatibility condition between the explicit \(x\)-dependence of the generator and the geometry of the critical set of the decoupling field. If the forcing term does not vanish on the set \(\{u=0\}\), the zero set of \(u=\nabla_x v\) is no longer rigid: the inhomogeneous \(x\)-dependence of the generator can cause \(u\) to vanish at intermediate times independently of the terminal condition. From the probabilistic viewpoint, this is precisely the obstruction, since the Malliavin derivative of \(Y_t\) factorizes through \(\nabla_x v\left(t,X_t\right)\), and the argument relies on showing that this quantity cannot vanish on a set of positive measure unless it vanishes identically.
\\

That said, it should not be interpreted as a necessary condition for the existence of a density in general. Rather, it is necessary for any approach that relies on rigidity of the zero set of the gradient of the decoupling field. Other approaches, notably the one-dimensional sign criteria of \cite{AB,AKH,MPR}, can accommodate generators with more general explicit state dependence, though those methods are themselves restricted to scalar state processes and rely on order-theoretic tools that are unavailable in higher dimensions.
\\

The relevance of the structural condition can already be seen on a simple example, which shows that without such a compatibility requirement the conclusion of our theorem may fail altogether. Consider the one-dimensional case \(d=1\), with \(b\equiv 0\) and \(\sigma\equiv 1\). Let \(\phi\) be a smooth function such that $\phi\left(T\right)=1$ and $\phi\left(t_0\right)=0$ for some \(t_0\in \left(0,T\right)\), and set $g\left(x\right)=\sin x$ and
\begin{equation*}
f(t,x,y,z)=\left(\tfrac12 \phi(t)-\phi'(t)\right)\sin x.
\end{equation*}
Then $v\left(t,x\right)=\phi\left(t\right)\sin x$ solves the semilinear PDE associated with the BSDE, with terminal condition \(g\). In particular, \(g\) is clearly non-constant, so the terminal datum carries genuine spatial variation. However,
\begin{equation*}
u(t,x)=\nabla_x v(t,x)=\phi(t)\cos x,
\end{equation*}
and therefore $u\left(t_0,\cdot\right)\equiv 0$. Although the terminal condition is non-constant, the gradient \(u(t_0,\cdot)\) vanishes identically, so that \(v(t_0,\cdot)\) is constant on \(\mathbb{R}\). Consequently, \(Y_{t_0}\) is deterministic and cannot admit a density.
\\

This is exactly the phenomenon that our structural condition is designed to exclude. In the differentiated equation for \(u=\nabla_x v\), the explicit \(x\)-dependence of the generator produces a forcing term that does not vanish when \(u=0\). As a result, the zero set of \(u\) is no longer rigid: even if \(u\) vanishes identically at some time, this does not force the terminal datum to be constant. The example therefore shows that the issue is not merely that the proof would fail without the condition \(\nabla_x f\left(t,x,y,0\right)=0\), but that without some restriction of this type the fixed-time density statement itself is false in general.

The terminal time \(t=T\) must be treated separately, since in that case
\begin{equation*}
Y_T = g(X_T),
\end{equation*}
and the density question is governed directly by the regularity of the
pushforward of the law of \(X_T\) through \(g\). Under
Assumption~\ref{ass:parabolic}, the law of \(X_T\) admits a density
\(p_T\left(x\right)\) with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and Aronson's bounds
imply that \(p_T\left(x\right)>0\) for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\) \cite{Aronson}. Thus,
from a measure-theoretic viewpoint, the natural condition for
\(g\left(X_T\right)\) to admit a density is that the pushforward measure
\(g_\#\left(p_T\left(x\right)dx\right)\) be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}\). Equivalently, denoting by \(\left\lvert \cdot \right\rvert\) the Lebesgue measure (on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) or on \(\mathbb{R}\), according to context), one must have
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert  g^{-1}(N)  \right\rvert=0
\quad
\text{for every Borel set } N\subset \mathbb{R} \text{ such that } \left\lvert N \right\rvert=0.
\end{equation*}
A convenient sufficient condition is that \(g\) be locally Lipschitz and that $\left\lvert \nabla g \right\rvert>0$ almost everywhere. In that case, the coarea formula yields
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{Law}\left( g(X_T) \right)(dy)=\rho_g(y)dy,
\qquad
\rho_g(y)=\int_{g^{-1}(y)} \frac{p_T(x)}{\left\lvert \nabla g(x) \right\rvert}d\mathcal H^{d-1}(x),
\end{equation*}
for almost every \(y\in\mathbb{R}\), where \(\mathcal H^{d-1}\) is the natural surface measure on the level set \(g^{-1}\left(y\right)\) -- see, for instance, \cite[Section~3.4.3]{lEvans2015}. In particular, the bounded-gradient assumption in Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U1} should not be viewed as an optimal condition for the terminal law \(g\left(X_T\right)\). Its role in the present paper is different: it is a convenient coefficient-level hypothesis ensuring the global boundedness of \(u=\nabla_x v\), which is needed in the rigidity argument at strictly positive times.

We split the proof into three steps. We first reduce the problem to a
BSDE whose generator is globally Lipschitz in \(\left(y,z\right)\), then establish
the continuity and local classical regularity of the decoupling field,
and finally identify the $Z$-component of the Markovian solution.
\\~\\
\noindent\emph{Step 1: Reduction to a BSDE globally Lipschitz in \(\left(y,z\right)\).}
Define the truncation maps \(\rho_Y:\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}\) and \(\rho_Z:\mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}^d\) by
\begin{equation*}
\rho_Y(y)
=
\begin{cases}
y, & \text{if } C_Y=\infty,
\\
\max(-C_Y,\min(y,C_Y)), & \text{if } C_Y<\infty,
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
and

\begin{equation*}
\rho_Z(z)
=
\begin{cases}
z, & \text{if } C_Z=\infty,\\
z, & \text{if } C_Z<\infty \text{ and } \left\lvert z \right\rvert\le C_Z,\\
C_Z\dfrac{z}{\left\lvert z \right\rvert}, & \text{if } C_Z<\infty \text{ and } \left\lvert z \right\rvert>C_Z.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
When $C_Z<\infty$, this is the metric projection of $\mathbb{R}^d$ onto the
closed ball $\{z\in\mathbb{R}^d:\left\lvert z \right\rvert\le C_Z\}$. Consequently, $\rho_Z$ is
$1$-Lipschitz,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhoZ_properties}
\left\lvert \rho_Z(z) \right\rvert\le \left\lvert z \right\rvert,
\qquad
\rho_Z(z)=z\quad\text{whenever }\left\lvert z \right\rvert\le C_Z,
\end{equation}
and the definition is well posed also when $C_Z=0$.

Set
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde f(t,x,y,z)
=
f\left(t,x,\rho_Y(y),\rho_Z(z)\right),
\qquad
(t,x,y,z)\in [0,T]\times \mathbb{R}^d\times \mathbb{R}\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
By \eqref{eq:holder_f_tx}, the truncated generator
\(\widetilde f\) is H\"older continuous in \(t\) and \(x\), with exponent
\(\alpha/2\) in time and \(\alpha\) in space, locally uniformly in
\(\left(y,z\right)\). More precisely, for every \(R>0\), there exists
\(K_{f,R}>0\) such that, for all \(t,s\in \left[0,T\right]\),
\(x,\xi\in \mathbb{R}^d\), \(y\in \mathbb{R}\), and \(z\in \mathbb{R}^d\) satisfying
\(\left\lvert y \right\rvert+\left\lvert z \right\rvert\le R\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:holder_tildef_tx}
\left\lvert 
\widetilde f(t,x,y,z)-\widetilde f(s,\xi,y,z)
 \right\rvert
\leq
K_{f,R}
\left(
\left\lvert t-s \right\rvert^{\alpha/2}
+
\left\lvert x-\xi \right\rvert^{\alpha}
\right).
\end{equation}

By Assumption~\ref{ass:backward}\ref{it:B2}, the derivatives
\(\partial_y f\) and \(\nabla_z f\) are bounded by \(K_f\) on the strip \(\mathcal{S}\). Since both truncation maps \(\rho_Y\) and \(\rho_Z\) are $1$-Lipschitz and take values in the corresponding components of \(\mathcal{S}\), it follows that \(\widetilde f\) is
globally Lipschitz in \(\left(y,z\right)\), uniformly in \(\left(t,x\right)\). Moreover, by Remark~\ref{rem:CYCZ},
\(\left\lvert Y_t^{s,x} \right\rvert\le C_Y\) for every \(t\in[s,T]\), almost surely, while
\(\left\lvert Z_t^{s,x} \right\rvert\le C_Z\) for \(\mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb P\)-almost every
\((t,\omega)\in[s,T]\times\Omega\). Consequently,
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde f\left(t,X_t^{s,x},Y_t^{s,x},Z_t^{s,x}\right)
=
f\left(t,X_t^{s,x},Y_t^{s,x},Z_t^{s,x}\right)
\end{equation*}
for \(\mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb P\)-almost every
\((t,\omega)\in[s,T]\times\Omega\).
\\

We next record two structural consequences of
Assumption~\ref{ass:backward}\ref{it:B3}. First, by
\eqref{eq:struct_fx_zero}, the map \(x\mapsto f\left(t,x,y,0\right)\) is constant
whenever \(\left\lvert y \right\rvert\le C_Y\). Hence
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde f(t,x,y,0)=\widetilde f(t,0,y,0).
\end{equation*}
Second, by \eqref{eq:struct_fx_lip},
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert \nabla_x \widetilde f(t,x,y,z) \right\rvert
=
\left\lvert \nabla_x f\left(t,x,\rho_Y(y),\rho_Z(z)\right) \right\rvert
\le K_f \left\lvert \rho_Z(z) \right\rvert
\le K_f \left\lvert z \right\rvert.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, for all \(x,x'\in \mathbb{R}^d\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tildef_x_control}
\left\lvert \widetilde f(t,x,y,z)-\widetilde f(t,x',y,z) \right\rvert
\le K_f \left\lvert z \right\rvert\left\lvert x-x' \right\rvert.
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:lip_fyz} together with the fact that
\(\widetilde f\left(t,x,y,0\right)=\widetilde f\left(t,0,y,0\right)\),
we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert \widetilde f(t,x,y,z) \right\rvert
\le
\left\lvert f(t,0,0,0) \right\rvert + K_f\left\lvert y \right\rvert + K_f\left\lvert z \right\rvert.
\end{equation*}
By \eqref{eq:holder_f_tx}, the map \(t\mapsto f(t,0,0,0)\) is bounded
on \(\left[0,T\right]\). Thus, \(\widetilde f\) has linear growth,
uniformly in \(t\).
\\

Because \(b\) and \(\sigma\) have at most linear growth and \(g\) has
at most polynomial growth, standard moment estimates for the forward
SDE yield
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert g(X_T^{s,x}) \right\rvert^p\right) < \infty
\end{equation*}
for every \(p\ge 2\) and every
\(\left(s,x\right)\in \left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d\). Since \(\widetilde f\)
coincides with \(f\) along the Markovian solution, the pair
\(\left(Y^{s,x},Z^{s,x}\right)\) solves the BSDE with terminal condition
\(g\left(X_T^{s,x}\right)\) and generator
\(\widetilde f\left(t,X_t^{s,x},y,z\right)\). Since \(\widetilde f\) is
globally Lipschitz in \(\left(y,z\right)\) with linear growth, and since
the integrability assumed in Definition~\ref{defn:markov_sol} places
\(\left(Y^{s,x},Z^{s,x}\right)\) in the uniqueness class of the standard
\(L^p\)-theory for Lipschitz BSDEs, the a priori estimates of that
theory yield
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[s,T]} \left\lvert Y_t^{s,x} \right\rvert^p\right) < \infty,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\int_s^T \left\lvert Z_r^{s,x} \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} r\right)^{p/2}\right) < \infty,
\end{equation*}
for every \(p\ge 2\) and every
\(\left(s,x\right)\in \left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d\).
\\~\\
\noindent\emph{Step 2: Continuity and local classical regularity of the decoupling field.}
We claim that \(v\) is continuous on \(\left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d\).
\\


Fix a compact set \(K\subset \left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d\) and two points
\(\left(s,x\right),\left(s',x'\right)\in K\). By symmetry, it is enough to
consider the case \(s\le s'\). Set
\begin{equation*}
F_r^{s,x}
:=
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s,x},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right),
\qquad r\in[s,T].
\end{equation*}
Taking expectations in the BSDE between \(s\) and \(s'\), and using
\(Y_s^{s,x}=v(s,x)\), gives
\begin{equation*}
v(s,x)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{s'}^{s,x}+\int_s^{s'}F_r^{s,x}\,\mathrm{d} r\right].
\end{equation*}
Since \(Y_{s'}^{s',x'}=v(s',x')\), it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cont_v_split}
\left\lvert v(s,x)-v(s',x') \right\rvert
\le
\left(\mathbb{E}\left\lvert Y_{s'}^{s,x}-Y_{s'}^{s',x'} \right\rvert^2\right)^{1/2}
+
\mathbb{E}\int_s^{s'}\left\lvert F_r^{s,x} \right\rvert\,\mathrm{d} r.
\end{equation}
The second term is negligible as \(s'\downarrow s\). Indeed, the linear
growth estimate for \(\widetilde f\) obtained in Step~1 yields
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}\int_s^{s'}\left\lvert F_r^{s,x} \right\rvert\,\mathrm{d} r
&\le
C\left\lvert s'-s \right\rvert
+
K_f\left\lvert s'-s \right\rvert\,
\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{r\in[s,T]}\left\lvert Y_r^{s,x} \right\rvert\right)
+
K_f\mathbb{E}\int_s^{s'}\left\lvert Z_r^{s,x} \right\rvert\,\mathrm{d} r
\notag\\
&\le
C_K\left(\left\lvert s'-s \right\rvert+\left\lvert s'-s \right\rvert^{1/2}\right),
\label{eq:short_interval_driver}
\end{align}
where in the last line we used Cauchy--Schwarz together with the
\(L^2\)-estimates of Step~1 and the standard forward moment estimates,
which are uniform when the initial point ranges over the compact set
\(K\).

We now compare the two BSDEs only on their common interval
\([s',T]\). The standard \(L^2\)-stability estimate for Lipschitz BSDEs
gives
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert Y_{s'}^{s,x}-Y_{s'}^{s',x'} \right\rvert^2
&\le C_K \left(
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert g(X_T^{s,x})-g(X_T^{s',x'}) \right\rvert^2\right)
\right.
\notag\\
&\quad +
\left.
\mathbb{E}\int_{s'}^T
\left\lvert 
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s,x},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right)
-
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s',x'},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right)
 \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} r
\right).
\label{eq:cont_v_est}
\end{align}
Here \(C_K\) may be chosen uniformly for
\((s,x),(s',x')\in K\), since the Lipschitz constant of
\(\widetilde f\) in \((y,z)\) is global.
\\
We first treat the terminal term. Let \(R>0\), and define
\begin{equation*}
\Omega_R
=
\left\{
\left\lvert X_T^{s,x} \right\rvert\le R,\ \left\lvert X_T^{s',x'} \right\rvert\le R
\right\}.
\end{equation*}
Since \(g\in C^1\left(\mathbb{R}^d\right)\), its gradient is bounded on the compact ball \(\overline{B_R}\), so that \(g\) is Lipschitz
on \(B_R\) with some constant \(L_R>0\).
Therefore,
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert g(X_T^{s,x})-g(X_T^{s',x'}) \right\rvert^2\right)
&\le
L_R^2\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert X_T^{s,x}-X_T^{s',x'} \right\rvert^2\mathbf 1_{\Omega_R}\right)
\\
&\quad+
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert g(X_T^{s,x})-g(X_T^{s',x'}) \right\rvert^2\mathbf 1_{\Omega_R^c}\right).
\end{align*}
The first term tends to \(0\) as \(\left(s',x'\right)\to \left(s,x\right)\) by the standard
continuity estimate for the forward flow. For the second term, the
polynomial growth of \(g\) and the uniform moment bounds for
\(X^{s,x}\) on compact sets imply, by Cauchy--Schwarz and Markov's
inequality, that
\begin{equation*}
\sup_{(s,x),(s',x')\in K}
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert g(X_T^{s,x})-g(X_T^{s',x'}) \right\rvert^2\mathbf 1_{\Omega_R^c}\right)
\longrightarrow 0
\qquad \text{as } R\to\infty.
\end{equation*}
Thus,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:terminal_term_cont}
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert g(X_T^{s,x})-g(X_T^{s',x'}) \right\rvert^2\right)
\longrightarrow 0
\qquad \text{as } (s',x')\to (s,x).
\end{equation}
\\
For the generator term in \eqref{eq:cont_v_est}, estimate
\eqref{eq:tildef_x_control} yields, for $r\in\left[s\vee s',T\right]$,
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert 
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s,x},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right)
-
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s',x'},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right)
 \right\rvert
\le
K_f \left\lvert Z_r^{s,x} \right\rvert\left\lvert X_r^{s,x}-X_r^{s',x'} \right\rvert.
\end{equation*}
By a supremum bound and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
\begin{align*}
&\mathbb{E}\int_{s\vee s'}^T
\left\lvert 
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s,x},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right)
-
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s',x'},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right)
 \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} r
\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad \le
K_f^2
\mathbb{E}\left(
\sup_{s\vee s'\le r\le T}\left\lvert X_r^{s,x}-X_r^{s',x'} \right\rvert^2
\int_{s\vee s'}^T \left\lvert Z_r^{s,x} \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} r
\right)
\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad \le
K_f^2
\left(
\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\vee s'\le r\le T}\left\lvert X_r^{s,x}-X_r^{s',x'} \right\rvert^4\right)
\right)^{1/2}
\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times
\left(
\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{s\vee s'}^T \left\lvert Z_r^{s,x} \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} r\right)^2\right)^{1/2}.
\end{align*}
The second factor is uniformly
bounded for \(\left(s,x\right)\in K\), by the \(L^p\)-estimate from Step~1 with $p=4$, while the first factor tends to \(0\) as
\(\left(s',x'\right)\to \left(s,x\right)\) by the standard continuity estimate for the forward flow. Therefore,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generator_term_cont}
\mathbb{E}\int_{s\vee s'}^T
\left\lvert 
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s,x},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right)
-
\widetilde f\left(r,X_r^{s',x'},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x}\right)
 \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} r
\longrightarrow 0
\end{equation}
as \(\left(s',x'\right)\to \left(s,x\right)\).
Combining \eqref{eq:cont_v_split},
\eqref{eq:short_interval_driver}, \eqref{eq:cont_v_est},
\eqref{eq:terminal_term_cont}, and \eqref{eq:generator_term_cont}, we
obtain
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert v(s,x)-v(s',x') \right\rvert\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }(s',x')\to(s,x)
\end{equation*}
whenever \(s\le s'\). The case \(s'\le s\) follows by interchanging
\((s,x)\) and \((s',x')\). Hence \(v\) is continuous on \(K\), and
therefore on all of \(\left[0,T\right]\times\mathbb{R}^d\).
\\

Now fix \(R>0\) and consider the parabolic cylinder and its parabolic boundary
\begin{equation*}
Q_R=[0,T)\times B_R,
\qquad
\partial_p Q_R
=
\left([0,T)\times \partial B_R\right)
\cup
\left(\{T\}\times \overline{B_R}\right).
\end{equation*}
On \(Q_R\), consider the Dirichlet problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local_dirichlet_v}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
& \partial_t \hat v
+ \sum_{i,j=1}^d A_{ij}(t,x)\partial_{ij}\hat v
+ \sum_{i=1}^d b_i(t,x)\partial_i\hat v
+ \widetilde f\left(t,x,\hat v,(\nabla_x \hat v)\sigma\right) = 0,
\\
&\hat v = v \text{ on } \partial_p Q_R.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}
Since \(v\) is continuous on \(\partial_p Q_R\), the boundary condition
in \eqref{eq:local_dirichlet_v} is continuous. Moreover, by \ref{it:P1} and \ref{it:P2}, together with Assumption \ref{ass:flow} and Remark \ref{rem:forward_roles}, the coefficients \(A\) and
\(b\) are H\"older continuous in \(t\) and \(x\), with exponent
\(\alpha/2\) in time and \(\alpha\) in space, on bounded cylinders, and
\(A\) is uniformly elliptic. By \eqref{eq:holder_tildef_tx},
\(\widetilde f\) is H\"older continuous in \(t\) and \(x\), with the same
exponents, locally uniformly in \(\left(y,z\right)\), and it is globally
Lipschitz in \(\left(y,z\right)\). Therefore the classical local theory
for semilinear uniformly parabolic Dirichlet problems yields a solution
\(\hat v\) such that
\begin{equation*}
\hat v\in C\left(Q_R\cup \partial_p Q_R\right)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\hat v\in C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}\left(Q'\right)
\end{equation*}
for every \(Q'\Subset Q_R\) -- see Lady\v{z}enskaja, Solonnikov and
Ural'ceva \cite[Chapter~VI, Theorem~4.2]{LadSolUra67}. The corresponding
interior Schauder estimates may also be found in Friedman
\cite[Chapter~3, Theorem~5]{friedman} and Krylov
\cite[Theorem~8.11.1]{Krylov1996}.
\\

Let $\tau_R
=
\inf\left\{ t\ge s : \left\lvert X_t^{s,x} \right\rvert\ge R \right\}\wedge T$, and define, for \(t\in \left[s,T\right]\),
\begin{equation*}
\hat Y_t^{s,x}
=
\hat v\left(t\wedge \tau_R,X_{t\wedge \tau_R}^{s,x}\right).
\end{equation*}
Applying It\^o's formula to \(\hat v\left(t,X_t^{s,x}\right)\) up to
\(\tau_R\wedge (T-\varepsilon)\), and then letting
\(\varepsilon\downarrow 0\), using the continuity of \(\hat v\) on
\(Q_R\cup \partial_p Q_R\), shows that \(\hat Y^{s,x}\) satisfies on
\(\left[s,\tau_R\right]\) the BSDE with generator \(\widetilde f\) and
terminal value
\begin{equation*}
\hat Y_{\tau_R}^{s,x}
=
\hat v\left(\tau_R,X_{\tau_R}^{s,x}\right)
=
v\left(\tau_R,X_{\tau_R}^{s,x}\right)
=
Y_{\tau_R}^{s,x},
\end{equation*}
because \(\hat v=v\) on the parabolic boundary. By uniqueness for BSDEs with generator globally Lipschitz in \((y,z)\) on
the random interval \([s,\tau_R]\), we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\hat Y_t^{s,x}=Y_t^{s,x} \quad \text{for } t\in [s,\tau_R],
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\hat Z_t^{s,x}=Z_t^{s,x}
\quad \mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\text{-a.e.\ on } [s,\tau_R],
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{equation*}
\hat Z_t^{s,x}
:=\bigl(\nabla_x\hat v\,\sigma\bigr)(t,X_t^{s,x})
\end{equation*}
denotes the martingale integrand furnished by the It\^o computation above.
In particular, $\hat v(t,x)=v(t,x)$ on $[0,T)\times B_R$. Since \(R>0\)
was arbitrary, \(v\) is a classical solution of
\eqref{eq:PDE_v}, with \(\widetilde f\) in place of \(f\), on \(\left[0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d\) and belongs to
$C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}\left(\left[0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d\right)$.
\\~\\

\noindent\emph{Step 3: Identification of the \(Z\)-component.}
Fix \((s,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d\). For \(t<\tau_R\) we have
\(X_t^{s,x}\in B_R\), where \(\hat v=v\) and hence \(\nabla_x\hat v=u\), so
the identification of Step~2 reads
\(Z_t^{s,x}=u(t,X_t^{s,x})\,\sigma(t,X_t^{s,x})\),
\(\mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\)-a.e.\ on \([s,\tau_R]\). Since \(\tau_R=T\)
for all \(R\) large enough almost surely (the paths of \(X^{s,x}\) being
bounded on \([s,T]\)), letting \(R\to\infty\) proves the first claim of
part~(b). Taking \(s=0\) and using the defining relation
\(Z_t^{0,x}=w(t,X_t^{0,x})\), which holds for
\(\mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb P\)-almost every \((t,\omega)\), together with the identification just proved, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
w(t,X_t^{0,x})=u(t,X_t^{0,x})\,\sigma(t,X_t^{0,x})
\qquad
\mathrm{d} t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb P\text{-a.e.}
\end{equation*}
Since the law of \(X_t^{0,x}\) is equivalent to Lebesgue measure
for every \(t\in(0,T)\) by Aronson's bounds \cite{Aronson}, Fubini's theorem then
yields \(w=u\sigma\) Lebesgue-almost everywhere on \([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d\).
Finally, since \(u\sigma\) is continuous on \([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d\) by part~(a)
and coincides almost everywhere with \(w\),
\(\sup_{[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d}\left\lvert u\sigma \right\rvert
=\operatorname*{esssup}_{[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d}\left\lvert w \right\rvert\le C_Z\).
Since also \(\left\lvert v \right\rvert\le C_Y\), this and \eqref{eq:rhoZ_properties} give
\(\widetilde f(t,x,v,u\sigma)=f(t,x,v,u\sigma)\) on \([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d\),
so the equation obtained in Step~2 is \eqref{eq:PDE_v}.
\\~\\
This proves both assertions of Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}.

The argument proceeds in three steps. We first differentiate the
equation for \(v\) by means of difference quotients and use local
Schauder estimates to obtain the improved regularity of \(u\). We then
pass to the limit and identify the differentiated system. Finally, we
derive the structural inequality \eqref{eq:struct_ineq}.
\\~\\
\noindent\emph{Step 1: Differentiating the semilinear equation.}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}, the decoupling field satisfies $v\in
C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}\left(\left[0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d\right)$ and solves \eqref{eq:PDE_v} classically.
This already gives two spatial derivatives of \(v\) with H\"older
control, but for the rigidity argument we need one further degree of regularity for $u$, with Hölder control on its second spatial derivatives and on its time derivative. To justify this, we differentiate
\eqref{eq:PDE_v} using difference quotients.
\\
Fix three cylinders
\begin{equation*}
Q'\Subset Q''\Subset Q\Subset \left[0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d,
\end{equation*}
and let \(k\in\{1,\dots,d\}\). Write
\begin{equation*}
Q=I\times U,
\end{equation*}
where \(I=(a,b)\) with \(0<a<b<T\) or \(I=[0,b)\) with \(0<b<T\), and
\(U\subset \mathbb{R}^d\) is open. For \(h\neq 0\)
sufficiently small, define
\begin{equation*}
\delta_h^k v(t,x)
=
\frac{v(t,x+h e_k)-v(t,x)}{h}.
\end{equation*}
Since \(Q''\Subset Q\), for \(\left\lvert h \right\rvert\) small enough the function
\(\delta_h^k v\) is well defined on \(Q''\). Subtracting the equation
for \(v\left(t,x\right)\) from the equation for
\(v\left(t,x+h e_k\right)\) and dividing by \(h\), we obtain on \(Q''\)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dq_equation}
\partial_t \delta_h^k v
+ \sum_{i,j=1}^d A_{ij}(t,x)\partial_{ij}\delta_h^k v
+ \sum_{i=1}^d b_i(t,x)\partial_i \delta_h^k v
= G_h.
\end{equation}
For brevity, set
\begin{equation*}
\Psi(t,x):=((\nabla_xv)\sigma)(t,x).
\end{equation*}
Then
\begin{align*}
G_h
&=
-\sum_{i,j=1}^d \delta_h^k A_{ij}(t,x)\partial_{ij}v(t,x+h e_k)
-\sum_{i=1}^d \delta_h^k b_i(t,x)\partial_i v(t,x+h e_k)
\\
&\quad
-\frac{1}{h}\Bigl[
f\bigl(t,x+h e_k,v(t,x+h e_k),\Psi(t,x+h e_k)\bigr)
-f\bigl(t,x,v(t,x),\Psi(t,x)\bigr)
\Bigr].
\end{align*}

The last term is handled by the mean value theorem in the variables
\(\left(x,y,z\right)\), applied along the segment joining
\(\Xi(t,x)\) and \(\Xi(t,x+he_k)\), where
\begin{equation*}
\Xi(t,x):=(t,x,v(t,x),\Psi(t,x)).
\end{equation*}
By the definition of \(C_Y\), the bound of
Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}(b), and the convexity of \(\mathcal{S}\) in the
\((y,z)\)-variables, these segments lie in \(\mathcal{S}\). The bracket thus
equals \(h\) times a linear combination of \(1\), \(\delta_h^k v\), and
\(\delta_h^k\bigl((\nabla_x v)\sigma\bigr)\), whose coefficients are the
segment averages of \(\partial_{x_k}f\), \(\partial_y f\), and
\(\nabla_z f\). For \((t,x)\in\overline{Q''}\) and \(\left\lvert h \right\rvert\) small
enough, the segments stay in a fixed compact subset of \(\mathcal{S}\), so the
estimate \eqref{eq:holder_R2} of \ref{it:R2} applies. Since \(v\),
\(\nabla_x v\), and \((\nabla_x v)\sigma\) are locally H\"older
continuous with time exponents at least \(1/2\) for the latter two, the
averaged coefficients are bounded in
\(C^{\alpha/2,\alpha}\left(\overline{Q''}\right)\), uniformly for
\(\left\lvert h \right\rvert\) small enough. Moreover, each difference quotient satisfies
\begin{equation*}
\delta_h^k\varphi(t,x)
=\int_0^1\partial_k\varphi(t,x+\theta he_k)\mathrm{d}\theta.
\end{equation*}
It therefore inherits, uniformly in \(h\), the local H\"older regularity of
\(\partial_k\varphi\). For \(\varphi\in\{A,b\}\), this is supplied by
\ref{it:R1} and the Lipschitz continuity of \(\sigma\). For
\(\varphi\in\{v,(\nabla_x v)\sigma\}\), it follows from
\(v\in C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}\) together with
\begin{equation*}
\partial_k\bigl((\nabla_x v)\sigma\bigr)
=(\partial_k\nabla_x v)\,\sigma+(\nabla_x v)\,\partial_k\sigma,
\end{equation*}
where the last term uses \ref{it:R1} once more. Consequently, \(G_h\) is bounded
in \(C^{\alpha/2,\alpha}\left(\overline{Q''}\right)\), uniformly for
\(\left\lvert h \right\rvert\) small enough.
\\~\\
\noindent\emph{Step 2: Passage to the limit and the differentiated system.}
We now convert the difference-quotient equation into genuine
regularity for \(u=\nabla_x v\). For \(\left\lvert h \right\rvert\) small enough, the
function \(\delta_h^k v\) solves on \(Q''\) a linear parabolic equation
with H\"older coefficients and H\"older right-hand side, uniformly in
\(h\). Therefore, the interior Schauder theory for linear parabolic
equations on bounded cylinders, see Friedman
\cite[Chapter~3, Theorem~5]{friedman} and Krylov
\cite[Theorem~8.11.1]{Krylov1996}, yields a constant
\(C=C\left(Q'',Q',\alpha\right)\), independent of \(h\), such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dq_schauder_bound}
\left\lVert \delta_h^k v \right\rVert_{C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}(Q')}
\leq
C
\left(
\left\lVert \delta_h^k v \right\rVert_{C(\overline{Q''})}
+
\left\lVert G_h \right\rVert_{C^{\alpha/2,\alpha}(\overline{Q''})}
\right).
\end{equation}
By the estimates obtained in Step~1, the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:dq_schauder_bound} is bounded uniformly for \(\left\lvert h \right\rvert\)
small enough, and hence there exists \(h_0>0\) such that
\begin{equation*}
\sup_{0<\left\lvert h \right\rvert<h_0}
\left\lVert \delta_h^k v \right\rVert_{C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}(Q')}
<\infty.
\end{equation*}

Since \(v\) is already \(C^1\) in the space variable, we know that
\(\delta_h^k v \longrightarrow \partial_k v\) locally uniformly on
\(Q'\) as \(h\to 0\). Combined with the uniform bound
\eqref{eq:dq_schauder_bound}, the Arzelà--Ascoli theorem yields
\(\delta_h^k v \longrightarrow \partial_k v\) in
\(C^{1+\beta/2,2+\beta}(Q')\) for every \(\beta\in(0,\alpha)\). In
particular, \(\partial_t\delta_h^k v\to\partial_t\partial_k v\) and
\(\partial_{ij}\delta_h^k v\to\partial_{ij}\partial_k v\) uniformly on
\(Q'\). By lower semicontinuity of the Hölder seminorms under uniform
convergence, the bound \eqref{eq:dq_schauder_bound} passes to the
limit, and
\begin{equation*}
\partial_k v\in C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}(Q').
\end{equation*}
As \(Q'\Subset Q''\Subset Q\Subset \left[0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d\) and
\(k\) were arbitrary, we conclude that
\begin{equation*}
u=\nabla_x v\in C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}\left([0,T)\times \mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d\right).
\end{equation*}

Once this regularity is available, one may pass to the limit in
\eqref{eq:dq_equation} as \(h\to 0\), which yields the differentiated
system \eqref{eq:PDE_u} in the classical sense.
\\~\\
\noindent\emph{Step 3: The structural inequality.}
We now derive \eqref{eq:struct_ineq}. By definition of \(\mathcal{P}\),
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{P} u_k
=
\partial_t u_k
+
\sum_{i,j=1}^d A_{ij}\partial_{ij}u_k
+
\sum_{i,j=1}^d (\partial_i A_{ij})\partial_j u_k.
\end{equation*}
Combining this identity with \eqref{eq:PDE_u}, we see that \(\mathcal{P} u_k\)
is a linear combination of \(u\), \(\nabla_xu\), and the inhomogeneous term \(\partial_{x_k}f\left(t,x,v,u\sigma\right)\).

By Assumption \ref{ass:parabolic}, the
forward-coefficient terms in~\eqref{eq:PDE_u} are bounded on
\([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d\). By the definition of \(C_Y\) and the bound of
Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}(b), one has \(\Xi(t,x)\in\mathcal{S}\) for every
\((t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d\). Since \eqref{eq:lip_fyz} holds on \(\mathcal{S}\), the
bound
\(\left\lvert \partial_y f(t,x,v,u\sigma) \right\rvert+\left\lvert \nabla_z f(t,x,v,u\sigma) \right\rvert\le K_f\)
holds uniformly. For the remaining term, the structural condition \ref{it:B3} gives
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert \partial_{x_k}f(t,x,v,u\sigma) \right\rvert
=
\left\lvert \partial_{x_k}f(t,x,v,u\sigma)-\partial_{x_k}f(t,x,v,0) \right\rvert
\le K_f\left\lvert u\sigma \right\rvert
\le C\left\lvert u \right\rvert,
\end{equation*}
where we used~\eqref{eq:struct_fx_zero}, \eqref{eq:struct_fx_lip}, and
boundedness of \(\sigma\). Hence
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert \mathcal{P} u_k(t,x) \right\rvert
\leq
C\left( \left\lvert u(t,x) \right\rvert + \left\lvert \nabla_xu(t,x) \right\rvert \right),
\qquad
(t,x)\in [0,T)\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
Summing over \(k\) yields \eqref{eq:struct_ineq}, which completes the proof.

We give the argument because the spatial derivative
$\nabla_xf$ is not assumed to be globally bounded, so that a direct
application of a parameter-differentiability theorem would require
additional hypotheses not used elsewhere in the paper. Fix a coordinate
vector $e_j$ and, for $h\ne0$ sufficiently small, set
\begin{equation*}
\Delta_hX:=\frac{X^{s,x+he_j}-X^{s,x}}{h},\qquad
\Delta_hY:=\frac{Y^{s,x+he_j}-Y^{s,x}}{h},\qquad
\Delta_hZ:=\frac{Z^{s,x+he_j}-Z^{s,x}}{h}.
\end{equation*}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:jacobian_flow} and the standard differentiability of the
stochastic flow,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diffquot_X}
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[s,T]}
\left|\Delta_hX_r-J_{s,r}e_j\right|^q\right]
\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{for every }q\ge2.
\end{equation}
Let
$\Theta_r^h=(X_r^{s,x+he_j},Y_r^{s,x+he_j},Z_r^{s,x+he_j})$.
Since $f$ is $C^1$ in $(x,y,z)$, the mean-value formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diffquot_bsde}
\Delta_hY_t
=
\Delta_hg
+
\int_t^T
\bigl(F_r^h+a_r^h\Delta_hY_r+c_r^h\Delta_hZ_r\bigr)\,\mathrm{d} r
-
\int_t^T\Delta_hZ_r\,\mathrm{d} W_r,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
\Delta_hg
&=
\int_0^1
\nabla_xg\!\left(X_T^{s,x}+\theta(X_T^{s,x+he_j}-X_T^{s,x})\right)
\Delta_hX_T\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\\
F_r^h
&=
\int_0^1
\nabla_xf\!\left(r,\Theta_r+\theta(\Theta_r^h-\Theta_r)\right)
\Delta_hX_r\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\\
a_r^h
&=
\int_0^1
\partial_yf\!\left(r,\Theta_r+\theta(\Theta_r^h-\Theta_r)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\\
c_r^h
&=
\int_0^1
\nabla_zf\!\left(r,\Theta_r+\theta(\Theta_r^h-\Theta_r)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.
\end{align*}
Here and below the notation
$\Theta_r+\theta(\Theta_r^h-\Theta_r)$ is understood componentwise.
Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U2} gives
$|a_r^h|+|c_r^h|\le C_f$. Moreover, by
Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U3},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fh_bound}
|F_r^h|
\le
C_f\bigl(|Z_r^{s,x}|+|Z_r^{s,x+he_j}|\bigr)|\Delta_hX_r|.
\end{equation}

We next record explicitly how the dependence of the generator on the
forward state is handled. Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U3}
implies, for every $(r,x,y,z)$,
\begin{equation*}
|\nabla_x f(r,x,y,z)|
\le
|\nabla_x f(r,x,y,z)-\nabla_x f(r,x,y,0)|
\le C_f|z|.
\end{equation*}
Consequently, the fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment
joining $x$ and $x'$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f_x_stability}
|f(r,x',y,z)-f(r,x,y,z)|
\le C_f|z|\,|x'-x|.
\end{equation}
Together with the global Lipschitz bound in $(y,z)$ from
\ref{it:U2} and the boundedness of $\nabla_x g$ from \ref{it:U1}, the
standard $L^2$ stability estimate for Lipschitz BSDEs therefore yields
\begin{align*}
&\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[s,T]}
\left|Y_r^{s,x+he_j}-Y_r^{s,x}\right|^2\right]
+
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_s^T
\left|Z_r^{s,x+he_j}-Z_r^{s,x}\right|^2\,\mathrm{d} r\right]
\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\le
C\,\mathbb{E}\left|X_T^{s,x+he_j}-X_T^{s,x}\right|^2
+
C\,\mathbb{E}\left(
\int_s^T
|Z_r^{s,x}|\,
|X_r^{s,x+he_j}-X_r^{s,x}|\,\mathrm{d} r
\right)^2.
\end{align*}
Since
$X^{s,x+he_j}-X^{s,x}=h\Delta_hX$, the first term on the right tends
to zero by \eqref{eq:diffquot_X}. For the second one, Cauchy--Schwarz
gives
\begin{multline*}
\mathbb{E}\left(
\int_s^T
|Z_r^{s,x}|\,
|X_r^{s,x+he_j}-X_r^{s,x}|\,\mathrm{d} r
\right)^2
\\
\le
T|h|^2
\left(
\mathbb{E}\sup_{r\in[s,T]}|\Delta_hX_r|^4
\right)^{1/2}
\left(
\mathbb{E}\left(\int_s^T|Z_r^{s,x}|^2\,\mathrm{d} r\right)^2
\right)^{1/2},
\end{multline*}
which also tends to zero by \eqref{eq:diffquot_X} with $q=4$ and the
$L^4$ estimate for $Z$ established in Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}.
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:YZ_state_stability}
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[s,T]}
\left|Y_r^{s,x+he_j}-Y_r^{s,x}\right|^2\right]
+
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_s^T
\left|Z_r^{s,x+he_j}-Z_r^{s,x}\right|^2\,\mathrm{d} r\right]
\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
In addition, the $L^q$ estimate of \cite[Theorem~4.2]{BDHPS}, the flow
moment bounds \eqref{eq:flow_moments}, and \eqref{eq:Fh_bound} show that,
for $h$ in a fixed neighbourhood of zero, the terminal variables
$\Delta_hg$ and the sources $F^h$ are uniformly square integrable. Hence
the linear BSDE estimate applied to \eqref{eq:diffquot_bsde} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diffquot_uniform}
\sup_{0<|h|\le h_0}
\left(
\mathbb{E}\sup_{r\in[s,T]}|\Delta_hY_r|^2
+
\mathbb{E}\int_s^T|\Delta_hZ_r|^2\,\mathrm{d} r
\right)<\infty.
\end{equation}

Let $(U^{(j)},V^{(j)})$ denote the unique solution of the linear BSDE
obtained by replacing in \eqref{eq:diffquot_bsde} $\Delta_hg,\ F^h,\
a^h,\ c^h$ respectively by
\begin{equation*}
\nabla_xg(X_T^{s,x})J_{s,T}e_j,
\quad
\nabla_xf(r,\Theta_r)J_{s,r}e_j,
\quad
\partial_yf(r,\Theta_r),
\quad
\nabla_zf(r,\Theta_r).
\end{equation*}
This limiting linear BSDE admits a unique adapted solution satisfying
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[s,T]}|U_r^{(j)}|^2
+\int_s^T|V_r^{(j)}|^2\,\mathrm{d} r\right]<\infty.
\end{equation*}
Indeed, $\partial_yf$ and $\nabla_zf$ are bounded, while
$|\nabla_xf(r,\Theta_r)|\le C_f|Z_r^{s,x}|$ by
\ref{it:U3}, and the latter source is square integrable after multiplication
by $J_{s,r}$ by \eqref{eq:flow_moments} and the $L^q$ estimates for $Z$.
We now justify the passage to the limit in the coefficients and in the
source. This is the only point where the local character of
Assumption~\ref{ass:extra_reg}\ref{it:R2} has to be taken into account.
Let
\begin{equation*}
\mu(\mathrm{d} r,\mathrm{d}\omega):=\mathrm{d} r\,\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega)
\end{equation*}
be the product measure on $[s,T]\times\Omega$. The convergence
\eqref{eq:diffquot_X} and the preceding BSDE stability imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Theta_h_measure}
(X^{s,x+he_j},Y^{s,x+he_j},Z^{s,x+he_j})
\longrightarrow
(X^{s,x},Y^{s,x},Z^{s,x})
\qquad\text{in }\mu\text{-measure}.
\end{equation}
For $R>0$, introduce
\begin{equation*}
\Gamma_R^h
:=
\bigl\{(r,\omega):
|X_r^{s,x}|\vee |X_r^{s,x+he_j}|
\vee |Y_r^{s,x}|\vee |Y_r^{s,x+he_j}|
\vee |Z_r^{s,x}|\vee |Z_r^{s,x+he_j}|
\le R\bigr\}.
\end{equation*}
On $\Gamma_R^h$, every segment joining $\Theta_r$ and $\Theta_r^h$
lies in a fixed compact subset $K_R\subset\mathcal{S}$: the $x$--coordinates
remain in a fixed ball, while the $y$-- and $z$--coordinates remain in
the solution strip by convexity. Hence \eqref{eq:holder_R2} applies on
$K_R$. Moreover, the flow moment bounds and the $L^q$ estimates for the
BSDE, uniformly for $x+he_j$ with $|h|\le h_0$, give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gamma_tail}
\lim_{R\to\infty}
\sup_{0<|h|\le h_0}
\mu\bigl((\Gamma_R^h)^c\bigr)=0.
\end{equation}
It follows from \eqref{eq:Theta_h_measure}--\eqref{eq:Gamma_tail} and
\ref{it:R2} that the segment averages defining $a^h$ and $c^h$ converge
in $\mu$-measure to
$\partial_yf(\cdot,\Theta)$ and $\nabla_zf(\cdot,\Theta)$, respectively.
Since these coefficients are uniformly bounded by
Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U2}, we in fact have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ac_convergence}
a^h\longrightarrow \partial_y f(\cdot,\Theta)
\quad\text{and}\quad
c^h\longrightarrow \nabla_z f(\cdot,\Theta)
\qquad\text{in }L^p(\mu)
\end{equation}
for every finite $p\ge1$.

The source term requires a little more care because $\nabla_xf$ is not
bounded. Set
\begin{equation*}
\overline{\nabla_x f}^{\,h}_r
:=
\int_0^1
\nabla_xf\!\left(r,\Theta_r+\theta(\Theta_r^h-\Theta_r)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,
\qquad
F_r^h=\overline{\nabla_x f}^{\,h}_r\,\Delta_hX_r.
\end{equation*}
Write
\begin{align*}
X_r^{h,\theta}&:=X_r^{s,x}+\theta(X_r^{s,x+he_j}-X_r^{s,x}),\\
Y_r^{h,\theta}&:=Y_r^{s,x}+\theta(Y_r^{s,x+he_j}-Y_r^{s,x}),\\
Z_r^{h,\theta}&:=Z_r^{s,x}+\theta(Z_r^{s,x+he_j}-Z_r^{s,x}).
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{equation*}
\overline{\nabla_x f}^{\,h}_r-\nabla_xf(r,\Theta_r)=A_r^h+B_r^h,
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{align*}
A_r^h
&:=\int_0^1\Bigl[
\nabla_xf(r,X_r^{h,\theta},Y_r^{h,\theta},Z_r^{h,\theta})
-\nabla_xf(r,X_r^{h,\theta},Y_r^{h,\theta},Z_r^{s,x})
\Bigr] \,\mathrm{d}\theta,\\
B_r^h
&:=\int_0^1\Bigl[
\nabla_xf(r,X_r^{h,\theta},Y_r^{h,\theta},Z_r^{s,x})
-\nabla_xf(r,X_r^{s,x},Y_r^{s,x},Z_r^{s,x})
\Bigr] \,\mathrm{d}\theta.
\end{align*}
By Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U3},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ah_bound}
|A_r^h|
\le C_f|Z_r^{s,x+he_j}-Z_r^{s,x}|,
\end{equation}
so that $A^h\to0$ in $L^2(\mu)$. For $B^h$, fix $R>0$ and restrict to
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde\Gamma_R^h
:=
\bigl\{(r,\omega):
|X_r^{s,x}|\vee|X_r^{s,x+he_j}|
\vee|Y_r^{s,x}|\vee|Y_r^{s,x+he_j}|
\vee|Z_r^{s,x}|\le R\bigr\}.
\end{equation*}
On this set the points entering $B^h$ remain in a fixed compact subset
of $\mathcal{S}$, and \ref{it:R2} gives
\begin{equation*}
|B_r^h|
\le C_R\left(
|X_r^{s,x+he_j}-X_r^{s,x}|^{\alpha}
+|Y_r^{s,x+he_j}-Y_r^{s,x}|^{\alpha}
\right).
\end{equation*}
Hence $B^h\mathbf 1_{\widetilde\Gamma_R^h}\to0$ in $L^2(\mu)$ by
\eqref{eq:Theta_h_measure}. On the complement, the structural condition
\ref{it:U3} and $\nabla_xf(t,x,y,0)=0$ give the deterministic bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bh_bound}
|B_r^h|\le 2C_f|Z_r^{s,x}|.
\end{equation}
The higher-moment estimates for $X$ and $Y$, together with the
$L^q$ estimates for $Z$, imply by H\"older's inequality that the
$|Z|^2$--weighted contribution of
$(\widetilde\Gamma_R^h)^c$ tends to zero as first $h\to0$ and then
$R\to\infty$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fxbar_convergence}
\overline{\nabla_x f}^{\,h}
\longrightarrow
\nabla_xf(\cdot,\Theta)
\qquad\text{in }L^2(\mu).
\end{equation}

We can now pass to the limit in $F^h$. Using
\begin{align*}
F_r^h-
\nabla_xf(r,\Theta_r)J_{s,r}e_j
&=
\left(\overline{\nabla_x f}^{\,h}_r-
\nabla_xf(r,\Theta_r)\right)\Delta_hX_r+
\nabla_xf(r,\Theta_r)
\left(\Delta_hX_r-J_{s,r}e_j\right),
\end{align*}
the second term tends to zero in $L^2(\mu)$ because
$|\nabla_xf(r,\Theta_r)|\le C_f|Z_r^{s,x}|$, the process $Z^{s,x}$
satisfies the $L^q$ estimates, and \eqref{eq:diffquot_X} holds for every
$q\ge2$. For the first term, truncate according to
$\sup_{r\in[s,T]}|\Delta_hX_r|\le M$. On this event,
\eqref{eq:fxbar_convergence} gives convergence to zero in $L^2(\mu)$.
On its complement, \ref{it:U3}, the uniform moment bounds for
$\Delta_hX$, and the $L^q$ estimates for $Z^{s,x}$ and
$Z^{s,x+he_j}$ make the squared products uniformly integrable. Letting
first $h\to0$, then $M\to\infty$, yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fh_convergence}
\mathbb{E}\int_s^T
\left|F_r^h-
\nabla_xf(r,\Theta_r)J_{s,r}e_j\right|^2\,\mathrm{d} r
\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}

The terminal term is handled in the same way, but more simply. Since
$\nabla_xg$ is bounded and continuous by \ref{it:U1}, localization on
$\{|X_T^{s,x}|\vee|X_T^{s,x+he_j}|\le R\}$, followed by removal of the
localization using the flow moment bounds, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Deltag_convergence}
\Delta_hg
\longrightarrow
\nabla_xg(X_T^{s,x})J_{s,T}e_j
\qquad\text{in }L^2(\Omega).
\end{equation}
Finally, since the differences in \eqref{eq:ac_convergence} are
uniformly bounded and $(U^{(j)},V^{(j)})$ satisfies the
square-integrability estimate above, we have
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}\int_s^T
|a_r^h-\partial_yf(r,\Theta_r)|^2|U_r^{(j)}|^2\,\mathrm{d} r
&\longrightarrow0,
\end{align*}
as well as
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}\int_s^T
|c_r^h-\nabla_zf(r,\Theta_r)|^2|V_r^{(j)}|^2\,\mathrm{d} r
&\longrightarrow0.
\end{align*}
Thus \eqref{eq:Fh_convergence}, \eqref{eq:Deltag_convergence}, and the
last two limits are precisely the data convergence required by the
standard $L^2$ stability estimate for linear BSDEs. Therefore
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[s,T]}|\Delta_hY_r-U_r^{(j)}|^2\right]
+
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_s^T|\Delta_hZ_r-V_r^{(j)}|^2\,\mathrm{d} r\right]
\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation*}
This is precisely \eqref{eq:bsde_diffquot_conv}. Doing this for
$j=1,\ldots,d$ proves differentiability with respect to the initial state
in the stated sense. Collecting the coordinate derivatives gives the
asserted matrix-valued pair $(U^{s,x},V^{s,x})$, which satisfies
\eqref{eq:UV_integrability} and the displayed variational BSDE.

\label{prop:u_bound}
Assume \ref{ass:rigidity}. Let $(v,w)$ be the Markovian solution fixed in Assumption~\ref{ass:backward} and set $u := \nabla_x v$. Then there exists a constant $C>0$, depending only on the
structural data, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:u_bound}
\left\lvert u(t,x) \right\rvert
\le
e^{C(T-t)/2}\left\lVert \nabla_x g \right\rVert_\infty,
\qquad
(t,x)\in [0,T)\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation}
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Fix $(s,x)\in [0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:jacobian_flow} the
Jacobian flow $J_{s,t}:=\nabla_x X^{s,x}_t$ of \eqref{eq:SDE} is invertible on $[s,T]$ and satisfies \eqref{eq:flow_moments}.
 
\medskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1: Variational FBSDE.}
Set $U^{s,x}_t := \nabla_x Y^{s,x}_t$ and $V^{s,x}_t := \nabla_x Z^{s,x}_t$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:bsde_diff}, these derivatives exist, satisfy
\eqref{eq:UV_integrability}, and $(U^{s,x},V^{s,x})$ is the unique
adapted pair with this integrability property that solves the linear FBSDE
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var_FBSDE}
U^{s,x}_t
=
\nabla_x g(X^{s,x}_T)\,J_{s,T}
+ \int_t^T \mathcal G_r\mathrm{d} r
- \int_t^T V^{s,x}_r\mathrm{d} W_r,
\qquad t\in [s,T],
\end{equation}
with driver
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal G_r
=
\nabla_x f(r,\Xi_r)\,J_{s,r}
+ \partial_y f(r,\Xi_r)\,U^{s,x}_r
+ \nabla_z f(r,\Xi_r)\,V^{s,x}_r,
\end{equation*}
where $\Xi_r$ denotes the forward state together with the Markovian
solution along it,
\begin{equation*}
\Xi_r
:=
\left(
X^{s,x}_r,\,
v\left(r,X^{s,x}_r\right),\,
u\left(r,X^{s,x}_r\right)\sigma\left(r,X^{s,x}_r\right)
\right).
\end{equation*}
By Definition~\ref{defn:markov_sol} and Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}(b), the
second and third components of \(\Xi_r\) coincide with \(Y_r^{s,x}\) and,
for \(\mathrm{d} r\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\)-almost every \((r,\omega)\), with
\(Z_r^{s,x}\). Therefore, the driver \(\mathcal G\) is exactly the variational driver obtained in Lemma~\ref{lem:bsde_diff}. By Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U2} the driver is Lipschitz in
$(U,V)$ with constant $C_f$. By
Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U3},
$\left\lvert \nabla_x f(r,\Xi_r) \right\rvert\le C_f\left\lvert Z^{s,x}_r \right\rvert$, and the standard
$L^q$ estimate for Markovian BSDEs with polynomial-growth data
\cite[Theorem~4.2]{BDHPS} yields
$\mathbb{E}\bigl(\int_s^T\left\lvert Z^{s,x}_r \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} r\bigr)^{q/2}<\infty$ for every
$q\ge 2$. Combined with \eqref{eq:flow_moments}, both the source
$\mathcal G_r - \partial_y f(r,\Xi_r)U^{s,x}_r - \nabla_z f(r,\Xi_r)V^{s,x}_r$
and the terminal $\nabla_x g(X^{s,x}_T)\,J_{s,T}$ belong to $L^q$ for
every $q\ge 2$, using Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U1} for the
terminal term. Applying \cite[Theorem~4.2]{BDHPS} once more to
\eqref{eq:var_FBSDE},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:UV_moments}
\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in [s,T]} \left\lvert U^{s,x}_t \right\rvert^q
< \infty
\qquad\text{for every } q\ge 2.
\end{equation}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}, $Y^{s,x}_t = v(t,X^{s,x}_t)$ on $[s,T)$
with $v\in C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}_\mathrm{loc}$. The chain rule, justified by
the $C^1$ regularity of the flow, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:identification}
U^{s,x}_t = u(t,X^{s,x}_t)\,J_{s,t},
\qquad t\in [s,T),\ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
\end{equation}
 
\noindent\emph{Step 2: Continuity at the terminal time.}
Being the first component of the solution of \eqref{eq:var_FBSDE},
$U^{s,x}$ has a.s.\ continuous sample paths on $[s,T]$, and
\begin{equation*}
U^{s,x}_t
\xrightarrow{t\toT^-}
U^{s,x}_T
=
\nabla_x g(X^{s,x}_T)\,J_{s,T}
\qquad \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
\end{equation*}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:jacobian_flow}, $J_{s,t}^{-1}\to J_{s,T}^{-1}$ a.s.\ as
$t\toT^-$. Combined with \eqref{eq:identification},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:path_along}
u(t,X^{s,x}_t)
=
U^{s,x}_t\,J_{s,t}^{-1}
\xrightarrow{t\toT^-}
\nabla_x g(X^{s,x}_T)
\qquad \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
\end{equation}
 
\noindent\emph{Step 3: Submartingale on truncated intervals.}
Set, for $t\in[s,T)$,
\begin{equation*}
\bar Y_t := u(t,X^{s,x}_t),
\qquad
\bar Z_t^{k\ell} := \sum_i \sigma_{i\ell}(t,X^{s,x}_t)\,\partial_i
u_k(t,X^{s,x}_t).
\end{equation*}
Both processes are well defined by Proposition~\ref{prop:extra_reg}. By \eqref{eq:identification},
$\bar Y_r = U^{s,x}_r J_{s,r}^{-1}$ on $[s,T)$. Together with
\eqref{eq:UV_moments}, \eqref{eq:flow_moments}, and Cauchy--Schwarz,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ybar_moments}
\mathbb{E}\sup_{r\in [s,T)} \left\lvert \bar Y_r \right\rvert^q
\le
\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[s,T]}\left\lvert U^{s,x}_t \right\rvert^{2q}\Bigr)^{1/2}
\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[s,T]}\left\lVert J_{s,t}^{-1} \right\rVert^{2q}\Bigr)^{1/2}
< \infty,
\qquad q\ge 2.
\end{equation}
Applying It\^o's formula componentwise
to $u_k(t,X^{s,x}_t)$ and substituting \eqref{eq:PDE_u} yields, for each
$k$,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{d}\bar Y_t^k = -G_t^k\mathrm{d} t + \sum_\ell \bar Z_t^{k\ell}\mathrm{d} W_t^\ell,
\qquad t\in [s,T),
\end{equation*}
with
\begin{align*}
G_t^k
=\ &\sum_i (\partial_k b_i)\bar Y_t^i
+ \sum_{i,j} (\partial_k A_{ij})\,\partial_i u_j(t,X^{s,x}_t)
+ \partial_{x_k}f(t,\Xi_t)
+ \partial_y f(t,\Xi_t)\bar Y_t^k
\\
&+ \sum_\ell \partial_{z_\ell}f(t,\Xi_t)\!
\left[\,\bar Z_t^{k\ell}
+ \sum_i \bar Y_t^i\,\partial_k \sigma_{i\ell}(t,X^{s,x}_t) \right].
\end{align*}
By Assumption~\ref{ass:parabolic}\ref{it:P3} the coefficient $\sigma$ is uniformly
elliptic with $\left\lVert \sigma^{-1} \right\rVert_\infty \le \lambda^{-1/2}$, and
$\partial_i u_k = \sum_\ell (\sigma^{-1})_{\ell i}\,\bar Z^{k\ell}$
gives $\left\lvert \partial_i u_k \right\rvert\le \lambda^{-1/2}\left\lvert \bar Z \right\rvert$. Since
$A$ is given by \eqref{eq:A_def}, the bounds on $\sigma$
(\ref{ass:parabolic}\ref{it:P1}) and on $\nabla_x b,\nabla_x\sigma$
(Assumption \ref{ass:flow}) render $\nabla_x b$, $\nabla_x A$,
$\nabla_x\sigma$ bounded. With \ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U2}
(boundedness of $\partial_y f$ and $\nabla_z f$) and
\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U3}---which, the third component of $\Xi_t$
being $u(t,X^{s,x}_t)\,\sigma(t,X^{s,x}_t)$, gives
$\left\lvert \partial_{x_k}f(t,\Xi_t) \right\rvert \le C_f\left\lvert u(t,X^{s,x}_t)\,\sigma(t,X^{s,x}_t) \right\rvert
\le C_f\left\lVert \sigma \right\rVert_\infty\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert$ pointwise on $[s,T)$, with
$C_f\left\lVert \sigma \right\rVert_\infty<\infty$ by
\ref{ass:parabolic}\ref{it:P1}---there exists a structural
$C^*>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:G_bound}
\left\lvert G_t \right\rvert
\le
C^*\bigl(\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert+\left\lvert \bar Z_t \right\rvert\bigr),
\qquad t\in [s,T).
\end{equation}
It\^o's formula on $\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert^2$ gives
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{d}\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert^2
=
-2\langle\bar Y_t, G_t\rangle\mathrm{d} t
+ \left\lvert \bar Z_t \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} t
+ 2\langle\bar Y_t, \bar Z_t\mathrm{d} W_t\rangle.
\end{equation*}
Using \eqref{eq:G_bound} and Young's
$2C^*\left\lvert \bar Y \right\rvert\left\lvert \bar Z \right\rvert\le (C^*)^2\left\lvert \bar Y \right\rvert^2 + \left\lvert \bar
Z \right\rvert^2$,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{d}\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert^2
\ge
-C\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert^2\mathrm{d} t
+ 2\langle\bar Y_t,\bar Z_t\mathrm{d} W_t\rangle,
\qquad
C := 2C^* + (C^*)^2,
\end{equation*}
so that $e^{Ct}\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert^2$ is a non-negative continuous local
submartingale on $[s,T)$. Fix $\varepsilon\in (0,T-s)$ and let
$\tau_n := \inf\{t\ge s : \left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert+\left\lvert \bar Z_t \right\rvert\ge n\}
\wedge (T-\varepsilon)$. On $[s,\tau_n]$ the integrand
$\langle\bar Y,\bar Z\rangle$ is bounded, so the stopped process
$e^{C(t\wedge\tau_n)}\left\lvert \bar Y_{t\wedge\tau_n} \right\rvert^2$ is a true
submartingale on $[s,T-\varepsilon]$, and for every
$t\in [s,T-\varepsilon]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stopped_submart}
\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[e^{C((T-\varepsilon)\wedge\tau_n)}
\left\lvert \bar Y_{(T-\varepsilon)\wedge\tau_n} \right\rvert^2\right]
\ge
e^{C(t\wedge\tau_n)}\left\lvert \bar Y_{t\wedge\tau_n} \right\rvert^2.
\end{equation}
Now $u$ and $\nabla_x u$ are continuous on $[s,T-\varepsilon]\times\mathbb{R}^d$
by Proposition~\ref{prop:extra_reg}, and the random set
$\{X^{s,x}_r : r\in [s,T-\varepsilon]\}$ is a.s.\ compact, so
$\sup_{r\in [s,T-\varepsilon]}(\left\lvert \bar Y_r \right\rvert+\left\lvert \bar Z_r \right\rvert)<\infty$
a.s., whence $\tau_n\to T-\varepsilon$ a.s.\ as $n\to\infty$. Passing
to the limit in \eqref{eq:stopped_submart} via dominated convergence,
with the $L^1$ dominator $e^{CT}\sup_{r\in [s,T)}\left\lvert \bar Y_r \right\rvert^2$
furnished by \eqref{eq:Ybar_moments}, yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:submart}
e^{Ct}\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert^2
\le
\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[e^{C(T-\varepsilon)}\left\lvert \bar Y_{T-\varepsilon} \right\rvert^2\right],
\qquad t\in [s,T-\varepsilon].
\end{equation}
 
\noindent\emph{Step 4: Limit $\varepsilon\to 0$.}
By \eqref{eq:Ybar_moments} with $q=4$,
$\sup_{r\in [s,T)}\mathbb{E}\left\lvert \bar Y_r \right\rvert^4<\infty$, so
$\{\left\lvert \bar Y_r \right\rvert^2\}_{r\in [s,T)}$ is uniformly integrable and
\eqref{eq:path_along} upgrades to convergence in $L^2(\mathbb{P})$. Passing to
the limit $\varepsilon\to 0$ in \eqref{eq:submart},
\begin{equation*}
e^{Ct}\left\lvert \bar Y_t \right\rvert^2
\le
e^{CT}\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\left\lvert \nabla_x g(X^{s,x}_T) \right\rvert^2\right]
\le
e^{CT}\left\lVert \nabla_x g \right\rVert_\infty^2
\qquad \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.,}
\end{equation*}
for every $t\in [s,T)$. The right-hand side is finite by
Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U1}. Specialising to $t=s$, where
$\bar Y_s = u(s,x)$,
\begin{equation*}
\left\lvert u(s,x) \right\rvert^2
\le
e^{C(T-s)}\left\lVert \nabla_x g \right\rVert_\infty^2.
\end{equation*}
The point $(s,x)\in [0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d$ being arbitrary,
\eqref{eq:u_bound} follows.

\label{prop:grad_slab_bound}
Assume \ref{ass:rigidity}. Let $(v,w)$ be the Markovian solution fixed in Assumption~\ref{ass:backward} and set $u := \nabla_x v$. Then, for every $\varepsilon\in\left(0,T/2\right)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grad_slab_bound}

\left\lvert \nabla_x u(t,x) \right\rvert < \infty.
\end{equation}
<!-- end prop-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We prove \eqref{eq:grad_slab_bound} in four steps.
 
\medskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1: Reduction to a linear system with bounded data.}
 
Set $M_0:=\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d}\left\lvert u(t,x) \right\rvert$, which is finite by
Proposition~\ref{prop:u_bound}. By Proposition~\ref{prop:extra_reg},
$u\in C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d)$ and solves
\eqref{eq:PDE_u} classically on $[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d$.
 
For each $k$, the second-order part of \eqref{eq:PDE_u} is
$\sum_{i,j=1}^d A_{ij}\partial_{ij}u_k$, the same scalar operator on
every component. The principal part is therefore diagonal, and the
components couple only at lower order, the sole first-order inter-component coupling
being the term $\sum_{i,j}(\partial_k A_{ij})\partial_i u_j$. By Remark \ref{rem:forward_roles}, the matrix
$A$ is bounded, uniformly elliptic and globally Lipschitz on
$[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^d$ --- hence uniformly continuous.
 
Each first- and zeroth-order coefficient of \eqref{eq:PDE_u} is bounded
on $[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d$: those formed from $b,\sigma,\nabla_x b,\nabla_x\sigma$
by \ref{ass:parabolic}\ref{it:P1} and Assumption \ref{ass:flow}, and
$\partial_y f(t,x,v,u\sigma)$, $\nabla_z f(t,x,v,u\sigma)$ by the bound
$\left\lvert \partial_y f \right\rvert+\left\lvert \nabla_z f \right\rvert\le C_f$ of
Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U2}, valid at every value of $y$.
The right-hand side of \eqref{eq:PDE_u} is
$F:=-\nabla_x f(\cdot,\cdot,v,u\sigma)$. By the structural identity
$\nabla_x f(t,x,v,0)=0$ and the $z$-Lipschitz bound of $\nabla_x f$
(Assumption~\ref{ass:rigidity}\ref{it:U3}),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source_bound}
\left\lvert F \right\rvert
=\left\lvert \nabla_x f(t,x,v,u\sigma)-\nabla_x f(t,x,v,0) \right\rvert
\le C_f\left\lvert u\sigma \right\rvert
\le C_f\left\lVert \sigma \right\rVert_\infty M_0,
\end{equation}
using $\left\lvert u \right\rvert\le M_0$. Since $u\in C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}$, it
lies in the parabolic Sobolev space
$W^{2,1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d)$, i.e.\ $\partial_t
u,\nabla_x u,\nabla_x^2 u\in L^p_{\mathrm{loc}}$, for every $p\in[1,\infty)$, and
is a strong solution of the linear, uniformly parabolic system
\eqref{eq:PDE_u}, with diagonal scalar principal part, bounded
lower-order coefficients, and right-hand side $F$ bounded as in
\eqref{eq:source_bound}.
 
\medskip
\noindent\emph{Step 2: Interior $L^p$ estimate on translated cylinders.}
 
Fix $p:=d+3$ and $\varepsilon\in\left(0,T/2\right)$. For $x_*\in\mathbb{R}^d$, set
the open cylinders
\begin{equation*}
Q'(x_*):=(\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon)\times B_1(x_*),
\qquad
Q(x_*):=\bigl(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},T-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigr)\times B_2(x_*),
\end{equation*}
so that $Q'(x_*)\Subset Q(x_*)$ and $\overline{Q(x_*)}\subset[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^d$.
By Step~1, $u\in C^{1+\alpha/2,2+\alpha}(\overline{Q(x_*)})$, hence
$u\in W^{2,1,p}(Q(x_*);\mathbb{R}^d)$, and $u$ solves on $Q(x_*)$ the system
\eqref{eq:PDE_u}, which is uniformly parabolic with diagonal scalar
principal part and bounded lower-order coefficients. The interior
$W^{2,1,p}$ estimate for such systems (Solonnikov~\cite[Theorem~5.7]{Solonnikov}) provides a constant
$C_\varepsilon>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lp_estimate}
\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{W^{2,1,p}(Q'(x_*))}
\le
C_\varepsilon\bigl(\left\lVert F \right\rVert_{L^p(Q(x_*))}+\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{L^p(Q(x_*))}\bigr).
\end{equation}
The constant $C_\varepsilon$ depends only on $d$, $p$, the ellipticity
constant and modulus of continuity of $A$, the bounds on the lower-order
coefficients, and the parabolic distance from $Q'(x_*)$ to
$\partial Q(x_*)$. These cylinders
are spatial translates of $Q'(0)$ and $Q(0)$, so their sizes and
parabolic separation are independent of $x_*$, and the remaining
quantities are global by Step~1. Hence $C_\varepsilon$ is independent of
$x_*$.
 
By the bounds $\left\lvert F \right\rvert\le C_f\left\lVert \sigma \right\rVert_\infty M_0$
(see~\eqref{eq:source_bound}) and $\left\lvert u \right\rvert\le M_0$ of Step~1, and since
$\left\lvert Q(x_*) \right\rvert=(T-\varepsilon)\left\lvert B_2 \right\rvert$ is independent of $x_*$,
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert F \right\rVert_{L^p(Q(x_*))}+\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{L^p(Q(x_*))}
\le
\left(C_f\left\lVert \sigma \right\rVert_\infty+1\right)M_0\,
\bigl((T-\varepsilon)\left\lvert B_2 \right\rvert\bigr)^{1/p}.
\end{equation*}
Together with \eqref{eq:Lp_estimate}, this yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lp_uniform}
\sup_{x_*\in\mathbb{R}^d}\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{W^{2,1,p}(Q'(x_*))}
\le
C_\varepsilon\left(C_f\left\lVert \sigma \right\rVert_\infty+1\right)M_0
\bigl((T-\varepsilon)\left\lvert B_2 \right\rvert\bigr)^{1/p}
<\infty,
\end{equation}
where $\left\lvert B_2 \right\rvert$ is the volume of a ball of radius $2$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$.
 
\medskip
\noindent\emph{Step 3: Parabolic Sobolev embedding.}
 
Set $\beta:=1-(d+2)/p=\tfrac{1}{d+3}\in(0,1)$. As $p=d+3>d+2$, the
parabolic Sobolev embedding (\cite[Ch.~II, Lemma~3.3]{LadSolUra67}),
applied componentwise, provides a constant $C_S$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:embedding}
\sup_{\overline{Q'(x_*)}}\left\lvert \nabla_x u \right\rvert
\le
\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{C^{(1+\beta)/2,\,1+\beta}(\overline{Q'(x_*)})}
\le
C_S\,\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{W^{2,1,p}(Q'(x_*))},
\qquad x_*\in\mathbb{R}^d,
\end{equation}
the first inequality because that norm dominates $\sup\left\lvert \nabla_x u \right\rvert$.
The constant $C_S$ depends only on $d$, $p$, and the geometry of
$Q'(x_*)$, hence not on $x_*$, the cylinders being translates of $Q'(0)$.
Taking the supremum over $x_*$ in \eqref{eq:embedding} and using
\eqref{eq:Lp_uniform},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grad_uniform_cyl}
\sup_{x_*\in\mathbb{R}^d}\ \sup_{\overline{Q'(x_*)}}\left\lvert \nabla_x u \right\rvert
\le
C_S\,\sup_{x_*\in\mathbb{R}^d}\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{W^{2,1,p}(Q'(x_*))}
<\infty.
\end{equation}
 
\medskip
\noindent\emph{Step 4: Covering and conclusion.}
 
Every point $(t,x)\in[\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon]\times\mathbb{R}^d$ lies in
$\overline{Q'(x)}=[\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon]\times\overline{B_1(x)}$,
since $t\in[\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon]$ and $x\in\overline{B_1(x)}$.
Therefore
\begin{equation*}
\sup_{(t,x)\in[\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon]\times\mathbb{R}^d}\left\lvert \nabla_x u(t,x) \right\rvert
\le
\sup_{x_*\in\mathbb{R}^d}\ \sup_{\overline{Q'(x_*)}}\left\lvert \nabla_x u \right\rvert
<\infty,
\end{equation*}
the last inequality by \eqref{eq:grad_uniform_cyl}. This is
\eqref{eq:grad_slab_bound}. As $\varepsilon\in(0,T/2)$ was arbitrary, the
bound holds for every such $\varepsilon$.

Assume \ref{ass:rigidity}. Then, for every $t\in[0,T)$,

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
By Assumption~\ref{ass:flow}, $X_t^{0,x_0}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}$ for every
$t\in[0,T]$. See Nualart \cite[Theorem~2.2.1]{dNualart}. Fix $t<T$.

Proposition~\ref{prop:u_bound} yields the global bound
\begin{equation*}
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^d}|\nabla_xv(t,x)|<\infty.
\end{equation*}
Thus $v(t,\cdot)$ is continuously differentiable with bounded gradient, and the
Malliavin chain rule \cite[Proposition~1.2.3]{dNualart} applies directly to
$Y_t^{0,x_0}=v(t,X_t^{0,x_0})$: it gives
$Y_t^{0,x_0}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}$ together with \eqref{eq:DY_formula}.

\label{lem:uc}
Let $u \colon \left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ belong to $C^{1,2}\left(\left(0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d\right)$. With Assumption \ref{ass:parabolic} in place, suppose there exists $C>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_ass}
\left\lvert \mathcal{P} u(t,x) \right\rvert \leq C\left( \left\lvert u(t,x) \right\rvert + \left\lvert \nabla_xu(t,x) \right\rvert \right),
\qquad (t,x)\in (0,T)\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation}
If for some $t_0\in \left(0,T\right)$ the set $\{x\in \mathbb{R}^d : u\left(t_0,x\right)=0\}$ has positive Lebesgue measure, then $u\left(t_0,\cdot\right)\equiv 0$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$.

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The proof combines a density-point argument with quantitative local
estimates for \(u\), namely Caccioppoli-type bounds and a two-sphere
one-cylinder inequality.

We first make precise how the scalar estimates of
\cite{lEscauriaza2006} are used for the vector-valued function
\(u=(u^1,\dots,u^d)\). The operator \(\mathcal{P}\) acts componentwise, with the
same principal coefficients on every component. All the identities in
the scalar argument that are used to obtain the local energy,
frequency/doubling, and Carleman estimates are quadratic. For a
vector-valued function, one applies the corresponding identity to each
component and sums. Equivalently, in the frequency-function part of the
argument one replaces the scalar quadratic quantities by their sums over
the components. All integrations by parts remain componentwise, while
the Cauchy--Schwarz steps are simply the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality in
the Euclidean target space. Likewise, the Carleman estimate is summed
over the components before any differential inequality is invoked.

The crucial point is that the system inequality \eqref{eq:uc_ass} is used
only after this summation. Indeed, the source terms then combine as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_vector_source}
\sum_{k=1}^d |\mathcal{P} u^k|^2
=
|\mathcal{P} u|^2
\leq
C^2\bigl(|u|+|\nabla_xu|\bigr)^2
\leq
2C^2\bigl(|u|^2+|\nabla_xu|^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
Consequently, the lower-order contributions are absorbed after summing,
exactly as in the scalar proof, with constants changed at most by a
factor depending on the constants already present in
\eqref{eq:uc_ass}. The resulting estimates are therefore the same
quadratic estimates with
\begin{equation*}
|u|^2=\sum_{k=1}^d|u^k|^2,
\qquad
|\nabla_xu|^2=\sum_{k=1}^d|\nabla_xu^k|^2.
\end{equation*}
This yields the vector-valued forms of
\cite[Equation~(2.20)]{lEscauriaza2006} and
\cite[Theorem~2]{lEscauriaza2006} used below. Notice in particular that
we never require, and do not claim, separate componentwise inequalities
of the form
\begin{equation*}
|\mathcal{P} u^k|\leq C\bigl(|u^k|+|\nabla_xu^k|\bigr).
\end{equation*}
By an affine change of the time variable, we may assume without loss of
generality that \(T=t_0+17\). This change rescales the coefficients of
\(\mathcal{P}\) and the constant in \eqref{eq:uc_ass} by a constant factor, so
all standing hypotheses are preserved, with constants now depending also
on \(t_0\), which is fixed throughout the proof. Let
\begin{equation*}
G  =  \{x\in \mathbb{R}^d : u(t_0,x)=0\}.
\end{equation*}
By assumption, \(G\) has positive Lebesgue measure. By the Lebesgue
density theorem (see \cite[Theorem~1.35]{lEvans2015}), almost every
point of \(G\) is a Lebesgue density point, that is, a point
\(\bar x\in G\) satisfying
\begin{equation*}
\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{|B_r(\bar x)\cap G|}{|B_r(\bar x)|}=1.
\end{equation*}
Fix such an \(\bar x\). After translating the space variable, we may
assume without loss of generality that \(\bar x=0\), so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_density_point}
\lim_{r\downarrow 0} \frac{|B_r\cap G|}{|B_r|}=1.
\end{equation}
We divide the proof into three steps.
\\~\\
\noindent\emph{Step 1: A small-ball estimate near a density point.}
We argue by contradiction. Suppose first that
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1)}>0.
\end{equation*}
Fix \(\varepsilon>0\). By \eqref{eq:uc_density_point}, there exists
\(r\left(\varepsilon\right)\in \left(0,1/2\right)\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_density_estimate}
|B_r\setminus G|<\varepsilon |B_r|,
\qquad 0<r<r(\varepsilon).
\end{equation}
Since \(u\left(t_0,\cdot\right)=0\) on \(G\), we have, for every
\(r<r\left(\varepsilon\right)\),
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
=
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r\setminus G)}.
\end{equation*}

We now distinguish between the cases \(d\ge2\) and \(d=1\). Suppose first that
\(d\ge2\). Applying H\"older's inequality with exponents \(d\) and
\(d/\left(d-1\right)\), we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_holder}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
\leq
C |B_r\setminus G|^{1/(2d)}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^{2d/(d-1)}(B_r)}.
\end{equation}
We next estimate the \(L^{2d/\left(d-1\right)}\)-norm by the \(L^2\)-norm of the
gradient. Since \(u\left(t_0,\cdot\right)\) vanishes on a subset of \(B_r\) with
measure at least \(|B_r|/2\) when \(r<r\left(1/2\right)\), Lemma~7.16 in
Gilbarg--Trudinger \cite{dGilbarg2001} gives, for each component
\(u^k\) of \(u\),
\begin{equation*}
|u^k(t_0,x)|
\leq
C \int_{B_r} |x-y|^{1-d} |\nabla_xu^k(t_0,y)| \mathrm{d}y,
\qquad \text{for a.e. } x\in B_r.
\end{equation*}
Combining this with Lemma~7.12 in \cite{dGilbarg2001} (with $\mu=1/d$) yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_sobolev_component}
\left\lVert u^k(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^{2d/(d-1)}(B_r)}
\leq
C r^{1/2}
\left\lVert \nabla_xu^k(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}.
\end{equation}
Summing over \(k=1,\dots,d\), we infer
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_sobolev_vector}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^{2d/(d-1)}(B_r)}
\leq
C r^{1/2}
\left\lVert \nabla_xu(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}.
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:uc_density_estimate} and \(|B_r|\asymp r^d\), we therefore obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_pre_caccioppoli_highd}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
\leq
C\varepsilon^{1/(2d)}r\,
\left\lVert \nabla_xu(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}.
\end{equation}

Suppose now that \(d=1\). Then \(B_r=(-r,r)\), and, for
\(r<r(1/2)\), the set \(G\cap B_r\) has positive measure and is therefore
nonempty. Choose \(y_r\in G\cap B_r\). Since
\(u(t_0,y_r)=0\), the fundamental theorem of calculus and
Cauchy--Schwarz give, for every \(x\in B_r\),
\begin{equation*}
|u(t_0,x)|
=
\left|\int_{y_r}^x \partial_xu(t_0,z)\,\mathrm{d} z\right|
\leq
(2r)^{1/2}
\left\lVert \partial_xu(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}.
\end{equation*}
Consequently, using again that \(u(t_0,\cdot)=0\) on \(G\) and
\eqref{eq:uc_density_estimate},
\begin{align}\label{eq:uc_pre_caccioppoli_oned}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
=
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r\setminus G)}
\notag
&\leq
|B_r\setminus G|^{1/2}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^\infty(B_r)}
\notag\\
&\leq
C\varepsilon^{1/2}r\,
\left\lVert \partial_xu(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}.
\end{align}
Since \(1/(2d)=1/2\) when \(d=1\), the estimates
\eqref{eq:uc_pre_caccioppoli_highd} and
\eqref{eq:uc_pre_caccioppoli_oned} can be written uniformly, for every
\(d\ge1\), as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_pre_caccioppoli}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
\leq
C\varepsilon^{1/(2d)}r\,
\left\lVert \nabla_xu(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}.
\end{equation}


To relate the gradient back to \(u\) itself, we use the
vector-valued form of \cite[Equation~(2.20)]{lEscauriaza2006} justified
above by summing the componentwise quadratic estimates and then invoking
\eqref{eq:uc_vector_source}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_caccioppoli}
\left\lVert \nabla_xu(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
\leq
\frac{C(\theta)}{r}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_{2r})},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
\theta
=
\frac{\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{L^2((t_0,t_0+16)\times B_4)}}{\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1)}}.
\end{equation*}
Notice that \(\theta\) is finite, since
\(\left[t_0,t_0+16\right]\times\overline{B_4}\) is a compact subset of
\(\left(0,T\right)\times\mathbb{R}^d\) on which \(u\) is continuous, and is well
defined because we are in the case
\(\left\lVert u\left(t_0,\cdot\right) \right\rVert_{L^2\left(B_1\right)}>0\). Combining
\eqref{eq:uc_pre_caccioppoli} and \eqref{eq:uc_caccioppoli}, we obtain, for every
\(r<r\left(\varepsilon\right)\wedge r\left(1/2\right)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_small_ball}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
\leq
C(\theta)\varepsilon^{1/(2d)}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_{2r})}.
\end{equation}
~\\
\noindent\emph{Step 2: Super-polynomial decay and vanishing on one ball.}
Fix \(\varepsilon>0\) sufficiently small that
\begin{equation*}
C(\theta)\varepsilon^{1/(2d)}<1.
\end{equation*}
For each \(r<r\left(\varepsilon\right)\wedge r\left(1/2\right)\), let \(k\left(r\right)\) be the unique
nonnegative integer such that
\begin{equation*}
2^{k(r)}r \leq r(\varepsilon) < 2^{k(r)+1}r.
\end{equation*}
Iterating \eqref{eq:uc_small_ball} exactly \(k\left(r\right)\) times yields
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
\leq
\left(C(\theta)\varepsilon^{1/(2d)}\right)^{k(r)}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_{2^{k(r)}r})}.
\end{equation*}
Since \(2^{k\left(r\right)}r\le r\left(\varepsilon\right)<1/2\), the ball \(B_{2^{k\left(r\right)}r}\)
is contained in \(B_1\), and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_iterated}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
\leq
\left(C(\theta)\varepsilon^{1/(2d)}\right)^{k(r)}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1)}.
\end{equation}
Moreover, by the definition of \(k\left(r\right)\),
\begin{equation*}
k(r)
\geq
\log_2\left(\frac{r(\varepsilon)}{2r}\right)
=
\log_2\left(\frac{r(\varepsilon)}{r}\right)-1.
\end{equation*}
Hence, \eqref{eq:uc_iterated} implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_iterated_log}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}
\leq
\left(C(\theta)\varepsilon^{1/(2d)}\right)^{\log_2(r(\varepsilon)/r)-1}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1)}.
\end{equation}
We now invoke the vector-valued two-sphere one-cylinder
inequality from \cite[Theorem~2]{lEscauriaza2006}, in the form justified
above. In the present notation it gives
\begin{multline}\label{eq:uc_two_sphere}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1)}
\leq
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_r)}^{1/(1+C(\theta)\log_2(1/r))}
\\
\times
\left(\sqrt{C(\theta)}\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{L^2((t_0,t_0+16)\times B_4)}\right)^{C(\theta)\log_2(1/r)/(1+C(\theta)\log_2(1/r))}.
\end{multline}
Substituting \eqref{eq:uc_iterated_log} into
\eqref{eq:uc_two_sphere}, dividing by the positive quantity
\(\left\lVert u\left(t_0,\cdot\right) \right\rVert_{L^2\left(B_1\right)}^{1/\left(1+C\left(\theta\right)\log_2\left(1/r\right)\right)}\),
and raising both sides to the power
\(\left(1+C\left(\theta\right)\log_2\left(1/r\right)\right)/\left(C\left(\theta\right)\log_2\left(1/r\right)\right)\), we
arrive at
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1)}
\leq
\left(C(\theta)\varepsilon^{1/(2d)}\right)^{\frac{\log_2(r(\varepsilon)/r)-1}{C(\theta)\log_2(1/r)}}
\sqrt{C(\theta)}
\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{L^2((t_0,t_0+16)\times B_4)}.
\end{equation*}
Letting \(r\downarrow 0\), the exponent on the right-hand side
converges to a strictly positive constant depending only on \(\theta\).
Since \(\varepsilon\) can then be sent to \(0\), the right-hand side
converges to \(0\). This contradicts our standing assumption that
\(\left\lVert u\left(t_0,\cdot\right) \right\rVert_{L^2\left(B_1\right)}>0\). We conclude that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_ball_one_zero}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1)}=0.
\end{equation}
~\\
\noindent\emph{Step 3: Propagation from one ball to all of \(\mathbb{R}^d\).}
We first record the translated propagation step that will be used
repeatedly. Fix \(y\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and assume that
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_{1/2}(y))}=0.
\end{equation*}
If
\(\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1(y))}=0\), there is nothing to prove.
Otherwise, define the parameter associated with the cylinder centered at
\(y\) by
\begin{equation*}
\theta_y
=
\frac{\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{L^2((t_0,t_0+16)\times B_4(y))}}
{\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1(y))}}.
\end{equation*}
This quantity is finite, by the same local boundedness argument as for
\(\theta\) above, and is well defined because its denominator is
positive. Applying the translated vector-valued two-sphere
one-cylinder inequality with center \(y\) and radius \(1/2\) gives
\begin{multline*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1(y))}
\leq
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_{1/2}(y))}^{1/(1+C(\theta_y))}
\\
\times
\left(
\sqrt{C(\theta_y)}
\left\lVert u \right\rVert_{L^2((t_0,t_0+16)\times B_4(y))}
\right)^{C(\theta_y)/(1+C(\theta_y))}
=0,
\end{multline*}
which contradicts the alternative under consideration. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uc_propagation_step}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_{1/2}(y))}=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1(y))}=0.
\end{equation}
Now fix \(x_1\in\mathbb{R}^d\) with \(|x_1|\le1/2\). Since
\(B_{1/2}(x_1)\subset B_1\), \eqref{eq:uc_ball_one_zero} and
\eqref{eq:uc_propagation_step} yield
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1(x_1))}=0.
\end{equation*}
If \(x_2\in\mathbb{R}^d\) satisfies \(|x_2-x_1|\le1/2\), then
\(B_{1/2}(x_2)\subset B_1(x_1)\), so another application of
\eqref{eq:uc_propagation_step} gives
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1(x_2))}=0.
\end{equation*}
Iterating along a finite chain of overlapping unit balls --- for every
\(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\), one can choose centers \(x_1,\dots,x_n=x\) such that
\(\left\lvert x_1 \right\rvert\le1/2\) and \(\left\lvert x_{j+1}-x_j \right\rvert\le1/2\) --- we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert u(t_0,\cdot) \right\rVert_{L^2(B_1(x))}=0
\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
Thus \(u(t_0,\cdot)=0\) almost everywhere on \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Since
\(u(t_0,\cdot)\) is continuous, it follows that \(u(t_0,x)=0\) for every
\(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\), as claimed.

\label{lem:bu}
Let $u \colon \left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ satisfy
\begin{equation*}u\in C^{1,2}\left(\left(0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d\right).
\end{equation*}

$M>0$ and $\kappa>0$ such that

\left\lvert u(t,x) \right\rvert + \left\lvert \nabla_xu(t,x) \right\rvert \leq M,
\qquad
\left\lvert \mathcal{P} u(t,x) \right\rvert \leq \kappa\left( \left\lvert u(t,x) \right\rvert + \left\lvert \nabla_xu(t,x) \right\rvert \right),
\end{equation}
for all $\left(t,x\right)\in \left(0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d$. If for some
$t_0\in \left(0,T\right)$ one has $u\left(t_0,\cdot\right)\equiv 0$, then $u \equiv 0$ on $\left[t_0,T\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d$.

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
We argue by combining a local vanishing result, based on a Carleman estimate, together with a time-iteration argument, following Wu and Zhang
\cite{jWu2019}. The only point that requires clarification is the
passage from scalar-valued to vector-valued functions. In the present
setting, \(\mathcal{P}\) acts componentwise on \(u=\left(u^1,\dots,u^d\right)\), and the
relevant Carleman estimate is quadratic in the unknown. Consequently,
although the statements in \cite{jWu2019} are written for scalar
equations, the same Carleman estimate extends to
\(\mathbb{R}^d\)-valued test functions by applying the scalar estimate to each
component and summing. The subsequent local-vanishing argument and its
iteration in time then carry over to the present vector-valued
setting. In particular, no separate pointwise inequalities of the form
$|\mathcal{P} u^k|\le C\left(|u^k|+|\nabla_xu^k|\right)$ are needed.
\\

By the affine change of variables
\begin{equation*}
\tau = \frac{t-t_0}{T-t_0},
\qquad
\widetilde u(\tau,x) = u\left(t_0 + (T-t_0)\tau,x\right),
\end{equation*}
it is enough to prove the statement in the normalized case \(t_0=0\)
and \(T=1\). Indeed, \(\widetilde u\) belongs to
\(C^{1,2}\left(\left(0,1\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d\right)\), satisfies bounds of the
same type as \eqref{eq:bu_ass}, possibly with different constants, and
the transformed coefficients still satisfy
Assumption~\ref{ass:parabolic}. We therefore work on
\(\left(0,1\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d\) and we continue to denote the rescaled function by $u$ and the rescaled operator by $\mathcal{P}$.
\\~\\
\noindent\emph{Step~1: Local vanishing after the initial time.}
We first show that if \(u\left(0,\cdot\right)=0\), then \(u\) vanishes on a short
time strip \(\left[0,T_1\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d\).
\\

By Assumption~\ref{ass:parabolic} and Remark \ref{rem:forward_roles}, there exist constants
\(0<\lambda\le \Lambda<\infty\) such that
\begin{equation*}
\lambda |\xi|^2 \le \xi^\mathsf{T} A(t,x)\xi \le \Lambda |\xi|^2,
\qquad
\xi\in \mathbb{R}^d,
\quad (t,x)\in [0,1]\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
Moreover, under Assumption~\ref{ass:parabolic}, the coefficients satisfy the hypotheses of \cite[Proposition~1.4]{jWu2019} with the Wu--Zhang growth exponent \(\alpha_{\mathrm{WZ}}=1\). Indeed, the boundedness in \eqref{eq:bu_ass} implies the admissible growth bound
\begin{equation*}
|u(t,x)|\le M e^{M|x|},
\end{equation*}
and, for \(\alpha_{\mathrm{WZ}}=1\), the spatial-decay exponent required of \(\nabla_xA\) in \cite{jWu2019} is zero. Hence the global boundedness of \(\nabla_xA\), together with the ellipticity and time regularity recorded in Remark~\ref{rem:forward_roles}, is sufficient. Thus there exists a constant \(K>0\), depending only on the dimension, the ellipticity constants, and the coefficient bounds entering \cite[Proposition~1.4]{jWu2019}, such that, with
\begin{equation*}
b=(8\Lambda)^{-1},\qquad \langle x\rangle=(1+|x|^2)^{1/2},
\end{equation*}
for every scalar-valued test function
\(\phi\in C_0^\infty\left(\left(0,1\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d\right)\) and every
\(\gamma>0\),
\begin{multline}\label{eq:bu_carleman}
\int_{(0,1)\times \mathbb{R}^d}
e^{2\gamma (t^{-K}-1)-\frac{b\langle x\rangle+K}{t}}
\left( |\phi(t,x)|^2 + |\nabla_x \phi(t,x)|^2 \right)\mathrm{d} x\mathrm{d} t
\\
\le
\int_{(0,1)\times \mathbb{R}^d}
e^{2\gamma (t^{-K}-1)-\frac{b\langle x\rangle+K}{t}}
|\mathcal{P} \phi(t,x)|^2\mathrm{d} x\mathrm{d} t.
\end{multline}
We claim that the same estimate holds for
\(\mathbb{R}^d\)-valued test functions. Indeed, if 
\begin{equation*}
\varphi=(\varphi^1,\dots,\varphi^d)\in
C_0^\infty\left((0,1)\times \mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d\right),
\end{equation*}
then applying
\eqref{eq:bu_carleman} to each component \(\varphi^k\) and summing
over \(k=1,\dots,d\) gives
\begin{multline*}
\int_{(0,1)\times \mathbb{R}^d}
e^{2\gamma (t^{-K}-1)-\frac{b\langle x\rangle+K}{t}}
\sum_{k=1}^d \left(|\varphi^k(t,x)|^2 + |\nabla_x\varphi^k(t,x)|^2\right)
\mathrm{d} x\mathrm{d} t
\\
\le
\int_{(0,1)\times \mathbb{R}^d}
e^{2\gamma (t^{-K}-1)-\frac{b\langle x\rangle+K}{t}}
\sum_{k=1}^d |\mathcal{P} \varphi^k(t,x)|^2\mathrm{d} x\mathrm{d} t.
\end{multline*}
Since
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{k=1}^d |\varphi^k|^2 = |\varphi|^2,
\quad
\sum_{k=1}^d |\nabla_x\varphi^k|^2 = |\nabla_x\varphi|^2\quad\mbox{and}\quad
\sum_{k=1}^d |\mathcal{P}\varphi^k|^2 = |\mathcal{P}\varphi|^2,
\end{equation*}
this is exactly the corresponding Carleman estimate for vector-valued
test functions.
\\

We now adapt the proof of \cite[Lemma~2.1]{jWu2019}. In the scalar
case, that lemma shows that a function satisfying a differential
inequality of the form
\begin{equation*}
|\mathcal{P} \phi|\le \widetilde\kappa\left(|\phi|+|\nabla_x\phi|\right),
\end{equation*}
together with a suitable growth condition, and vanishing at time \(0\), vanishes on a strip \(\left(0,T_1\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d\) for some \(T_1\in \left(0,1\right)\).
In our setting, the boundedness in \eqref{eq:bu_ass} is
stronger than the \(\alpha_{\mathrm{WZ}}=1\) growth condition used there. The spatial factor
\(e^{-b\langle x\rangle/t}\) in \eqref{eq:bu_carleman} is precisely the decay needed to pass to the limit in the spatial cutoffs in the proof of \cite[Lemma~2.1]{jWu2019}. Since that proof relies only on this Carleman estimate, the differential inequality, and the corresponding growth control, the same argument applies to the \(\mathbb{R}^d\)-valued function \(u\), using the vector-valued Carleman estimate established above. We therefore obtain the existence of \(T_1\in \left(0,1\right)\) such that
\begin{equation*}
u \equiv 0
\qquad \text{on } (0,T_1)\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
Together with \(u\left(0,\cdot\right)=0\) and the continuity of \(u\), this gives
\begin{equation*}
u \equiv 0
\qquad \text{on } [0,T_1]\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
~\\
\noindent\emph{Step~2: Propagation up to the terminal time.}
We now iterate the local vanishing result in time, exactly as in the
proof of \cite[Theorem~1.2]{jWu2019}. Define
\begin{equation*}
S
=
\left\{ s\in [0,1] : u \equiv 0 \text{ on } [0,s]\times \mathbb{R}^d \right\}.
\end{equation*}
By Step~1, the set \(S\) is nonempty. Let \(s_*=\sup S\). We claim
that \(s_*=1\). Suppose for contradiction that \(s_*<1\). Since \(u\)
is continuous and vanishes on \(\left[0,s\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d\) for every
\(s<s_*\), we have
\begin{equation*}
u(s_*,\cdot)=0 \qquad \text{on } \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
Consider the shifted and rescaled function
\begin{equation*}
\widehat u(\tau,x)=u\left(s_*+(1-s_*)\tau,x\right),
\qquad
(\tau,x)\in [0,1]\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
Exactly as before, \(\widehat u\) satisfies bounds of the form
\eqref{eq:bu_ass} on \(\left(0,1\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d\), possibly with different
constants, and \(\widehat u\left(0,\cdot\right)=0\). Applying Step~1 to
\(\widehat u\), we find \(\delta\in \left(0,1\right)\) such that
\begin{equation*}
\widehat u \equiv 0
\qquad \text{on } [0,\delta]\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
Returning to the original time variable, this means that
\begin{equation*}
u \equiv 0
\qquad \text{on } [0,s_*+\delta(1-s_*)]\times \mathbb{R}^d,
\end{equation*}
which contradicts the definition of \(s_*\) as the supremum of \(S\).
Hence \(s_*=1\), and therefore
\begin{equation*}
u \equiv 0 \qquad \text{on } [0,1)\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
Undoing the initial affine change of variables yields
\begin{equation*}
u \equiv 0 \qquad \text{on } [t_0,T)\times \mathbb{R}^d,
\end{equation*}
which proves the lemma.

\label{prop:mm_inv}
Assume \ref{ass:rigidity}, and suppose that \(g\) is non-constant. Let \((v,w)\) be

Fix \(t_0\in \left(0,T\right)\) and set \(u = \nabla_x v\). By
Proposition~\ref{prop:extra_reg}, the field \(u\) belongs to
\(C^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}\) and satisfies \eqref{eq:struct_ineq}. Suppose, for
contradiction, that the zero set
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{U}_{t_0} = \{x\in \mathbb{R}^d : u(t_0,x)=0\}
\end{equation*}
has positive Lebesgue measure. By Lemma~\ref{lem:uc}, this implies that \(u\left(t_0,\cdot\right)\equiv 0\) on \(\mathbb{R}^d\). We now want to apply backward uniqueness. Lemma~\ref{lem:bu}

vanishing time interior to that interval. The boundedness is available,
through Corollary~\ref{cor:slab_bounds}, on slabs staying strictly away
from \(0\) and \(T\). We therefore apply the lemma on such a slab
containing \(t_0\) in its interior. Fix an arbitrary
\(\varepsilon\in \left(0,\min\{t_0,T-t_0\}\right)\) and apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:bu} with the interval
\(\left[\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon\right]\) in the role of
\(\left[0,T\right]\) and with vanishing time
\(t_0\in \left(\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon\right)\). Its hypotheses hold
there: \(u\) belongs to
\(C^{1,2}\left(\left(\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon\right)\times \mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d\right)\)
and satisfies \eqref{eq:struct_ineq} by
Proposition~\ref{prop:extra_reg}, as noted above, and, since
\(\varepsilon<\min\{t_0,T-t_0\}\le T/2\),
Corollary~\ref{cor:slab_bounds} yields
\begin{equation*}
\sup_{(t,x)\in [\varepsilon,T-\varepsilon]\times \mathbb{R}^d}
\left( \left\lvert u(t,x) \right\rvert + \left\lvert \nabla_xu(t,x) \right\rvert \right) < \infty.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, Lemma~\ref{lem:bu} gives
\begin{equation*}
u \equiv 0 \quad \text{on } [t_0,T-\varepsilon)\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}
As \(\varepsilon\in \left(0,\min\{t_0,T-t_0\}\right)\) was arbitrary, we
conclude that
\begin{equation*}
u \equiv 0 \quad \text{on } [t_0,T)\times \mathbb{R}^d.
\end{equation*}

At this point the contradiction is immediate. Since \(u=\nabla_x v\),
the function \(x\mapsto v\left(t,x\right)\) is constant on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) for each
\(t\in \left[t_0,T\right)\). Let us denote this constant by \(c\left(t\right)\). By
Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}, the function \(v\) is continuous on
\(\left[0,T\right]\times \mathbb{R}^d\), so letting \(t\uparrow T\) gives, for every
\(x\in \mathbb{R}^d\),
\begin{equation*}
g(x) = v(T,x) = \lim_{t\uparrow T} v(t,x) = \lim_{t\uparrow T} c(t).
\end{equation*}
Thus, \(g\) is constant on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), contradicting the assumed non-constancy of \(g\). Hence, the set \(\mathcal{U}_{t_0}\)

\\

Finally, Assumption \ref{ass:parabolic} implies that
the uniformly elliptic diffusion \(X_{t_0}^{0,x_0}\) admits a density
with respect to the Lebesgue measure -- see Aronson \cite{Aronson}. Since
\(|\mathcal{U}_{t_0}|=0\), it follows that
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{P}\left( \nabla_x v(t_0,X_{t_0}^{0,x_0}) = 0 \right) = 0.
\end{equation*}
As \(t_0\in \left(0,T\right)\) was arbitrary, the proof is complete.

Fix \(t\in \left(0,T\right)\). By Proposition~\ref{prop:mm_inv},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grad_nonzero}
\nabla_x v(t,X_t^{0,x_0}) \neq 0
\quad \text{a.s.}
\end{equation}
This is the decisive deterministic input produced by the rigidity
argument of the previous section.
We now turn to the Malliavin derivative of \(Y_t^{0,x_0}\). By
Lemma~\ref{lem:malliavin_rep}, the random variable \(Y_t^{0,x_0}\)
belongs to \(\mathbb{D}^{1,2}\), and for every \(r\in \left[0,T\right]\),
\begin{equation*}
D_rY_t^{0,x_0}
=
\nabla_x v(t,X_t^{0,x_0})D_rX_t^{0,x_0}\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}(r).
\end{equation*}
Thus, the Malliavin derivative of \(Y_t^{0,x_0}\) factorizes through
the spatial gradient of the decoupling field evaluated along the
forward diffusion. To prove that \(Y_t^{0,x_0}\) has a density, it suffices to show that this derivative is almost surely
nonzero in \(L^2\left(\left[0,T\right]\right)\).
\\

For \(0\le r\le t\), the standard variational representation of the Malliavin derivative gives
\begin{equation*}
D_rX_t^{0,x_0}
=
J_{r,t}\sigma(r,X_r^{0,x_0})
\qquad \text{for a.e. }r\in[0,t].
\end{equation*}
By the flow property of the Jacobian and Lemma~\ref{lem:jacobian_flow},
\(J_{r,t}=J_{0,t}J_{0,r}^{-1}\), and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:DX_flow_rep}
D_rX_t^{0,x_0}
=
J_{0,t}J_{0,r}^{-1}\sigma(r,X_r^{0,x_0})
\qquad \text{for a.e. }r\in[0,t],
\end{equation}
where the Jacobians are evaluated along the trajectory starting from
\(x_0\). Define, for \(r\in[0,t]\),
\begin{equation*}
H_r
:=
J_{0,t}J_{0,r}^{-1}\sigma(r,X_r^{0,x_0}).
\end{equation*}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:jacobian_flow}, the processes \(r\mapsto J_{0,r}\) and
\(r\mapsto J_{0,r}^{-1}\) have continuous paths, and the continuity of
\(X\) together with Assumptions~\ref{ass:flow} and \ref{ass:parabolic}
shows that \(r\mapsto H_r\) is continuous. Moreover,
\begin{equation*}
H_t=\sigma(t,X_t^{0,x_0}).
\end{equation*}
Hence, by the uniform ellipticity assumption \ref{it:P3},
\begin{equation*}
H_tH_t^\mathsf{T}
=
\sigma(t,X_t^{0,x_0})\sigma(t,X_t^{0,x_0})^\mathsf{T}
\ge \lambda I_d.
\end{equation*}
Consequently, for almost every sample point there exists
\(\delta=\delta(\omega)\in(0,t]\) such that
\begin{equation*}
H_rH_r^\mathsf{T}\ge \frac{\lambda}{2}I_d,
\qquad r\in[t-\delta,t].
\end{equation*}
Using \eqref{eq:DX_flow_rep}, the Malliavin covariance matrix of
\(X_t^{0,x_0}\) can therefore be written as
\begin{equation*}
C_t
=
\int_0^t H_rH_r^\mathsf{T}\,\mathrm{d} r,
\end{equation*}
and satisfies, pathwise,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ct_positive}
C_t
\ge
\frac{\lambda\delta}{2}I_d.
\end{equation}
Thus \(C_t\) is positive definite almost surely. In particular,
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert DY_t^{0,x_0} \right\rVert_{L^2([0,T])}^2
=
\nabla_x v(t,X_t^{0,x_0})
C_t
\nabla_x v(t,X_t^{0,x_0})^\mathsf{T}.
\end{equation*}
Combining \eqref{eq:Ct_positive} with \eqref{eq:grad_nonzero}, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\left\lVert DY_t^{0,x_0} \right\rVert_{L^2([0,T])} > 0
\quad \text{a.s.}
\end{equation*}
We are now exactly in the setting of the Bouleau--Hirsch criterion.
Since \(Y_t^{0,x_0}\in \mathbb{D}^{1,2}\) and its Malliavin derivative is
almost surely nonzero in \(L^2\left(\left[0,T\right]\right)\), the law of \(Y_t^{0,x_0}\) is absolutely continuous with respect to the
Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}\) -- see Nualart
\cite[Theorem~2.1.2]{dNualart}. This completes the proof.
