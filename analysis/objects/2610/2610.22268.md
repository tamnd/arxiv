<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finite group. The \emph{uniform intersection number} of $G$,
	denoted by $\operatorname{ui}(G)$, is the smallest positive integer $k$ such that the
	intersection of any $k$ distinct maximal subgroups of $G$ is equal to the
	Frattini subgroup $\Phi(G)$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finite group. The graph $\Gamma(G)$ has as its vertices the
	nontrivial proper subgroups of $G$, and two distinct vertices $S_1$ and $S_2$
	are adjacent if and only if $ \langle S_1,S_2\rangle=G$.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finitely generated group. The \emph{subgroup-based generating graph} of $G$, denoted
	by $\mathcal{G}(G)$, is the graph whose vertices are the nontrivial proper
	subgroups of $G$ that are not contained in $\Phi(G)$. Two distinct vertices
	$S_1$ and $S_2$ are adjacent if and only if $ \langle S_1,S_2\rangle=G$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{propprodphi}
    Let $G_1$ and $G_2$ be two finite groups. Then $\operatorname{ui}(G_1\times G_2)\leq \operatorname{ui}(G_1)+ \operatorname{ui}(G_2)$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $\operatorname{ui}(G_1)=k$ and $\operatorname{ui}(G_2)=l$. Then $\bigcap_{i=1}^km_i=\Phi(G_1)$ and $\bigcap_{j=1}^ln_j=\Phi(G_2)$, where $m_i\in \mathcal{M}(G_1)$ for $i\in [k]$ and $n_j\in \mathcal{M}(G_2)$ for $j\in[l]$. Notice that, for each $i\in [k]$, $m_i\times G_2$ is a maximal subgroup of $G_1\times G_2$ and, for each $i\in[l]$, $G_1\times n_i$ is a maximal subgroup of $G_1\times G_2$. Since $\Phi(G_1\times G_2)=\Phi(G_1)\times \Phi(G_2)$, we have 
\begin{equation*}
    \bigcap_{i=1}^k(m_i\times G_2)\cap \bigcap_{j=1}^l(G_1\times n_j)=\Phi(G_1)\times \Phi(G_2)=\Phi(G_1\times G_2).
\end{equation*}
Thus $\operatorname{ui}(G_1\times G_2)\leq \operatorname{ui}(G_1)+\operatorname{ui}(G_2)$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->

\label{corabliui}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\cite[Theorem $1$]{aiva} \label{lmfrat}
	Let $N$ be a normal subgroup of a finite group $G$ such that $N\subseteq \Phi(G)$. Then $\Phi(G)/N=\Phi\left(G/N\right)$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
Let $N$ be a normal subgroup of a finite group $G$. If $N\subseteq \Phi(G)$, then $\operatorname{ui}({G}/{N})=\operatorname{ui}(G)$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $\operatorname{ui}(G)=k$. Since $N\subseteq \Phi(G)$, there is a one to one correspondence between the set of maximal subgroups of $G$ and ${G}/{N}$. Using Lemma \ref{lmfrat}, we have \[\bigcap_{i=1}^k\left({m_i}/{N}\right)={\bigcap_{i=1}^km_i}/{N}={\Phi(G)}/{N}=\Phi({G}/{N})\]
   for any distinct $k$-maximal subgroups $m_1,\ldots,m_k$ of $G$.  Also note that there must exist $m_1$, $m_2, \dots , m_{k-1}\in \mathcal{M}(G)$ such that $\bigcap_{i=1}^{k-1}\left({m_i}/{N}\right)\not=\Phi({G}/{N})$, otherwise $\operatorname{ui}(G)<k$, which contradicts our hypothesis. Hence, the result follows.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thmuidic}
Let $n=\prod_{i=1}^kp_i^{t_i}$ be with $p_1\geq p_2\geq \dots \geq p_k$. Then
                
 \[
\operatorname{ui}(\operatorname{Dic}_n)=\begin{cases}2~ &\text{if}~ k=1~\\ \sum\limits_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i +2~ &\text{if}~ k\geq 2~~
\end{cases}\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By \cite[Theorem 5.1]{hum}, the maximal subgroups of the group $\operatorname{Dic}_n$ are of the form $\langle a\rangle$ and $\langle a^{p_j}, a^ix\rangle$, where $j\in [k]$ and $i\in \{0,1,\dots, p_j-1\}$. Thus $\Phi(\operatorname{Dic}_n)=\langle a^{p_1\dots p_k}\rangle$ and $|\mathcal{M}(\operatorname{Dic}_n)|=\sum_{i=1}^kp_i+1$. For each \(t\in[k]\), let \(C_{p_t}\) denote the conjugacy class of maximal subgroups of the form 
  \[
  \langle a^{p_t},a^ix\rangle,
  \qquad 0\leq i\leq p_t-1.
  \]
Clearly, $|C_{p_t}|=p_t$. If $k=1$, then the intersection of any two distinct maximal subgroups of $\operatorname{Dic}_n$ is equal to the $\Phi(\operatorname{Dic}_n)$ and hence $\operatorname{ui}(\operatorname{Dic}_n)=2$.

 Let $k\geq 2$. Then 
  \begin{equation*}
  	\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k-1}C_{p_i}\right)\cap \langle a\rangle=\langle a^{p_1p_2\cdots p_{k-1}} \rangle\not=\Phi(\operatorname{Dic}_n)=\langle a^{p_1p_2\cdots p_k}\rangle.
  \end{equation*}
  Therefore, $\operatorname{ui}(Dic_n)>\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i+1$. Now, consider any collection of $\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i+2$ maximal subgroups of $\operatorname{Dic}_n$. Then this collection must contain at least one maximal subgroup from each conjugacy class. Hence, the intersection of any $\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i+2$ distinct maximal subgroups of $\operatorname{Dic}_n$ is $\Phi(G)$. 
  Therefore, $\operatorname{ui}(\operatorname{Dic}_n)=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i+2$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lmiff}
   Let $G$ be a finitely generated group. Then the two vertices $S_1$ and $S_2$ in $\mathcal{G}(G)$ are adjacent if and only if $M(S_1)\cap M(S_2)=\emptyset$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We prove the equivalent contrapositive statement:  the two vertices $S_1$ and $S_2$ in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$ are adjacent if and only if $M(S_1)\cap M(S_2)\not=\emptyset$.\smallskip
	
  Let $S_1\sim S_2$ in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$. Then $\langle S_1, S_2\rangle\not=G$.                                                            
   Since $G$ is finitely generated, by Zorn's lemma, we have $\langle S_1, S_2\rangle \subseteq m$ for some $m\in \mathcal{M}(G)$. Then $m\in M(S_1)\cap M(S_2)$. Thus $M(S_1)\cap M(S_2)\not=\emptyset$.

  To prove the converse, suppose that there exist nontrivial proper subgroups \(S_1,S_2\leq G\), with \(S_1,S_2\nsubseteq \Phi(G)\), such that $M(S_1)\cap M(S_2)\not=\emptyset$. Then there exists $m\in \mathcal{M}(G)$ such that $m\in M(S_1)\cap M(S_2)$. This implies $\langle S_1, S_2\rangle \subseteq m\not=G$. Hence $S_1\sim S_2$ in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{propdiam}
 Let $G$ be a finitely generated group. Then $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a nontrivial connected graph if and only if $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 2$. In this case, $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))\leq 3$.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let $\mathcal{G}(G)$ be a nontrivial connected graph. Since $G$ is finitely generated, we have $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 1$. If $|\mathcal{M}(G)|=1$, then $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a null graph, a contradiction. Consequently, $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 2$.

Conversely, suppose that $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 2$, and let $S_1, S_2$ be two arbitrary vertices of $\mathcal{G}(G)$. If $S_1\sim S_2$, there is nothing to prove. Thus, assume that $S_1\nsim S_2$. Then $M(S_1)\cap M(S_2)\not=\emptyset$. Since $S_1$, $S_2\nsubseteq \Phi(G)$, there exist $m_1$, $m_2\in \mathcal{M}(G)$ such that $S_1\nsubseteq m_2$ and $S_2\nsubseteq m_1$. Thus, \(S_1\sim m_2\) and \(m_1\sim S_2\). If $m_1=m_2=m$, then  $S_1\sim m\sim S_2$.  On the other hand, if \(m_1\neq m_2\),  the maximality of \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) yields $ \langle m_1,m_2\rangle=G$ and hence \(m_1\sim m_2\). Consequently,  $S_1\sim m_2\sim m_1\sim S_2$ is a $S_1$-$S_2$ path of length at most \(3\). Therefore, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is  connected and $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))\leq 3$.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thmdiam}
    Let $G$ be a finitely generated group with $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 2$. Then 
    \begin{enumerate}[{(1)}]
    	\item   $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))=1$ if and only if $V(\mathcal{G}(G))= \mathcal{M}(G)$ .
    	\item  $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))=2$ if and only if either $\vert V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal{M}(G)\vert=1$, or $\vert V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal{M}(G)\vert\geq 2$ and, for every pair of nonadjacent vertices $S_1$, $S_2\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))$,  $M(S_1)\cup M(S_2)\subsetneq \mathcal{M}(G)$.
    	
    	\item  $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))=3$ if and only if  $|V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 2$ and there exist nonadjacent vertices $S_1$, $S_2\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))$ such that  $M(S_1)\cup M(S_2)=\mathcal{M}(G)$.
                                                                                                                                                                     
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                
    \end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Since $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 2$, by Proposition \ref{propdiam}, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is connected. 
\begin{enumerate}[{(1)}]
    \item Since $G$ is finitely generated, each subgroup of $G$ is contained in some maximal subgroups of $G$. Therefore, by Lemma \ref{lmiff}, we have $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))=1$ if and only if every vertex of $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a maximal subgroup of $G$.
\item Suppose that $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))=2$.  By the first part, $\vert V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal \mathcal{M}(G)\vert\geq 1$. If $\vert V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal \mathcal{M}(G)\vert=1$, we are done. Let $\vert V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal \mathcal{M}(G)\vert\geq 2$. It remains to show  for every pair of nonadjacent vertices $S_1$, $S_2\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))$,  $M(S_1)\cup M(S_2)\subsetneq \mathcal{M}(G)$. Suppose, to the contrary, that there exist nonadjacent vertices $S_1$, $S_2$ in $ V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal{M}(G)$ such that $M(S_1)\cup M(S_2)=\mathcal{M}(G)$. Then there is no vertex $H\in V(\mathcal{G})$ for which $S_1\sim H\sim S_2$. Therefore, $\operatorname{d}(S_1, S_2)\geq 3$, a contradiction. Consequently, $M(S_1)\cup M(S_2)\subsetneq \mathcal{M}(G)$ for every pair of nonadjacent vertices \(S_1,S_2\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))\).

To prove the converse, first assume that $\vert V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal \mathcal{M}(G)\vert=1$, and  let  $A\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))\setminus \mathcal{M}(G)$. Thus, every pair of nonadjacent vertices of \(\mathcal{G}(G)\) is at distance \(2\), and consequently, $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))=2$. 


\item  This follows immediately from the preceding two cases, together with the fact that $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{G}(G))\leq 3$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{propgirthfgc}
    Let $G$ be a finitely generated group. Then 
   \begin{align*}
\operatorname{gr} (\mathcal{G}(G))=\begin{cases}$3$~ &\text{if $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 3$,}~\\ $4$~ &\text{if $|\mathcal{M}(G)|=2$ with for each $m\in \mathcal{M}(G)$ there exists a subgroup $v\subsetneq m$ of $G$}~\\ &\text{such that $v\nsubseteq \Phi(G)$},  
~\\ 
\infty~ &\text{otherwise}.~
\end{cases}
\end{align*}
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
Suppose that \(|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 3\), and choose three distinct maximal subgroups \(m_1,m_2,m_3\in\mathcal{M}(G)\). Since any two distinct maximal subgroups of \(G\) generate \(G\),  $m_1\sim m_2\sim m_3\sim m_1$ forms a triangle inn $\mathcal{G}(G)$. Thus $\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{G}(G))=3$.\smallskip 


Now, suppose that $\mathcal{M}(G)=\{m_1,m_2\}$ and there exist subgroups $S_1, S_2\in V(\mathcal{G}(G)) \setminus \mathcal{M}(G)$ such that $M(S_i)=\{m_i\}$ for $i\in[2]$. Then $S_1\sim m_2\sim m_1\sim S_2\sim S_1$ forms a $4$-cycle in $\mathcal{G}(G)$. We claim that \(\mathcal{G}(G)\) contains no triangle. Suppose, to the contrary, that $H_1\sim H_2\sim H_3\sim H_1$ is a triangle. Since $H_1\sim H_2$, $M(H_1)\cap M(H_2)=\emptyset$. As \(\mathcal{M}(G)=\{m_1,m_2\}\), we may assume, without loss of generality, that $M(H_1)=\{m_1\}$ and $M(H_2)=\{m_2\}$. Again, since $H_1\sim H_3$ and $H_2\sim H_3$, we must have $M(H_1)\cap M(H_3)=\emptyset$ and $M(H_2)\cap M(H_3)=\emptyset$. Thus $M(H_3)=\emptyset$, which is impossible for a vertex corresponding to a proper subgroup of the finitely generated group \(G\). Therefore, \(\mathcal{G}(G)\) contains no triangle and we conclude that $\operatorname{gr} (\mathcal{G}(G))=4$.
 
Finally, suppose that, for some \(i\in[2]\), \(m_i\) is the only vertex \(S\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))\) satisfying $M(S)=\{m_i\}$.  Then \(\mathcal{G}(G)\) is a star graph, and hence $\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{G}(G))=\infty$.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{propmul}
Let $G$ be a finitely generated group with $2\leq |\mathcal{M}(G)|<\infty$. Then $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a k-partite graph if and only if $|\mathcal{M}(G)|=k$.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Suppose that \(\mathcal{G}(G)\) is a \(k\)-partite graph with partite sets \(V_1,\ldots,V_k\). Assume, for contradiction, that $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq k+1$. By the pigeonhole principle, some partite set, say \(V_t\), contains at least two maximal subgroups, say $m_1$ and $m_2$. Since $m_1\sim m_2$ in $\mathcal{G}(G)$, the partite set \(V_t\) is not independent, a contradiction. Thus, $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\leq k$. 

 We next show that $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq k$. If not,  then some partite set, say \(V_k\), contains no maximal subgroup. Let \(S\in V_k\). Since \(G\) is finitely generated, \(S\) is contained in a maximal subgroup \(m_j\), which lies in some partite set \(V_j\), \(j\neq k\). As \(V_j\) is independent and \(m_j\in V_j\), every vertex of \(V_j\) is contained in \(m_j\). Hence \(S\) may be moved to \(V_j\) without destroying independence. Repeating this for every \(S\in V_k\), we obtain a \((k-1)\)-partite representation of \(\mathcal{G}(G)\), contradicting the minimality of \(k\). Therefore, $|\mathcal{M}(G)|= k.$\smallskip

To prove the converse, suppose that  $\mathcal{M}(G)=\{m_1,\ldots,m_k\}$, where $2\leq k<\infty$. Then $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a connected graph. Define 
 \begin{align*}
     \begin{split}
         V_1&:=\{H\in V(\mathcal{M}(G)): H\subseteq m_1\}\\
         V_2&:=\{H\in V(\mathcal{M}(G)): H\subseteq m_2~\text{and}~H\nsubseteq m_1\}\\
         \vdots
         \\
         V_t&:=\{H\in V(\mathcal{M}(G): H\subseteq m_t~\text{and}~v\nsubseteq m_i,\forall i\in [t-1] \},
         \\
         \vdots\\
         V_k&:=\{H\in V(\mathcal{M}(G)): H\subseteq m_k~\text{and}~H\nsubseteq m_i, \forall i\in [k-1]\}.
     \end{split}
     \end{align*}
     Clearly, $V_i\not=\emptyset$ for each \(i\in[k]\) and $\{V_i:i\in [k]\}$ form a partition of $V(\mathcal{G}(G))$. Since every \(V_i\) is an independent set, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a $k$-partite graph.

<!-- corollary-9: body_md -->
\label{corui}
    Let $G$ be a finitely generated group with $|\mathcal{M}(G)|=k\geq 2$. Then $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete $k$-partite graph if and only if $m_1\cap m_2=\Phi(G)$ for all distinct $m_1$, $m_2\in \mathcal{M}(G)$.
<!-- end corollary-9 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
We first assume that $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete $k$-partite graph.  Let $m_1$, $m_2\in \mathcal{M}(G)$ such that $m_1\cap m_2\not=\Phi(G)$. Then $m_1\cap m_2\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))$ and $m_1\cap m_2$ must belong to some partite set. Since $m_1\sim m_2$, $m_1$ and $m_2$ belong to different partite sets. Moreover, $m_1\cap m_2$ is neither adjacent to $m_1$ nor $m_2$. This contradicts the fact that $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete $k$-partite graph. Thus $m_1\cap m_2=\Phi(G)$ for all distinct $m_1$, $m_2\in \mathcal{M}(G)$. 

Conversely, let $\mathcal{M}(G)=\{m_1,\ldots,m_k\}$ and $m_i\cap m_j=\Phi(G)$ for all $i\neq j$. By Proposition \ref{propmul}, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a $k$-partite graph with partite sets $V_1$, $V_2$, $\dots$, $V_k$. Since any two maximal subgroups of $G$ are adjacent in $\mathcal{G}(G)$, without loss of generality assume that $m_i\in V_i$ for $i\in [k]$.  If a vertex $H\in V_i$ that is not adjacent to some vertex in $V_j$ for $i\neq j$, then $H\subseteq m_i\cap m_j=\Phi(G)$, which contradicts $H\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))$. Thus $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete $k$-partite graph.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lmqmul}
Let $G$ be a finite group and $N$ be any proper normal subgroup of $G$. If $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete multipartite graph, then $\mathcal{G}({G}/{N})$ is also a complete multipartite graph.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-22: body_md -->
Let $N$ be a proper normal subgroup of $G$, and suppose that $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is complete multipartite. If $|\mathcal{M}(G/N)|\leq 1$, then $G/N$ is a null graph. Hence, assume that $|\mathcal{M}(G/N)|\geq 2$ and let ${m_1}/{N}$, ${m_2}/{N}\in \mathcal{M}(G/N)$ be distinct maximal subgroups. Then $m_1, m_2\in \mathcal{M}(G)$. Since $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete multipartite graph, by Corollary \ref{corui}, $m_1\cap m_2=\Phi(G)$. Moreover, \(N\subseteq m_1\cap m_2\), and hence $N\subseteq\Phi(G).$ Consequently, 
\begin{equation*}
    m_1/N \bigcap m_2/N={m_1\cap m_2}/N=\Phi(G)/N.
\end{equation*}
By Lemma \ref{lmfrat},  $\Phi({G}/{N})={\Phi(G)}/{N}$. This implies $m_1/N\bigcap m_2/N=\Phi\left(G/N\right)$. By Corollary \ref{corui}, $\mathcal{G}({G}/{N})$ is a complete multipartite graph.

\label{propcmppg}

<!-- end proposition-7 -->
<!-- proof-24: body_md -->
Since $G$ be a $p$-group of order $p^n$, one of the following holds: $(i)$ $G\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p^n}$;	$(ii)$ $G\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p^s} \times \mathbb{Z}_{p^t}$ with $s+t=n$; $(iii)$ $G$ is an abelian $p$-group of rank $k\geq 3$; $(iv)$ $G$ is a nonabelian $p$-group. If $G\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p^n}$, then $G$ is a null graph.

Let $G\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p^s} \times \mathbb{Z}_{p^t}$, where $s+t=n$. Then $|\mathcal{M}(G)|=p+1$. Observe that $\Phi(G)\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p^{t-1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p^{s-1}}$.  Furthermore, for any two distinct maximal subgroups \(m_1,m_2\in\mathcal{M}(G)\), $m_1\cap m_2=\Phi(G)$. Consequently, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete $(p+1)$-partite graph.

Now consider the case where $G$ be an abelian $p$-group of rank $k\geq 3$. Then there exists a normal subgroup $H$ of $G$ such that $G/H$ is isomorphic to the elementary abelian group $\mathbb{Z}_p\times \mathbb{Z}_p\times \dots \times \mathbb{Z}_p$ ($k$ times). By Lemma \ref{lmqmul} and Lemma \ref{lmzppp}, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is not a complete multipartite graph.\smallskip

Finally, suppose that $G$ be a non abelian $p$-group. Let $G$ be $2$-generated. Then $G/\Phi(G)\cong \mathbb{Z}_p\times \mathbb{Z}_p$, which is a $2$-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{Z}_p$. Since any two distinct one-dimensional subspaces of \(G/\Phi(G)\) intersect trivially, it follows that $m_1\cap m_2=\Phi(G)$ for any two distinct maximal subgroups \(m_1,m_2\) of \(G\). By Corollary \ref{corui}, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is complete multipartite.  On the other hand, if \(G\) requires \(k\geq3\) generator, then $G/\Phi(G)\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p}^k$. Therefore, by Lemma \ref{lmzppp}, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is not a complete multipartite graph.

Suppose that $G\cong P_1\rtimes P_2$, where $P_1$ is an elementary abelian sylow $p_1$-group of order $p_1$ and $P_2$ is a Sylow $p_2$-subgroup of $G$ of order $p_2$, and $p_2|(p_1-1)$. Then $P_1\cong \mathbb Z_{p_1}$ and $P_2\cong \mathbb Z_{p_2}$. Hence $G\cong \mathbb Z_{p_1}\rtimes \mathbb Z_{p_2},$ where \(p_1\neq p_2\). Since \(G\) is non-nilpotent, the action of \(\mathbb Z_{p_2}\) on \(\mathbb Z_{p_1}\) is nontrivial and  $\Phi(G)=\{1\}$. Thus $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete multipartite graph.\smallskip
	
Conversely, suppose that $\mathcal{G}(G)$ be a complete multipartite graph. We divide the remainder of the proof into two cases.

\textbf{Case 1.} $\Phi(G)$ is trivial: Since \(G\) is non-nilpotent, there exists a non-normal maximal subgroup \(m\) of \(G\). As \(m\leq N_G(m)\leq G\) and \(m\) is maximal, the non-normality of \(m\) implies that  $m=N_G(m)$. Hence, for every \(x\in G\setminus m\), we have \(m^x\neq m\). Thus \(m\) and \(m^x\) are distinct maximal subgroups, and Corollary~\ref{corui} yields $m\cap m^x=\Phi(G)=\{1\}$. This implies $G$ is a Frobenius group with the Frobenius complement $m$. Hence $G\cong k_G\rtimes m$, where $k_G$ is the Frobenius Kernel of $G$ and $\operatorname{gcd}(|m|,|k_G|)=1$.  We next show that the group $m$ contains only one maximal subgroup i.e., $|\mathcal{M}(m)|=1$. If not, then $|\mathcal{M}({G}/{k_G})|=|\mathcal{M}(m)|\geq 2$. By Corollary \ref{corui}, $k_G$ is a subgroup of $\Phi(G)$,  a contradiction. Therefore, $|\mathcal{M}(m)|=1$.
This implies $m\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p_2^t}$ for some prime $p_2$ and a natural number $t$. If $t\geq 2$, then $1\not=m_{p_2}=\{x\in m:x^{p_2}=1\}$ is a non trivial proper subgroup of $m$. Hence, $\langle k_G,m_{p_2}\rangle$ is a nontrivial proper subgroup of $G$ contained in a maximal subgroup $m_1\in \mathcal{M}(G)\setminus\{m\}$. Since $G$ is a complete multipartite, $m_{p_2}\subseteq m\cap m_1=\Phi(G)=\{1\}$, which is a contradiction. Therefore, $m\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p_2}$.

We now show that $k_G\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p_1}^r$ for some prime $p_1\not=p_2$. Since $k_G$ is nilpotent,it is isomorphic to the direct product product of its Sylow subgroups, i.e. $k_G\cong \prod_{i=1}^nS_i$. If possible, let $n\geq 2$. Then $m\subsetneq \langle S_1,m\rangle \not=G$, which contradicts the maximality of $m$. Therefore, $k_G$ is a Sylow $p_1$ subgroup of $G$ for some prime $p_1\not=p_2$ such that $p_2|p_1-1$. Also, since $G$ is solvable, $\Phi(k_G)=k_G\cap \Phi(G)=\{1\}$. Consequently, $k_G\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p_1}^r$, where $r$ is the rank of $r$.   If $r\geq 2$, then there exist maximal subgroups $k_1\rtimes \mathbb{Z}_{p_2}$ and $k_2\rtimes \mathbb{Z}_{p_2}$ such that $k_1\rtimes \mathbb{Z}_{p_2}\cap k_2\rtimes \mathbb{Z}_{p_2}\not=\{1\}$, which contradicts that is complete multipartite graph
Consequently, $G\cong \mathbb Z_{p_1}\rtimes \mathbb Z_{p_2}$.

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             

\textbf{Case $2$.} $\Phi(G)$ is non trivial: Since $\mathcal{G}(G)$ be a complete multipartite graph, by Lemma \ref{lmqmul}, ${G}/{\Phi(G)}$ is a complete multipartite graph. If possible, let ${G}/{\Phi(G)}$ be a nilpotent group.  Since $G$ is non abelian, ${G}/{\Phi(G)}$ is non cyclic. By Lemma \ref{lmpdf}, every prime divisor of the order of a finite group also divides $[G:\Phi(G)]$. Thus, from Propositions \ref{propcmppg} and \ref{propnilcmg}), we must have $|\pi({G}/{\Phi(G)})|=|\pi(G)|=1$, which is a contradiction. Thus ${G}/{\Phi(G)}$ is not a nilpotent group. Similarly as above, we have ${G}/{\Phi(G)}\cong {P_1}/{\Phi(P_1)}\rtimes {P_2}/{\Phi(P_2)}\cong \overline{P_1}\times \overline{P_2}$, where $P_i$ is a Sylow $p_i$ subgroup of $G$ for $i\in \{1,2\}$. Thus $G=P_1P_2$. Since $P_1\Phi(G)$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, we have $G=N(P_1)$, the normalizer of $P_1$ in $G$ (\cite{robin}, 5.2.14). Hence $P_1$ is normal in $G$. Since $\overline{P_1}= \frac{P_1\Phi(G)}{\Phi(G)}\cong \frac{P_1}{P_1\cap \Phi(G)}=\frac{P_1}{\Phi(P_1)}\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p_1}$, we have $P_1\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p_1^s}$ for some $s\in \mathbb{N}$. Now, if possible, let $|\mathcal{M}(P_1)|\geq 2$. Since $\frac{G}{P_1}\cong P_2$, we have $|M(P_2)|\geq 2$. Also, since $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a complete multipartite graph, $P_2\subseteq m_1\cap m_2=\Phi(G)$ ($cf.$ Corollary \ref{corui}), which is a contradiction. Therefore, $|\mathcal{M}(P_2)|=1$, i.e. $P_2\cong \mathbb{Z}_{p_2^t}$ for some $t\in \mathbb{N}$. 

If possible, let $s=t=1$. Then $\Phi(G)=\{1\}$, which contradicts our hypothesis. 
Let at least one of $s$ and $t$ be greater than one. Then $\Phi(G)\cong \mathbb{Z}_{{p_1}^{s-1}}\times Z_{p_2}^{t-1}$ and one can verify that there exist maximal subgroups whose intersection is not equal to the Frattini subgroup of $G$. This concludes the proof.

A graph \(G\) is perfect if and only if it is a Berge.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{propper}
   Let $G$ be a finitely generated group with at most four maximal subgroups. Then the graph $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is perfect.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-27: body_md -->
By Lemma \ref{lmspgt}, $\mathcal{G}(G)$, it suffices to show that neither $\mathcal{G}(G)$ nor its complement $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$ contains an induced odd cycle of length at least five.	


Suppose, for contradiction, that $S:S_1\sim S_2\sim \cdots \sim S_{2k+1}\sim S_1$ is an induced cycle in $\mathcal{G}(G)$, where $k\geq 2$. First, we show that $|M(S_t)|\geq 2$ for every vertex $S_t$ of $S$. Suppose $|M(S_t)|=1$ for some $S_t\in S$. Then $M(S_t)=\{m\}$ for some $m\in \mathcal{M}(G)$. Since $S_t\nsim S_{t+2}$ in $S$, $m\in M(S_{t+2})$. Similarly $S_t\nsim S_{t+3}$ implies $m\in M(S_{t+3})$. Hence $m\in M(S_{t+2})\cap M(S_{t+3})$ which implies $S_{t+2}\nsim S_{t+3}$, a contradiction. Hence $M(S_t)\geq 2$ for any $t\in [2k+1]$. By hypothesis, $G$ has at most four maximal subgroups. Let $\mathcal{M}(G)=\{m_1,m_2,m_3,m_4\}$. Without loss of generality, let $ M(S_1)=\{m_1, m_2\}$. Since $S_1\sim S_2$, we must have $m_3$, $m_4\in M(S_2)$. Again, as $S_2\sim S_3$ in $S$, we must have $m_1, m_2\in M(S_3)$. Also, $S_1\sim S_{2k+1}$ and therefore, $m_3$, $m_4\in M(S_{2k+1})$. Since $M(S_3)\cap M(S_{2k+1})=\emptyset$, we must have $S_3\sim S_{2k+1}$, which contradicts that $S$ is an induced cycle. Hence, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ contains no induced odd cycle of length at least $5$. By a similar argument, the same conclusion holds when $|\mathcal{M}(G)|=2$ or $3$.


Now, suppose for contradiction that $S':S_1'\sim S_2'\sim \cdots \sim S_{2k+1}'\sim S_1'$ be an induced cycle in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$, where $k\geq 2$. To prove that $|M(S'_t)|\geq 2$ for any $t\in[2k+1]$, let $M(S'_t)=\{m\}$ for some $t\in [2k+1]$ and $m\in \mathcal{M}(G)$. Since $S'_t\sim S_{t+1}$ in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$, we must have $m\in M(S'_{t+1})$. Again, as $S'_t\sim S'_{t-1}$, we must have $m\in M(S'_{t-1})$. Therefore, $m\in M(S'_{t+1})\cap M(S'_{t-1})$ and therefore $S'_{t-1}\sim S'_{t+1}$ in $S'$. This contradicts the notion that $S'$ is an induced cycle in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$. Hence $|M(S'_t)|\geq 2$ for any $t\in [2k+1]$.
Let $\mathcal{M}(G)=\{m_1,m_2,m_3,m_4\}$ and without loss of generality, let $M(S'_1)=\{m_1,m_2\}$. Since $S'_1\nsim S'_3$ in $S'$, we must have $m_3$, $m_4\in M(S'_3)$. Again, as $S'_3\nsim S'_{2k+1}$, we must have $m_1$, $m_2\in M(S'_{2k+1})$. In addition, $S'_{2k+1}\nsim S'_2$ implies $m_3$, $m_4\in M(S'_2)$. Then $M(S'_1)\cap M(S'_2)=\emptyset$ and hence, they are not adjacent in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$, a contradiction. Similarly, we can show that if $\mathcal{M}(G)\leq 3$, then there does not exist any induced cycle of odd length of at least five in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$.

<!-- corollary-11: body_md -->
\label{corcmo}
	Let $C$ be an induced odd cycle of length at least five either in $\mathcal{G}(G)$ or in $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$. Then $|M(S)|\geq 2$ for any $S\in V(C)$.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{corperboth}
    Let $G$ be a finitely generated group. Then the graph $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is perfect if $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\leq 4$ or $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 5$ and $m_1\cap m_2=\Phi(G)$ for all distinct $m_1$, $m_2\in \mathcal{M}(G)$.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
If $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\leq 4$, then by Proposition $\ref{propper}$, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a perfect graph. Now, assume that $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 5$ and $m_1\cap m_2=\Phi(G)$ for all distinct $m_1$, $m_2\in \Phi(G)$. If possible, let $C:C_1\sim C_2\sim \dots\sim C_{2k+1}\sim C_1$ be an induced cycle in $\mathcal{G}(G)$, where $k\geq 2$. By an argument similar to that used in the proof of Proposition \ref{propper}, every vertex of $C$ is contained in at least two maximal subgroups of $G$. Let \(C_i\in C\) and write $M(C_i)=\{m_1,\ldots,m_t\}$,
$t\geq 2$. Then $C_i\subseteq \bigcap_{i=1}^tm_i=\Phi(G)$, contradicting \(C_i\in V(\mathcal{G}(G))\). Hence, the result follows.

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lmnperf}
Let $G$ be a finitely generated group with $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 5$, and suppose that $m_i\cap m_j\not=\Phi(G)$ for all $m_i, m_j\in \mathcal{M}(G)$. Then $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is not a perfect graph.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-29: body_md -->
Let $\mathcal{M}(G)=\{m_1,\ldots,m_t\}$, where $t\geq 5$. Then $m_1\cap m_2\sim m_3\cap m_4\sim m_1\cap m_5\sim m_2\cap m_3\sim m_4\cap m_5\cap m_1\cap m_2$ is an induced $C_5$ in $\mathcal{G}(G)$. Thus $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is not a perfect graph.

<!-- proof-30: body_md -->
If $G$ is isomorphic to one of the groups in $(a)$, $(b)$ or $(c)$, then $G$ has at most four maximal subgroups. Hence by Corollary \ref{corperboth}, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a perfect graph. Now, suppose $G$ is isomorphic to one of the groups in $(d)$, $(e)$, then the uniform intersection number of $G$ is two. Therefore, Corollary~\ref{corperboth} again implies that \(\mathcal{G}(G)\) is perfect. Let $G$ be a group satisfying condition $(f)$. Suppose, to the contrary, that $C: S_1\sim S_2\sim S_3\sim \cdots \sim S_{2k+1}\sim S_1$ is an induced odd cycle in either $\mathcal{G}(G)$ or  $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$, where $k\geq 2$. By Corollary \ref{corcmo}, we have $|M(S_i)|\geq 2$ for each $i$. Hence, for each $i$, there exists $p_i\in \mathcal{S}_{P}(G)$ such that $p_1\sim p_2\sim \cdots\sim p_{2k+1}\sim p_1$ form an induced odd cycle of length $2k+1$ in either
  $\mathcal{G}(G)$ or $\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}$, a contradiction. Consequently $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is a perfect graph.

  Conversely, suppose that $G$ is a finite group such that $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is perfect. If $G$ has at most four maximal subgroups, then $G$ is isomorphic to one of groups listed in $(a)$, $(b)$ or $(c)$. Thus, assume that $|\mathcal{M}(G)|\geq 5$. If $\operatorname{i}(G)\geq 3$, then by Lemma \ref{lmnperf}, $\mathcal{G}(G)$ is not perfect. Hence $\operatorname{i}(G)=2$. We now consider three mutually exclusive cases according to the value of $\operatorname{ui}(G)$. Suppose first that $\operatorname{ui}(G)=2$. Then, by Theorem~\ref{thmcmg}, \(G\) is  isomorphic to one of the groups listed in \((d)\) or \((e)\). Next, suppose that $3\leq \operatorname{ui}(G)\leq 5$. If there exist \(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_{2k+1}\in\mathcal{S}_P(G)\), with \(k\geq2\), that form an induced odd cycle in either \(\mathcal{G}(G)\) or \(\overline{\mathcal{G}(G)}\), then, by Lemma \ref{lmspgt}, \(\mathcal{G}(G)\) is not perfect. Hence, no such induced odd cycle exists, and $G$ is one of the groups described in $(f)$. 
  
  Finally we show that  $\operatorname{ui}(G)\geq 6$ is not possible. Indeed, if $\operatorname{ui}(G)\geq 6$, then there exist five distinct maximal subgroups \(m_{i_1},m_{i_2},m_{i_3},m_{i_4},m_{i_5}\) of \(G\) such that $m_{i_1}\cap m_{i_2}\cap m_{i_3}\cap m_{i_4}\cap m_{i_5}
  \neq \Phi(G)$.  Consequently,  $m_{i_1}\cap m_{i_2}\sim m_{i_3}\cap m_{i_4}\sim m_{i_1}\cap m_{i_5}\sim m_{i_2}\cap m_{i_3}\sim m_{i_4}\cap m_{i_5}\sim m_{i_1}\cap m_{i_2}$ form an induced $C_5$ in $\mathcal{G}(G)$, a contradiction. This completes the proof.

<!-- corollary-12: body_md -->
The graph $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$ is perfect if and only if $n$ has at most four distinct prime divisors.
<!-- end corollary-12 -->
<!-- corollary-13: body_md -->
Let $x$, $y\in V(\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n))$. Then $\deg(x)=\deg(y)$ if and only if
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{i\in [k]-\operatorname{in}(\pi(x))}(t_i+1)=\sum_{j\in [k]-\operatorname{in}(\pi(y))}(t_j+1),\end{equation*}
    where $\pi(x)$ is the set of all prime divisors of $x$ and $\operatorname{in}(\pi(x))$ denotes the set of indices corresponding to the prime divisors of $x$.
<!-- end corollary-13 -->
<!-- corollary-14: body_md -->
\label{cormm}
    Let $n=\prod_{i=1}^kp_i^{t_i}$, where $t_1\geq t_2\geq \dots\geq t_k$. Then the maximum and minimum degrees of $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$ are $\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(t_i+1)-1$ and $t_k$, respectively.
<!-- end corollary-14 -->
<!-- proposition-10: body_md -->
\label{proppen}
    Let $n=\prod_{i=1}^kp_i^{t_i}$, where $t_1\geq t_2\geq \dots \geq t_k$ and $k\geq 2$. Then the following holds:
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    	\item  $\mathcal{G}(G)$ has a pendant vertex if and only if  \(t_i=1\) for some \(i\in[k]\).
    	\item  Suppose that $t_i\geq 2 \quad \text{for } i\in[l],$ and 
    	$ t_i=1 \quad \text{for } i\in\{l+1,\ldots,k\}$.The number of pendant vertex is $(k-l)\prod_{i=1}^lt_i$.
    \end{enumerate} 
    Moreover, if \(t_k=1\), any vertex of the form $(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i^{s_i})$ with $s_i\in[t_i]$, is a pendant vertex of $\mathcal{G}(G)$.
<!-- end proposition-10 -->
<!-- proof-31: body_md -->
$(i)$ Let $P$ be a pendant vertex in $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$. Since $P\in V(\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n))$, we have \(P\nleq \Phi(\mathbb{Z}_n)\). Hence there exists a prime divisor \(p_i\) of \(n\) such that $\langle p_i\rangle \notin M(P)$. If $t_i\geq 2$, then both $\langle p_i\rangle $ and $\langle p_i^2\rangle $ are adjacent to $P$, which is a contradiction. Hence  $t_i=1$.

  Conversely, suppose that $t_k=1$. Then every vertex of the form $(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i^{s_i})$ with $1\leq s_i\leq t_i,$ is adjacent only to $\langle p_k\rangle $.  Thus  $\langle p_k\rangle $ is pendent.\smallskip

  $(ii)$ Let $t_i\geq 2$ for $i\in [l]$ and $t_i=1$ for $i\in \{l+1, \dots,k\}$. Then any vertex of the form \\$\langle p_1^{s_1}p_2^{s_2}\dots p_l^{s_l}p_{l+1}\dots p_{l+i-1}p_{l+i+1}\dots p_{l+r}\rangle $ is adjacent to $\langle p_i\rangle $ only, where $1\leq i\leq r$ and $l+r=k$. Therefore, the number of pendant vertex of $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$ is $(k-l)\prod_{i=1}^lt_i$.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
Let $n=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_i^{t_i},$ where \(p_1,\ldots,p_k\) are distinct primes and \(t_i\geq1\). Then the graph $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$ is Hamiltonian if and only if $n=p_1^{r}p_2^{r}$, where $r\geq 2$.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-32: body_md -->
Let $k=2$ with $t_1=t_2=r\geq 2$. Then
		\begin{equation*}
			\langle p_1\rangle \sim \langle {p_2}^{r}\rangle\sim \langle {p_1}^{r}\rangle\sim \langle {p_2}^{r-1}\rangle\sim \langle {p_1}^{r-1}\rangle \sim \cdots \sim \langle{p_2}\rangle\sim \langle{p_1}\rangle 
		\end{equation*}
		is a Hamiltonian cycle in $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$. Thus $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_{{p_1^r}{p_2^r}})$ is a Hamiltonian graph.
		
		Conversely, suppose that $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$ is a Hamiltonian graph. We show that none of the following can occur: \(k\neq 2\), \(t_1=t_2=1\), or \(t_1\neq t_2\), since in each of these cases $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$ is not Hamiltonian. 
		
		Let $k=2$. If $t_1=t_2=1$, then $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)\cong K_2$, which is not a Hamiltonian graph. Let $t_1\not=t_2$. Without loss of generality, assume that $t_1> t_2$. If $t_1-t_2=1$, then $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$ is a bipartite graph of odd order and hence is not a Hamiltonian Graph. If $t_1-t_2\geq 2$, then one can observe that the circumference of the complete bipartite graph $\mathcal{G}({\mathbb{Z}_n})$ is $2t_2<t_1+t_2=\vert \mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)\vert$ and hence the graph $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$  is again not Hamiltonian.
		
		Finally, let $k\geq 3$. If $t_i=1$ for some $i\in [k]$, then by Corollary \ref{cormm}, $\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)$ contains a pendant vertex and hence is not a Hamiltonian graph. Now suppose that $t_i\geq 2$ for all $i\in [k]$, and let $t_k=\min\{t_i:i\in [k]\}$. Consider the sets $ A=\left\{\left\langle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}p_i^{s_i}\right\rangle:1\leq s_i\leq t_i\right\}$ and $B=\left\{\langle p_k^{s_k}\rangle:1\leq s_k\leq t_k\right\}$. Every vertex in \(A\) is adjacent to every vertex in \(B\), while no two vertices in \(A\) are adjacent and no two vertices in \(B\) are adjacent. Thus, the subgraph of \(\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_n)\) induced by \(A\cup B\) is complete bipartite.
		Suppose that \(\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}n)\) contains a Hamiltonian cycle. Then this cycle must contain a path whose internal vertices include all the vertices of \(A\) and whose two end vertices lie in \(B\). However,  Since $k\geq 3$ and $t_i\geq 2$ for all $i\in [k]$, we have $\vert A\vert=\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}t_i>t_k=\vert B\vert$. By the same argument as above, no such path can exist in the subgraph induced by $V(A\cup B)$, a contradiction. This concludes the proof.
