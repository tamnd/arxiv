On the other hand, every strongly connected digraph can be obtained from a cycle by successive additions of ears \cite[Theorem 7.2.2]{BG08}.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
If $G$ is a cycle, then any $f \in F(G)$ has either zero or two fixed points \cite{DNS12}. We now prove the converse.

Adding an ear in a directed digraph from a vertex $a$ to a vertex $b$, not necessarily distinct, consists either in adding an arc from $a$ to $b$ if it does not already exist, or in adding a path whose vertices are new, say from $a'$ to $b'$ (possibly $a'=b'$), together with an arc from $a$ to $a'$ and from $b'$ to $b$. In the resulting digraph, $b$ has a new in-neighbor, which is $a$ in the first case, and $b'$ in the second; in the second case $b'$ is linear and there is a path from $a$ to $b'$ whose internal vertices are linear. 

 
Every strongly connected digraph can be obtained from a cycle by successive additions of ears \cite[Theorem 7.2.2]{BG08}. Consequently, a strongly connected digraph $G$ which is not a cycle can be obtained from a strongly connected digraph $H$ by adding one ear, say from $a$ to $b$. Let $c$ be the corresponding new in-neighbor of $b$. Let $N_v$ be the in-neighbors of a vertex $v$ in $G$. Let $f\in F(G)$ be defined by
\begin{itemize}
\item
$f_v(x)=\bigwedge_{u\in N_v} x_u$ for all vertices $v\neq b$, and 
\item
$f_b(x)=\neg x_{c}\land \bigwedge_{u\in N_b\setminus \{c\}} x_u$.
\end{itemize} 
It is clear that $f(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}$. Suppose that $f(x)=x$. First assume that $x_a=0$. Since $H$ has a path from $a$ to $b$ (which does not contain $c$) we have $x_b=0$. Suppose now that $x_a=1$. If $a=c$ then $x_b=0$. Otherwise, there is a path from $a$ to $c$ whose internal vertices are linear, and thus $x_{c}=1$ (since $c$ is also linear), so that $x_b=0$. Thus, in every case, $x_b=0$. Since $G$ has a path from $b$ to all the other vertices, we deduce that $x=\mathbf{0}$. Thus $\mathbf{0}$ is the unique fixed point of $f$.

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Recall that the decision problems we are interested in can be expressed as languages of the following form:
    $$
    L_\phi = \{G | \exists f \in F(G) : f\models \phi\}
    $$
    where $\phi$ is a formula in MSO2 and $G$ is a digraph.
    
    Recall that a language $L$ is in $\mathrm{\sf NEXPTIME}$ if and only if there is a Turing machine $M$ with runtime $O(2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)})$ such that:
    $$
    L = \{w | \exists c : M \textrm{ accepts } \langle w,c \rangle\}
    $$
    where $c$ is called the certificate (we refer the interested reader to, e.g., \cite{P94}) and $n$ is the size of the input $w$ (in our case the number of vertices in $G$).

    For our purposes, $w$ is always a digraph $G$. The intention is that $c$ shall represent a function $f: \{0,1\}^{V(G)} \to \{0,1\}^{V(G)}$. 

    We shall evidence that for any MSO2 sentence $\phi$, $L_\phi$ is in $\mathrm{\sf NEXPTIME}$ by describing the machine $M_\phi$ which yields $L_\phi$ when plugged into the definition above. 
    
    As a first step, $M_\phi$ verifies that the function $f$ (as encoded in $c$) is indeed in $F(G)$ by computing $G_f$. This is straightforwardly doable in exponential time -- checking whether $f_i$ depends essentially on $x_j$ (and thus whether $G_f$ has an arc from $j$ to $i$) can be done by brute force in time $O(2^n)$ for each $i,j \in [n]$, so verifying that $(i,j) \in E(G) \iff (i,j) \in E(G_f)$ is doable in time $O(n^2\cdot 2^n)$. (If $c$ does not encode a function $f\in F(G)$ then $M_\phi$ rejects.)
    
    Next, $M_\phi$ decides whether $D_f \models \phi$. The functional digraph $D_f$ of $f$ can be  represented explicitly in space $O(n\cdot 2^n)$ (such a representation would be computed in the previous step). Also, $D_f$ has bounded treewidth: each connected component of $D_f$ contains at most one cycle, and thus the component (and $D_f$ as a whole) has treewidth at most $2$. 
    
    Consequently, by applying Courcelle's theorem (see \cite{DF13}, chapter 13) $M_\phi$ can decide whether $D_f \models \phi$ in time $|D_f| \cdot g(\mathrm{tw}(D_f)+|\phi|)) \in O(|D_f|)$ for some function $g : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$. Since $|D_f|=2^n$, for any fixed $\phi$ the runtime is simply $O(2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)})$. The result follows.

    Likewise if $c^j$ does not encode an assignment $a^j$ to the (existentially-quantified) variables in $X^j$ for some odd $j$.

    Thus, we may henceforth assume that the input is a tuple of the form described above, and make use of the notation we had introduced to explain how $M_{d,\phi}$ processes the input.

    $M_{\phi,d}$ makes a linear pass over the quantifier-free formula $\psi$. Each variable in $\psi$ is $x^{j,k}$ for some $j,k \in \mathbb{N}^*$, and has corresponding assignment $a^{j,k} \in \{0,1\}^{V(G)}$.     \begin{itemize}
        \item For a primitive $(x = y)$, the value \textrm{true} is substituted by $M_{\phi,d}$ if $x=y$ and \textrm{false} is substituted otherwise.
        \item For a primitive $(x \to y)$, $f(x)$ is evaluated using $\mathcal{A}_f$ in time $O(n \cdot 2^d)$ as a subroutine in $M_{\phi,d}$ and the value \textrm{true} is substituted if $f(x)=y$ and \textrm{false} is substituted otherwise.
    \end{itemize}
    Evaluating whether $\psi$ is satisfied by the given assignment is thus feasible in time $O(|\psi|\cdot n \cdot 2^d)$.

    $M_{d,\phi}$ accepts $\langle G, \langle \mathcal{A}_f, a^1 \rangle, a^2, \ldots, a^i\rangle$ exactly according to the characterization above. 
    On the other hand, it is clear that if $M_{d,\phi}$ accepts a graph $G$ then there exists some $f \in F(G)$ such that $D_f \models \phi$.

If $G$ is acyclic, then $f$ has a unique limit cycle, which is of length one (Lemma~\ref{lem:robert}), so we are done. Let $G$ have a cycle. Let $f\in F(G)$ and let $f^2=f\circ f$.  

\begin{claim}\label{clm:power-1}
If every limit cycle of $f^2$ has length a power of $2$, then every limit cycle of $f$ has length a power of $2$.
\end{claim}

\begin{claimproof}
Suppose that $f$ has a limit cycle of length $q$, and let $X$ be its set of configurations. If $q$ is odd then $f^2$ has a limit cycle of length $q$ whose set of configurations is also $X$, so the claim vacuously holds. So $q=2p$ for some $p\geq 1$. Then there is a partition of $X$ in two sets $X_0,X_1$ of equal size such that $f^2$ has two limit cycles of length $p$ whose set of configurations are $X_0$ and $X_1$. If $p$ is a power of two, then $q$ is a power of two and the claim holds. Otherwise, $p$ is not a power of two and the claim vacuously holds.
\end{claimproof}

We denote by $\ell(G)$ the maximum length of a cycle in $G$. We denote by $N(G)$ the set of vertices $i$ in $G$ that have an out-neighbor $j\neq i$. Let $G^2$ be the digraph with vertex set $V(G)$ and an arc from $a$ to $b$ if there exists $c$ (not necessarily distinct from $a,b$) such that $G$ has an arc from $a$ to $c$ and from $c$ to $b$. Let $G^{(2)}$ be the interaction graph of $f^2$. 

\begin{claim}\label{clm:power-2}
Let $G$ be as in the lemma statement. Then the following holds:
\begin{itemize}
\item \label{item:G2_satisfies}
$G^2$ satisfies the conditions of the lemma,  
\item \label{item:l(G2)}
$\ell(G^2)=\max(1,\ell(G)/2)$,
\item \label{item:N(G2)}
$N(G^2)\subseteq N(G)$,
\item \label{item:G2_subgraph}
$G^{(2)}\subseteq G^2$.
\end{itemize}
\end{claim}
\begin{claimproof}
Let the set of loops of $G$ be $L$. Denote the other cycles of $G$ as $C^s = \{ v^s_0, v^s_1, \dots, v^s_{2 n_s - 1} \}$ for all $s \in S$. Then the collection of cycles of $G^2$ is $L \cup \{ C_0^s, C_1^s : s \in S \}$, where $C_0^s = \{ v^s_0, v^s_2, \dots, v^s_{2 n_s - 2} \}$ and $C_1^s = \{ v^s_1, v^s_3, \dots, v^s_{2 n_s - 1} \}$ for all $s \in S$. The first two items immediately follow.

We now prove the third item. If $i \in N(G^2)$, then there exist $k$ and $j \ne i$ such that $(i,k)$ and $(k,j)$ are arcs of $G$. If $k \ne i$, then $i \in N(G)$ because of $(i,k)$; if $k = i$, then $i \in N(G)$ because of $(k,j)$.

We finally prove the fourth item. For any configuration $c \in \{0,1\}^{V(G)}$, let $c^1 = f( c )$ and $c^2 = f^2( c )$ and denote $c_{-i} = ( c_v : v \in V(G) \setminus\{ i \} )$. 
Let $(i,j)$ be an arc of $G^{(2)}$.
Then there exist $a, b \in \{0,1\}^{V(G)}$ such that $a_{-i} = b_{-i}$ and $a^2_j \ne a^2_j$. Since $a^2_j \ne b^2_j$, there exists $k$ such that $(k,j) \in E( G )$ and $a^1_k \ne b^1_k$. But then, $a_{-i} = b_{-i}$ and $a^1_k \ne b^1_k$ imply that $(i,k) \in E(G)$ as well. Thus $(i,j)$ is an arc of $G^2$.
\end{claimproof}

We now prove the lemma by induction on $\ell(G)$, and then on $N(G)$.  Suppose that $\ell(G)=1$. If $N(G)=\emptyset$, then all the arcs of $G$ are loops, and we easily check that every limit cycle of $f$ has length $1$ or $2$. Suppose that $N(G)\neq\emptyset$. Since $\ell(G)=1$, there exists a vertex $i\in N(G)$ such that either $i$ is a source or has itself as unique in-neighbor. 

 
Suppose that $i$ is a source. Then there exists $\alpha$ such that $f_i(x)=\alpha$ for all $x\in\{0,1\}^G$. Let $x'=x$ if $x_i=\alpha$, and $x'=x+e_i$ otherwise, and let $f' : \{0,1\}^G\to\{0,1\}^G$ defined by $f'(x)=f(x')$. Then the interaction graph $G'$ of $f'$ is a subgraph of $G$ and $i$ has no out-neighbor in $G'$. Thus $N(G')\subsetneq N(G)$ and, by induction, every limit cycle of $f'$ has length a power of $2$. Since $f$ and $f'$ have the same limit cycles we are done.

 
If $i$ is not a source (i.e., $i$ has itself as unique in-neighbor), then $f^2_i(x)=x_i$ for all $x\in\{0,1\}^G$. By Claim \ref{clm:power-2}, $\ell(G^{(2)})\leq 1$ and $N(G^{(2)})\subseteq N(G^2)\subseteq N(G)$. For all $x\in \{0,1\}^G$ and $\alpha\in\{0,1\}$, let $x^\alpha=x$ if $x_i=\alpha$, and $x^\alpha=x+e_i$ otherwise. Let $f^{(\alpha)}:\{0,1\}^G\to\{0,1\}^G$ defined by $f^{(\alpha)}(x)=f(x^\alpha)$.  Then the interaction graph $G^\alpha$ of $f^{(\alpha)}$ is a subgraph of $G^2$ and $i$ has no out-neighbor in $G^\alpha$. Thus $N(G^\alpha)\subsetneq N(G)$ and, by induction, every limit cycle of $f^{(\alpha)}$ has length a power of $2$. Since every limit cycle of $f^2$ is a limit cycle of $f^{(0)}$ or $f^{(1)}$, we deduce that every limit cycle of $f^2$ has length a power of $2$. Thus, by Claim \ref{clm:power-1}, every limit cycle of $f$ has length a power of $2$. This completes the case $\ell(G)=1$. 

 
Suppose that $\ell(G)\geq 2$. By Claim \ref{clm:power-2} and the induction hypothesis, every limit cycle of $f^2$ has length a power of $2$. Thus, by Claim \ref{clm:power-1}, every limit cycle of $f$ has length a power of $2$.

Recall our instance $G$ of $\textrm{\sc UFP}$ is a loop-to-cycle digraph.
The construction of $G_{\Lambda, \Sigma}$ is as follows. 
Let $k$ be an integer greater than any element of $\Lambda$ and greater than the degree of any permutation in $\Sigma$. 
Let $m$ be the length of a longest terminal cycle in $G$ and let $N = \max(\lceil\log_2 m\rceil,\lceil\log_2 k\rceil)$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:subdivision}, $G'$ is UFP-dynamic if and only if $G$ is UFP-dynamic. Let $n = \lceil\log_2 k\rceil + 2$, and let $G'_1, \dots, G'_n$ be disjoint copies of $G'$.

Finally, $G_{\Lambda, \Sigma}$ is the disjoint union of $G'_1, \dots, G'_n, H_\sigma$. 

 
We now prove that $G'$ is UFP-dynamic if and only if $G_{\Lambda, \Sigma}$ is Period Piece$(\Lambda, \Sigma)$-dynamic.

\begin{claim}
    If $G_{\Lambda, \Sigma}$ is Period Piece$(\Lambda, \Sigma)$-dynamic then $G$ is UFP-dynamic.
\end{claim}

\begin{claimproof}
Suppose that $G_{\Lambda, \Sigma}$ is Period Piece$(\Lambda, \Sigma)$-dynamic. Let $f \in F( G_{\Lambda, \Sigma} )$ with $\mathrm{Per}_\Lambda( f ) = \tau$ for some $\tau \in \Sigma$. Let $f^i$ be the restriction of $f$ on $G_i$, and let $h'$ be the restriction of $f$ on $H_\sigma$. Hence $f=(f^1, \dots, f^n, h')$. Suppose, for a contradiction, that each $f^i$ does not have a unique fixed point. By Lemma~\ref{lem:power}, every limit cycle of $f^i$ has length a power of $2$. Let $\ell$ with $n_\ell( \tau ) \geq 1$; thus $\ell \in \Lambda$. Since $\mathrm{Per}_\Lambda( f ) = \tau$, we have $n_\ell(f) = n_\ell(\tau) \geq 1$. But, by Lemma~\ref{lem:two-copies}, we have 
\[
    n_\ell( f ) \ge 2^{n-1} > k \ge n_\ell( \tau ),
\]
a contradiction. Thus $f^i$ has a unique fixed point for some $1\leq i\leq n$. So $G'_i$ is UFP-dynamic and thus $G'$ is UFP-dynamic. 
\end{claimproof}

\begin{claim}
    If 
    $G$ is UFP-dynamic
    then 
    $G_{\Lambda, \Sigma}$ is Period Piece$(\Lambda, \Sigma)$-dynamic.
\end{claim}
\begin{claimproof}
Suppose that $G'$ is UFP-dynamic. For $1\leq i\leq n$, let $f^i\in F(G'_i)$ with a unique fixed point $x^i$. Let $f' = (f^1, \dots, f^n)$ and $f=(f',h)$, where $h$ is the Boolean network, already constructed, with $\mathrm{Per}( h ) = \sigma$ for $\sigma \in \Sigma$ chosen above. Then $f\in F( G_{\Lambda, \Sigma} )$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:long_cycle}, for all $1\leq i\leq n$, all the periodic configurations of $f^i$ distinct from $x^i$ have period at least $2^{N+1}$. We deduce that $x=(x^1,\dots,x^n)$ is the unique fixed point of $f'$ and that all the periodic configurations of $f'$ distinct from $x$ have period at least $2^{N+1}$: there is no $2\leq p<2^{N+1}$ with $n_p(f')\geq 1$. \[

\]
So $\mathrm{Per}_\Lambda( f ) = \sigma$ and thus $G_{\Lambda, \Sigma}$ is Period Piece$(\Lambda, \Sigma)$-dynamic. 
\end{claimproof}
