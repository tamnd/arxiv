<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:carrier-zero-set}
    Let $Z$ be a proper, relatively closed real-analytic subset of $N$. If $\varphi^t(Z)=Z$ for all $t\in\mathbb R$, then $\mu(Z)=0$. Consequently, if an analytic section of an analytic vector bundle over $N$ has a flow-invariant zero set and vanishes $\mu$-almost everywhere, then it vanishes identically on $N$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
First, we show that a proper relatively closed analytic subset of the connected analytic manifold $N$ has empty interior.

    To see this directly, let $E=\operatorname{int}_N Z$. By definition, $E$ is an open subset of $N$. Suppose that $E \neq \emptyset$. Then, for each point $p \in \overline{E}^{N}$ (the closure of $E$ in $N$), since $Z$ is an analytic subset, we can choose a connected local analytic coordinate chart (neighborhood) $V$ such that
    \begin{displaymath}
        Z \cap V = \{ g_{1}=\cdot\cdot\cdot=g_{r} = 0\},
    \end{displaymath}
    where $g_{i} \in C^{\omega}(V)$ for each $i \in \{1,\dots,r\}$. Since $p \in \overline{E}^{N}$ and $V$ is a neighborhood of $p$, $V \cap E \neq \emptyset$. While both $E$ and $V$ are open subsets in $N$, $V \cap E$ is a non-empty open subset of $N$, and
    \begin{displaymath}
        E \cap V \subseteq  Z \cap V = \{ g_{1}=\cdot\cdot\cdot=g_{r} = 0\}.
    \end{displaymath}
    Thus, each $g_{i}$ vanishes identically on $E \cap V$. By the connectedness of $V$ and the real analyticity of $g_{i}$, we know that $g_{i}\equiv 0$ on $V$. Therefore
    \begin{displaymath}
        V \subseteq \{ g_{1}=\cdot\cdot\cdot=g_{r} = 0\}=Z \cap V \subseteq Z.
    \end{displaymath}
    Since $V$ is a neighborhood of the point $p$, the above inclusion implies that $p \in E$. Thus $E = \overline{E}^{N}$, i.e., $E$ is both open and closed in $N$, and the connectedness of $N$ implies that either $E = \emptyset$ or $E = N$. If $E = N$, then $Z = N$, which contradicts the fact that $Z$ is a proper subset of $N$. Therefore $E =\operatorname{int}_N Z= \emptyset$, i.e., $Z$ has no interior points. Furthermore, by Bierstone-Milman's stratification theory \cite{BierstoneMilman1988}, we have $\dim Z<d =\dim N$.

    Second, we prove that $Z$ can be decomposed into countably many connected, flow-invariant analytic pieces.

    We will use the analytic regular-locus theorem and the local stratification theorem for semianalytic sets \cite[Corollary~2.11, Theorem~7.2, and Remark~7.3]{BierstoneMilman1988} in the following intrinsic form. For a semianalytic set $X$ of dimension $r$, let
    \begin{displaymath}
        \mathcal R_r(X)=\{x\in X\mid X~\text{is an embedded analytic}~r\text{-manifold near}~x\}.
    \end{displaymath}
    Here ``analytic manifold'' is essential; see \cite[Definitions~3.3 and~7.1]{BierstoneMilman1988}. The set $\mathcal R_r(X)$ is relatively open and semianalytic, and
    \begin{displaymath}
        \dim\left(X\setminus\mathcal R_r(X)\right)<r.
    \end{displaymath}
    We recall the dimension argument to make clear how the stratification is used. Take a compatible locally finite analytic stratification with the frontier condition. At a point of an $r$-dimensional stratum $S$, no other stratum of $X$ can have that point in its frontier: the frontier condition would require a stratum of dimension strictly larger than $r$. Local finiteness permits a neighborhood meeting only finitely many strata, and shrinking this neighborhood away from the closures of the other strata makes $X$ locally equal to $S$. Hence, every top-dimensional stratum lies in $\mathcal{R} _ {r} (X)$, and the complement has a smaller dimension. Semianalyticity of the regular locus is the additional conclusion of \cite[Remark~7.3]{BierstoneMilman1988}.

    Starting with $X_0=Z$, define, as long as $X_j\ne\varnothing$,
    \begin{displaymath}
        r_j=\dim X_j,\qquad X_{j+1}=X_j\setminus\mathcal R_{r_j}(X_j).
    \end{displaymath}

    By Bierstone-Milman's theory as stated above, $\mathcal R_{r_j}(X_j)$ is relatively open and semianalytic, and
    \begin{displaymath}
        \dim\left(X_{j}\setminus\mathcal R_{r_j}(X_j)\right)<r_{j}.
    \end{displaymath}
    Since the dimensions decrease strictly, this procedure stops after finitely many steps. Each connected component of $\mathcal R_{r_j}(X_j)$ is an embedded analytic submanifold. The connected components of the sets $\mathcal R_{r_j}(X_j)$ form a countable decomposition of $Z$ into connected embedded analytic submanifolds of dimension less than $d$. Countability follows because each regular locus is a second-countable manifold and its connected components are open. See \cite{BierstoneMilman1988} for more details.

    This decomposition is defined by the local analytic structure of the set, rather than by a choice of charts. Every analytic diffeomorphism preserving $Z$ therefore preserves each $X_j$ and each regular locus $\mathcal R_{r_j}(X_j)$. In particular, the flow does so. If $x$ belongs to a connected component $C$ of one of these regular loci, the path $t\mapsto\varphi^t(x)$ remains in that regular locus and starts in $C$. It stays in $C$. Applying the same argument to negative time shows $\varphi^t(C)=C$. Thus $Z$ can be decomposed into countably many connected, flow-invariant analytic pieces $C_{l}$; each $C_{l}$ satisfies $\dim C_{l}\leq \dim Z < d$.

    Third, we show that $\mu(Z)=0$.

    Since $Z$ is an $f$-invariant Borel set, the ergodicity shows that $\mu(Z)\in\{0,1\}$. If $\mu(Z)=1$, by the fact that $Z = \bigcup_{l}C_{l}$, i.e., $Z$ is a countable union, there exists an $l_{0}$ such that $\mu(C_{l_{0}})>0$. Since $C_{l_{0}}$ is flow-invariant, it is $f$-invariant, while $\mu$ is an ergodic measure of $f$; this implies that $\mu(C_{l_{0}})=1$. Thus $C_{l_{0}}$ is a connected, flow-invariant, embedded real-analytic submanifold with $\mu(C_{l_{0}})=1$, and $\dim C_{l_{0}} < d =\dim N$. This contradicts the minimal choice of $d$. Hence $\mu(Z)=0$.

    Finally, in a local analytic frame, the zero set of an analytic section is the common zero set of its finitely many coefficients. It is a relatively closed analytic subset. If the section were not identically zero, the assertion just proved would contradict its almost-everywhere vanishing.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:finite-normal-order}
    A nonzero analytic section on the connected set $U$ has finite normal order along $N$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Fix $x_{0}\in N$ and adapted analytic coordinates $(y,z)\in\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^q$ centered at $x_{0}$ as above. Let $e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{r}$ be a local analytic frame of $\mathcal E$ on $U_{x_{0}}$; in this frame $s$ can be written as
    \begin{displaymath}
        s=\sum^{r}_{j=1}s_{j}e_{j},\qquad s_{j}\in C^{\omega}(U_{x_{0}}),~j=1,\ldots,r.
    \end{displaymath}
    Each $s_{j}$ can be written in the following form in the adapted coordinates
    \begin{displaymath}
        s_{j}(y,z)=\sum_{\alpha\in \mathbb{N}^{q}}c_{j,\alpha}(y)z^{\alpha},\qquad j=1,\ldots,r,
    \end{displaymath}
    where $c_{j,\alpha}$ is an analytic function on the neighborhood of $0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$. Furthermore, $s$ can be written as
    \begin{displaymath}
        s=\sum^{r}_{j=1}\Big(\sum_{\alpha\in \mathbb{N}^{q}}c_{j,\alpha}(y)z^{\alpha} \Big)e_{j},\qquad  s_{j}\in C^{\omega}(U_{x_{0}}),~j=1,\ldots,r.
    \end{displaymath}

    Suppose that $\operatorname{ord}_N s =+\infty$. By definition, for each $a \in \mathbb{N}$, we have $s_{x_{0}}\in\mathcal I_{N,x_{0}}^{a}\mathcal E_{x_{0}}$. As we have seen in \eqref{eq1}, in the adapted coordinates, $\mathcal I_{N,x_{0}} = \big(z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\big)$, and
    \begin{displaymath}
        \mathcal I_{N,x_{0}}^{a}\mathcal E_{x_{0}} = \left\{ \sum^{r}_{j=1}f_{j}e_{j}~\mid~f_{j} \in (z_{1},\ldots,z_{q})^{a} \right\}.
    \end{displaymath}

    Thus,
    \begin{displaymath}
        \begin{aligned}
            s_{x_{0}}\in\mathcal I_{N,x_{0}}^{a}\mathcal E_{x_{0}} & \Longleftrightarrow  s_{j} \in (z_{1},\ldots,z_{q})^{a}  \\
                                                                   & \Longleftrightarrow c_{j,\alpha}(y) = 0, \quad |\alpha|< a.\\
        \end{aligned}
    \end{displaymath}
    The fact that $\operatorname{ord}_N s =+\infty$ implies that the above equality is valid for every $a\in \mathbb{N}$; thus for all $j$ and $\alpha$, we have $c_{j,\alpha}(y) = 0$. Furthermore $s \equiv 0$ in $U_{x_{0}}$, where $U_{x_{0}}\subset U$ is the adapted coordinate neighborhood of $x_{0}$ above. By the identity theorem for real-analytic functions, we get that $s \equiv 0$ in $U$, which contradicts the assumption that $s$ is not identically zero.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:order-rules}
    If $\operatorname{ord}_NJ=a\geq1$, then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:derivative-order}
        \operatorname{ord}_N(d_PJ)\geq a-1.
    \end{equation}

In the full coframe $dy_1,\ldots,dy_d,dz_1,\ldots,dz_q$, normal differentiation lowers normal degree by at most one, while tangential differentiation does not lower it. This gives \eqref{eq:derivative-order}.

    Let the two orders in \eqref{eq:product-order} be $a$ and $b$. The product has order at least $a+b$, and its degree-$a+b$ term is $P_J(x;v)P_s(x;v)$. Each of the two leading sections has a proper analytic zero set in the connected manifold $N$. Their nonzero sets are open and dense, so they have a common point $x_0$. At $x_0$, choose a nonzero component of the vector-valued polynomial $P_s(x_0;\cdot)$. The product of that component with the nonzero polynomial $P_J(x_0;\cdot)$ is nonzero, because a real polynomial ring has no zero divisors. Thus, the leading product section is not identically zero, which proves equality.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:finite-elimination}
    Starting with \eqref{eq:current-list-properties}, one can make finitely many constant-coefficient polynomial replacements of the $A_i$ so that these properties continue to hold and the resulting leading normal polynomials $P_1(x),\ldots, P_n(x)$ are algebraically independent on $W_x$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
If $\mu(B)=0$, there is nothing to do. Otherwise, \eqref{eq:global-polynomial-relation} supplies a nonzero global weighted homogeneous relation. Among all such relations, choose one of the smallest positive weighted degree $D$, and denote it by $R$.

    Since the coefficient field has characteristic zero and $D>0$, there is an index $j$ such that $Q_0:=\partial_jR\ne0$.  Indeed, if all partial derivatives of a real polynomial were zero, every nonconstant monomial would have zero coefficient: differentiating a monomial with $\beta_j>0$ gives the nonzero coefficient $\beta_jc_\beta$, and distinct monomials cannot cancel after differentiating with respect to the same variable. The resulting constant polynomial would be incompatible with a positive weighted degree.

    The polynomial $Q_0$ is weighted homogeneous of degree $D-a_j\geq0$. If this degree is zero, $Q_0$ is a nonzero constant. If it is positive and $Q_0(P_1,\ldots, P_n)$ were identically zero on $N$, it would be a nonzero global relation of smaller degree than $D$. Thus, in either case
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:derivative-relation-nonzero}
        Q_0(P_1,\ldots,P_n)\not\equiv0\quad\text{on }N.
    \end{equation}
    Set
    \begin{displaymath}
        Q=Q_0(A_1,\ldots,A_n),\qquad
        A'_j=R(A_1,\ldots,A_n),\qquad A'_i=A_i\ (i\ne j).
    \end{displaymath}
    Weighted expansion and \eqref{eq:derivative-relation-nonzero} show
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:multiplier-order}
        Q\not\equiv0,
        \qquad \operatorname{ord}_NQ=D-a_j.
    \end{equation}
    For completeness, every monomial of $Q_0$ has weighted degree $D-a_j$. Substituting the leading terms gives its degree-$(D-a_j)$ normal term, namely $Q_0(P)$, while using at least one higher-order remainder raises the normal degree. The displayed nonvanishing gives equality of orders.

    We next estimate the order of $A'_j$ without assuming that its first possible remainder coefficient is nonzero. In adapted coordinates, write $A_i=P_i+\varepsilon_i$, where $\varepsilon_i=O(|z|^{a_i+1})$.  For a monomial $A^\beta$ of $R(A)$, $\sum_i a_i\beta_i=D$.  If $b_i$ of the $\beta_i$ factors of type $i$ are chosen from $\varepsilon_i$, its normal order is at least
    \begin{displaymath}
        \sum_i\left((\beta_i-b_i)a_i+b_i(a_i+1)\right)
        =D+\sum_i b_i.
    \end{displaymath}
    The sum of terms with every $b_i=0$ is $R(P)$, which vanishes identically. Every other term has order at least $D+1$. Hence
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:new-order-lower-bound}
        \operatorname{ord}_NA'_j\geq D+1,
    \end{equation}
    where at this stage the left side could a priori be infinite.

    To rule out that possibility, use the exact ambient chain rule:
    \begin{displaymath}
        d_PA'_j=\sum_{k=1}^n(\partial_kR)(A)\,d_PA_k.
    \end{displaymath}
    In the exterior product with the other $n-1$ differentials in their original positions, all terms except $k=j$ vanish because a factor is repeated. The surviving term is in the original position, so there is no extra sign. Therefore
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:exact-wedge-identity}
        \Omega':=d_PA'_1\wedge\cdots\wedge d_PA'_n=Q\Omega.
    \end{equation}
    Both factors on the right are nonzero analytic objects on the connected set $U$. Their nonzero sets are open and dense, so $Q\Omega\not\equiv0$. In particular, $A'_j\not\equiv0$ and the new list is generically independent in $U$. Its order $a'_j$ is finite by Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-normal-order}, and \eqref{eq:new-order-lower-bound} now gives $a'_j>D$.

    By \eqref{eq:product-order}, \eqref{eq:multiplier-order}, and \eqref{eq:exact-wedge-identity},
    \begin{displaymath}
        w':=\operatorname{ord}_N\Omega'=w+D-a_j.
    \end{displaymath}
    Since $a'_i=a_i$ for $i\ne j$, the new decreasing quantity satisfies
    \begin{displaymath}
        \begin{aligned}
            \delta'
 &=w'-\left(\sum_{i\ne j}a_i+a'_j\right)+n \notag\\
 &=w+D-a_j-\left(\sum_i a_i-a_j+a'_j\right)+n \notag\\
 &=\delta+D-a'_j<\delta.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{displaymath}
    All entries of the new list remain analytic, vanish on $N$, and are constant-coefficient polynomials in the old entries. The Leibniz rule for the Poisson bracket therefore preserves both their mutual commutativity and their commutativity with $H$. The new exterior product is nonzero by \eqref{eq:exact-wedge-identity}, and its new quantity $\delta'$ is again a nonnegative integer.

    Whenever the dependent-fiber set has positive measure, the construction strictly decreases this nonnegative integer. There can be at most the initial value of $\delta$ such replacements. The process must therefore reach a list for which the dependent-fiber set has measure zero. This is a finite analytic construction, with no infinite formal substitution or convergence issue.

\label{lem:jacobian-criterion}
    Assume that $P_1,\ldots,P_n\in\mathbb R[z_1,\ldots,z_q]$ are algebraically independent. Then
    \begin{displaymath}
        \{v\in\mathbb R^q:dP_1(v),\ldots,dP_n(v)~\text{are linearly independent}\}
    \end{displaymath}

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $K=\mathbb R(z_1,\ldots,z_q)$. Extend $P_1,\ldots,P_n$ to a transcendence basis $P_1,\ldots,P_n,T_{n+1},\ldots,T_q$ of $K/\mathbb R$, and put
    \begin{displaymath}
        K_0=\mathbb R(P_1,\ldots,P_n,T_{n+1},\ldots,T_q).
    \end{displaymath}

    The extension $K/K_0$ is algebraic and is generated by the finitely many elements $z_1,\ldots,z_q$, so it is finite. It is separable because the characteristic is zero.

    For each $1\leq j\leq n$, algebraic independence allows us to define an $\mathbb R$-derivation $D_j$ on $K_0$ by
    \begin{displaymath}
        D_jP_i=\delta_{ij}\quad (1\leq i\leq n), \qquad D_jT_k=0\quad (n+1\leq k\leq q).
    \end{displaymath}
    We extend these derivations to $K$ by successively adjoining $z_1,\ldots,z_q$.

    To justify each extension, let $D$ be an $\mathbb R$-derivation on an intermediate field $L$, and let $\alpha$ be algebraic over $L$, with minimal polynomial $M(t)\in L[t]$. For $F(t)\in L[t]$, write $DF$ for the polynomial obtained by applying $D$ to its coefficients, and write $F'$ for its formal derivative with respect to $t$. Since the characteristic is zero, $M'(\alpha)\ne0$. Set
    \begin{displaymath}
        b=-\frac{(DM)(\alpha)}{M'(\alpha)}
    \end{displaymath}
    and define
    \begin{displaymath}
        \widetilde D(F(\alpha))=(DF)(\alpha)+F'(\alpha)b.
    \end{displaymath}
    If $F$ is replaced by $F+MG$, the right-hand side changes by
    \begin{displaymath}
        G(\alpha)\bigl((DM)(\alpha)+M'(\alpha)b\bigr)=0.
    \end{displaymath}
    Thus the definition is independent of the polynomial representative. It is additive, satisfies the Leibniz rule, and agrees with $D$ on $L$. Since $L[\alpha]=L(\alpha)$, it defines a derivation on $L(\alpha)$. Its value on $\alpha$ is forced by differentiating $M(\alpha)=0$, so the extension is unique. Applying this construction successively gives derivations $D_j:K\to K$ with the prescribed values on $P_1,\ldots,P_n$.

    Let $J$ be the $n\times q$ Jacobian matrix
    \begin{displaymath}
        J=\left(\frac{\partial P_i}{\partial z_k}\right),
    \end{displaymath}
    and let $B$ be the $q\times n$ matrix over $K$ defined by
    \begin{displaymath}
        B_{kj}=D_jz_k.
    \end{displaymath}
    Since each $D_j$ annihilates $\mathbb R$, the Leibniz rule applied to the polynomials $P_i$ gives
    \begin{displaymath}
        (JB)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^q\frac{\partial P_i}{\partial z_k}\,D_jz_k=D_jP_i=\delta_{ij}.
    \end{displaymath}
    Hence $JB=I_n$, and $J$ has rank $n$ over $K$.

    Consequently, some $n\times n$ minor $\Delta$ of $J$ is a nonzero real polynomial. Its nonzero set
    \begin{displaymath}
        \{v\in\mathbb R^q:\Delta(v)\ne0\}
    \end{displaymath}
    is nonempty, open, and dense. The set on which $J(v)$ has rank $n$ is open, since it is the union of the nonzero sets of its $n\times n$ minors, and it contains the above dense set. Finally, $J(v)$ has rank $n$ exactly when $dP_1(v),\ldots,dP_n(v)$ are linearly independent. This proves the assertion.

\label{lem:invariant-section-zero-exponent}
    Let $L$ be an invertible finite-dimensional measurable linear cocycle over an invertible ergodic probability-preserving transformation, satisfying \eqref{eq:oseledets-integrability}. Suppose a measurable section $s$ with $\parallel s(x)\parallel < \infty$ almost everywhere satisfies
    \begin{displaymath}
        s(fx)=L_xs(x)
    \end{displaymath}
    almost everywhere. Then $s(x)$ belongs to the zero Lyapunov subspace almost everywhere. Neither integrability nor boundedness of $\|s\|$ is required.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
By \eqref{eq:oseledets-integrability}, the invertible multiplicative ergodic theorem applies to the measurable vector bundle $\mathcal E$ over the probability-preserving system $(N,\mu,f)$. It yields a full-measure $f$-invariant Oseledets regular set $\mathcal R$ on which
    \begin{displaymath}
        \mathcal E_x=\bigoplus_\rho \mathcal E_x^\rho,
    \end{displaymath}
    where $\rho$ ranges over finitely many Lyapunov exponents. Each subspace $\mathcal {E} _ {x} ^\rho$ is invariant along the orbit,
    \begin{displaymath}
        L_x\mathcal E_x^\rho=\mathcal E_{fx}^\rho.
    \end{displaymath}
    Let
    \begin{displaymath}
        L_x:\mathcal E_x\longrightarrow \mathcal E_{fx}
    \end{displaymath}
    be the cocycle. We work on a common invariant set of full measure on which the Oseledets splitting and the equivariance identity for $s$ hold along the entire two-sided orbit. If the identity $s(fx)=L_xs(x)$ holds only on a full-measure set $E$, we replace $E$ by
    \begin{displaymath}
        \bigcap_{k\in\mathbb Z}f^kE
    \end{displaymath}
    and intersect it with the Oseledets regular set; this remains a full-measure set, and all iterated identities below are then valid simultaneously. For $m\ge 1$, define
    \begin{displaymath}
        L_x^{(m)}=L_{f^{m-1}x}\cdots L_x,
        \qquad
        L_x^{(-m)}=\left(L_{f^{-m}x}^{(m)}\right)^{-1}.
    \end{displaymath}
    Then, for every nonzero $v\in \mathcal E_x^\rho$,
    \begin{displaymath}
        \lim_{m\to\infty}\frac1m\log\|L_x^{(m)}v\|=\rho,
        \qquad
        \lim_{m\to\infty}\frac1m\log\|L_x^{(-m)}v\|=-\rho.
    \end{displaymath}
    Since $f$ is ergodic, the Lyapunov exponents are $\mu$-almost everywhere constant, and there are only finitely many distinct values.

    For each $\rho$, let $\pi_\rho(x):\mathcal E_x\to \mathcal E_x$ be the projection onto $\mathcal{E}_x^\rho$ along the other Oseledets subspaces.  It is linear, idempotent, and satisfies
    \begin{displaymath}
        \operatorname{im}\pi_\rho(x)=\mathcal E_x^\rho,
        \qquad
        \ker\pi_\rho(x)=\bigoplus_{\rho'\neq\rho}\mathcal E_x^{\rho'},
        \qquad
        \sum_\rho\pi_\rho(x)=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal E_x}.
    \end{displaymath}
    Moreover, it is equivariant:
    \begin{displaymath}
        \pi_\rho(fx)\circ L_x=L_x\circ\pi_\rho(x).
    \end{displaymath}
    Consequently, if $s(fx)=L_xs(x)$ and we set $s_\rho(x)=\pi_\rho(x)s(x)$, then
    \begin{displaymath}
        s_\rho(fx)
        =\pi_\rho(fx)s(fx)
        =\pi_\rho(fx)L_xs(x)
        =L_x\pi_\rho(x)s(x)
        =L_xs_\rho(x),
    \end{displaymath}
    so $s_\rho$ is again an invariant section. It is measurable and has finite norm almost everywhere, because $\pi_\rho$ is measurable and $s$ has finite norm almost everywhere.

    The projection $\pi_\rho(x)$ need not have uniformly bounded norm: its norm may grow with $x$, since $\mathcal{E}_x^\rho$ and the sum of the other Oseledets subspaces may become nearly parallel in the original norm. Thus we cannot assert that $\|s_\rho\|$ is bounded or integrable. This is precisely why the present lemma assumes only that $s$ has finite norm almost everywhere.

    For each positive integer $r$, set
    \begin{displaymath}
        B_{r,\rho}=\{x:\|s_\rho(x)\|\le r\}.
    \end{displaymath}
    These sets cover the Oseledets set as $r$ varies. By the Poincar\'e recurrence theorem, for each $B_{r,\rho}$ and for both $f$ and $f^{-1}$, almost every point of $B_{r,\rho}$ returns to $B_{r,\rho}$ infinitely often in both time directions.  There are countably many sets $B_{r,\rho}$, so the union of the resulting null exceptional sets is again null. For every remaining $x$, choose $r$ with $x\in B_{r,\rho}$; then there are arbitrarily large positive and negative return times $m_j$ with $f^{m_j}x\in B_{r,\rho}$.

    If $\rho>0$ and $s_\rho(x)\ne0$, the multiplicative ergodic theorem gives
    \begin{displaymath}
        \lim_{m\to\infty}\frac1m
        \log\|L_x^{(m)}s_\rho(x)\|=\rho>0.
    \end{displaymath}
    At positive return times $m_j\to\infty$, however,
    \begin{displaymath}
        \|L_x^{(m_j)}s_\rho(x)\|
        =\|s_\rho(f^{m_j}x)\|\le r,
    \end{displaymath}
    which is a contradiction. If $\rho<0$, apply the same argument in negative time: the backward growth exponent is $-\rho>0$, again contradicting the bounded values at return times. Every component with a nonzero exponent vanishes, as claimed.

\label{lem:entropy-tangent}
    Let $g: Q\to Q$ be a $C^2$ diffeomorphism of a compact smooth manifold without boundary, and let $\nu$ be a $g$-ergodic Borel probability measure with $h_\nu(g)>0$. Let $E_g^u(x)$ be the sum of its strictly positive Lyapunov subspaces. If an embedded $C^1$ submanifold $S\subset Q$ satisfies $\nu(S)=1$, then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:unstable-tangent-intersection}
        T_xS\cap E_g^u(x)\ne\{0\}
        \quad\text{for }\nu\text{-almost every }x.
    \end{equation}
    More generally, without the assumption $\nu(S)=1$, the set of regular points $x\in S$ with $T_xS\cap E_g^u(x)=\{0\}$ has $\nu$-measure zero. No compactness, closedness, or invariance of $S$ is required.

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
We first explain the precise consequence of the entropy-dimension formula that we need. Let $\lambda_1>\cdots>\lambda_r>0$ be the distinct positive Lyapunov exponents. Pesin theory gives full unstable manifolds on a full-measure regular set. Ledrappier--Young's conditional-dimension theorem and entropy formula give nonnegative numbers $\gamma_i$ with
    \begin{displaymath}
        h_\nu(g)=\sum_{i=1}^r\lambda_i\gamma_i,
        \qquad \delta^u=\sum_{i=1}^r\gamma_i,
    \end{displaymath}
    where each $\gamma_i$ is the unstable-dimension contribution of the conditional measures in the direction of the $i$-th positive exponent, and $\delta^u$ is the almost-sure pointwise dimension of the conditional measures along full unstable leaves \cite[Section~7.3, Proposition~7.3.1, and Theorem~C$'$]{LedrappierYoung1985b}. These results allow for zero Lyapunov exponents and do not require the unstable conditional measures to be absolutely continuous with respect to leaf volume. They therefore apply to time-one maps of smooth flows, which may have zero Lyapunov exponents, in particular in the flow direction. The positive-entropy hypothesis gives $\delta^u>0$.

    Choose a measurable partition $\eta$ subordinate to full unstable manifolds, as in \cite[Section~7.1 and Lemma~9.1.1]{LedrappierYoung1985b}, and disintegrate
    \begin{displaymath}
        \nu=\int\nu_\zeta\,d\overline\nu(\zeta).
    \end{displaymath}
    For almost every partition element $\zeta$, its conditional probability $\nu_\zeta$ is concentrated on one unstable leaf, and for $\nu_\zeta$-almost every $y$, its leafwise local dimension is $\delta^u$. In particular, $\nu_\zeta$ is not atomic. To verify this implication, suppose $\nu_\zeta(\{y\})=a>0$.  For intrinsic unstable balls, $\nu_\zeta(B^u(y,r))\to a$ as $r\downarrow0$, so
    \begin{displaymath}
        \lim_{r\downarrow0}
        \frac{\log\nu_\zeta(B^u(y,r))}{\log r}=0,
    \end{displaymath}
    whereas the almost-sure value of this limit on the regular set is $\delta^u>0$. An atom of positive mass cannot belong to a conditional null exceptional set. This contradicts $\delta^u>0$.  No absolute continuity of the unstable conditional measures is being assumed.


    Let $\mathcal R$ be a common full-measure set on which the following hold simultaneously: the Oseledets splitting exists and the Lyapunov exponents, together with their multiplicities, are constant; the Pesin unstable manifolds are defined and depend measurably on the point; and the Ledrappier--Young conditional-dimension conclusions and that non-atomicity of the unstable conditional measures are valid. Put
    \begin{displaymath}
        B=\{x\in S\cap \mathcal R:T_xS\cap E_g^u(x)=\{0\}\}.
    \end{displaymath}


    This set is measurable: $S$ is Borel, $x\mapsto T_xS$ is continuous on $S$, and $x\mapsto E_g^u(x)$ is measurable on $\mathcal R$; trivial intersection is a measurable condition on the relevant Grassmannians, since it is equivalent to the identity
    \begin{displaymath}
        \dim(T_xS)+\dim E_g^u(x)
        =\dim\left(T_xS+E_g^u(x)\right).
    \end{displaymath}

    Take $x\in B$.  In an ambient neighborhood $V$ of $x$, express $S$ as
    \begin{displaymath}
        S\cap V=\rho^{-1}(0),\qquad
        \rho:V\longrightarrow\mathbb R^c,
        \qquad \ker D\rho_x=T_xS,
    \end{displaymath}
    where $\rho$ is a $C^1$ submersion.  On a small embedded unstable plaque through $x$, the derivative of the restricted map $\rho$ is injective, since its kernel is $T_xS\cap E_g^u(x)=\{0\}$. Write $k=\dim E_g^u(x)$.  Taking $k$ suitable linear combinations of the components of $\rho$, we obtain a linear map $L:\mathbb R^c\to\mathbb R^k$ such that the derivative of $L\circ\rho$ restricted to the plaque is invertible at $x$. By the inverse function theorem in leaf coordinates, $x$ is the only zero of this restricted map in a sufficiently small intrinsic leaf neighborhood. Every point of $S$ is a zero of $L\circ\rho$. Consequently, $x$ is an isolated intersection of $S$ with its unstable leaf.

    Now fix a full unstable leaf $W^u$. At each regular point $x\in B\cap W^u$, this leaf agrees with the unstable leaf through $x$ and has tangent space $E_g^u(x)$. The preceding argument shows that every point of $B\cap W^u$ is isolated in the intrinsic leaf topology. A Pesin leaf is a second-countable immersed manifold, for instance, by its construction as a countable union of iterated local disks. Every discrete subset of a second-countable space is at most countable: choose for each point the least member of a fixed countable basis that contains it and no other point of the subset. Distinct points receive distinct basis members. Thus $B\cap W^u$ is at most countable. We do not need the whole intersection $S\cap W^u$ to be discrete or the leaf to be properly embedded.

    For almost every $\eta$-element $\zeta$, the set $B\cap\zeta$ is countable, since $\zeta$ lies in a single unstable leaf, and $\nu_\zeta$ is not atomic. Hence $\nu_\zeta(B)=0$.  Disintegration gives
    \begin{displaymath}
        \nu(B)=\int_{Q/\eta}\nu_\zeta(B)\,d\overline{\nu}(\zeta)=0,
    \end{displaymath}
    where $\overline{\nu}$ is the pushforward probability on the quotient space $Q/\eta$.

    This proves the general assertion. When $\nu(S)=1$, its complement in the regular part of $S$ has full $\nu$-measure, so the set of regular points of $S$ with $T_xS\cap E_g^u(x)=\{0\}$ is $\nu$-null, which yields \eqref{eq:unstable-tangent-intersection}.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:analytic-versus-flat}
    The proof does not show that the singular set of the original moment map has zero measure for every invariant probability. A minimal carrier $N$ may be entirely contained in that singular set. What holds is that a proper, relatively closed, flow-invariant analytic subset of this particular $N$ has measure zero.

    The crucial normal expansion consequence of analyticity is that a nonzero integral cannot vanish to infinite normal order. If $A|_N=0$ and $A\not\equiv0$, then some finite normal term $P_A$ survives. For the complete family obtained in Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-elimination}, whose fiber differentials are independent on the sets provided by Lemma~\ref{lem:jacobian-criterion}, the Poisson identities, equivariance, and the zero total weight property together force the tangent Lyapunov exponents of the carrier to vanish. For a smooth function, the finite-order starting point can fail:
    \begin{displaymath}
        \rho(z)=
        \begin{cases}
            e^{-1/z^2},&z\ne0,\\
            0,&z=0
        \end{cases}
    \end{displaymath}
    is nonzero and smooth, but every derivative at zero vanishes. There is then no first nonzero normal polynomial to which the argument could be applied. This explains the obstruction to carrying the proof over to smooth Liouville integrability without an additional hypothesis.
