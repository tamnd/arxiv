An {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{intrinsic Riemannian framework}}} is a pair $(G,p)$, where
\[
 p=(p_1,\dots,p_n)\in \mathcal{M}^n
\]
is a placement of the vertices, which we call a {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{realization}}}.  We call the graph's edges the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{bars}}} of the framework. For each edge $ij\in E$, the bar length is given by the intrinsic Riemannian distance $\ell_g(p_i,p_j)$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:admissibility}
A placement $p\in \mathcal{M}^n$ is {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{admissible for $G$}}} if
\[
 (p_i,p_j)\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{Log}}\quad\text{ and }\quad (p_j,p_i)\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{Log}}
 \qquad\text{for every }ij\in E.
\]
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
One may instead use the unsquared edge-distance map $q\mapsto \ell_g(q_i,q_j)$ whenever adjacent vertices are distinct.  At such a placement, the two differentials differ edgewise by multiplication by the nonzero factor $\ell_g(p_i,p_j)$.  Therefore the two choices have the same kernel and the same rank.  The squared-distance normalization is more convenient because its gradient is the logarithm vector itself.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:rigidity-operator}
For an admissible framework $(G,p)$, the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{intrinsic Riemannian rigidity operator}}} is
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:rigidity-operator}
 R_\mathcal{M}(G,p):=d(F_G)_p:
 \bigoplus_{i=1}^n T_{p_i}\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^E.
\end{equation*}
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:edge-row}
Let $(G,p)$ be admissible.  For $u=(u_1,\dots,u_n)\in T_p\mathcal{M}^n$ and $ij\in E$,
\begin{eqnarray}\label{eq:row-formula}
 \bigl(R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)u\bigr)_{ij}
 &=&-\bigl\langle \operatorname{Log}_{p_i}(p_j),u_i\bigr\rangle_{p_i}
  -\bigl\langle \operatorname{Log}_{p_j}(p_i),u_j\bigr\rangle_{p_j}.
\end{eqnarray}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For fixed $y\in\mathcal{M}$ with $(x,y)\in \mathcal{U}_\mathrm{Log}$, the corresponding Riemannian logarithm is well-defined, smooth and the first variation of squared distance gives
\[
 \mathrm{grad}_x\frac12 \ell_g(x,y)^2=-\operatorname{Log}_x(y)
\]
(see \cite[Thm.\ 1.2]{Karcher1977}). Applying this identity to both variables of $\frac12\ell_g(q_i,q_j)^2$ and evaluating on $(u_i,u_j)$ yields Equation \eqref{eq:row-formula}.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
A particularly convenient representation of the rigidity operator is in the form of a matrix. For that, let $d=\dim \mathcal{M}$ and choose an orthonormal basis $e_{i1},\dots,e_{id}$
of $T_{p_i}\mathcal{M}$ for each vertex $i$. We express
\[
 \operatorname{Log}_{p_i}(p_j)=\sum_{k=1}^d \lambda_{ij}^{(k)}e_{ik}
\]
in terms of the chosen orthonormal basis. In the product basis of $T_p\mathcal{M}^n$, the operator $R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)$ is represented by an $|E|\times dn$ matrix.  The row indexed by $ij$ has only two nonzero $d$-blocks:
\begin{eqnarray*}
 \begin{pmatrix}
  0&\cdots&-(\lambda_{ij})^{\mathsf T}&\cdots
  &-(\lambda_{ji})^{\mathsf T}&\cdots&0
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{eqnarray*}
Changing the orthonormal frame at a vertex right-multiplies the corresponding block column by an orthogonal matrix.  Thus the kernel dimension and rank are independent of these choices.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
An {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{infinitesimal flex}}} of $(G,p)$ is a vector
\[
 u\in\ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p).
\]
Equivalently, an infinitesimal flex $u$ satisfies the following equation for all edges $ij\in E$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edge-equation}
	\bigl\langle \operatorname{Log}_{p_i}(p_j),u_i\bigr\rangle_{p_i}
	+\bigl\langle \operatorname{Log}_{p_j}(p_i),u_j\bigr\rangle_{p_j}=0.
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-4 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:killing-kernel}
For every admissible framework $(G,p)$,
\[
 \mathcal{K}_p\subseteq \ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p).
\]
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Consider the unique one-parameter family of isometries $\Phi^A_t\in \textsc{Isom}(\mathcal{M},g)$ induced by some $A\in \mathfrak{isom}(\mathcal{M},g)$ (see \Cref{section:rie_man}).  Since $\Phi^A_t$ is an isometry for each $t$, it preserves Riemannian distance, so
\[
 \ell_g(\Phi^A_t(p_i),\,\Phi^A_t(p_j))=\ell_g(p_i,\,p_j)
\]
for every pair of edges $i,j$.  Differentiating at $t=0$ shows that the vector $\mathrm{ev}_p(A)$
lies in the kernel of the rigidity operator, since $\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \Phi^A_t\big\lvert_{t=0}=A$. In other words, the tangent vectors obtained by differentiating ambient isometries always lie in the kernel of the intrinsic rigidity matrix.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- definition-5: body_md -->
\label{def:inf_rigidity}
An admissible framework $(G,p)$ is {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{infinitesimally rigid}}} if
\[
 \ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)=\mathcal{K}_p.
\]
It is {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{infinitesimally flexible}}} otherwise.
<!-- end definition-5 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:maxwell}
If $(G,p)$ is infinitesimally rigid, then
\begin{eqnarray*}
 |E|\ge dn-t_p.
\end{eqnarray*}
In particular, if $\dim\textsc{Isom}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathcal{M},g)=k$ and the placement has discrete stabilizer, then
\begin{eqnarray*}
 |E|\ge dn-k.
\end{eqnarray*}
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The rank of an $|E|\times dn$ matrix is at most $|E|$.  Then, apply \eqref{eq:rank-criterion}.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:hyperbolictriang}
	Consider a 3-cycle graph in the upper half-plane model $\mathbb{H}\subset \mathbb{R}^2$ of the hyperbolic plane, so that $p_1$ is placed at $(0,1)$, $p_2$ is placed at $(1,1)$ and $p_3$ is placed at $(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})$. This triangle is depicted in \Cref{fig:triangandsaddle}(l.). Using the Riemannian metric tensor $ds^2=\frac{d{x_1}^2+d{x_2}^2}{{x_2}^2}$, $\mathbb{H}$ becomes a Riemannian manifold. 
	
	The geodesics are given by circular arcs and straight lines that are perpendicular to the line $\mathbb{R}\cdot (1,0)$ and the geodesic distance is characterized by the distance formula (cf.\ \cite[p.\ 81f.]{geometrie})
	\[\cosh d_\mathbb{H}(p,q)\,=\,1+\frac{\|p-q\|^2}{2\cdot p_2\cdot q_2}.\] 
	The hyperbolic triangle constructed above is therefore equilateral and has the edge length $\mathrm{arccosh}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)$. Now, the group of isometries consists of horizontal translations, dilations and inversions. This group is isomorphic to $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, which has dimension 3. Therefore, Corollary \ref{cor:maxwell} predicts that the necessary Maxwell count is exactly satisfied and the intrinsic bar-and-joint framework has the potential to be infinitesimally rigid.
	
	Using the tangential information (see Proposition \ref{prop:edge-row}) of the geodesic arcs that make up the edges of the intrinsic 3-cycle framework, i.e.\ segments of the three circles $\|p-(m_1,0)\|^2=\frac{5}{4}$ for $m_1\in \{-\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{3}{2}\}$, we obtain the following matrix representation of the rigidity operator:
	\[\frac{\ell}{\sinh(\ell)}\cdot \begin{pmatrix}
		-1 & -\frac{1}{2} & 1 & -\frac{1}{2} & 0 & 0\\
		-1 & \frac{1}{2} & 0 & 0 & 1 & -2\\
		0 & 0 & 1 & \frac{1}{2} & -1 & -2
	\end{pmatrix}.\]
	for $\ell=\mathrm{acosh}(3/2)$. Since this matrix has rank 3, its kernel is 3-dimensional, which implies that all infinitesimal flexes corresponding to the 3-cycle are trivial. Therefore, the 3-cycle is infinitesimally rigid according to Definition \ref{def:inf_rigidity}.

<!-- definition-6: body_md -->
\label{def:local_rigidity}
An admissible framework $(G,p)$ is {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{(locally) rigid}}} if there exists a neighborhood $U$ of $p$ in $\mathcal{M}^n$ such that
\[
 F_G^{-1}(F_G(p))\cap U
 \subseteq \mathcal{O}_p.
\]
It is called {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{flexible}}} otherwise.
<!-- end definition-6 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:local_isometry_group}
	 In Definition \ref{def:local_rigidity}, trivial motions are induced by the identity component of the global isometry group. This differs from local isometries, which are metric-preserving diffeomorphisms between open subsets of $\mathcal{M}$ and need not extend globally.
	
	For example, consider the interval $\mathcal{M}=(0,1)$ with the Euclidean metric. Translating all vertices of a framework by a sufficiently small amount preserves every intrinsic edge length. However, since the isometry group $\operatorname{Isom}_{\mathrm{id}}(M,g)$ is trivial, these motions are nontrivial according to Definition \ref{def:local_rigidity}. This phenomenon can occur even for complete graphs at regular placements.
	
	One could alternatively define trivial motions using local isometries \cite[p.\ 13]{Lee2018}. However, these generally form a pseudogroup rather than a global Lie-group action. Since we extensively use this property throughout this article, we therefore retain global isometries, whose proper action permits the application of the Slice Theorem in Sections \ref{section:local_rigidity} and \ref{section:second-order-theory}. Consequently, all rigidity notions in this article are to be understood modulo global ambient isometries of $\mathcal{M}$.
<!-- end remark-3 -->

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:regular-equivalence}
Let $(G,p)$ be admissible and regular. Then $(G,p)$ is rigid if and only if it is infinitesimally rigid.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Set $\mathcal{L}:={F_G}^{-1}(F_G(p))$. The regularity of the realization $p$ implies that $\mathcal{L}$ is a smooth manifold near $p$ with tangent space $T_p\mathcal{L}=\ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)$ according to the Constant Rank Theorem. The trivial motions form a smooth immersed submanifold of this level set, with tangent space $T_p\mathcal{O}_p=\mathcal{K}_p$. In fact, $\mathcal{O}_p$ is an embedded submanifold of $\mathcal{M}^n$ and hence, $\mathcal{O}_p$ is locally embedded in $\mathcal{L}$.
Assume now that $(G,p)$ is infinitesimally rigid, implying that 
\[T_p\mathcal{O}_p=\mathcal{K}_p=\ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)=T_p\mathcal{L}.\] 
Since $\mathcal{O}_p\subseteq \mathcal{L}$, the inclusion $\iota:\mathcal{O}_p\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}$ is smooth and the differential $d\iota_p:T_p\mathcal{O}_p\rightarrow T_p\mathcal{L}$ is an isomorphism. The Inverse Function Theorem therefore says that $\iota$ maps a neighborhood of $p$ in $\mathcal{O}_p$ diffeomorphically into an open neighborhood of $p$ in $\mathcal{L}$. Thus, after sufficiently shrinking the ambient neighborhood $U\subset \mathcal{L}$ it holds that $\mathcal{L}\cap U = \mathcal{O}_p\cap U$,
so $(G,p)$ is rigid.

Conversely, suppose that $(G,p)$ is rigid, so that \[{F_G}^{-1}(F_G(p))\cap U = \mathcal{O}_p\cap U\] for a sufficiently small open neighborhood $U$ of $p$. The manifolds $\mathcal{O}_p$ and $\mathcal{L}={F_G}^{-1}(F_G(p))$ therefore have the same tangent space at $p$ and hence
\[\ker R_\mathcal{M} (G,p)=T_p\mathcal{L}=T_p\mathcal{O}_p=\mathcal{K}_p.\]
Thus, $(G,p)$ is infinitesimally rigid.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:inf-rigid}
	The infinitesimal rigidity of a bar-and-joint framework $(G,p)$ in the intrinsic metric of a Riemannian manifold $(\mathcal{M},g)$ implies its rigidity.
	\begin{proof}
		Assume for the contraposition that $(G,p)$ is flexible. The identity component of the isometry group $\textsc{Isom}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathcal{M},g)$ acts properly and diagonally on $\mathcal{M}^n$, since $\mathcal{M}$ is connected by assumption. Therefore, by the Slice Theorem \cite[Prop.\ 2.3.1]{palaisslice} there exists a local slice $\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{M}^n$ through $p$ such that 
		\[T_p\mathcal{M}^n\,=\,T_p\mathcal{O}_p\oplus T_p\mathcal{S}\,=\,\mathcal{K}_p\oplus T_p\mathcal{S}.\]
		After shrinking $\mathcal{S}$ if necessary, every realization $q\in\mathcal{M}^n$ in a sufficiently small neighborhood $U\subset \mathcal{M}^n$ of $p$ can be written as $q=h\cdot s$ for some $h\in \textsc{Isom}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathcal{M},g)$ and $s\in \mathcal{S}$.
		
		
		Assume now that $(G,p)$ was infinitesimally rigid. In other words, $\ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)=\mathcal{K}_p$. Since $R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)=d(F_G)_p$, the restricted differential $d(F_G\big\lvert_\mathcal{S})$ satisfies
		\[\ker d(F_G\big\lvert_\mathcal{S})_p=\ker d(F_G)_p\cap T_p\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{K}_p\cap T_p\mathcal{S}=\{0\},\]
		which implies that $d(F_G\big\lvert_\mathcal{S}):T_p\mathcal{S}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^E$ is injective. Thus, there exists a linear $\pi:\mathbb{R}^E\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{\dim \mathcal{S}}$ such that 
		\[d(\pi\circ F_G\big\lvert_\mathcal{S})_p:T_p\mathcal{S}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{\dim \mathcal{S}}\]
		is an isomorphism. 
		Hence, by the inverse function theorem $\pi\circ F_G\big\lvert_S$ is injective after shrinking $\mathcal{S}$ around $p$. In particular, the equation $F_G(s)=F_G(p)$ implies $s=p$ for any $s\in \mathcal{S}$. 
		
		Now, let $q\in U$ satisfy $F_G(q)=F_G(p)$. Since $F_G$ is invariant under isometries, we obtain
		\[F_G(s)=F_G(h\cdot s)=F_G(q)=F_G(p)\]
		for some $s\in \mathcal{S}$. By the local injectivity established above, it follows that $s=p$. Therefore, $q=h\cdot s\in \mathcal{O}_p$ for all $q\in U$ and consequently, ${F_G}^{-1}(F_G(p))\cap U \subseteq \mathcal{O}_p$, so $(G,p)$ is locally rigid. This contradicts the assumption that the framework is flexible. Therefore, $\ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)\neq \mathcal{K}_p$ and the framework is infinitesimally flexible.
	\end{proof}

<!-- example-3: body_md -->
\label{ex:hyperboloid}
	Consider a 3-cycle graph realized in a special position on the one-sheeted hyperboloid $\mathcal{H}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ cut out by the equation $x^2+y^2-z^2=1$ and equipped with the induced Riemannian metric $g$ from the ambient Euclidean space. The vertices of the corresponding framework are placed in the points $p_1=(1,0,0)$, $p_2=(\cos(\frac{2\pi}{3}), \,\sin(\frac{2\pi}{3}),\,0)$ and $p_3=(\cos(\frac{4\pi}{3}), \,\sin(\frac{4\pi}{3}),\,0)$ so that all vertices are adjacent. This framework is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:hyperboloid_square}
	
	\begin{figure}[h!]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.35\linewidth]{hyperboloid_square}
		\caption{A 3-cycle graph on the one-sheeted hyperboloid. }
		\label{fig:hyperboloid_square}
	\end{figure}
	
	As a surface of revolution, the dimension of the hyperboloid's isometry group is 1 \cite{NixonOwenPower2012}. Therefore, the Maxwell count (cf.\ Corollary \ref{cor:maxwell}) is deficient and the framework is infinitesimally flexible. In fact, the kernel of the rigidity operator is generated by 4 vectors, leaving 3 nontrivial infinitesimal flexes. For instance, one such flex is given by applying the infinitesimal translation vector $(0,0,1)$ to each vertex. 
	
	Still, the framework is rigid. To demonstrate the claim, let us choose angular coordinates: $r=\sqrt{1+z^2}$ and $\theta\in [0,2\pi)$ so that the one-sheeted hyperboloid is given by 
	\[\mathcal{H}=\left\{\left(\sqrt{1+z^2}\cdot \cos(\theta),\, \sqrt{1+z^2}\cdot \sin(\theta),\,z\right)~ \bigg\lvert ~z\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and } \theta\in [0,2\pi)\right\}.\]
	Let us now consider any continuously differentiable curve \[\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow \mathcal{H},\quad\gamma(t)=\left(\sqrt{1+z(t)^2}\cdot\cos(\theta(t)),\, \sqrt{1+z(t)^2}\cdot\sin(\theta(t)),\,z(t)\right)\] 
	Its Riemannian curve length satisfies
	\begin{eqnarray*}
	L_g(\gamma)&=&\int_{0}^{1} \sqrt{\frac{(z(t)\cdot z'(t))^2}{1+z(t)^2}+(1+z(t)^2)\cdot \theta'(t)^2+z'(t)^2}~dt \\
	&\geq & \int_{0}^{1} \sqrt{1+z(t)^2}\cdot \lvert \theta'(t)\lvert \,dt~\geq ~\int_{0}^{1} \lvert \theta'(t)\lvert \,dt
	\end{eqnarray*}
	In other words, the Riemannian distance between two points $p,q$ is bounded below by the absolute angular difference between $p$ and $q$. Returning to this chain of inequalities, we realize that for the length of geodesic arcs, equality to the absolute angular difference can only occur when $z'(t)=z(t)=0$. This is only the case when the curve lies on the hyperboloid's equator. 
	
	Now, the three intrinsic edges of the 3-cycle each have arc length $\frac{2\pi}{3}$. Suppose now that $q_1,q_2,q_3$ is a sufficiently small perturbation that preserves the edge lengths, i.e.\ $\ell_g(q_1,q_2)=\ell_g(q_2,q_3)=\ell_g(q_1,q_3)=\frac{2\pi}{3}$, and let 
	\[\theta_{i,j}=\arccos\left(\frac{\big\langle (q_{i,1},q_{i,2}),~(q_{j,1},q_{j,2})\big\rangle}{\sqrt{q_{i,1}^2+q_{i,2}^2}\cdot \sqrt{q_{j,1}^2+q_{j,2}^2}}\right)\] 
	be the angular separation between the vertices $q_i$ and $q_j$. By the admissibility of the realization $p$, it satisfies $\theta_{i,j}<\pi$ for all sufficiently close realizations $q$. By a previous argument, it holds that $\theta_{i,j}\leq \ell_g(q_i,q_j)$ for each $1\leq i < j \leq 3$. Since the cyclic order of the 3-cycle is unchanged by sufficiently small perturbations, it further holds that $\theta_{1,2}+\theta_{2,3}+\theta_{1,3}=2\pi$. Together, these observations imply that $\theta_{1,2}=\theta_{2,3}=\theta_{1,3}=\frac{2\pi}{3}$.
	
	However, this forces $q_1$, $q_2$ and $q_3$ to lie on the equator. Since the edge lengths are preserved by this perturbation, this implies that the deformed framework can only be obtained by a rotation of the original framework, which is an isometry of $\mathcal{H}$. Therefore, the 3-cycle is rigid, but not infinitesimally rigid, on $\mathcal{H}$.

<!-- definition-8: body_md -->
A {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{continuous/smooth/analytic motion}}} of $(G,p)$ is a continuous/smooth/analytic curve $\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow {F_G}^{-1}(F_G(p))$ so that $\gamma(0)=p$. It is called {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{trivial}}} if $\gamma(t)\in \mathcal{O}_p$ for all $t\in[0,1]$ and {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{nontrivial}}} otherwise.
<!-- end definition-8 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:existenceofcontmotion}
	Let $(G,p)$ be admissible and regular. If $(G,p)$ is flexible, then there exists a smooth nontrivial motion in ${F_G}^{-1}(F_G(p))$ through $p$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
By the Constant Rank Theorem, $\mathcal{L}:={F_G}^{-1}(F_G(p))$ is a smooth submanifold near $p$ and $T_p\mathcal{L}=\ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)$. According to Proposition \ref{prop:regular-equivalence}, the flexibility of $(G,p)$ implies that the framework is infinitesimally flexible. Therefore, we can choose $u\in \ker R_\mathcal{M} (G,p)\setminus \mathcal{K}_p = T_p\mathcal{L}\setminus \mathcal{K}_p$. By the definition of the tangent space, there exists an $\varepsilon>0$ and a smooth curve $\gamma:(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)\rightarrow \mathcal{L}$ so that $\gamma(0)=p$ and $\gamma'(0)=u$. Since $\gamma(t)\in \mathcal{L}$, it further holds that $F_G(\gamma(t))=F_G(p)$ for all $t\in (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, so that $\gamma$, after reparametrization, is a smooth motion of $(G,p)$. It is nontrivial, because $u\notin \mathcal{K}_p=T_p\mathcal{O}_p$, so $\gamma((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon))\not\subset \mathcal{O}_p$.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:existencecontinuousmotionsrealanalytic}
	Assume that $(\mathcal{M},g)$ is a real-analytic Riemannian manifold.
		If $(G,p)$ is flexible, then there exists a
	 real-analytic nontrivial motion through $p$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Since $p$ is admissible, every pair of vertices incident to an edge lies in $\mathcal{U}_\mathrm{Log}$.
		For a real-analytic Riemannian metric, the exponential map is real
		analytic, and therefore the logarithm map is locally real analytic
		wherever it is defined. While the Riemannian distance $\ell_g$ generally fails to be differentiable at $x=y$, the squared Riemannian distance ${\ell_g}^2$, and thus $F_G$, is real analytic
		in a neighborhood $U$ of $p$.
		Set
		\[
		\mathcal C=F_G^{-1}(F_G(p))\cap U.
		\]
		Then $\mathcal C$ is a real-analytic set. The isometry group acts diagonally and properly, as well as real-analytically on $\mathcal{M}^n$ (cf.\ \cite{baouendietal}), so the orbit $\mathcal O_p$ is
		locally an embedded real-analytic submanifold. In particular, $\mathcal{O}_p$ is locally defined by analytic coordinate equations. Its complement
		\[
		\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal C\setminus\mathcal O_p
		\]
		is therefore semi-analytic (cf.\ \cite{bierstone-millman}).
		
		Flexibility is characterized by the property that every neighborhood of $p$ contains a realization
		with the same edge lengths as $p$ which is not contained in $\mathcal O_p$ or, equivalently,

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $\mathcal{M}=\mathbb R\times S^1$ be a cylinder with coordinates $(r,\theta)$ and angle $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. Define the smooth function
	\begin{equation*}\label{eq:h}
		h(r)=\begin{cases}
			e^{-1/r^2}\sin(\pi/r)^2,&r>0,\\
			0,&r=0,\\
			e^{-1/r^2},&r<0.
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation*}
	Its zero set is the totally disconnected set $
	Z=h^{-1}(0)=\{0\}\cup\{1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}.$
	For $\varepsilon>0$, we define the Riemannian metric
	\begin{equation*}\label{eq:metric}
		g_\varepsilon=d r^2+(1+\varepsilon h(r))^2\,d\theta^2
	\end{equation*}
	on $\mathcal{M}$. Let $G$ be the 3-cycle with vertices $1,2,3$, and the realizations
	\begin{equation*}
	p_i=\left(0,~2\pi\cdot\frac{i-1}{3}\right)\qquad \text{and}\qquad
	q_i^{(n)}=\left(\frac{1}{n},~2\pi\cdot\frac{i-1}{3}\right).
	\end{equation*}
	For a piecewise $C^1$ curve $\gamma=(r,\theta)$, using any lift of its
	angular coordinate to $\mathbb R$, it holds that
	\begin{equation*}\label{eq:lengthbound}
		L_{g_\varepsilon}(\gamma)
		=\int\sqrt{\dot r^{\,2}+(1+\varepsilon h(r))^2\dot\theta^{\,2}}\,\mathrm d t
		\geq\int|\dot\theta|\,\mathrm d t.
	\end{equation*}
	If two points have the same height $r\in Z$ and angular separation less
	than $\pi$, the shorter horizontal circular arc attains this lower bound.
	It is the unique minimizing geodesic: equality forces $\dot r=0$ along
	any minimizing curve, and the shorter angular arc is unique.
	Consequently, all three edges of $p$ and of every $q^{(n)}$ have length
	$2\pi/3$, so
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:sequence}
		F_{G}(q^{(n)})=F_{G}(p),\qquad q^{(n)}\longrightarrow p.
	\end{equation}
	
	We first prove that there is no nontrivial continuous motion through $p$.
	Let \mbox{$q:[0,1]\to F_{G}^{-1}(F_{G}(p))$} be such a motion, where the
	realization space is restricted to admissible placements. Join the three
	successive vertices of $q(t)$ by their minimizing geodesic arcs, obtaining
	a closed curve $\Gamma_t$. Admissibility makes these arcs continuous in
	their endpoints. The winding number of $\Gamma_t$ around the cylinder is
	therefore constant and equals $1$, which is exactly its value at $t=0$.
	
	Each edge has length $2\pi/3$. A lift of $\Gamma_t$ to the universal cover
	thus satisfies
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:equality}
		2\pi=L_{g_\varepsilon}(\Gamma_t)
		\geq \int_{\Gamma_t}|d\theta|
		\geq \left|\int_{\Gamma_t}d\theta\right|=2\pi.
	\end{equation}
	This implies that $r$ is constant along every edge,
	hence all three vertices have one common height $r(t)$. It also implies
	$h(r(t))=0$, since the winding number of $\Gamma_t$ is exactly 1, so $r(t)\in Z$. Since $r(t)$ is continuous, $r(0)=0$, and since $Z$ is
	totally disconnected, it necessarily holds that $r(t)=0$ for all $t$.
	Moreover, equality in \eqref{eq:equality} forces all angular increments
	to have the same sign; as each edge has length $2\pi/3$ and the total
	increment is $2\pi$, each increment equals $2\pi/3$. Hence $q(t)$ differs
	from $p$ only by a common angular rotation. Such rotations are global
	isometries in the metric $g_\varepsilon$. Every continuous motion from $p$ is thus
	trivial.
	
	It follows that none of the distinct realizations $q^{(n)}$ belong to the
	$\textsc{Isom}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathcal{M},g_\varepsilon)$-orbit of $p$:
	otherwise a one-parameter family of isometries $\Phi_t$ would yield a continuous
	motion from $p$ to $q^{(n)}$. Together with the convergence property from \eqref{eq:sequence}, this proves the
	local flexibility of $(G,p)$ in the sense of Definition \ref{def:local_rigidity}
	
	Finally, choose $\varepsilon>0$ sufficiently small to ensure admissibility
	in the sense of Definition \ref{def:admissibility}. Indeed, $h$ and its first two derivatives
	are bounded, so $g_\varepsilon$ converges uniformly in $C^2$ to the flat
	cylinder metric as $\varepsilon\to0$. On the universal cover, the
	exponential maps on tangent balls of any fixed radius $R<\pi$ then converge
	uniformly in $C^1$ to the induced Euclidean exponential maps. For sufficiently
	small $\varepsilon$ they remain injective on these balls, and distinct
	lifts cannot differ by a deck translation of angular size $2\pi$.
	Taking $2\pi/3<R$ gives an injectivity radius greater than $2\pi/3$
	at all the displayed vertices. The framework and nearby realizations
	are therefore admissible.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:riemannian-edge-hessian}
	Given an intrinsic bar-and-joint framework $(G,p)$ on a Riemannian manifold $(\mathcal{M},g)$ that has an admissible placement $p$, let $\gamma(t)=\operatorname{Exp}_{p_i}(t\cdot v_{ij})$ be the unique minimizing geodesic between $p_{i}$ and $p_j$ for an edge $ij\in E$ with $v_{ij}=\operatorname{Log}_{p_i}(p_j)$ so that $\gamma(0)=p_i$ and $\gamma(1)=p_j$. For $u,v\in T_p\mathcal{M}^n$, let $J_u$ be the Jacobi field along $\gamma$ satisfying $J_u(0)=u_i$ and $J_u(1)=u_j$. Then, for any edge $e\in E(G)$ the edge Hessian has the form
	\[(\mathcal{H}_p(u,v))_e=-\big\langle D_tJ_u(0),\,v_i\big\rangle_{p_i}+\big\langle D_tJ_u(1),\,v_j\big\rangle_{p_j}\]
	\begin{proof}
		First of all, the variation of the Riemannian logarithms is given by
		\[\nabla_u \operatorname{Log}_{p_i}(p_j)=D_tJ_u(0)\quad\text{ and }\quad \nabla_u \operatorname{Log}_{p_j}(p_i)=-D_tJ_u(1),\]
		where $\nabla_u \operatorname{Log}_{p_i}(p_j)$ means varying both points $p_i$ and $p_j$ according to $u_i$ and $u_j$ (cf.\ \cite{pennec}).
		Since the Riemannian gradient of $f_e$ satisfies $\mathrm{grad}\,f_e(p)=(0 \dots 0 ~ -\operatorname{Log}_{p_i}(p_j) ~ 0 \dots 0  ~ -\operatorname{Log}_{p_j}(p_i) ~ 0 \dots 0)$ (see Proposition \ref{prop:edge-row}), we can compute
		\begin{eqnarray*}
			(\mathcal{H}_p(u,v))_e &=& \mathrm{Hess}\, f_e(p)[u,v] \\
			&\overset{\text{Def. }\ref{def:hessian}}{=}& \big\langle \nabla_u\mathrm{grad}\,f_e(p),\,v\rangle_p\\
			&\overset{\text{Prop. }\ref{prop:edge-row}}{=}& -\big\langle \nabla_u \operatorname{Log}_{p_i}(p_j),\,v_i\big\rangle_{p_i} - \big\langle \nabla_u\operatorname{Log}_{p_j}(p_i),\,v_j\big\rangle_{p_j}\\
			&=& -\big\langle D_tJ_u(0),\,v_i\big\rangle_{p_i}+\big\langle D_tJ_u(1),\,v_j\big\rangle_{p_j}
		\end{eqnarray*}
	because the Levi-Civita connection is compatible with the Riemannian metric $g$, which concludes the proof.
	\end{proof}

\label{def:self-stress}
	A vector $\omega=(\omega_e)_{e\in E}\in\mathbb R^E$
	is a {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{self-stress}}} at $p$ if $R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)^*\omega=0$

	\[
	\Psi_\omega(q):=\sum_{e\in E}\omega_e f_e(q)
	\]

	\begin{equation*}
		Q_\omega(u,v)
		:=\left\langle \omega,\mathcal H_p(u,v)\right\rangle_{\mathbb R^E}
		=\sum_{e\in E}\omega_e\,\operatorname{Hess} f_e(p)[u,v].
	\end{equation*}

\label{lem:radical}

	\]
	for all $k\in \mathcal{K}_p$ and all $v\in T_p\mathcal{M}^n$.

<!-- proof-11: body_md -->

Let $\widetilde X$ be the vector field on $\mathcal{M}^n$ induced by an element of $\mathfrak{isom}(\mathcal{M},g)$ (see \Cref{section:inf_flexes}).  Then $\widetilde X(\Psi_\omega)=0$ identically.  At the critical point $p$ of $\Psi_\omega$,

\label{thm:stress-test}
	Let $u\in\ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)$.  The following are equivalent:
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item there exists $a\in T_p\mathcal{M}^n$ such that $(u,a)$ is a second-order flex;
		\item $\mathcal H_p(u,u)\in\operatorname{im} R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)$;
		\item $Q_\omega(u,u)=0$ for every self-stress $\omega\in\ker R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)^*$.
	\end{enumerate}

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:so-local}
 If $(G,p)$ is second-order rigid, then it is locally rigid.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
We prove the statement's contraposition: Suppose the framework is flexible. Since the identity component of $\textsc{Isom}(\mathcal{M},g)$ acts properly on $\mathcal{M}^n$, there exists a local slice $\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{M}^n$ through $p$ for which $T_p\mathcal{M}^n=\mathcal{K}_p\oplus T_p\mathcal{S}$, so that $\mathcal{S}\cap \mathcal{O}_p=\{p\}$ and so that every sufficiently close realization to $p$ can be moved into $\mathcal{S}$ by an isometry. 
	After applying such isometries, there exists a sequence
	$(q_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{S}$, for which $q_k\to p$ and $q_k\neq p$ for $k\in \mathbb{N}$ so that $F_G(q_k)=F_G(p)$.

	Choose a smooth chart $\chi:U\subset T_p\mathcal{S}\rightarrow \mathcal{S}$ with $\chi(0)=p$ and $d\chi_0=\mathrm{id}_{T_p\mathcal{S}}$ and write
	\[
	q_k=\chi(\xi_k)\quad\text{for some}\quad \xi_k\in T_p\mathcal{S}\quad\text{so that}
	\quad r_k:=\left\lVert \xi_k\right\rVert\to0.
	\]
	After passing to a subsequence, we can assume that
	\[
	u_k:=\frac{\xi_k}{r_k}\longrightarrow u
	\]
	for some $u\in T_p\mathcal{S}$ with $\left\lVert u\right\rVert=1$. Since the set of admissible placements $\mathcal{C}_G$ is an open set, $F_G$ is smooth in a neighborhood of $p$ and due to the convergence of $(q_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ it holds that $q_k\in \mathcal{C}_G$ for all sufficiently large $k\in \mathbb{N}$. The first-order Taylor expansion of the constraint map then yields
	\[F_G(\chi(\xi))=F_G(p)+R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)\xi+o(\|\xi\|).\]
	When applying this equation to the sequence $(\xi_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$, we obtain
	\[0=R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)\xi_k+o(r_k).\]
	Dividing by $r_k$ and passing to the limit yields $
	R_\mathcal{M}(G,p)u=0$.
	Since $u\in T_p\mathcal{S}$ and $T_p\mathcal{S}\cap \mathcal{K}_p=\{0\}$, $u$ therefore is a nontrivial infinitesimal flex.
	
	Now let $\omega$ be any self-stress and consider the scalar stress potential $\Psi_\omega(q)=\langle \omega,\,F_G(q)\rangle$, which has the property that $d(\Psi_\omega)_p=0$ and $\mathrm{Hess}\,\Psi_\omega(p)=Q_\omega$ (see Equation \eqref{eq:stresspotential}). Therefore, the second derivative of $\Psi_\omega \circ \chi$ at $0$ agrees with the Riemannian Hessian:
	\[d^2(\Psi_\omega\circ \chi)_0[u,u]=\mathrm{Hess}\,\Psi_\omega(p) [u,u] = Q_\omega(u,u).\]
	Putting all of these observations together, we find that the second-order Taylor expansion of $\Psi\circ\chi$ at $\chi(0)=p$ has the form
	\[\Psi_\omega(\chi(\xi))=\Psi_\omega(p)+\frac{1}{2}Q_\omega(\xi,\xi)+o(\|\xi\|^2).\]
	Since $F_G(q_k)=F_G(p)$, it also holds that $\Psi_\omega(q_k)=\Psi_\omega(p)$. Therefore,
	\[
	0
	=\frac12 {r_k}^2 Q_\omega(u_k,u_k)+o({r_k}^2).
	\]
	After dividing by ${r_k}^2$ and passing to the limit, it holds that $Q_\omega(u,u)=0$
	for every self-stress $\omega$.  By Theorem~\ref{thm:stress-test}, there exists $a\in T_p\mathcal{M}^n$ such that $(u,a)$ is a second-order flex.  Since $u\notin \mathcal{K}_p$, this implies that $(G,p)$ is second-order flexible.

<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
Deciding second-order rigidity or prestress stability makes the computation of the bilinear form $Q_\omega$ necessary which, in turn, demands the computation of all edge Hessians. Typically, this is significantly harder than in Euclidean space, as it requires the evaluation of covariant derivatives at the realization $p$. Analytically finding the Riemannian Hessian is often out of reach. However, we can use numerical differentiation schemes and ODE-solvers to approximate the coefficients of $Q_\omega$. If the matrix associated with this bilinear form is positive or negative definite and if the corresponding eigenvalues are bounded away from $0$, this approach provides reasonable numerical evidence.

<!-- example-4: body_md -->
\label{ex:second-order-comp}
	Again consider the 3-cycle embedded at height $z=0$ on the one-sheeted hyperboloid $\mathcal{H}$ with the induced Euclidean metric. We show that this framework is prestress stable using different techniques than before. Using the parametrization of $\mathcal{H}$ in terms of $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$ and $z\in \mathbb{R}$ from before with the induced local frame $(\partial_\theta,\partial_z)$. Therefore, every infinitesimal flex has the form $u_i=a_i\partial_\theta+b_i\partial_z$. By the first variation formula for geodesic length, the bar constraints imply that $a_2-a_1=0$, $a_3-a_2=0$ and $a_1-a_3=0$, so that $a_1=a_2=a_3$. Since $\mathcal{H}$ is a surface of revolution, $a_i\neq 0$ and $b_i=0$ corresponds to a trivial infinitesimal rotation, so we can set $a_1=a_2=a_3=0$ to factor out the trivial infinitesimal flexes. In this local frame, the nontrivial infinitesimal flexes are therefore generated by linear combinations of the transverse motions $u_i=b_i(\partial_z)\lvert_{p_i}$ with $u_j=0$ for $j\neq i$. We set $u=(b_1(\partial_z)\lvert_{p_1},\,b_2(\partial_z)\lvert_{p_2},\,b_3(\partial_z)\lvert_{p_3})$ for $b_i\in\mathbb{R}$. This already generates a three-dimensional space of infinitesimal motions. By the Maxwell condition from Corollary \ref{cor:maxwell}, there is one more infinitesimal flex than expected. Since at any vertex, the two geodesics directions are opposite and have equal length, there exists an equilibrium stress of the form $\omega=(1,1,1)$. 
	
	Let us now consider the edge $ij$ of length $\ell>0$ with normal endpoints velocities $b_i$ and $b_j$ as before. Since the Gaussian curvature of the one-sheeted hyperboloid is $K=-1$ in the points on the geodesic $\gamma(\theta)=(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta),0)$ at height $z=0$ with unit-speed parametrization, its normal Jacobi field $J(s)=j(s)\partial_z$ satisfies
	\[j''(s)-j(s)=0,\quad j(0)=b_i\quad\text{ and }\quad j(\ell)=b_j\]
	We can analytically solve this differential equation and find that
	\[j(s)=\frac{\sinh(\ell-s)}{\sinh(\ell)}b_i+\frac{\sinh(s)}{\sinh(\ell)}b_j.\]
	Therefore, the edge Hessians of the constraint map's components are given by (cf.\ \cite[Thm.\ 4.1.1]{JostRieGeo})
	\begin{eqnarray*}
	\left(\mathcal{H}_p(u,u)\right)_{ij}&=&\ell\cdot\int_{0}^\ell \left(j'(s)^2+j(s)^2\right) \,ds\\&=&\ell\cdot \frac{\cosh(\ell)\cdot({b_i}^2+{b_j}^2)-2b_ib_j}{\sinh(\ell)}\\&=&\ell\cdot \frac{(b_i-b_j)^2+(\cosh(\ell)-1)\cdot({b_i}^2+{b_j}^2)}{\sinh(\ell)}
	\end{eqnarray*}
	The integral comes from so-called {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{variation fields}}} of the geodesic $\gamma$ (see also \cite[p.\ 153f.]{Lee2018}). Since $\ell>0$, it holds that $\sinh(\ell)>0$ and $\cosh(\ell)>1$. This implies that $\left(\mathcal{H}_p(u,u)\right)_{ij}>0$ as long as $b_i\neq 0\neq b_j$. Therefore, 
	\[Q_\omega(u,u)~=~\sum_{ij\in E}\omega_{ij}\cdot\left(\mathcal{H}_p(u,u)\right)_{ij}~=~\sum_{ij\in E}\left(\mathcal{H}_p(u,u)\right)_{ij}.\]
	Every summand is nonnegative and the sum is zero if and only if $b_1=b_2=b_3=0$ by a previous argument. However, this exactly represents the condition that $u$ is trivial. Thus, $Q_\omega(u,u)>0$ for every nontrivial infinitesimal flex $u$ and the bar-joint framework is prestress stable according to Definition \ref{def:prestress}.
<!-- end example-4 -->
<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
The condition $x_i^\top\dot{x}_j+x_j^\top \dot{x}_i=0$ for infinitesimal flexes looks identical to the spherical condition \ref{eq:spherical_condition}. However, contrary to the sphere, the isometry group of $\Delta_{d-1}$ is discrete. Therefore, for $d\geq 2$ every finite intrinsic framework on the open probability simplex is locally flexible, as in square root coordinates sufficiently small infinitesimal rotations on the positive orthant of a sphere preserve the geodesic edge lengths. Since the global isometries of $\Delta_{d-1}$ are coordinate permutations, these infinitesimal rotations are nontrivial and locally extend to smooth motions. Consequently, while the rigidity operator presented in this section is a useful differential formula, it does not produce locally rigid frameworks after factoring out the Lie group of isometries. For that specific reason, Remark \ref{rem:local_isometry_group} discusses using local isometries instead, which come with several major theoretical drawbacks.
<!-- end remark-7 -->
<!-- remark-8: body_md -->
Under the square-root transformation, the Fisher probability simplex is isometric to the positive orthant of a round sphere. Small spherical rotations of a finite configuration preserve its intrinsic pairwise distances without necessarily extending to global isometries of the simplex. Consequently, even complete frameworks may be locally flexible under Definition \ref{def:local_rigidity}, further illustrating the distinction between global and local isometries discussed in Remark \ref{rem:local_isometry_group}.

\label{def:tangent-space-and-local-frame}
		Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a smooth manifold. We denote open intervals in $\mathbb{R}$ that contain 0 by $\mathcal{I}_0$. For every point $p\in \mathcal{M}$, consider the set of smooth curves passing through $p$ at $t=0$:
		\[C_p = \{c~\big\lvert ~ c:\mathcal{I}_0\rightarrow \mathcal{M} \text{ is smooth and } c(0)=p\}.\]
		Let $(U,\varphi)$ be a chart of $\mathcal{M}$ around $p$ and consider $c_1,c_2\in C_p$. We define an equivalence relation:
		\[c_1\sim c_2 ~~\Longleftrightarrow ~~ (\varphi \circ c_1)'(0)=(\varphi\circ c_2)'(0).\]
		A {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{tangent vector at $p$}}} is an equivalence class $[c]_\sim$ of curves under the equivalence relation $\sim$. 
		The set of all tangent vectors at $p$ is called the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{tangent space}}} $T_p\mathcal{M}=C_p~/\sim$ and is an $\mathbb{R}$-vector space. Its dimension is called the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{dimension}}} $\dim \mathcal{M}$ of $\mathcal{M}$. The disjoint union of all tangent spaces on $\mathcal{M}$ is called the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{tangent bundle}}} $T\mathcal{M} = \bigsqcup_{p\in\mathcal{M}} T_p\mathcal{M}$.
<!-- end definition-13 -->
<!-- definition-14: body_md -->
\label{def:local_frame}
		Denote the set of {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{smooth vector fields}}} on a manifold $\mathcal{M}$ by $\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})$:
		\[\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})~=~\{E:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}~\big\lvert~E \text{ is smooth and } E\lvert_p \,\in T_p\mathcal{M} \text{ for every } p\in\mathcal{M} \}.\]
		A {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{local frame}}} for $T\mathcal{M}$ is an ordered collection of smooth vector fields $(E_1,\dots, E_n)$ defined on an open set $U\subset \mathcal{M}$ whose values at each $p\in U$ constitute a basis for $T_p\mathcal{M}$. Given $p\in U$ and a chart $(U,\varphi)$ , we define the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{coordinate vector fields}}}
		\[\partial_i\big\lvert_x ~=~ [t\mapsto \varphi^{-1}(\varphi(x)+t\cdot e_i)]_\sim\]
		for $i=1,\dots, n$ and the standard unit basis $(e_1,\dots, e_n)$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. We also call these vector fields the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{local frame induced by the chart}}}.
<!-- end definition-14 -->
<!-- definition-15: body_md -->
\label{def:differential}
		The {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{differential}}} of a smooth function $f:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ at $p\in \mathcal{M}$ is the linear map $df_p:T_p\mathcal{M}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ defined by $df_p(v)=v(f)$. Equivalently, if $v\in T_p\mathcal{M}$ is represented by a smooth curve $c:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow \mathcal{M}$ with $c(0)=p$ and $c'(0)=v$ (cf.\ Proposition \ref{prop:tangent_space}), then
		\[df_p(v)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigg\lvert_{t=0} f(c(t)).\]

<!-- definition-16: body_md -->
\label{def:riemannian-manifold}
	Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a smooth manifold. Define an inner product $g_p : T_p\mathcal{M}\times T_p \mathcal{M}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ for each $p\in \mathcal{M}$. Assume that $g$ is smoothly varying, meaning that for any smooth coordinate chart $(U,x)$ of $\mathcal{M}$ the $(\dim{\mathcal{M}})^2$ functions $g_x( \partial _i\big\lvert_x,\,\partial_j\big\lvert_x): U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ are smooth for the induced local frame $(\partial_1,\dots, \partial_n)$. Then, we call $g$ a {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Riemannian metric}}} and the pair $(\mathcal{M}, g)$ is called a {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Riemannian manifold}}}.

<!-- definition-17: body_md -->
\label{def:riemannian-submanifold}
	Suppose $(\mathcal{M},g)$ is a Riemannian manifold and let $\iota: \mathcal{S} \hookrightarrow \mathcal{M}$ be an embedded submanifold. The {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{pullback metric}}} $\iota^\star g$ makes $(\mathcal{S},\iota^\star g)$ a Riemannian submanifold. Here, the pullback metric is pointwise defined as the function
	\[(\iota^\star g)_p(X,Y)\, = \,g_p(d\iota_p(X),\,d\iota_p(Y))\]
	for $X,Y\in T_p\mathcal{S}$ with the differential $d\iota_p$ at $p$ mapping 
	$T_pS\rightarrow T_p\mathcal{M}$.

\label{def:geodesics}
	Let $c:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow \mathcal{M}$ denote a smooth curve defined on an open interval $\mathcal{I}$. Its {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{velocity}}} is the vector field $c'\in \mathfrak{X}(c)$. Its {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{acceleration}}} is the smooth vector field $c''\in \mathfrak{X}(c)$ defined by $c''=D_t\,c'$. If $\mathcal{M}$ is a Riemannian manifold with covariant derivative $D_t$ induced by the Levi-Civita connection, a {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{geodesic}}} is a smooth curve such that $c''(t)=0$ for all $t\in \mathcal{I}$. A geodesic is called {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{maximal}}} if it cannot be extended to a larger interval. A {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{geodesic arc}}} is given by $\gamma([a,b])\subset \mathcal{M}$ for a closed interval $[a,b]\subset \mathcal{I}$.

<!-- proposition-8: body_md -->
\label{prop:tangent_space}
	Fix a Riemannian manifold $(\mathcal{M},g)$ with Levi-Civita connection $\nabla$. Then, it holds that
	\[T_p\mathcal{M}=\{	c'(0)~\big\lvert ~ c:(-\varepsilon_c, \varepsilon_c)\rightarrow \mathcal{M}\text{ is a geodesic with } c(0)=p \text{ for some } \varepsilon_c>0\}.\] 
	\begin{proof}
		Fix $p\in \mathcal{M}$ and any $v\in T_p\mathcal{M}$. According to  {\cite[Cor.\ 4.28]{Lee2018}} there exists a unique maximal geodesic $\gamma:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow \mathcal{M}$ defined on an open interval $\mathcal{I}\subset \mathbb{R}$ with $0\in \mathcal{I}$ such that $\gamma(0)=p$ and $\gamma'(0)=v$. Fix $\varepsilon>0$ so that $(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\subset \mathcal{I}$. By {\cite[Thm.\ 4.27]{Lee2018}}, any geodesic $\Tilde{\gamma}:(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)\rightarrow \mathcal{M}$ with $\Tilde{\gamma}(0)=p$ and $\Tilde{\gamma}'(0)=v$ agrees with the restriction of $\gamma$ to $(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$. Therefore, the tangent space is contained in the set of geodesic velocities. 
		
		For the other inclusion, we first recall {\cite[Thm.\ 4.27]{Lee2018}}, stating that any two geodesics $\gamma, \Tilde{\gamma}$ that pass through $p$ at $t=0$ and have the same velocity at $t=0$ agree on a sufficiently small interval that contains $0$. Hence, the operation $\sim$ is well-defined on the set of geodesic velocities. Since any geodesic is a also smooth curve, the right-hand-side is therefore contained in $T_p\mathcal{M}$. 
	\end{proof}

\label{def:riemannian-distance}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},g)$ be a Riemannian manifold. A smooth curve $\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow \mathcal{M}$ is {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{admissible}}} if there exists a partition of $[a,b]$ into finite sequence of points $a=a_0<a_1<\dots <a_k=b$ so that $\gamma'(t)\neq 0$ for all $a_i<t<a_{i+1}$ and $0\leq i \leq k-1$. For an admissible curve $\gamma$, we define the length of $\gamma$ as 
	\[L_g(\gamma)=\int_{a}^b \|\gamma'(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}\,dt.\]
	The {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Riemannian distance}}} between $p,q\in \mathcal{M}$ is then $\ell_g(p,q)=\inf_\gamma L_g(\gamma)$ where the infimum ranges over all admissible curves.

\label{def:exponential-map}

\label{def:parallel-transport}

<!-- definition-24: body_md -->
\label{def:jacobi-field}
	Let $(\mathcal{M},g)$ be a Riemannian manifold, let $\gamma$ be a geodesic in $\mathcal{M}$ and let $J$ be a vector field along $\gamma$. For the Levi-Civita connection $\nabla$, we define the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Riemannian curvature tensor}}} via the equation
	\[R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_Y Z-\nabla_Y\nabla_X Z-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z\]
	for vector fields $X,Y,Z\in \mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})$ and the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Lie bracket}}} $[X,Y]=XY-YX$. Then, $J$ is called a {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Jacobi field}}} if it satisfies the {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Jacobi equation}}}
	\[D_t(D_t J)+R(J,\gamma')\gamma'=0.\]

<!-- definition-25: body_md -->
\label{def:hessian}
	Let $f:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a smooth function on a Riemannian manifold $(\mathcal{M},\langle \text{-},\text{-}\rangle)$. The {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Riemannian gradient}}} $\mathrm{grad}\, f$ is the unique vector field on $\mathcal{M}$ so that 
	\[(f\circ c)'(0)=\langle v,\,\mathrm{grad}\, f\rangle_x\]
	for any smooth curve $c:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow \mathcal{M}$ with $c'(0)=v$ and all $(x,v)\in T\mathcal{M}$.
	
	The {\relax\color{NiceBlue}{\textit{Riemannian Hessian}}}  is the bilinear form $\mathrm{Hess}\,f(x):T_x\mathcal{M}\times T_x\mathcal{M}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ defined by 
	\[\mathrm{Hess}\,f(x)[u,v]=\big\langle\nabla_u\mathrm{grad}\,f(x),\,v\rangle_{x}\]
	where $\nabla$ is the Levi-Civita connection on $\mathcal{M}$.

\label{def:isometries}
