<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{1disc}
In each interval $(-\infty,0)$ and $\big(n^2,(n+1)^2\big),\; n\in\mathbb{N}_0$, there is at most $N$ eigenvalues of $H_{\beta,Y}$. We have $\lambda_1(\beta,Y)=0$ for any $\beta<0$, while for $\beta>0$ and $N\ge 2$ the negative spectrum is nonempty of total multiplicity at least $N-1$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The first claim follows from general principles \cite[Corr.~to Thm.~8.19]{We80} because $H_{\beta,Y}$ and the Laplacian of the loop have a common symmetric restriction of deficiency indices $(N,N)$. By \eqref{loopform}, the operator $H_{\beta,Y}$ is positive in the repulsive case, $\beta<0$, and zero is an eigenvalue for any $\beta$ referring to the constant eigenfunction $f(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}$. On the other hand, using step functions constant in each interval $(y_j,y_{j+1})$ one can check that $\inf_{f\in H^1(I\setminus Y)} q_{\beta,Y}[f]<0$ for $\beta>0$. Moreover, among such trial functions one find $N-1$ linearly independent mutually and with the trivial one, so the last claim follows by minimax principle. This argument naturally fails for $N=1$, and indeed, in that case the negative spectrum may be empty; it is straightforward to check that this happens if and only if $\beta\ge 2\pi$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:nopreserve}
{\rm While the $\delta$ interaction counterpart $H^\delta_{\alpha,Y}$ to our operator, associated with the form \eqref{deltaform} below shares the property of the `usual' Schr\"odinger operators having a simple ground state with positive eigenfunction, the situation with  $H_{\beta,Y}$ is different. The positivity is violated already for $N=1$ where the (unnormalized) ground state eigenfunction for $\beta\in(0,2\pi)$ equals $f(x)= \sinh\kappa(x-\pi)$, where $\kappa$ is the solution to $\frac{2}{\kappa}\tanh\kappa\pi = \beta$. More generally, the existence of simple ground state with positive eigenfunction requires $\exp(-tH_{\beta,Y})$ to be positivity preserving to any $t>0$ which, by Beurling-Deny criterion \cite[Thm.~XIII.50]{RS78}, is equivalent the inequality $q[f]\ge q[|f|]$ for all $f\in H^1(I\setminus Y)$. This is not the case, though, since one can find easily an $f$ for which $[|f|](y_j) < [f](y_j)$, and indeed, the ground state may be degenerated which happens for any odd $N$, cf.~Example~\ref{ex:threepoint} and Remark~\ref{rem:spectrum}.}
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{1nomin}
Given $\beta>0$, to any $c<0$ one can find an $Y$ such that $\lambda_1(\beta,Y)<c$.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
It is convenient to extend $Y$ periodically; then one can obviously replace $(0,2\pi)$ by any interval on length $2\pi$ the endpoints of which have an empty intersection with $Y$. It is also clear from \eqref{loopform} that we may restrict ourselves to the case $N=2$. We then take $I=(-\pi,\pi)$ and place the interaction to $y_j=(-1)^j\eta,\: j=1,2$. Choosing $f(x)=\cosh x$ for $|x|<\eta$ and zero otherwise, we have $\|f\|^2= \eta+\frac12\sinh2\eta$ a and $\|f'\|^2= -\eta+\frac12\sinh2\eta$, hence the Rayleigh quotient for this function is
                   
$$
\frac{q_{\beta,Y}[f]}{\|f\|^2} = \frac{\|f'\|^2 - 2\beta^{-1}(|f[-\eta]|^2 + |f[\eta]|^2)}{\|f\|^2} = \frac{-\eta+\frac12\sinh2\eta - 2\beta^{-1}\cosh2\eta}{\eta+\frac12\sinh2\eta}.
$$
                   
Taking now Taylor expansion of the right-hand side expression, we get $-\frac{\beta^{-1}}{\eta}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\eta^2)\big)$ which can be obviously made arbitrarily large negative by choosing $\eta$ small enough.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:1repuls}
Let $\tilde{Y}:= \{ \frac{\pi}{N}(2j-1)\}_{j=1}^N$, then to any $N\ge 2$ there is an $\alpha_N<0$ such that for any $Y$ with $\#Y=N$ we have $\lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,Y) \le \lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,\tilde{Y})$ provided $\alpha> \alpha_N$, the inequality being saturated if and only if $Y$ is congruent with $\tilde{Y}$.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For $\alpha<0$ the claim was proved in Theorem~2.1 of \cite{Ex19}; for small enough positive $\alpha$ see Theorem~5.1 there.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:small}
{\rm (a) The coupling constant smallness of repulsive $\delta$ interactions is not necessary for symmetric configuration to maximize the ground state eigenvalue, cf.~\cite[Theorem~5.2]{Ex19}. The question whether the claim is true generally remains open. One can prove at least that $\tilde{Y}$ is a \emph{local} maximizer for any $\alpha<0$, see the appendix. \\ (b) The value of $\lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,\tilde{Y})$ is not difficult to find. The eigenfunction is hyperbolic or trigonometric depending on the sign of $\alpha$, $\,\frac{2\pi}{N}$-periodic, with $N$ points where the derivative vanishes, and the corresponding eigenvalue comes from the solution of the transcendent equations $2\kappa\tanh\frac{\pi\kappa}{N}=\alpha$ and $2k\tan\frac{\pi k}{N}=\alpha$ for $\pm\alpha>0$, respectively. This shows that the eigenvalue behaves as $\frac{N}{2\pi}\alpha + \mathcal{O}(\alpha^2)$ around $\alpha=0$, as $-\frac14\alpha^2 +\mathcal{O}(\alpha)$ when $\alpha\to\infty$, and it tends to the Dirichlet value $\frac14 N^2$ as $\alpha\to-\infty$.}
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:1bound}
Let $\chi$ be the chromatic number of the partition of $I$ referring to $Y$, two for an even $N$ and three for an odd one. Suppose that $\beta\le \frac{4}{\alpha}\sin^2\frac{\pi}{\chi}$, than there is a unitary operator $U$ such that $U^{-1} H_{\beta,Y}U \le H^\delta_{\alpha,Y}$.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The argument is analogous to the one used in \cite[Thm.~3.6]{BEL14} for attractive $\delta'$ interactions on a Lipschitz partition of a plane; let us sketch it. Consider complex numbers of modulus one, $z_1=1$ and $z_2=-1$ if $N$ is even and $z_{2,3}=\frac12\big(-1\pm\sqrt{3}\big)$ in $N$ is odd; for any $i\ne j$ we obviously have $|z_i-z_j|^2=4\sin^2\frac{\pi}{\chi}$, four if $N$ is even and three if it is odd. We put $\alpha_{\mathcal{Z}}:= \frac{1}{\beta}\sin^2\frac{\pi}{\chi}$ so by assumption $\alpha_{\mathcal{Z}}\le\alpha$. Denoting $I_j:=(y_j,y_{j+1})$, we define the operator $U_{\mathcal{Z}}$ by
                   
\begin{equation} \label{1unit}
(U_{\mathcal{Z}}f)(x) = z_jf_j(z) \quad\text{for}\;\; x\in I_f,\;\;\text{where} \\;\;\; f_j=f\big|_{I_j}.
\end{equation}
                   
The operator is obviously unitary and allows us to define the form $\breve{q}_\beta[f]:= q_\beta[U_{\mathcal{Z}} f]$ with $\mathrm{dom}\,\breve{q}_\beta = \mathrm{dom}\,q_\beta$, which satisfies $\breve{q}_\beta \le q^\delta_\alpha$. Indeed, we have
                   
\begin{align*}
\breve{q}_\beta[f] & = \sum_{j=1}^N \|z_j\nabla f_j\|^2_{L^2(I_j)} + \frac{1}{\beta} \sum_{j=1}^N |z_j-z_{j+1}|^2 |f(y_j)|^2 \\
& = \|\nabla f\|^2_{L^2(I)} + \alpha_{\mathcal{Z}} \sum_{j=1}^N |f(y_j)|^2 = q^\delta_{\alpha_{\mathcal{Z}}}[f] \le q^\delta_{\alpha}[f].
\end{align*}
                   
for any $f\in \mathrm{dom}\,q_{\alpha_{\mathcal{Z}}} = H^1(I) \subset \bigoplus_{j=1}^N H^1(I_j) = \mathrm{dom}\,\breve{q}_\beta$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:1even}
Suppose that $N$ is even and $\beta>0$, then the ground state is simple and we have $\lambda_1(\beta,Y) \le \lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y})$, the inequality being saturated if and only if $Y$ is congruent with $\tilde{Y}$. The function $\lambda_1(\cdot,\tilde{Y})$ is increasing with $\lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y})=-\frac{4}{\beta^2}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-1})$ as $\beta\to 0+$ and $\lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y})=-\frac{2N}{\beta}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-2})$ as $\beta\to\infty$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Since $\chi=2$ in this case, $\lambda_1(\beta,Y) \le \lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},Y)$ holds by Proposition~\ref{prop:1bound}, thus Proposition~\ref{prop:1repuls} implies that $\lambda_1(\beta,Y) \le \lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde{Y})$ for any $Y$. Let $f_\mathrm{max}$ be the eigenfunction of $H^\delta_{4\beta^{-1},\tilde{Y}}$ corresponding to $\lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde{Y})$, that is, satisfying $-f_\mathrm{max}''(x) =\lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde{Y})f_\mathrm{max}(x)$ for $x\in I\setminus\tilde{Y}$ and $f_\mathrm{max}'(\tilde{y}_j\pm) = \pm 2\beta^{-1}f_\mathrm{max}(\tilde{y}_j)$ with $\tilde{y}_j=\frac{\pi}{N}(2j-1)$. The function $\breve{f}_\mathrm{max}:\, \breve{f}_\mathrm{max}(x) = (-1)^j f_\mathrm{max}(x)$ for $x\in I_j$ satisfies the same equation; its derivative is continuous while the value switches sign at the points $\tilde{y}_j$ satisfying the conditions
                   
$$
\breve{f}_\mathrm{max}(\tilde{y}_j+) - \breve{f}_\mathrm{max}(\tilde{y}_j-) = \beta \breve{f}'_\mathrm{max}(\tilde{y}_j),
$$
                   
in other words, it is nothing but an eigenfunction of $H(\beta,\tilde{Y})$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde{Y})$. Among the eigenvalues of $H(\beta,\tilde{Y})$, this one is the lowest, $\lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y})$. Suppose that this is not the case. Such a ground state, possibly degenerate, would be between the points of $\tilde{Y}$ a hyperbolic function of $\kappa x$ with $\kappa> \sqrt{-\lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde{Y})}$ and in view of the symmetry of $\tilde{Y}$ it would be a linear combination of functions either symmetric or antisymmetric with respect to all the points of $\tilde{Y}$. However, the former will not satisfy conditions \eqref{1Ddelta'} while the latter would by the inverse unitary transformation produce an eigenfunction of $H^\delta_{\alpha,\tilde{Y}}$ corresponding to an eigenvalue below $\lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde{Y})$ which is a contradiction. From the same reason the ground state of $H(\beta,\tilde{Y})$ cannot be degenerate. The behavior of the ground state eigenvalue follows easily from the relation $\kappa\tanh\frac{\pi\kappa}{N}=\frac{2}{\beta}$ determining it, which follows from the equation mentioned in Remark~\ref{rem:small}(b) with the substitution $\alpha=4\beta^{-1}$
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:attr_only}
{\rm (a) $H(\beta,\tilde{Y})$ has for each $\beta\in\big(0,\frac{2\pi}{N}\big)$ an eigenvalue with the eigenfunction locally antisymmetric with respect to all the points of $\tilde{Y}$; it consists of the segments of the form $\sinh\kappa\big(x-\frac12(\tilde{y}_j+\tilde{y}_{j+1})\big)$. The corresponding eigenvalue $-\kappa^2$ comes from solution to $\kappa\coth\frac{\pi\kappa}{N}=\frac{2}{\beta}$. Comparing this to $\kappa\tanh\frac{\pi\kappa}{N}=\frac{2}{\beta}$, we see this eigenvalue is strictly larger that $\lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y})$. It is also not difficult to find the eigenfunction refererring to $\lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y})$; without normalization, it equals $(-1)^j \cosh\kappa\big(x-\frac12(\tilde{y}_j+\tilde{y}_{j+1})\big)$ for $x\in(\tilde{y}_j,\tilde{y}_{j+1})$.\\
(b) While the claim of Proposition~\ref{prop:1bound} holds for both coupling constant signs, the above corollary does not extend to the repulsive case even if we assume $\beta<\frac{4}{\alpha_N}$ only. The reason is that the ground state eigenvalue of $H_{\beta,\tilde{Y}}$ does not coincide in this case with that of $H^\delta_{\alpha,\tilde{Y}}$. Indeed, the latter is positive, however, the former is zero (for any $Y$) as pointed out in Proposition~\ref{1disc}.}

<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:threepoint}
{\rm Consider the case $N=3$ and denote $d_{ij}=y_i-y_j$ cyclically; then we have $d_{31}=2\pi-(d_{12}+d_{23})$. For simplicity, we put $g_{ij}=G_\kappa(d_{ij})$; the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue is squeezed between $\max_i \sum_j g_{ij}$ and $\min_i \sum_j g_{ij}$. The function $f(d):=\cosh\kappa(\pi-d)$ is strictly convex, hence $f(d_{12}+d_{23}+d_{31})\ge 3f\big(\frac23\pi\big)$, the inequality being saturated \emph{iff} $d_{12}=d_{23}=d_{31}=\frac23\pi$. Consequently, the sum $\frac13\big(3g_{ii}+2(d_{12}+d_{23}+d_{31})\big)$ appearing in both the indicated bounds is strictly minimized by the equidistant configuration for which $\mu_3(\kappa)=a+2b$, where $a=\frac{\kappa}{2}\coth\pi\kappa$ and $b=\frac{\kappa\cosh(\pi\kappa/3)}{2\sinh\pi\kappa}$; this gives $\mu_3(\kappa)=\frac{\kappa}{2}\coth\frac{\pi\kappa}{3}$ corresponding to then eigenvector $3^{-1/2}(1,1,1)$, and therefore the highest negative eigenvalue of $H(\beta,Y_3)$ is \emph{strictly minimized} by the one with the maximum symmetry, $\lambda_3(\beta,Y_3) > \lambda_3(\beta,\tilde{Y}_3)$, unless $Y_3$ is congruent with $\tilde{Y}_3$.

Furthermore, the two remaining eigenvalues of $\Gamma_{\beta,\tilde{Y}_3}(\kappa)$ coincide mutually,
                   
$$
\mu_1(\kappa)=\mu_2(\kappa)=a-b = \frac{\kappa \sinh\frac{2\pi\kappa}{3}}{1+2\cosh\frac{2\pi\kappa}{3}}
$$
                   
with the eigenvectors $3^{-1/2}(1,\mathrm{e}^{\pm 2\pi i/3},\mathrm{e}^{\pm 4\pi i/3})$, and as a result, the ground state eigenvalue of $H(\beta,\tilde{Y}_3)$ has \emph{multiplicity two}. In the weak coupling case, $\beta\to\infty$, we have $\lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y}_3) = \lambda_2(\beta,\tilde{Y}_3) = -\frac{3}{2\pi\beta} + \mathcal{O}(\beta^{-2})$, while for strong coupling, $\beta\to 0+$, all the three eigenvalues behave as $\lambda_j(\beta,\tilde{Y})=-\frac{4}{\beta^2}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-1})$, the differences concerning the error term only.}

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:1lambdaN}
For any $\beta\in\big(0,\frac{2\pi}{N}\big)$, the eigenvalue $\lambda_N(\beta,Y)$ of $H(\beta,Y)$ is mini\-mized, uniquely up to shifts, by the symmetric configuration, $Y=\tilde{Y}$, the eigenvalue $-\kappa^2$ being determined by the condition $\kappa \coth\!\frac{\kappa\pi}{N} = \frac{2}{\beta}$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Denote again $d_{jk} = \min\bigl\{|y_j-y_k|,\, 2\pi-|y_j-y_k|\bigr\}$ the geodesic distances on the loop. The largest eigenvalue of the matrix $\Gamma_{\infty,Y}(\kappa)$ equals
                   
$$
\mu_\mathrm{\max}(\kappa) = \max_{\|c\|_2=1} \sum_{j,k=1}^{N} c_jc_k\,\kappa^2G_\kappa(d_{jk}).
$$
                   
and, again by Perron-Frobenius, the corresponding eigenvector $c\in\mathbb{C}^N$ can be chosen positive. The function $\kappa^2G_\kappa(\cdot)$ is decreasing, hence an increase of pairwise distances decreases the matrix entries, and strictly convex. Next we denote $\delta_j:=y_{j+1}-y_j$, cyclically, the successive gaps between the points of $Y$; they are positive and $\sum_{j=1}^N \delta_j=2\pi$. The convexity allows us to apply a Fejes T\'oth type majorization argument, replacing neighboring gaps $(\delta_j,\delta_{j+1})$ by their arithmetic mean, $(\delta_j,\delta_{j+1}) \mapsto \left( \frac12(\delta_j+\delta_{j+1}), \frac12(\delta_j+\delta_{j+1})\right)$ which makes the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue smaller whenever $\delta_{j+1}\ne \delta_j$. Iterating this averaging procedure, we arrive at the conclusion that the minimum of the form is reached when all the gaps are equal, $\delta_j=\frac{2\pi}{N},\, j=1,\dots,N$, i.e. if $Y$ is congruent with $\tilde{Y}$.

One can also write the equation determining the extremal value of $\lambda_N(\beta,Y)$. For a symmetric $Y$ the matrix $\Gamma_{\infty,Y}(\kappa)$ is circulant \cite{Da79} and its largest eigenvalue equals the sum of any row or column,
                   
$$
\min_Y \mu_\mathrm{\max}(\kappa) = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} \frac{\kappa}{2\sinh(\pi\kappa)} \cosh\!\left( \kappa \Bigl( \pi-\frac{2\pi}{N}\min(m,N-m) \Bigr) \right) = \frac{\kappa}{2}\coth\!\left(\frac{\kappa\pi}{N}\right),
$$
                   
which in view of \eqref{1secular} gives rise to the spectral condition $\kappa \coth\!\frac{\kappa\pi}{N} = \frac{2}{\beta}$ which we have already encountered in Remark~\ref{rem:attr_only}.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:1lambdaN}
The eigenvalue $\lambda_1(\beta,Y)$ of $H(\beta,Y)$ is maxi\-mized, uniquely up to shifts, by the symmetric configuration, $Y=\tilde{Y}$. The multiplicity of $\lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y})$ is one for $N$ even and two for $N$ odd.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We can no longer employ Perron-Frobenius, instead we rephrase the question in terms of the inverse matrix. The function \eqref{1Dkernel_attr} is even and symmetric with respect to $x=\pi$ and extends periodically to the real line; it satisfies $-G_\kappa''+\kappa^2G_\kappa=0$ for $x\neq 2\pi k,\, k\in\mathbb{N}$, and $G_\kappa'(0\pm)=\mp\tfrac12$. Knowing the spectrum of the free operator on the loop, one write it also as $G_\kappa(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e^{inx}}{n^2+\kappa^2}$, which means that
                   
$$
\langle c,\Gamma_{\infty,Y}(\kappa)c\rangle =\frac{\kappa^2}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\big|\sum_{j}c_je^{iny_j}\big|^2}{n^2+\kappa^2}>0
$$
                   
holds for any nonzero $c\in\mathbb{C}^N$ since for distinct $y_j$ the trigonometric sums cannot all vanish. The matrix $\Gamma_{\infty,Y}(\kappa)$ is thus positive and so is its inverse, and we have
                   
\begin{equation} \label{invtrick}
\mu_\mathrm{\min}(\kappa)=\big(\nu_\mathrm{\max}(\kappa)\big)^{-1},
\end{equation}
                   
where $\nu_\mathrm{\max}(\kappa)$ is the maximal eigenvalue of $\Gamma_{\infty,Y}(\kappa)^{-1}$. To use relation \eqref{invtrick}, one has derive the inverse matrix which appears a particular Jacobi matrix. To find it, note that for any given $c\in\mathbb{C}^N$, the function $\psi=\sum_{j'}c_{j'}\,G_\kappa(\,\cdot\,-y_{j'})$ solves the equation $-\psi''+\kappa^2\psi=0$ on each interval $(y_j,y_{j+1})$, is continuous there and has derivative jumps at the endpoints, $\psi'(y_j-)-\psi'(y_j+)=c_j$. Its values at the points $y_j$, expressed in terms of $\Gamma_{\infty,Y}(\kappa)$, are $v_j:=\psi(y_j)=\kappa^{-2}(\Gamma_{\infty,Y}(\kappa)c)_j$.

Now we need to express the vector $c$ corresponding to given $v$. To this aim, we use the explicit form of the function $\psi$ as a solution to $-\psi''+\kappa^2\psi=0$: on
an interval of length $\ell$ with end values $a,b$ one has
                   
$$
\psi(x)=\frac{a\sinh\kappa(\ell-x)+b\sinh\kappa x}{\sinh\kappa\ell}
$$
                   
so computing the jumps $c_j$ of $\psi'$ from the values $v_k$ gives $c=M_Yv$ with
                   
$$                                 
(M_Y)_{jj}:=\kappa\big(\coth\kappa\delta_{j-1}+\coth\kappa\delta_j\big),
\quad
(M_Y)_{j,j+1}:=(M_Y)_{j+1,j}=-\frac{\kappa}{\sinh\kappa\delta_j}
$$                
                   
where $\delta_j=y_{j+1}-y_j$ are the distances of the neighboring points introduced in the previous proof and the indices are again taken modulo $N$. The sought inverse matrix is thus $\Gamma_{\infty,Y}(\kappa)^{-1}= \kappa^{-2}M_Y$. Using further the identities $\coth t-\frac1{\sinh t}=\tanh\frac t2$ and $\coth t+\frac1{\sinh t}=\coth\frac t2$, one can write the quadratic form of the matrix $M_Y$ as a sum over the intervals between the neighboring points,
                   
\begin{equation}\label{qformM_Y}
\langle v,M_Yv\rangle=\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{j=1}^N\Big[\tanh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa\delta_j}{2}\Big)\,|v_j+v_{j+1}|^2
+\coth\!\Big(\frac{\kappa\delta_j}{2}\Big)\,|v_j-v_{j+1}|^2\Big].
\end{equation}
                   
This can be in view of \eqref{invtrick} used to estimate the eigenvalue in question,
                   
\begin{equation} \label{invtrick2}
\mu_\mathrm{\min}(\kappa)= \frac{\kappa^2}{\lambda_{\max}(M_Y)} \;\le\;\frac{\kappa^2}{\langle v,M_Yv\rangle}
\end{equation}
                   
which holds for any unit vector $v\in\mathbb{C}^N$. This is the key point: we have to find a vector which minimizes $\langle v,M_Yv\rangle$ and show that it corresponds to $Y=\tilde{Y}$.

We note that both $M_{\tilde{Y}}$ and $\Gamma_{\infty,\tilde{Y}}(\kappa)$ are circulant, and as such they have common eigenvectors the components are complex roots of unity, namely $v^{(k)}_j=N^{-1/2}e^{2\pi i kj/N}$, $k=0,\dots,N-1$; the corresponding eigenvalues are easily found to be
                   
$$                                     
\Lambda_k(M_{\tilde{Y}})=\frac{2\kappa}{\sinh\kappa\delta}\Big[\cosh\kappa\delta-\cos\frac{2\pi k}{N}\Big]
$$                
                   
and
                   
\begin{equation} \label{tilded_ev}
\Lambda_k(\Gamma_{\infty,\tilde{Y}}(\kappa))=\frac{\kappa^2}{\lambda_k(M_{\tilde{Y}})}
=\frac{\kappa}{2}\,\frac{\sinh(2\pi\kappa/N)}{\cosh(2\pi\kappa/N)-\cos(2\pi k/N)},
\end{equation}
                   
where $\delta=2\pi/N$ is the common value of the distance $\delta_j$. The eigenvalues \eqref{tilded_ev} differ from the naturally ordered ones, $\mu_j(\kappa),\,j=1,\dots,N$, we are interested, in by numeration only; we have $\mu_j=\Lambda_{j-1}$ for $N$ even and $\mu_j=\mu_{j+1}=\Lambda_{\lfloor{(N\pm j)/2\rfloor}}$ for $N$ odd. In particular, the smallest eigenvalue, $\mu_1(\kappa)$, corresponds to $k$ closest to $N/2$,
                   
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mu_min_opt}
\lambda_1(\beta,\tilde{Y})=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\kappa}{2}\tanh\dfrac{\pi\kappa}{N}, & N \text{ even},\\[4mm]
\dfrac{\kappa}{2}\,\dfrac{\sinh(2\pi\kappa/N)}{\cosh(2\pi\kappa/N)+\cos(\pi/N)}, & N \text{ odd},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
                   
being simple in the former case and of multiplicity two in the latter.

This allows us show by means of \eqref{invtrick2} that the optimal configuration is equidistant using the eigenvectors corresponding to \eqref{eq:mu_min_opt}. Consider first $N$ even and take $v_j:=(-1)^j\,N^{-1/2}$, then $v_j+v_{j+1}=0$ and $|v_j-v_{j+1}|^2=\frac{4}{N}$, hence \eqref{qformM_Y} gives
                   
$$
\langle v,M_Yv\rangle=\frac{2\kappa}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N\coth\frac{\kappa\delta_j}{2}\;\ge\;2\kappa\coth\frac{\pi\kappa}{N},
$$
                   
by Jensen's inequality, since $\coth$ is strictly convex on $(0,\infty)$ and $\frac1N\sum_j\delta_j=2\pi/N$, the inequality being saturated if and only if all the $\delta_j$ are equal. This yields
                   
$$
\mu_{\min}(\kappa)\le\frac{\kappa^2}{2\kappa\coth(\pi\kappa/N)}=\frac{\kappa}{2}\tanh\frac{\pi\kappa}{N},
$$
                   
showing that $\mu_{\min}(\kappa)$ is uniquely maximized by configurations $Y$ congruent with $\tilde{Y}$.

The argument for $N$ odd is similar, just slightly more complicated. One uses now $v_j=N^{-1/2}\mathrm{e}^{i\theta j}$ with $\theta=\pi-\pi/N$ (note that this does not violate the periodicity since $e^{i\theta N}=e^{i\pi(N-1)}=1$ holds for odd $N$); then we have
                   
$$
|v_j+v_{j+1}|^2=\frac2N\Big(1-\cos\frac{\pi}{N}\Big),\quad
|v_j-v_{j+1}|^2=\frac2N\Big(1+\cos\frac{\pi}{N}\Big),
$$
                   
and
                   
$$
\langle v,M_Yv\rangle=\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N h(\delta_j) \quad \text{with }\;
h(\delta):=\Big(1-\cos\frac{\pi}{N}\Big)\tanh\frac{\kappa\delta}{2}+\Big(1+\cos\frac{\pi}{N}\Big)\coth\frac{\kappa\delta}{2},
$$
                   
where $h$ is strictly convex: putting $t=\kappa\delta/2$, $a=1-\cos\frac\pi N$, $b=1+\cos\frac\pi N$, we find
                   
$$
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\big[a\tanh t+b\coth t\big]
=2\Big(b\,\frac{\cosh t}{\sinh^3 t}-a\,\frac{\sinh t}{\cosh^3 t}\Big)
=2\,\frac{\sinh t}{\cosh^3 t}\,\big(b\coth^4 t-a\big)>0,
$$
                   
because $b>a$ and $\coth^4 t>1$. Jensen's inequality then yields $\sum_j h(\delta_j)\ge N h(2\pi/N)$, with equality valid if and only if all the $\delta_j=2\pi/N$; using $\tanh t+\coth t=2\coth 2t$ and $\coth t-\tanh t=2/\sinh 2t$ with $t=\pi\kappa/N$, we get
                   
$$
\langle v,M_Yv\rangle\;\ge\;\frac{2\kappa\,\big[\cosh(2\pi\kappa/N)+\cos(\pi/N)\big]}{\sinh(2\pi\kappa/N)}.
$$
                   
Consequently, $\mu_{\min}(\kappa)$ is bounded from above by the second expression in \eqref{eq:mu_min_opt} with the equality valid if and only if $\delta_1=\dots=\delta_N=2\pi/N$; this concludes the proof.

As in Corollary~\ref{cor:1even}, we use a comparison with the corresponding $\delta$ leaky graph with the Hamiltonian $H^\delta_{\alpha,\Gamma}$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$ associated with the quadratic form,
                   
$$                                        
     q^\delta_{\alpha,\Gamma}[f] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} |\nabla f(x)|^2\mathrm{d} x - \alpha \int_\Gamma |f(s)|^2\mathrm{d} s
$$                
                   
defined on $H^1(\mathbb{R}^2)$. We choose $\alpha=\frac{4}{\beta}$ and use Theorem~1.1 of \cite{EL18} by which the ground state of $H^\delta_{4\beta^{-1},\Gamma}$ is maximized by that of $H^\delta_{4\beta^{-1},\tilde\Gamma}$, uniquely so, cf.~Proposition~\ref{prop:uniqdelta} below. Combining the two results, we arrive at the inequality
                   
$$
\lambda_1(\beta,\Gamma) \le \lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde\Gamma).
$$
                   
Denote by $\phi_\mathrm{max}$ the corresponding positive eigenfunction of $H^\delta_{4\beta^{-1},\tilde\Gamma}$. Since $N$ is even, $\tilde\Gamma_j$ and $\tilde\Gamma_{j+N/2}$ form a line for any $j$, and $\phi_\mathrm{max}$ is symmetric with respect to it, which in particular means that the normal derivative of $\phi_\mathrm{max}$ switches sign at the points of $\Gamma_j$.

The unitary operator $U$ we are using is known explicitly: denoting by $\Sigma_j$ the segment of the plane determined by the halflines $\tilde\Gamma_j$ and $\tilde\Gamma_{j+1}$, it acts as $(U\phi)(x) = (-1)^j \phi(x)$. Consequently, the function $U\phi_\mathrm{max}$ solves the equation $-\Delta\phi= \lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde\Gamma)\phi$ in $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\tilde\Gamma$ and its normal derivative is continuous at each $\tilde\Gamma_j$, while its value has a jump there; denoting by $U\phi_\mathrm{max}(x\pm)$ the one-sided normal limits of this function at a point $x\in\tilde\Gamma_j$, we have
                   
\begin{align*}
U\phi_\mathrm{max}(x+) - U\phi_\mathrm{max}(x-) & \: = 2\phi_\mathrm{max}(x) = -\frac{\beta}{2}\big(\phi'_\mathrm{max}(x+) - \phi'_\mathrm{max}(x-) \\ & \: = -\beta\phi'_\mathrm{max}(x) = -\beta U\phi'_\mathrm{max}(x),
\end{align*}
                   
so that $U\phi_\mathrm{max}$ is an eigenfunction of $H_{\beta,\tilde\Gamma}$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda^\delta_1(4\beta^{-1},\tilde\Gamma)$, and repeating the argument from the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:1even}, we conclude that it is nothing but the ground state of the operator.

<!-- proposition-7: body_md -->
\label{prop:uniqdelta}
$\inf\sigma(H^\delta_{\alpha,\Gamma})$ is maximized by $\inf\sigma(H^\delta_{\alpha,\tilde\Gamma})$ uniquely.
<!-- end proposition-7 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
The result follows by Birman-Schwinger principle as in \cite{EL18} where stars of finite edges length were considered and the present $\Gamma$ appears only as a limit; we focus on the differences using the notation of \cite[Example~10.2.1]{EK15}. Since $H^\delta_{\alpha,\Gamma}$ is unitarily equivalent to $\alpha^2 H^\delta_{-1,\Gamma}$ we can without loss of generality consider the last named operator and its Birman-Schwinger counterpart, an integral operator on $\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^N L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$ acting  as
                   
$$
(Q^\kappa\psi)_i(s) = \sum_{j=1}^N \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\infty K_0\big(\kappa d_{ij}(s,s')\big)\, \psi_j(s')\,\mathrm{d} s',
$$
                   
where $d_{ij}(s,s') = \sqrt{s^2+s'^{2} - 2ss'\cos\phi_{ij}}$ with $\phi_{ij}=\theta_i - \theta_j= \sum_{k=ji}^{i-1} \omega_k$ and $K_0$ is the Macdonald function; the largest eigenvalue of $Q^\kappa$ is simple and the value of $\kappa$ for which $Q^\kappa\psi_\mathrm{g}=\psi_\mathrm{g}$ determines the ground state energy of $H^\delta_{\alpha,\Gamma}$ as $-\kappa^2$. As in \cite[Prop.~3.1]{EL18}, the operator $Q^\kappa$ is self-adjoint and positive for each $\kappa>0$, however, it is no longer compact for semiinfinite edges; mimicking the reasoning of \cite[Sec.~5]{EI01} one can check that it has a continuous spectrum in $[0,\frac12\kappa]$ and a nonempty discrete one in $(\frac12\kappa,\infty)$ with the eigenvalues monotonously decreasing towards zero as $\kappa\to\infty$.

Putting $\mathcal{H}_\mathrm{sym}= \{\psi\oplus \cdots\oplus\psi:\, \psi\in L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)\}\subset\mathcal{H}$ one finds easily that $Q^\kappa$ is reduced by this subspace and the eigenfunction $\psi_\mathrm{g}$ belongs to $\mathcal{H}_\mathrm{sym}$, as does the eigenfunction $\tilde\psi$ of $\tilde{Q}^\kappa$. As in \cite{EL18} one checks that $K_0\big(\kappa d_{ij}(s,s')$ is strictly decreasing and strictly convex as a function of the angle $\phi_{ij}$ so using Jensen's inequality we get
                   
\begin{align*}
\sup\sigma(Q^\kappa) & = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty \sum_{j=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^N K_0\big(\kappa d_{jk}(s,s')\big)\,\psi_\mathrm{g}(s)\psi_\mathrm{g}(s')\,\mathrm{d} s\mathrm{d} s' \\
& \ge \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty \sum_{j=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^N K_0\big(\kappa d_{jk}(s,s')\big)\,\tilde\psi(s)\tilde\psi(s')\,\mathrm{d} s\mathrm{d} s' \\
& =\frac{1}{2\pi} \sum_{j=1}^N \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty \Big(\sum_{k=1}^N K_0\big(\kappa d_{jk}(s,s')\big)\Big)\,\tilde\psi(s)\tilde\psi(s')\,\mathrm{d} s\mathrm{d} s' \\
& > \frac{N}{2\pi} \sum_{j=1}^N \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty \sum_{k=1}^N K_0\Big(\kappa \Big(\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{d_{jk}(s,s')}{N}\Big)\Big)\,\tilde\psi(s)\tilde\psi(s')\,\mathrm{d} s\mathrm{d} s' \\
& = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sum_{j=1}^N \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty \Big( \sum_{k=1}^N K_0\big(\kappa \tilde{d}_{jk}(s,s')\big)\Big)\,\tilde\psi(s)\tilde\psi(s')\,\mathrm{d} s\mathrm{d} s' \\
& = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty \Big(\sum_{j=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^N K_0\big(\kappa \tilde{d}_{jk}(s,s')\big)\Big)\,\tilde\psi(s)\tilde\psi(s')\,\mathrm{d} s\mathrm{d} s' = \sup\sigma(\tilde{Q}^\kappa)
\end{align*}
                   
unless $\tilde\Gamma$ is congruent with $\Gamma$ which is what we have set out to prove.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For every $N\ge3$ and $\beta>0$ the lowest eigenvalue $\lambda_1(H_{\beta,\Gamma_\omega})$ exists and is strictly maximized by the equiangular star $\tilde\Gamma$ with $\omega_j=\frac{2\pi}{N},\, j=1,\dots,N$, uniquely up to rotations. The maximal value is $-\frac{\mu_N^2}{\beta^2}$, where
                   
$$
  \mu_N=\sup\sigma\Big(\mathbf{r}^{1/2}U_1^{*}\,\frac{m_N^*(\tau)}{\tau}\,U_1\mathbf{r}^{1/2}\Big)>2
  \quad \text{and} \quad
  m_N^*(\tau)=2\,\frac{\cosh\frac{2\pi\tau}{N}+c_N}{\sinh\frac{2\pi\tau}{N}}.
$$
                   
with $c_N=1$ for even $N$ and $c_N=\cos\frac\pi N$ for odd $N$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
According to \eqref{through_Q1} it is sufficient to consider $\kappa=1$, by \eqref{Qtransf} we have
                   
$$
\langle\phi,Q_1^{(\omega)}\phi\rangle
=\int_0^\infty\big\langle \hat\Phi(\tau), M_\omega(\tau)\hat\Phi(\tau)\big\rangle\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau}
\quad \text{with}\;\;\; \hat\Phi=\mathbb{U}_1\,\mathbf{r}^{1/2}\phi
$$
                   
for any $\phi\in L^2(\Gamma)$; we add to the operator symbol the label indicating the configuration $\omega=\{\omega_1,\dots,\omega_N\}$. The matrix $M(\tau)$ is the sum of $N\times N$ matrices, each of which contributes through a nonzero $2\times2$ block $\begin{pmatrix}\coth\omega_j\tau&-\operatorname{csch}\omega_j\tau \\ -\operatorname{csch}\omega_j\tau &\coth\omega_j\tau\end{pmatrix}$ acting on the pair $(\hat\Phi_j,\hat\Phi_{j+1})$. They have eigenvalues $(1,\pm 1)$ with the eigenvalues $\tanh\frac{\omega_j\tau}{2}$ and $\coth\frac{\omega_j\tau}{2}$, respectively, which yields
                   
\begin{equation}\label{Msector}
  \big\langle \hat\Phi, M_\omega(\tau)\hat\Phi \big\rangle
  =\sum_{j=1}^N\Big[\tfrac12\coth\tfrac{\omega_j\tau}{2}\,\big|\hat\Phi_j-\hat\Phi_{j+1}\big|^2
  +\tfrac12\tanh\tfrac{\omega_j\tau}{2}\,\big|\hat\Phi_j+\hat\Phi_{j+1}\big|^2\Big]\,;
\end{equation}
                   
note the similarity of this relation with \eqref{qformM_Y}.

Our aim is to compare this form with the one of the symmetric star, $\tilde\omega_j=2\pi/N$. In this case the matrix $M(\tau)$ is circulant, and by the discrete Fourier transform in $j$ one can diagonalize it: we get
$Q_1\simeq\bigoplus_{k=0}^{N-1}Q_1^{(k)}$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$, where
                   
$$
  Q^{(k)}_1=\mathbf{r}^{1/2}U_1^{*}\,\frac{m_k(\tau)}{\tau}\,U_1\mathbf{r}^{1/2}
  \quad \text{with}\;\;\;
  m_k(\tau):=2\coth\frac{2\pi\tau}N-2\cos\frac{2\pi k}N\,\operatorname{csch}\frac{2\pi\tau}N.
$$
                   
By $k^*=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ we denote the index closest to $N/2$ and we put $m_N^*:=m_{k^*}$, that is
                   
$$
  m_N^*(\tau)=2\,\frac{\cosh\frac{2\pi\tau}{N}+c_N}{\sinh\frac{2\pi\tau}{N}},
  \quad
  c_N=-\cos\frac{2\pi k^*}{N}=
  \begin{cases}1,&N\text{ even},\\[1mm]\cos\frac{\pi}{N},&N\text{ odd}.\end{cases}
$$
                   
Since $m_k\le m_{k^*}$ holds for all $k$, we have $Q^{(k)}\le Q^{(k^*)}$ and therefore
                   
$$
  \mu_{\max}(\omega_{\mathrm{sym}})=\sup\sigma\big(Q^{(k^*)}\big)
  =\sup_{\psi\neq0}\frac{1}{\|\psi\|^2}\int_0^\infty m_N^*(\tau)\,|\hat\psi(\tau)|^2\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau},
  \quad \hat\psi=U_1\mathbf{r}^{1/2}\psi\,;
$$
                   
as expected, the top value is attained twice, for $k^*=\frac{N\pm1}{2}$, if $N$ is odd.

Returning to a general angular configuration $\omega$, we have to choose now trial functions to be inserted into \eqref{Msector}. We take an arbitrary $\psi\in L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$ and put
                   
$$
  \phi_j=N^{-1/2}\,e^{2\pi i jk^*/N}\,\psi,\quad j=1,\dots,N,
$$
                   
so that $\|\phi\|=\|\psi\|$ and we have
                   
$$
  \big|\hat\Phi_j-\hat\Phi_{j+1}\big|^2=\frac4N\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi k^*}{N}\Big)|\hat\psi|^2,
  \quad
  \big|\hat\Phi_j+\hat\Phi_{j+1}\big|^2=\frac4N\cos^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi k^*}{N}\Big)|\hat\psi|^2.
$$
                   
What is important, with this choice the above values are independent of $j$ and we get
                   
\begin{equation}\label{Mtrial}
  \langle \phi,Q_1^{(\omega)}\phi\rangle
  =\frac2N\int_0^\infty\Big[\sum_{j=1}^N F_N(\omega_j\tau)\Big]|\hat\psi(\tau)|^2\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau},
  \quad
  F_N(x):=\gamma_N\coth\frac x2+(1-\gamma_N)\tanh\frac x2,
\end{equation}
                   
where
                   
$$
  \gamma_N=\sin^2\frac{\pi k^*}{N}=
  \begin{cases}1,&N\text{ even},\\[1mm]\cos^2\frac{\pi}{2N},&N\text{ odd}.\end{cases}
$$
                   
It is easy to see that $F_N$ is strictly convex on $(0,\infty)$ for every $N\ge2$; for $N$ even it is obvious, for $N$ it is enough to note up to the factor $\frac12$ it coincides with the function $h$ appearing in the concluding part of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:1lambdaN}. Since $\sum_j\omega_j=2\pi$, by Jensen's inequality, we then get
                   
$$
  \frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N F_N(\omega_j\tau)\ \ge\ F_N\Big(\frac{2\pi\tau}{N}\Big)
$$
                   
for every $\tau>0$, with equality if and only if all $\omega_j$'s are equal. Inserting this into \eqref{Mtrial} and noting that $2F_N\!\big(\tfrac{2\pi\tau}{N}\big)=m_N^*(\tau)$, we obtain the inequality
                   
$$
  \langle \phi,Q_1^{(\omega)}\phi\rangle
  \ \ge\ \int_0^\infty m_N^*(\tau)|\hat\psi(\tau)|^2\,\frac{d\tau}{\tau}
  =\langle \psi, Q^{(k^*)}_{\mathrm{sym}}\psi\rangle
$$
                   
valid for every $\psi\in L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)$; taking the supremum over all of them, we arrive at
                   
\begin{equation} \label{finalbound}
  \mu_{\max}(\omega)\ \ge\ \sup_{\psi\ne0}\frac{\langle \phi Q_1^{(\omega)}\phi\rangle}{\|\phi\|^2}
  \ \ge\ \mu_{\max}(\tilde\omega)\,;
\end{equation}
                   
this implies the bound we are looking for, $\lambda_1(H_{\beta,\Gamma_\omega})\le\lambda_1(H_{\beta,\Gamma_{\tilde\omega}})$.

It remain to check the existence and uniqueness. The existence requires $m_N^*(\tau)>m_2^*(\tau):=2\coth\frac{\pi\tau}{2}$, where the right-hand side corresponds to the straight line (and the essential spectrum threshold of the original operator). With $y=\pi\tau/N$, this is equivalent to
                   
$$
 \sinh\frac{(N-4)y}{2}+c_N\sinh\frac{Ny}{2}>0,
$$
                   
which is obvious for $N\ge4$; for $N=3$ the left-hand side equals $\tfrac12\sinh\frac y2+2\sinh^3\frac y2>0$. Since the ground state exists, the supremum in \eqref{finalbound} is attained at some $\psi_0$, then it follows from the bijectivity of KL transformation that $\hat\psi_0$ is nonzero on a set of positive measure; the uniqueness is then a consequence of the strict convexity of~$F_N$.

<!-- proposition-9: body_md -->
\label{prop:1local}
For any $Y\in\mathcal{Y}_0$ not congruent with $\tilde Y$ we have $\lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,Y) < \lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,\tilde{Y})$.
<!-- end proposition-9 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
We replace the operator $H^\delta_{\alpha,Y}$ by $H^{\delta,\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha,Y}$ in which Neumann conditions are imposed at the stationary points; it is obvious that $\lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,Y) = \inf\sigma(H^\delta_{\alpha,Y}) = \inf\sigma(H^{\delta,\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha,Y})$. The last named operator is a direct sum, $\bigoplus_{j=1}^N H^{\delta,\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha,\hat{I}_j}$, of Neumann Laplacians on intervals between two stationary points containing the interaction sites $y_j$; we use hat to distinguish them from the intervals $I_j$ appearing in Proposition~\ref{prop:1bound} and consider each of them separately. Let us first check that the lowest eigenvalue of $H^{\delta,\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha,\hat{I}_j}$ is maximal if the $\delta$ interaction is placed at the center of $\hat{I}_j$; in view of the natural scaling we can assume without loss of generality that $|\hat{I}_j|=1$. If the interaction position is $a\in(0,1)$, the spectral condition is easily seen to be $k\big(\tan k(1-a)+\tan ka\big) = \alpha$. In view of the strict convexity of the tangent in $(0,\frac12\pi)$, the sum in the bracket is $\ge 2\tan\frac{k}{2}$, sharply so unless $a=\frac12$, and the monotonicity of $k\tan\frac{k}{2}$ yields the result. Using the obvious notation, we thus conclude that $\lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,\hat{I}_j) \le \lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,\tilde{I}_j)$.

Furthermore, a simple scaling argument shows that the lowest eigenvalue of the symmetrically placed $\delta$ interaction is decreasing with the interval $\tilde{I}_j$, which means that to maximize the ground state eigenvalue of the direct sum we have to choose all the $\tilde{I}_j$'s of the same length. However, this is nothing but the operator $H^{\delta,\mathrm{N}}_{\alpha,\tilde{Y}}$, and by Neumann bracketing, its spectral bottom is not larger than $\lambda^\delta_1(\alpha,\tilde{Y})$.
