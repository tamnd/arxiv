<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:drift}
	Almost surely $\int_0^T|\alpha_s|\,ds<\infty$, while
	$\mathbb E\int_0^T|\alpha_s|^2\,ds=\infty$.
	More precisely, for every $t<T$,
	$\mathbb E|\alpha_t|=\sqrt{2/\pi}(T-t)^{-1/2}$ and
	$\mathbb E[\alpha_t^2]=(T-t)^{-1}$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Since $B_T-B_t$ is centered Gaussian with variance
	$T-t$, we have $\alpha_t\overset{d}{=}N/\sqrt{T-t}$,
	where $N$ is a standard normal random variable.
	Using $\mathbb E|N|=\sqrt{2/\pi}$ and $\mathbb E[N^2]=1$, we obtain
	\[
	\mathbb E|\alpha_t|
	=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}(T-t)^{-1/2},
	\qquad
	\mathbb E[\alpha_t^2]=(T-t)^{-1}.
	\]
	Since the process $(|\alpha_t|)_{0 \leq t \leq T}$ is nonnegative, Tonelli's theorem
	yields
	\[
	\mathbb E\int_0^T|\alpha_s|\,ds
	=\int_0^T\mathbb E|\alpha_s|\,ds
	=2\sqrt{\frac{2T}{\pi}}<\infty.
	\]
	As the random variable $\int_0^T|\alpha_s|\,ds$
	is nonnegative and has finite expectation, it is
	finite almost surely.
	
	Similarly, applying Tonelli's theorem to the

\label{thm:PRP}
	The process $W$ in \eqref{eq:alpha} extends continuously
	to $T$ and is a $\mathbb G$-Brownian motion. It is independent
	of $B_T$, and
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:recovery}
		B_t=\frac{t}{T}B_T
		+(T-t)\int_0^t\frac{dW_s}{T-s},
		\quad 0\le t<T.
	\end{equation}
	Consequently,
	$\mathcal G_t=\sigma(B_T)\vee\mathcal F_t^W$
	up to completion for $t<T$, and also at $T$.
	Moreover, every $\xi\in L^2(\mathcal G_T)$ admits
	the unique representation
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:PRP}
		\xi=\mathbb E[\xi\mid B_T]+\int_0^T H_s\,dW_s,
		\qquad H\in\mathcal H^2(\mathbb G).
	\end{equation}

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We first show that $W$ is a $\mathbb G$-Brownian motion.
	For $0\le s\le u<T$, the Gaussian regression
	formula, applied to the jointly Gaussian
	increments $B_T-B_u$ and $B_T-B_s$, gives
	\[
	\mathbb E[B_T-B_u\mid\mathcal G_s]
	=\frac{T-u}{T-s}(B_T-B_s).
	\]
	
	It follows that, for $s\le u<T$,
	$\mathbb E[\alpha_u\mid\mathcal G_s]
	=(B_T-B_s)/(T-s)$. Hence, by conditional
	Fubini's theorem, for $s<t<T$,
	\[
	\mathbb E\left[\int_s^t\alpha_u\,du
	\Bigm|\mathcal G_s\right]
	=\int_s^t\mathbb E[\alpha_u\mid\mathcal G_s]\,du
	=\frac{t-s}{T-s}(B_T-B_s).
	\]
	It follows that
	$\mathbb E[W_t-W_s\mid\mathcal G_s]=0$.
	Thus $W$ is a continuous $\mathbb G$-martingale on
	every compact subinterval of $[0,T)$.
	Since $\int_0^t\alpha_s\,ds$ is continuous and has finite
	variation (from Lemma \ref{lem:drift}) and $[B]_t=t$, we have $[W]_t=t$.
	By L\'evy's characterization, $W$ is a
	$\mathbb G$-Brownian motion on $[0,T)$ (see Theorem 39 in \cite[Ch II]{Protter2004}).
	
	By Lemma~\ref{lem:drift},
	$\int_0^T|\alpha_s|\,ds<\infty$ almost surely.
	Therefore, the continuity of $B$ implies that
	$W_t$ converges almost surely as $t\uparrow T$ to
	$W_T=B_T-\int_0^T\alpha_s\,ds$. Moreover, for $s<t<T$, the increment $W_t-W_s$
is independent of $\mathcal G_s$ and has distribution
$\mathcal N(0,t-s)$. Since $W_t\to W_T$ almost surely
as $t\uparrow T$, the convergence of Gaussian laws
and the preservation of independence under almost
sure convergence imply that $W_T-W_s$ is independent
of $\mathcal G_s$ and has distribution
$\mathcal N(0,T-s)$. Hence, $W$ is a
$\mathbb G$-Brownian motion on $[0,T]$.
Since $B_T$ is $\mathcal G_0$-measurable,
the Brownian property also implies that
$W$ is independent of $B_T$.

	
	Next, setting $U_t=B_T-B_t$, we obtain
	$dU_t=-dW_t-U_t(T-t)^{-1}dt$.
	Applying the product rule to $U_t/(T-t)$
	gives
	$$
	d\big(\frac{U_t}{T-t}\big)
	=-\frac{dW_t}{T-t}.
	$$
	Since $U_0=B_T$, integration yields
	\eqref{eq:recovery}. As $W$ is constructed
	from $B$ and $B_T$, we have
	$\sigma(B_T)\vee\mathcal F_t^W
	\subseteq\mathcal G_t$.
	The reverse inclusion follows from
	\eqref{eq:recovery}. The identity at $T$
	follows by continuity and completion.
	
	
	It remains to establish the predictable
	representation property.\footnote{
		For related results on martingale representation
		under initial enlargement of filtrations,
		see \cite{Amendinger200}.
	} By the filtration identity,
	$\mathcal G_T=\sigma(B_T)\vee\mathcal F_T^W$.
	Since $W$ and $B_T$ are independent,
	finite linear combinations of random
	variables of the form
	$\xi=h(B_T)\eta$, with bounded measurable
	$h$ and $\eta\in L^2(\mathcal F_T^W)$,
	are dense in $L^2(\mathcal G_T)$.
	
	By the classical Brownian martingale
	representation theorem, we may write
	$\eta=\mathbb E[\eta]+\int_0^T H_s^\eta\,dW_s$,
	where $H^\eta$ is $\mathcal F^W$-predictable
	and square-integrable. Thus
	\[
	\xi=\mathbb E[\xi\mid B_T]
	+\int_0^T h(B_T)H_s^\eta\,dW_s.
	\]
	The integrand is $\mathbb G$-predictable and
	belongs to $\mathcal H^2(\mathbb G)$.
	
	For an arbitrary $\xi\in L^2(\mathcal G_T)$,
	choose a sequence of such finite linear
	combinations converging to $\xi$ in $L^2$.
	The conditional expectation is an
	orthogonal projection in $L^2$, and
	the It\^o isometry shows that the
	corresponding integrands form a Cauchy
	sequence in $\mathcal H^2(\mathbb G)$. Passing to the
	limit gives \eqref{eq:PRP}.
	
	Finally, if two integrands yield the
	same representation, the It\^o isometry
	implies that their difference vanishes
	in $\mathcal H^2(\mathbb G)$. This proves uniqueness.

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
By the integrability assumption and Tonelli's
	theorem, the data $(\xi^x,g^x)$ satisfy the
	standard Lipschitz BSDE hypotheses for
	$\mu$-almost every $x$. Hence, the corresponding
	Brownian BSDE admits a unique solution
	$(Y^x,Z^x)$.
	

To construct a jointly measurable family, we use
Picard iteration on the product space introduced
above. Starting from
$(Y^{x,0},Z^{x,0})=(0,0)$, define, for $n\geq 0$,
\[
Y_t^{x,n+1}
=\xi^x+\int_t^T
g^x(s,Y_s^{x,n},Z_s^{x,n})\,ds
-\int_t^T Z_s^{x,n+1}\,dW_s.
\]
More precisely, setting
\[
\eta^{x,n}
=\xi^x+\int_0^T
g^x(s,Y_s^{x,n},Z_s^{x,n})\,ds,
\]

gives
$\mathbb E_W[\eta^{x,n}\mid\mathcal F_t^W]
=\mathbb E_W[\eta^{x,n}]
+\int_0^t Z_s^{x,n+1}\,dW_s$,

\[
Y_t^{x,n+1}
=\mathbb E_W[\eta^{x,n}\mid\mathcal F_t^W]
-\int_0^t
g^x(s,Y_s^{x,n},Z_s^{x,n})\,ds.
\]

The joint measurability of the data ensures

jointly measurable in $(x,t,\omega_W)$.
The corresponding martingale integrands can
also be chosen jointly predictable by the
parametrized martingale representation
argument, using approximation by elementary
predictable processes and the It\^o isometry.

Since the Lipschitz constant is independent
of $x$, the standard BSDE estimates yield
convergence of the Picard iterates in the
integrated norm
\[
\int_{\mathbb R}\mathbb E_W\left[
\sup_{0\le t\le T}|Y_t^{x,n}-Y_t^x|^2
+\int_0^T|Z_s^{x,n}-Z_s^x|^2\,ds
\right]\mu(dx)\longrightarrow 0.

	\int_{\mathbb R}\mathbb E_W\int_0^T

	Y_t=B_1-\int_t^1Z_s\alpha_s\,ds
	+K_1-K_t-\int_t^1Z_s\,dW_s.
	\]
	
	A first solution is
	\[
	Y_t^1=B_t+c(1-t),\qquad
	Z_t^1=1,\qquad K_t^1=ct.
	\]
	Indeed, $dY_t^1=dB_t-c\,dt
	=Z_t^1\,dB_t-dK_t^1$, and
	$Y^1=L$, so the Skorokhod minimality
	condition holds.
	
	A second solution is given by
	\[
	Y_t^2=B_t+(B_t-B_1)^2+1-t,\qquad
	Z_t^2=1+2(B_t-B_1),\qquad K_t^2=0.
	\]
	By It\^o's formula,
	$dY_t^2=Z_t^2\,dB_t$ and $Y_1^2=B_1$.
	Moreover,
	\[
	Y_t^2-L_t
	=(B_t-B_1)^2+(1-c)(1-t)\ge0.
	\]
	Thus the obstacle and minimality
	conditions are satisfied.
	
	Using $dB_t=dW_t+\alpha_t\,dt$,
	both triples satisfy the transferred
	reflected equation. They belong to the
	usual square-integrable solution spaces,
	and their drift integrals are absolutely
	convergent, since the corresponding
	integrands have bounded trajectories
	and $\int_0^1|\alpha_s|\,ds<\infty$
	almost surely.
	
	Since $K_t^1=ct$ whereas $K_t^2=0$,
	the two solutions are distinct.
	Hence nonuniqueness may occur even
	when the reflection mechanism is
	active for one of the solutions.

\label{rem:pathwise}
	The conditional comparison
	\eqref{eq:value} does not imply
	$S_t^{\mathbb G}\ge S_t^{\mathbb F}$ almost surely.

	$\mathbb E[B_1\mid\mathcal F_{1/2}]=B_{1/2}$,
	the tower property gives
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		\mathbb E[\tau B_\tau]
		=\frac12\mathbb E[B_{1/2}
		\mathbf1_{\{B_{1/2}<0\}}]
		+\mathbb E[B_1\mathbf1_{\{B_{1/2}\ge0\}}]
		&=\frac12\mathbb E[B_{1/2}
		\mathbf1_{\{B_{1/2}<0\}}]
		+\mathbb E[B_{1/2}
		\mathbf1_{\{B_{1/2}\ge0\}}]\\
		&=\frac12\mathbb E[B_{1/2}^+]
		=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}>0.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Consequently,
	$S_0^{\mathbb F}\ge\mathbb E[\tau B_\tau]>0$.
	
	


Conditionally on $B_1=x$, we may write
$B_t=tx+\beta_t$, where
$\beta_t=B_t-tB_1$ is a centered Brownian
bridge independent of $B_1$.
The corresponding conditional optimal
stopping value is
$$
v(x)=
\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal T_{0,1}^{\beta}}
\mathbb E_\beta\left[\tau^2x+\tau\beta_\tau\right],
$$
where $\mathcal T_{0,1}^{\beta}$ denotes the
stopping times of the natural filtration
of $\beta$ taking values in $[0,1]$.
In particular, $S_0^{\mathbb G}=v(B_1)$
almost surely.

For $x<0$, we have
\[
0\le v(x)\le
\mathbb E_\beta\left[
\sup_{0\le t\le1}
(t^2x+t\beta_t)^+
\right],
\]
where $\mathbb E_\beta$ denotes expectation
with respect to the law of $\beta$.

To study the limit as $x\to-\infty$,
fix a continuous trajectory of $\beta$.
For every $\varepsilon>0$, continuity
at $0$ and $\beta_0=0$ yield
$\delta>0$ such that
$|\beta_t|<\varepsilon$ for $t\le\delta$.
Since $x<0$, we have
$(t^2x+t\beta_t)^+\le\varepsilon$
on $[0,\delta]$.
On $[\delta,1]$,
$$
t^2x+t\beta_t
\le\delta^2x+\sup_{0\le s\le1}|\beta_s|,
$$
which is negative for sufficiently
large $|x|$. Therefore,
$\sup_{0\le t\le1}(t^2x+t\beta_t)^+
\to0$ almost surely.

Moreover, for $x<0$, this supremum
is bounded by
$\sup_{0\le t\le1}|\beta_t|$,
which is integrable.
By dominated convergence,
$v(x)\to0$ as $x\to-\infty$.

	$\{B_1<-M\}$, an event of positive
	probability. Thus pathwise domination
	may fail, even though the conditional
	inequality \eqref{eq:value} always holds.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:endpoint}
	Let $h:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be Borel
	measurable with $h(B_T)\in L^2$.
	For the zero-driver BSDEs, the solutions
	in $\mathbb F$ and $\mathbb G$ satisfy
	\[
	Y_t^{\mathbb F}=\mathbb E[h(B_T)\mid\mathcal F_t],
	\qquad
	Y_t^{\mathbb G}=h(B_T),\qquad Z_t^{\mathbb G}=0.
	\]
	Consequently,
	\[
	\mathbb E[Y_t^{\mathbb G}\mid\mathcal F_t]=Y_t^{\mathbb F},
	\qquad 0\le t\le T.
	\]
	If $h\in C_b^1(\mathbb R)$,
	$h\in C_b^1(\mathbb R)$\footnote{
		$C_b^1(\mathbb R)$ denotes the space of
		continuously differentiable functions
		$h:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ such that both
		$h$ and its derivative $h'$ are bounded.
	}
	 then
	\[
	Y_t^{\mathbb F}=P_{T-t}h(B_t),
	\qquad
	Z_t^{\mathbb F}=\partial_xP_{T-t}h(B_t),
	\]
	where $P_sh(x)=\mathbb E[h(x+\sqrt{s}N)]$
	is the heat semigroup, with
	$N\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
We first consider the BSDE in the original
	filtration $\mathbb F$:
	\[
	Y_t^{\mathbb F}
	=h(B_T)-\int_t^T Z_s^{\mathbb F}\,dB_s.
	\]
	Since $h(B_T)\in L^2(\mathcal F_T)$,
	the process
	$M_t=\mathbb E[h(B_T)\mid\mathcal F_t]$
	is a square-integrable $\mathbb F$-martingale.
	By the martingale representation theorem
	for Brownian filtrations (see, e.g.,
	Karatzas and Shreve~\cite{KaratzasShreve1991}),
	there exists a unique
	$Z^{\mathbb F}\in\mathcal H^2(\mathbb F)$ such that
	$$
	M_t=\mathbb E[h(B_T)]
	+\int_0^t Z_s^{\mathbb F}\,dB_s.
	$$
	Evaluating this identity at $T$ and
	subtracting the expression at time $t$
	gives
	$M_t=h(B_T)-\int_t^T Z_s^{\mathbb F}\,dB_s$.
	Thus $(M,Z^{\mathbb F})$ solves the zero-driver
	BSDE, and uniqueness yields
	$Y_t^{\mathbb F}=\mathbb E[h(B_T)\mid\mathcal F_t]$.
	
	We now consider the enlarged filtration
	$\mathbb G$. Since $B_T$ is $\mathcal G_0$-measurable,
	so is $h(B_T)$. Hence the constant process
	$Y_t^{\mathbb G}=h(B_T)$, together with
	$Z_t^{\mathbb G}=0$, satisfies
	$$
	Y_t^{\mathbb G}
	=h(B_T)-\int_t^T Z_s^{\mathbb G}\,dW_s.
	$$
	This solution is square-integrable.
	Its uniqueness follows from the
	well-posedness of the zero-driver BSDE
	in $\mathbb G$, since $W$ is a $\mathbb G$-Brownian
	motion with the martingale representation
	property (Theorem \ref{thm:PRP}). Consequently, the tower property
	gives
	$\mathbb E[Y_t^{\mathbb G}\mid\mathcal F_t]
	=\mathbb E[h(B_T)\mid\mathcal F_t]=Y_t^{\mathbb F}$.
	
	Finally, suppose that
	$h\in C_b^1(\mathbb R)$.
	Since $B_T=B_t+(B_T-B_t)$ and the
	increment $B_T-B_t$ is independent
	of $\mathcal F_t$ with distribution
	$\mathcal N(0,T-t)$, we obtain
	\[
	Y_t^{\mathbb F}
	=\mathbb E[h(B_t+\sqrt{T-t}\,N)\mid\mathcal F_t]
	=P_{T-t}h(B_t),
	\]
	where $N\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ is independent
	of $\mathcal F_t$ and
	$P_sh(x)=\mathbb E[h(x+\sqrt{s}\,N)]$.
	
	
	Set $u(t,x)=P_{T-t}h(x)$.
	By the classical heat-semigroup representation
	(see Karatzas and Shreve
	\cite[Sections 4.3 \& 5.7.B]{KaratzasShreve1991}),
	$u\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times\mathbb R)$
	and satisfies
	\[
	\partial_tu(t,x)
	+\frac12\partial_{xx}u(t,x)=0,
	\qquad t<T,
	\]
	with terminal condition $u(T,x)=h(x)$.
	
	
	Applying It\^o's formula to $u(t,B_t)$,
	and using $d\langle B\rangle_t=dt$, we obtain
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		dY_t^{\mathbb F}
		=du(t,B_t)
		&=\left(\partial_tu(t,B_t)
		+\frac12\partial_{xx}u(t,B_t)\right)dt
		+\partial_xu(t,B_t)\,dB_t\\
		&=\partial_xu(t,B_t)\,dB_t,
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	where the last equality follows from
	the backward heat equation
	$\partial_tu+\frac12\partial_{xx}u=0$.
	
	Moreover, since $h'$ is bounded,
	differentiation under the expectation
	gives
	$\partial_xP_{T-t}h=P_{T-t}h'$.
	The resulting integrand is bounded
	and hence belongs to $\mathcal H^2(\mathbb F)$.
	By uniqueness of the Brownian martingale
	representation,
	$$
	Z_t^{\mathbb F}
	=\partial_xP_{T-t}h(B_t)
	=P_{T-t}h'(B_t),
	$$
	for $dt\otimes d\mathbb P$-almost every
	$(t,\omega)$, completing the proof.

<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Since $S$ is an $\mathbb F$-martingale
	and $x\mapsto(x-K)^+$ is convex,
	$X$ is an $\mathbb F$-submartingale.
	The optional sampling theorem
	therefore implies
	\[
	\mathbb E[X_\tau]\leq\mathbb E[X_T],
	\qquad
	\tau\in\mathcal T_{0,T}^{\mathbb F}.
	\]
	Since $\tau=T$ is admissible,
	\[
	V_0^{\mathbb F}=\mathbb E[(S_T-K)^+].
	\]
	
	
	In the enlarged filtration $\mathbb G$, the terminal
	asset price
	\[
	S_T=S_0\exp\left(
	\sigma B_T-\frac12\sigma^2T
	\right)
	\]
	is $\mathcal G_0$-measurable, since
	$\mathcal G_0=\sigma(B_T)$ up to completion.
	Consequently, the informed investor knows
	the terminal payoff $(S_T-K)^+$ at time zero.
	
	Let
	\[
	A=\{(S_T-K)^+<S_0-K\}.
	\]
	Since $S_T$ is $\mathcal G_0$-measurable,
	we have $A\in\mathcal G_0$. Define
	\[
	\tau^*
	=0\,\mathbf 1_A+T\,\mathbf 1_{A^c}.
	\]
	This random time is a $\mathbb G$-stopping time.
	Indeed, for every $0\leq t<T$,
	\[
	\{\tau^*\leq t\}=A\in\mathcal G_0
	\subseteq\mathcal G_t,
	\]
	while $\{\tau^*\leq T\}=\Omega$.
	Thus $\tau^*\in\mathcal T_{0,T}^{\mathbb G}$.
	
	By construction, the investor exercises
	immediately on $A$, when the immediate
	payoff exceeds the terminal payoff, and
	waits until maturity on $A^c$. Therefore,
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		X_{\tau^*}
		&=X_0\mathbf 1_A+X_T\mathbf 1_{A^c}\\
		&=(S_0-K)\mathbf 1_A
		+(S_T-K)^+\mathbf 1_{A^c}\\
		&=\max\{S_0-K,(S_T-K)^+\}.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Since both $S_T$ and $\tau^*$ are
	$\mathcal G_0$-measurable, the payoff
	$X_{\tau^*}$ is also
	$\mathcal G_0$-measurable. Hence
	\[
	\mathbb E[X_{\tau^*}\mid\mathcal G_0]
	=X_{\tau^*}.
	\]
	By the definition of the Snell envelope,
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		V_0^{\mathbb G}
		&=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{
			\tau\in\mathcal T_{0,T}^{\mathbb G}}
		\mathbb E[X_\tau\mid\mathcal G_0]\\
		&\geq
		\mathbb E[X_{\tau^*}\mid\mathcal G_0]\\
		&=\max\{S_0-K,(S_T-K)^+\}.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	
	Taking expectations on both sides yields
	\[
	\mathbb E[V_0^{\mathbb G}]
	\geq
	\mathbb E\left[\max\{S_0-K,(S_T-K)^+\}\right].
	\]
	Using the elementary identity
	$
	\max\{a,b\}=b+(a-b)^+,
	$
	we obtain
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		\mathbb E[V_0^{\mathbb G}]
		&\geq \mathbb E[(S_T-K)^+]\\
		&\quad+
		\mathbb E\left[
		\big(S_0-K-(S_T-K)^+\big)^+
		\right].
	\end{aligned}

	V_0^{\mathbb F}=\mathbb E[(S_T-K)^+],

	\mathbb E[V_0^{\mathbb G}]-V_0^{\mathbb F}
	\geq
	\mathbb E\left[
	\big(S_0-K-(S_T-K)^+\big)^+
	\right].

	C=\{S_T\leq K\}.

	\big(S_0-K-(S_T-K)^+\big)^+
	=S_0-K
	\qquad\text{on }C.
	\]
	As the integrand is nonnegative,
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		&\mathbb E\left[
		\big(S_0-K-(S_T-K)^+\big)^+
		\right]\\
		&\quad\geq
		\mathbb E\left[
		\big(S_0-K-(S_T-K)^+\big)^+

		&\quad=(S_0-K)\mathbb P(S_T\leq K).

	\mathbb E[V_0^{\mathbb G}]-V_0^{\mathbb F}
	\geq

	\[
	\log\left(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\right)
	=\sigma B_T-\frac12\sigma^2T

	\mathbb E[V_0^{\mathbb G}]>V_0^{\mathbb F}.

		={}&S_T
		-\int_t^T Z_s^{\mathbb F}\alpha_s\,ds\\
		&+K_T^{\mathbb F}-K_t^{\mathbb F}
		-\int_t^T Z_s^{\mathbb F}\,dW_s,
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	together with the same obstacle
	and Skorokhod conditions.
	
	
	On the other hand, since $S_T$ is
	$\mathcal G_0$-measurable, the informed
	investor knows the terminal payoff at
	time zero. Consider the random time
	\[
	\tau^*
	=
	\begin{cases}
		0, & S_T<S_0+cT,\\
		T, & S_T\geq S_0+cT.
	\end{cases}
	\]
	Since $\{S_T<S_0+cT\}\in\mathcal G_0$,
	it follows that $\tau^*$ is a
	$\mathbb G$-stopping time. Moreover,
	\[
	X_{\tau^*}
	=
	\max\{S_0+cT,S_T\}.
	\]
	As this payoff is $\mathcal G_0$-measurable,
	the definition of the Snell envelope yields
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		V_0^{\mathbb G}
		&\geq
		\mathbb E[X_{\tau^*}\mid\mathcal G_0]\\
		&=\max\{S_0+cT,S_T\}.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Taking expectations and using
	$V_0^{\mathbb F}=S_0+cT$, we obtain
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		\mathbb E[V_0^{\mathbb G}]-V_0^{\mathbb F}
		&\geq
		\mathbb E[\max\{S_0+cT,S_T\}]
		-(S_0+cT)\\
		&=\mathbb E[(S_T-S_0-cT)^+].
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Finally, since $S_T$ has a lognormal
	distribution with support $(0,\infty)$,
	\[
	\mathbb P(S_T>S_0+cT)>0.
	\]
	Therefore,
	\[
	\mathbb E[(S_T-S_0-cT)^+]>0,
	\]
	which establishes the strict inequality.
	
	
	Finally, on the event
	$A=\{S_T>S_0+cT\}$,
	which has positive probability,
	\[
	V_0^{\mathbb G}
	\geq S_T
	>S_0+cT
	=Y_0^{\mathbb F}.
	\]
	Thus the transferred first
	component cannot coincide
	with the regular Snell envelope
	in $\mathbb G$.

<!-- remark-11: body_md -->
This example distinguishes two
	different operations. The first
	transfers an $\mathbb F$-reflected BSDE
	to the enlarged filtration by
	introducing the singular drift
	term $-z\alpha_t$. The second
	solves a regular zero-driver
	optimal stopping problem in $\mathbb G$.
	
	Although the transferred triple
	preserves the obstacle and
	Skorokhod conditions, it need
	not represent the Snell envelope
	under the enlarged information.
	The two equations have different
	generators, and their solutions
	need not coincide.

<!-- proposition-8: body_md -->
\label{prop:financial_european}
	
	For the regular zero-driver BSDE
	in $\mathbb F$, the solution is
	\[
	Y_t^{\mathbb F}
	=
	S_t\Phi(d_1(t))-K\Phi(d_2(t)),
	\qquad t<T,
	\]
	where $\Phi$ is the standard
	normal distribution function,
	\[
	d_1(t)=
	\frac{\log(S_t/K)
		+\frac12\sigma^2(T-t)}
	{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}},
	\]
	and
	\[
	d_2(t)=d_1(t)-\sigma\sqrt{T-t}.
	\]
	The martingale integrand is
	\[
	Z_t^{\mathbb F}
	=\sigma S_t\Phi(d_1(t)).
	\]
	
	In the enlarged filtration $\mathbb G$,
	the regular zero-driver BSDE
	driven by $W$ has the solution
	\[
	Y_t^{\mathbb G}=\xi,\qquad Z_t^{\mathbb G}=0.
	\]
	Consequently,
	\[
	\mathbb E[Y_t^{\mathbb G}\mid\mathcal F_t]
	=Y_t^{\mathbb F}.
	\]

<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
The conditional distribution
	of $S_T$ given $\mathcal F_t$
	is lognormal. Standard Gaussian
	integration gives
	\[
	\mathbb E[(S_T-K)^+\mid\mathcal F_t]
	=
	S_t\Phi(d_1(t))-K\Phi(d_2(t)).
	\]
	Writing this expression as
	$u(t,S_t)$ and applying
	It\^o's formula yields
	\[
	dY_t^{\mathbb F}
	=
	\sigma S_t\partial_su(t,S_t)\,dB_t.
	\]
	Since
	\[
	\partial_su(t,S_t)=\Phi(d_1(t)),
	\]
	we obtain
	\[
	Z_t^{\mathbb F}
	=\sigma S_t\Phi(d_1(t)).
	\]
	
	In $\mathbb G$, the terminal payoff
	$\xi$ is $\mathcal G_0$-measurable,
	because it is a function of $B_T$.
	Therefore $(\xi,0)$ solves
	the regular zero-driver BSDE
	driven by $W$.
	
	Finally,
	\[
	\mathbb E[Y_t^{\mathbb G}\mid\mathcal F_t]
	=
	\mathbb E[\xi\mid\mathcal F_t]
	=
	Y_t^{\mathbb F}.
	\]

<!-- remark-12: body_md -->
The preceding identity is a
	concrete financial illustration
	of the conditional projection
	result for endpoint-dependent
	payoffs.
	
	In the natural filtration,
	the martingale integrand is
	generally nonzero, whereas
	the payoff is already known
	at time zero in the enlarged
	filtration and the regular
	zero-driver representation
	has a vanishing martingale
	integrand.
	
	These conditional expectations
	should not automatically be
	interpreted as arbitrage-free
	prices or replicating values
	in an insider trading market.
