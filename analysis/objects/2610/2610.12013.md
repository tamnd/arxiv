<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{iweq}
 Let $W$ be a finite-dimensional $L$-representation of the form
 $$W \simeq \bigoplus_{\theta} L(\theta)^{m(\theta)},$$
 where the $\theta: \Gamma \rightarrow 1+ p \mathcal{O}_L$ are distinct continuous characters with multiplicities $m(\theta).$ Regard $W$ as an $R$-module via the induced map $\theta: R \rightarrow L;$ $R$ acts on each summand through its character. Then
    $$\dim_L \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R(Y_R,W) \leq b_Y \sum_{\delta(\theta)=0} m(\theta).$$
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We can write
    $$\operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R(Y_R,W)=\operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R\left( Y_R, \bigoplus L(\theta)^{m(\theta)}\right) \simeq \bigoplus_\theta \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R \left(Y_R, L(\theta) \right)^{m(\theta)}.$$
    Any $\varphi \in \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R(Y_R, L(\theta))$ satisfies, for all $y \in Y_R,$ $\delta(\theta)\varphi(y) = \varphi(\delta y)=\varphi(0)=0,$ meaning $\varphi = 0$ unless $\delta(\theta) = 0,$ in which case $\varphi$ is determined by its values on the $b_Y$ generators, so
    $$\dim_L \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R(Y_R, L(\theta)) \leq b_Y,$$
    and the result follows from summing over the characters.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{warning}
Choosing $A=\langle \chi_1, \dots, \chi_{2g} \rangle$ for the measure construction seems tempting, but the resulting measure can't converge to the Haar probability measure $\mu$ on $A_p.$ The issue is the following. Consider the degree map $\deg: A \rightarrow \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}$ given by $\deg(\chi_i)=1.$ To see this is well-defined, assume $\theta=\prod_i \chi_i^{r_i}=\prod_i \chi_i^{s_i}.$ Let $\gamma_w$ be the image of $\operatorname{Frob}_w \in G_{F_w}$ for some $w \notin S.$ Since $V_L$ is pure of weight $-1,$ $|\chi_i(\gamma_w)|_{\operatorname{\mathbf{C}}}=N(w)^{-1/2}$ for all $i.$ Therefore, $\sum_i r_i = \sum_i s_i,$ and $\deg(\theta)=\sum_i r_i$ is well-defined. Define the measures $$\nu_n=\frac{1}{d_n} \sum_{\theta \in A} m_n(\theta)\delta_\theta.$$
Characters in $L_n$ are products of $n$ degree-one characters, so $m_n(\theta) \neq 0$ implies $\deg(\theta)=n.$ On the $p$-adic completion $A_p$ of $A,$ we can consider the map
$$\overline{\deg}: A_p \xrightarrow{\pi_1} A/A^p \xrightarrow{\deg} \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}/p\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}.$$
For $D=\ker \left(\overline{\deg}\right),$ we have
$$\nu_n(D) = \begin{cases}
    1, \qquad p \mid n \\
    0, \qquad p \nmid n
\end{cases}, \qquad \mu(D)=\frac{1}{p}.$$
This shows that $(\nu_n)_n$ can't weakly converge to $\mu.$ Now, notice that $B = \ker (\deg).$ Indeed, $B \subset \ker (\deg)$ is clear, and given $\prod_i \chi_i^{r_i}$ with $\sum_i r_i = 0,$ we can write
$$\prod_i \chi_i^{r_i} = \chi_1^{\sum_i r_i} \prod_i \left(\chi_1^{-1}\chi_i\right)^{r_i} =\prod_i\left(\chi_1^{-1}\chi_i\right)^{r_i} \in B.$$
This overcomes the obstruction, because $\deg(u_i)=0$ now! Hence for
$$\overline{\deg}: B_p \rightarrow \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}/p\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}$$ one has $\ker (\overline{\deg}) = B_p$ and $\mu_n(\ker(\overline{\deg}))=1=\mu(\ker (\overline{\deg})).$

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{trace}
Let $h \in \mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\operatorname{\mathbf{C}})$ such that $h \neq \pm I, \ h^N=I$ for some $N >0.$ Then $\operatorname{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(h \ | L_{n,\operatorname{\mathbf{C}}} \right) =  o(d_n).$
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $s_e=\operatorname{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(h^e \ | \ V_{\operatorname{\mathbf{C}}} \right).$ Since $h^N=I,$ it is a diagonalizable matrix and its eigenvalues have absolute value $1.$ $h^e$ is symplectic as well, so we can organize its eigenvalues in pairs $\alpha_1, \overline{\alpha_1}, \dots, \alpha_g, \overline{\alpha_g}.$ We then have $\alpha_i + \overline{\alpha_i}=2\mathrm{Re}(\alpha_i) \in [-2,2],$ hence $s_e \in [-2g, \ 2g].$ Given $s \in \mathbb{R},$ let $\alpha, \beta$ be the roots of $x^2-sx+1,$ and let $P_m(s)=\alpha^m+\beta^m.$ The symplectic character formula \cite[Theorem~4.3(ii)]{MSS15} in the form of \cite[Proposition~4.4]{Fil13} gives
\begin{equation}\label{inversion}
    \operatorname{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(h \ | \ L_{n, \operatorname{\mathbf{C}}} \right) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{e \mid n} \mu_{\mathrm{Mob}}(e) P_{n/e}(s_e),
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{\mathrm{Mob}}$ is the Möbius function. If $|s| \leq 2g,$ then $|P_m(s)| \leq 2 \lambda^m.$ In particular, $|s_1| < 2g,$ as equality would make all its eigenvalues equal, and $h \in \lbrace I, -I \rbrace.$ Hence $\rho_h:=\max \lbrace |\alpha|, |\beta|: \alpha, \beta \ \text{roots of } x^2-s_1x+1 \rbrace < \lambda,$ and we conclude
\begin{align*}
    \left| \operatorname{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(h \ | \ L_{n, \operatorname{\mathbf{C}}} \right) \right| \leq \frac{2\rho_h^n}{n}+\frac{2}{n} \sum_{\substack{e\mid n\\ e\geq 2}} \lambda^{n/e} 
     \leq \frac{2\rho_h^n}{n} + 2 \lambda^{n/2} = o(d_n).
\end{align*}
The last step uses $d_n \sim \frac{\lambda^n}{n},$ which is recorded in \cite[p.~123]{Kim09}. This can also be seen by choosing $h=I$ in (\ref{inversion}).

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{conv}
    The measures $\mu_n$ converge weakly to $\mu.$ In particular, for every $k \geq 1$ and $r \in B_p,$ we have, for the projection $\pi_k: B_p \rightarrow B/B^{p^k},$
    $$\mu_n\left(\pi_k^{-1} \left( \pi_k(r)\right) \right) = \frac{1}{d_n} \sum_{\substack{u \in B \\ \pi_k(u)=\pi_k(r)}} m_n(\chi_1^n u) \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} p^{-ak}.$$
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Consider the Pontryagin dual $\widehat{B_p}=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{cts}}\left(B_p, S^1 \right).$ By Weyl's criterion for compact abelian groups \cite[Theorem B.6.3]{Kow21}, it suffices to show, for all $1 \neq \eta \in \widehat{B_p},$ that
    $$\int_{B_p} \eta \  d\mu_n \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0.$$
    Fix one such $\eta.$ Since $\eta(1)=1$ and $\eta$ is continuous, there exists $k \geq 1$ such that $$\eta \left(B_p^{p^k}\right) \subset \lbrace z \in S^1: \mathrm{Re}(z)>0 \rbrace.$$ However, the only such subgroup of $S^1$ is $\lbrace 1 \rbrace,$ so $\eta(x)^{p^k}=1$ for all $x \in B_p.$ Fix $z=\sqrt{\eta\left(\chi_{\mathrm{cyc}} \chi_1^{-2}\right)},$ and define
$$h_\eta=\mathrm{diag}\left(z^{-1}\eta(u_i)\right)_{i=1}^{2g},$$ acting on $V_{\operatorname{\mathbf{C}}}$ with respect to the symplectic CM eigenbasis. It then follows that $h_\eta^{2p^k}=I.$ Paired characters have product $\chi_{\mathrm{cyc}},$ so paired entries of $h_\eta$ have product $1.$ Therefore, $h_\eta \in \mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\operatorname{\mathbf{C}}).$ If $h_\eta$ were a scalar matrix, every $\eta(u_i)$ would be equal to $\eta(u_1)=\eta(1)=1.$ This contradicts $\eta \neq 1,$ because the $u_i$ topologically generate $B_p,$ and $\eta$ is continuous. Therefore, we can apply Lemma \ref{trace} to conclude
\begin{equation}\label{comp}
    \int_{B_p} \eta \ d\mu_n = \frac{1}{d_n} \sum_{u \in B} m_n\left(\chi_1^n u \right) \eta(u) = \frac{z^n}{d_n} \operatorname{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(h_\eta \ | \ L_{n, \operatorname{\mathbf{C}}} \right) \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0.
\end{equation}
For the second equality, we are using (\ref{traceh}) with $\lambda_i=z^{-1}\eta(u_i)$ to conclude that the eigenvalue of $h_\eta$ on $L_{n,\operatorname{\mathbf{C}}}[\chi_1^n u]$ is $z^{-n}\eta(u).$ We have shown weak convergence. 

Finally, the indicator function $\mathrm{1}_D$ on $D=\pi_k^{-1} \left( \pi_k(r)\right)=rB_p^{p^k}$ is constant on each open coset of $B_p^{p^k},$ and they cover $B_p,$ so it is continuous. Weak convergence then gives
$$\mu_n(D)=\int_{B_p} \mathrm{1}_D\ d \mu_n \rightarrow \int_{B_p} \mathrm{1}_D \ d \mu = \mu(D)=p^{-ak}. $$

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{strassman}
    Let $0 \neq h = \sum_{j=0}^\infty a_j t^j \in L \langle t \rangle,$ and let $N$ be the largest index with $|a_N|=\max_j |a_j|.$ Then $h$ has at most $N$ zeros in $\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p.$
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Proof by induction on $N.$ If $N=0$ then $|a_0| > \max_{j \geq 1}|a_j|,$  so $|h(t)|=|a_0|>0$ for all $t \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p.$ If $N \geq 1$ and $u \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p$ is a zero, then $h(t)= h(t)-h(u)=(t-u) \sum_{j=0}^\infty b_j t^j$ with $b_j = \sum_{k > j} a_k u^{k-j-1}.$ Hence $b_j \to 0, \ |b_j| \leq \max_{k > j} |a_k|,$ and $b_{N-1}= a_N + \sum_{k > N} a_k u^{k-N}$ satisfies $|b_{N-1}|=|a_N|,$ so $N-1$ is the largest index at which the maximum of the $|b_j|$ is attained, and the quotient has at most $N-1$ zeros by induction, so $h$ has at most $N$ zeros.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{slice}
    Let $0 \neq f \in L\langle x_1, \dots, x_a,t \rangle,$ and let $(\mu_n)_n$ be a sequence of probability measures on $\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a$ that, for every fixed $k,$ $\mu_n(x_0+p^k\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a) \longrightarrow \mu(x_0+p^k\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a)$ for all $x_0 \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a$. Then,
    $$\mu_n \lbrace x \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a: f(x,n)=0 \rbrace \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0.$$
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Write $f=\sum_{\alpha} c_{\alpha}(t) x^\alpha$ with $c_\alpha \in L\langle t \rangle,$ and fix $\alpha_0$ with $c_{\alpha_0} \neq 0.$ If $f(\cdot, t_*)$ is identically zero for some $t_* \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p$, then $c_{\alpha_0}(t_*)=0,$ so by Strassmann's theorem there are only finitely many such $t_*$, denote them by $t_1, \dots, t_m.$ $c_{\alpha_0}$ vanishes at each $t_i$ with finite multiplicity at least $e_i=\min_{\alpha:\,c_\alpha\not\equiv 0}
       \operatorname{ord}_{t=t_i} c_\alpha(t).$ Then, as in the proof of Lemma \ref{strassman}, $f=(t-t_1) f_1,$ with $f_1 \in L\langle x_1, \dots, x_a, t \rangle.$ After doing this for all $i=1, \dots, m,$ we can write
    $$f=P(t)g, \ \ \text{where} \ \ P = \prod_{i=1}^m (t-t_i)^{e_i} \in L[t] \ \backslash \ \lbrace 0 \rbrace, \ \ g \in L \langle x_1, \dots, x_a, t \rangle.$$ By construction, there isn't any $t_* \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p$ such that $g( \cdot, t_*)$  identically vanishes now. Since $P$ has finitely many zeros, it suffices to prove the result for $g.$ 
    
    Assume the result is false for $g.$ In particular, there exist $\varepsilon > 0$ and infinitely many $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $\mu_n \lbrace g(\cdot, n)=0 \rbrace \geq \varepsilon.$ Since $\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p$ is compact, we can find a convergent subsequence $(n_i)_i,$ and we denote its limit by $t_0 \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p.$ The zero set $Z_0(g)= \lbrace x \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a: g(x,t_0)=0 \rbrace$ is compact and $\mu(Z_0(g))=0$ by Lemma \ref{fubini}. Therefore, there exists an open set $Z_0(g) \subset U$ with $\mu(U) < \varepsilon/2.$ Express $U$ as a union of basis opens: $$U=\bigcup_{\alpha } x_\alpha + p^{k_\alpha} \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a.$$ Since $Z_0(g)$ is compact, we can choose a finite subcover. Moreover, two $p$-adic balls are either nested or disjoint, so we can remove the redundant ones, and get a disjoint open subcover
    $$Z_0(g) \subset \bigsqcup_{j=1}^n x_j + p^{k_j} \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a=:U_0.$$ Since $\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a \backslash U_0$ is also compact, the continuous function $|g(x, t_0)|$ attains a minimum $\eta$ there, which is positive by construction since $Z_0(g) \subset U_0.$ $g$ is continuous on the compact $\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a \times \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p,$ hence uniformly continuous. This means that there exists $i_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $|g(x, n_i) - g(x,t_0)| < \eta$ for all $x$ and $i \geq i_0.$ Every zero of $g(\cdot, n_i)$ lies in $U_0,$ because otherwise $|g(x,t_0)| < \eta$ at some point in $\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a \backslash U_0,$ but we were assuming $\eta$ to be the minimum, and that's a contradiction. 
    
    We conclude $\mu_{n_i} \lbrace g(\cdot, n_i) =0 \rbrace \leq \mu_{n_i}(U_0)$ for all $i \geq i_0.$ The hypothesis about $\mu_n$ in the statement implies $\mu_{n_i}(U_0) \rightarrow \mu(U_0) < \varepsilon/2$ because $U_0$ is a disjoint union of such sets. This is a contradiction because $\mu_{n_i} \lbrace g(\cdot, n_i)=0 \rbrace \geq \varepsilon$ by assumption.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{rank}
    The matrix $\left(\log \chi_i(\gamma_j)\right)_{j,i} \in M_{c,2g}(L)$ has rank $c.$
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
$\lbrace \log \gamma_1, \dots, \log \gamma_{c} \rbrace$ is a $\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p$-basis of $\log \Gamma,$ so it is linearly independent over $\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p$ in $K_p$, hence over $L$ after the base change $K_p \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L \simeq L^{2g},$ where the image of $\log \gamma_j$ is 
    $$(\iota_i(\log \gamma_j))_i=(\log \chi_i(\gamma_j))_i.$$
    This equality follows from the fact that the $p$-adic logarithm commutes with continuous ring homomorphisms. These are the rows of the matrix, so  $\mathrm{rank}_L\left(\log \chi_i(\gamma_j)\right)_{j,i}=c.$

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{nonzero}
    $f \in L \langle x_1, \dots, x_a, t \rangle$ and it is non-zero.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We first prove it is restricted.  For $1 \leq j \leq c,$ let
    $$\ell_j(x,t):=-t\log \chi_1(\gamma_j)-\sum_{i=1}^a x_i \log m_i(\gamma_j), \qquad c_j:= \chi_{\mathrm{cyc}}(\gamma_j).$$ The coefficients of $\ell_j$ lie in $p \mathcal{O}_L$ and $c_j \in 1+ p \mathcal{O}_L,$ so 
    $$s_j(x,t):=c_j \exp(\ell_j(x,t))-1$$ is a restricted power series with $\lVert s_j \rVert \leq |p| < 1.$
    Write $\delta \in R$ as $\delta=\sum_\beta d_\beta T^\beta,$ with bounded coefficients. Then
    $$\lVert d_\beta s^\beta \rVert \leq \lVert \delta \rVert |p|^{|\beta|} \to 0,$$ and $f=\sum_\beta d_\beta s^\beta \in L\langle x_1, \dots, x_a,t \rangle.$ 

    Let us now prove that $f \neq 0.$ Consider the automorphism 
    $$\tau: R \rightarrow R, \qquad T_j \mapsto c_j(1+T_j)-1,$$ whose inverse replaces $c_j$ by $c_j^{-1}.$ A direct substitution shows $$f(x,t)=\delta(\theta_{x,t})=\tau(\delta)(\exp(\ell_1(x,t))-1, \dots, \exp(\ell_c(x,t))-1).$$
    By Lemma \ref{rank}, $(\log \chi_i(\gamma_j))_{j,i}$ has rank $c.$ Since $\langle \chi_1, m_1, \dots, m_a \rangle_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}} = \langle \chi_1, \dots, \chi_{2g} \rangle_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}},$ their logarithms span the same $L$-vector space, and the linear map
    $$(x,t) \mapsto (\ell_j(x,t))_j$$ has rank $c.$ Choose a right inverse linear map $z \mapsto (x(z), t(z)).$ Writing everything in terms of power series, we get
    $$f(x(z), t(z))=\tau(\delta)(\exp(z_1)-1, \dots, \exp(z_c)-1).$$ Since $$L\llbracket T_1, \dots, T_c \rrbracket \rightarrow L \llbracket z_1, \dots, z_c \rrbracket, \qquad T_j \mapsto \exp(z_j)-1$$ is an isomorphism (with inverse $z_j \mapsto \log(1+T_j)),$ it follows that $f(x,t) \neq 0$ because $\tau(\delta) \neq 0.$ (Recall that $0 \neq \delta \in R.)$

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{estimate}
   We have
    $$\frac{\dim_L \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R\left(Y_R, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L\right)}{d_n} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0.$$
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
By Lemma \ref{iweq} applied to the character decomposition of $L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L$,
    \begin{align*}
        \dim_L \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R(Y_R, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L) &\leq b_Y \sum_{u: f(u, n)=0} m_n(\chi_1^n u) \\
        &= b_Y d_n \cdot \mu_n \lbrace x \in \operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p^a: f(x,n)=0 \rbrace.
    \end{align*}
  By Lemma \ref{nonzero}, $ 0 \neq f \in L\langle x_1, \dots, x_a, t \rangle$, so Lemma \ref{slice} gives $\mu_n \lbrace f(\cdot, n)=0 \rbrace \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0,$ and the result follows after dividing by $d_n$ at both sides.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{vanish}
    For every rational prime $\ell,$
    $$H^0(G_\ell, L_n)=0 \ \ \ (n \geq 1), \qquad H^0(G_\ell, L_n^\vee(1))=0 \ \ \ (n \geq 3).$$
    In particular, $H^0(G_T, L_n)=0$ for all $n \geq 1.$
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
If $\ell \neq p,$ potential good reduction of CM abelian varieties gives a finite extension $E/\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_\ell$ over which $J$ has good reduction, making $V_pJ|_{G_E}$ unramified over $E.$ The geometric Frobenius $\operatorname{Frob}_E$ has eigenvalues of complex absolute value $q_E^{-1/2},$ so the quotient $\left(V_pJ|_{G_E}\right)^{\otimes n} \twoheadrightarrow \left(L_{n}\right)|_{G_E}$ is pure of weight $-n.$ Hence $L_{n}^\vee(1)|_{G_E}$ is pure of weight $n-2.$ If we denote the cardinality of the residue field of $E$ by $q_E,$ it is clear that $q_E^{-n/2} < 1$ for all $n \geq 1$ and $q_E^{(n-2)/2} > 1$ for $n \geq 3.$ Then Frobenius doesn't fix any non-zero vector, and both local vanishing results follow. 
    
    At $\ell=p,$ $J$ has good reduction, and the same argument shows that $D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize cris}}}(L_n^\vee(1))$ is pure of weight $n-2$ for crystalline Frobenius, and $D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize cris}}}(L_n)$ pure of weight $-n.$ Both local vanishings follow as above. Finally, notice that 
    $$H^0(G_T, L_n)=L_n^{G_T} \subset L_n^{G_p} = H^0(G_p, L_n)=0.$$

<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{asy}
     For $n \geq 3,$ restriction induces an injection
     \begin{equation*}
         \Sh_{F,S}^1(L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R(Y_R, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L).
     \end{equation*}
     Therefore,
     $$\frac{\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} \Sh_T^1(L_n^\vee(1))}{d_n} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0.$$
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Fix $n \geq 3,$ and let $H=\mathrm{Ker}(G_{F,S} \rightarrow \Gamma)=\operatorname{\mathrm{Gal}}(F_S/F_\infty).$ It acts trivially on $L_n^\vee(1)$ because it fixes both $L_n$ and $\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p(1).$ We first claim that $H^1(\Gamma, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L)=0.$ To see this, choose a place $w \notin S$ and let $\gamma_0 \in \Gamma$ be the image of geometric Frobenius via $G_{F_w} \rightarrow G_F \rightarrow G_{F,S} \rightarrow \Gamma.$ Its eigenvalues on $L_n^\vee(1)$ have absolute value $N(w)^{(n-2)/2} > 1,$ so $\gamma_0-1$ is invertible as an endomorphism of $L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L.$ Given $[c] \in H^1(\Gamma, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L),$ applying the cocycle condition to $c(\gamma \gamma_0)=c(\gamma_0 \gamma)$ (since $\Gamma$ is abelian) and comparing them gives
    $c(\gamma)=(\gamma-1) \cdot \left((\gamma_0-1)^{-1} c(\gamma_0)\right),$ so $c$ is a coboundary and $H^1(\Gamma, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L)=0.$
    
    Inflation-restriction applied to $1 \rightarrow H \rightarrow G_{F,S} \rightarrow \Gamma \rightarrow 1$ then gives an injection
    \begin{equation}\label{inj}
        H^1(G_{F,S}, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L) \hookrightarrow H^1(H, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L)^\Gamma = \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathrm{cts}}(H, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L)^\Gamma.
    \end{equation}
     Here the $\Gamma$ superscript means $\Gamma$-equivariant. For the equality, we are using that $H$ acts trivially on $L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L,$ so the cocycle condition is equivalent to the maps being group homomorphisms. Given $[c] \in \Sh_{F,S}^1(L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L),$ consider its restriction $\phi=c|_H: H \rightarrow L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L.$ By construction, $\operatorname{\mathrm{loc}}_v([c])=0$ for all $v \in S,$ so there exists $a_v \in L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L$ such that $c(\sigma)=(\sigma-1)a_v$ for all $\sigma \in D_v=\mathrm{Im}(G_{F_v} \rightarrow G_{F,S}).$ Therefore, $\phi$ vanishes on $D_v \cap H$ by trivial action of $H.$ By $\Gamma$-equivariance, $\phi$ also vanishes in all of its conjugate subgroups. Therefore, we claim that the extension $F_S^{\mathrm{Ker} \phi}/F_\infty,$ which is unramified outside of $S$ (because $F_S^{\mathrm{Ker} \phi} \subset F_S$), is completely split above $S.$ Indeed, if $w$ is a place of $F_\infty$ above $ v \in S,$ let $\widetilde{w}$ be a place on $F_S$ above it. Notice that, up to conjugacy, $D_{\widetilde{w}}(F_S/F_\infty) = D_{v} \cap H \subset \mathrm{Ker} \ \phi.$ Write $w^\circ=\widetilde{w}|_{F_S^{\mathrm{Ker} \phi}}.$ Under the projection $H \rightarrow H/{\mathrm{Ker} \  \phi},$ $$D_{w^\circ}\left(F_S^{\mathrm{Ker} \ \phi}/F_\infty \right)=\mathrm{Im}(D_{\widetilde{w}} \rightarrow H/\mathrm{Ker} \ \phi)=1,$$ as we wanted to show. Moreover, $\phi(H)$ is compact and lies in a $\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p$-lattice, so it is an abelian pro-$p$ group. Hence $F_S^{\mathrm{Ker}  \phi} \subset M.$ Recall that $M/F_\infty$ is the maximal abelian pro-$p$ extension everywhere unramified and totally split above $S;$ recall also our notation $Y=\operatorname{\mathrm{Gal}}(M/F_\infty).$ The restriction map
     $$r: H=\operatorname{\mathrm{Gal}}(F_S/F_\infty) \rightarrow Y = \operatorname{\mathrm{Gal}}(M/F_\infty)$$ satisfies $$\mathrm{Ker} \  r = \operatorname{\mathrm{Gal}}(F_S/M) \subset \mathrm{Ker} \ \phi = \operatorname{\mathrm{Gal}}(F_S/F_S^{\ker \phi}).$$  Consequently, $\phi$ factors through $Y,$ and we write $\phi = \bar{\phi} \circ r,$ with $\bar{\phi}: Y \rightarrow L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L.$ Notice that $\bar{\phi}$ is continuous and $\Gamma$-equivariant by (\ref{inj}), hence $\Lambda=\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p\llbracket \Gamma \rrbracket$-linear (because it is a group homomorphism as well). The target is an $R$-module because $R \simeq \Lambda \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Z}}_p} L$, so $\bar{\phi}$ extends uniquely to an $R$-linear map $\bar{\phi}_R: Y_R \rightarrow L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L.$ If $\bar{\phi}_R=0,$ $\bar{\phi}=0,$ so $c|_H=\bar{\phi} \circ r = 0.$ Since (\ref{inj}) is injective, this implies $[c]=0.$ Hence $[c] \mapsto \bar{\phi}_R$ defines an $L$-linear injection
          \begin{equation*}
         \Sh_{F,S}^1(L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{\mathrm{Hom}}_R(Y_R, L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L).
     \end{equation*}
     Now Theorem \ref{estimate} gives
     $$\dim_L \Sh_{F,S}^1(L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p}L)=o(d_n).$$
     Finally, we descend this estimate to $\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}.$ 
     
     Fix $n \geq 3$. Recall that $T'$ is the union of $T$ and the primes ramified in $F/\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}$. At $\ell \in T'\backslash T$ the curve has good reduction and a class in $H^1(G_T,L_n^\vee(1))$ is unramified at $\ell,$ so its localization lies in $H^1_{\mathrm{ur}}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_\ell, L_n^\vee(1)) = L_n^\vee(1)/(\mathrm{Frob}_\ell -1)L_n^\vee(1),$ which is zero because $\mathrm{Frob}_\ell$ has no eigenvalue $1$ on $L_n^\vee(1)$ for $n \geq 3.$ Hence $\Sh_T^1(L_n^\vee(1)) \hookrightarrow \Sh_{T'}^1(L_n^\vee(1)).$ Moreover, the restriction map $\mathrm{Res}: H^1(G_{T'}, L_n^\vee(1)) \rightarrow H^1(G_{F,S}, L_n^\vee(1))$ is injective, because $F_S=\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_{T'}$ gives $G_{F,S}=\operatorname{\mathrm{Gal}}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_{T'}/F) \subset G_{T'},$ hence $\mathrm{Cor} \circ \mathrm{Res} = [F:\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}] \cdot \mathrm{Id}$ (see e.g. \cite[VIII, \S~2.]{Ser79}), which is invertible on a $\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p$-vector space. So we have injections
     $$\Sh_T^1(L_n^\vee(1)) \hookrightarrow \Sh_{T'}^1(L_n^\vee(1)) \hookrightarrow \Sh_{F,S}^1(L_n^\vee(1)).$$ Since $L/\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p$ is finite, we can view cocycles in coordinates, and it follows that $- \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L$ commutes with local and global $H^1.$ It also commutes with kernels by exactness, giving
     $$\Sh_T^1(L_n^\vee(1)) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L \simeq \Sh_T^1(L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L) \hookrightarrow \Sh_{F,S}^1(L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L).$$ We conclude that
     $$\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} \Sh_T^1(L_n^\vee(1)) \leq \dim_L \Sh_{F,S}^1(L_n^\vee(1) \otimes_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} L) = o(d_n).$$

For $n \geq 3$ and $\ell \in T,$ local Tate duality and Lemma \ref{vanish} give
$$H^2(G_\ell, L_n) \simeq H^0(G_\ell, L_n^\vee(1))^\vee=0.$$
It follows that $\mathrm{Ker} \left( H^2(G_T, L_n) \rightarrow \bigoplus_{v \in T} H^2(G_v, L_n) \right)=H^2(G_T, L_n).$ As in the proof of \cite[Theorem~0.1]{CK10}, Poitou-Tate duality \cite[Theorem 4.10]{Mil86} consequently gives
$$H^2(G_T, L_n) \simeq \left(\Sh_T^1(L_n^\vee(1))\right)^\vee.$$ By Proposition \ref{asy}, it follows that $h^2(G_T, L_n)=o(d_n).$


Choose a basis of $V_pJ$ where the Weil pairing's matrix form is $$\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}
    0 & I_{g} \\
    -I_{g} & 0
\end{pmatrix},$$ and let $C$ be the matrix of complex conjugation, denoted by $c,$ in that basis. As before, we write $(C \ | \ L_n)$ for the induced matrix. Lemma \ref{vanish} combined with the global Euler characteristic formula \cite[Lemma~2]{Jan89} reads
\begin{align*}
    h^1(G_T, L_n) = h^2(G_T, L_n) + \dim L_n^{c=-1} =h^2(G_T, L_n)+ \frac{d_n-\operatorname{\mathrm{Tr}}(C \ | \ L_n)}{2}.
\end{align*}
We have $C^T \Omega C = -\Omega$ and $C^2=I,$ so after extending scalars to $\operatorname{\mathbf{C}},$ $h_c=-iC \in \mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\operatorname{\mathbf{C}})$ with $h_c \neq \pm I, \ h_c^4=I,$ and Lemma \ref{trace} gives
$$\operatorname{\mathrm{Tr}}(C \ | \ L_n)=i^n\operatorname{\mathrm{Tr}}(h_c \ | \ L_{n,\operatorname{\mathbf{C}}})=o(d_n).$$ We conclude $h^1(G_T, L_n)=\frac{1}{2}d_n + o(d_n).$

\label{thm}
    When $n \to \infty$,
    \begin{align*}
        \dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} H^1_f(G_T, U_n) &\leq \frac{1}{2} D_n + o(D_n), \\
        \dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}/F^0 &= D_n - o(D_n).
    \end{align*}
    In particular, for all $n \gg 0,$
    $$\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} H^1_f(G_T, U_n) < \dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}/F^0, \qquad \limsup_{n \to \infty}\frac{\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} H^1_f(G_T, U_n)}{\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}/F^0} \leq \frac{1}{2}.$$

<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
By \cite[Corollary~2.2.7]{Bet23} and the proof of \cite[Proposition~3.2.4]{Bet23} respectively, there are closed immersions of schemes
$$H^1(G_T, U_n) \hookrightarrow \prod_{j=1}^n H^1(G_T, L_j), \qquad H^1_f(G_T, U_n) \hookrightarrow H^1(G_T, U_n).$$ Proposition \ref{odn} hence gives the global estimate
$$\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} H^1_f(G_T, U_n) \leq \sum_{j=1}^n h^1(G_T, L_j) = \frac{1}{2} D_n + o(D_n). $$
On the local side, first notice that the group schemes $U_n, \ U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}$ and $F^0 U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}$ are smooth because they are of finite type over $\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p,$ hence they have the same $\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p$-dimension as their Lie algebras (and they are isomorphic as schemes since $U_n$ is unipotent). Moreover, \cite[Theorem~1.8]{Ols11} gives a group isomorphism $D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(U_n) \simeq U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}$ and shows that $U_n$ is crystalline. By \cite[Definition-Lemma~4.14]{Bet24} applied to $B=B_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize cris}}}$, $\mathrm{Lie} \ U_n$ is also crystalline, hence de Rham. The Hodge filtration on $D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(V_pJ) \simeq H^1_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(X_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p})^\vee$ satisfies $\dim F^0D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(V_pJ) = g$ and $F^1 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(V_pJ) =0,$ so \cite[Proposition~6.3.3]{BC09} gives a quotient map $$\left(F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(V_p J)\right)^{\otimes j} = F^0(D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(V_pJ^{\otimes j})) \rightarrow F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(L_j),$$ hence $\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(L_j) \leq g^j.$ Since $d_n \sim \lambda^n/n$ \cite[p.~123]{Kim09}, $\sum_{j=1}^n \dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(L_j) \leq \sum_{j=1}^n g^j = O(g^n)=o(D_n).$ \cite[Corollary~8.13]{Ols16} identifies $F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(U_n) \simeq F^0 U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}},$ and differentiation at the identity gives $\mathrm{Lie}\left(F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(U_n)\right) \simeq \mathrm{Lie}\left(F^0 U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}\right).$ On the other hand, \cite[Lemma~4.13]{Bet24} for $B=B_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}^+$ gives $\mathrm{Lie}\left(F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}} (U_n) \right) \simeq F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(\mathrm{Lie}(U_n)).$ Combining both isomorphisms,  we conclude $$\mathrm{Lie}\left(F^0 U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}\right) \simeq F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}} (\mathrm{Lie} \ U_n).$$
Using \cite[Proposition~6.3.3]{BC09} inductively on $0 \rightarrow L_j \rightarrow \mathrm{Lie} \ U_j \rightarrow \mathrm{Lie} \ U_{j-1} \rightarrow 0$ and applying $F^0$ gives, after putting everything together:
\begin{align*}
    \dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} F^0 U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}} & = \dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} \mathrm{Lie} \left( F^0 U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}\right) = \dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}} (\mathrm{Lie} \ U_n)
     = \sum_{j=1}^n \dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} F^0 D_{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}(L_j)
    = o(D_n).
\end{align*}
Finally, by \cite[Lemma~2.1.11]{Bet23} we have
$$\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}} /F^0U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}} =\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}}-\dim_{\operatorname{\mathbf{Q}}_p} F^0 U_n^{\operatorname{\mbox{\scriptsize dR}}} =  D_n - o(D_n).$$
