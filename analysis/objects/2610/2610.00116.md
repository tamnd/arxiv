<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Let \(R\) be a ring.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item \(R\) has the \emph{annihilator condition}, abbreviated
\emph{(a.c.)}, if for every \(a,b\in R\) there is \(c\in R\) such that
\[
  \operatorname{Ann}_R(a,b)=\operatorname{Ann}_R(c).
\]
\item For \(n\geq 1\), \(R\) has \emph{\((A_n)\)} if every ideal generated
by at most \(n\) elements and contained in \(Z(R)\) has a nonzero
annihilator.
\item \(R\) is a \emph{McCoy ring} if every finitely generated ideal
contained in \(Z(R)\) has a nonzero annihilator.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- question-1: body_md -->
\label{question:main}
Does there exist a reduced ring \(R\) satisfying \emph{(a.c.)} and
\((A_n)\) for some integer \(n\geq2\) that is not McCoy?
<!-- end question-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
There exists a countable reduced ring satisfying \emph{(a.c.)} and
\((A_2)\) but not \((A_3)\).  In particular, this ring is not McCoy.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:threshold}
Every ring satisfying \emph{(a.c.)} and \((A_3)\) is McCoy.
Consequently, for each fixed \(n\geq3\), a ring with \emph{(a.c.)}
satisfies \((A_n)\) if and only if it is McCoy.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let \(R\) satisfy (a.c.) and \((A_3)\).  Suppose that \(R\) is not
McCoy.  Among all finite lists generating an ideal contained in
\(Z(R)\) with zero annihilator, choose one of least length, say
\[
  I=(a_1,\ldots,a_m)\subseteq Z(R),
  \qquad \operatorname{Ann}_R(I)=0.
\]
Such a list exists because \(R\) is not McCoy, and \((A_3)\)
forces \(m\geq4\).  By (a.c.), choose \(t\in R\) with
\(\operatorname{Ann}_R(t)=\operatorname{Ann}_R(a_1,a_2)\), and set
\[
  J=(t,a_3,\ldots,a_m).
\]
Then
\[
  \operatorname{Ann}_R(J)
  =\operatorname{Ann}_R(t)\cap\bigcap_{i=3}^{m}\operatorname{Ann}_R(a_i)
  =\operatorname{Ann}_R(I)=0.
\]

We show that \(J\subseteq Z(R)\).  Given \(v\in J\), write
\(v=rt+q\), where \(r\in R\) and \(q\in(a_3,\ldots,a_m)\).
The three-generated ideal
\[
  K=(a_1,a_2,q)\subseteq I\subseteq Z(R)
\]
has nonzero annihilator by \((A_3)\).  Choose
\(0\neq u\in\operatorname{Ann}_R(K)\).  Then
\(ua_1=ua_2=uq=0\); the choice of \(t\) gives \(ut=0\), and
\[
  uv=r(ut)+uq=0.
\]
Hence \(v\in Z(R)\).  The element \(u\) may depend on \(v\), which
is sufficient to prove \(J\subseteq Z(R)\).  Since \(J\) has zero
annihilator and at most \(m-1\) generators, this contradicts the
minimality of \(m\).  Therefore \(R\) is McCoy.

For any fixed \(n\geq3\), \((A_n)\) implies \((A_3)\), so the same
conclusion holds.  The reverse implication follows directly from the
definition of a McCoy ring.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:dictionary}
The ring \(R\) in \eqref{eq:trace-ring} has the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item \(R\) is reduced, and for every finite tuple \(r_1,\ldots,r_s\in R\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ann-dictionary}
 \operatorname{Ann}_R(r_1,\ldots,r_s)
 =
 \{c\in R:\operatorname{supp}(c)\subseteq\textstyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}Z_\Omega(r_i)\}.
\end{equation}
This annihilator is nonzero exactly when the tuple has a common
zero in \(\Omega\).  Equal common zero sets give equal annihilators,
and an element of \(R\) is regular exactly when it is zero-free on
\(\Omega\).
\item \(R\) satisfies \emph{(a.c.)} if for every \(f,g\in B\) there is
\(t\in B\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:binary-coder}
  Z_X(t)=Z_X(f)\cap Z_X(g).
\end{equation}
\item \(R\) satisfies \((A_2)\) if every \(f,g\in B\) with no common
zero on \(X\) admit \(u,v\in B\) such that \(uf+vg\) is zero-free on \(X\).
\end{enumerate}

A subring of a product of fields is reduced.  Coordinatewise
multiplication gives \eqref{eq:ann-dictionary}.  If a tuple has a common
zero \(\omega\), its annihilator contains the nonzero idempotent
\(\delta_\omega\in D\) that is \(1\) at \(\omega\) and \(0\) elsewhere.
If the common zero set is empty, \eqref{eq:ann-dictionary} makes the
annihilator zero.  The remaining assertions of (a) follow from the
same formula.

For (b), write \(r=\Delta f+d_1\) and \(s=\Delta g+d_2\), where
\(d_1,d_2\in D\), and choose \(t\) as in \eqref{eq:binary-coder}.
Let \(F=\operatorname{supp}(d_1)\cup\operatorname{supp}(d_2)\).  Outside \(F\), \(\Delta t\)
vanishes exactly where both \(r\) and \(s\) vanish.  Choose \(e\in D\),
supported on \(F\), so that
\[
 (\Delta t+e)(\omega)=
 \begin{cases}
  0,&r(\omega)=s(\omega)=0,\\
  1,&\text{otherwise}
 \end{cases}
 \qquad(\omega\in F).
\]
Then \(Z_\Omega(\Delta t+e)=Z_\Omega(r)\cap Z_\Omega(s)\), and (a)
gives \(\operatorname{Ann}_R(\Delta t+e)=\operatorname{Ann}_R(r,s)\).

For (c), we use the contrapositive formulation of \((A_2)\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A2-contrapositive}
 \operatorname{Ann}_R(r,s)=0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 (r,s)\text{ contains a regular element}.
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to the definition because an ideal is not contained
in \(Z(R)\) precisely when it contains a regular element; principal
ideals are covered by taking \(s=0\).

Suppose that \(r=\Delta f+d_1\) and \(s=\Delta g+d_2\) have zero common
annihilator.  By (a), they have no common zero in \(\Omega\).  The
functions \(f,g\) have no common zero on \(X\): such a zero \(p\)
would give a common zero of \(r,s\) at any coordinate of the infinite
fiber \(\{p\}\times\mathbb N\) outside
\(\operatorname{supp}(d_1)\cup\operatorname{supp}(d_2)\).  Choose \(u,v\in B\) so that
\(h=uf+vg\) is zero-free on \(X\).  Then
\[
  w_0=\Delta u\,r+\Delta v\,s=\Delta h+e
  \qquad\text{for some }e\in D.
\]
Its zero set \(E\) is contained in the finite set \(\operatorname{supp}(e)\).
At each \(\omega\in E\), at least one of \(r(\omega),s(\omega)\) is
nonzero.  Define \(c_1,c_2\in D\), supported on \(E\), by
\[
 (c_1(\omega),c_2(\omega))=
 \begin{cases}
  (r(\omega)^{-1},0),&r(\omega)\neq0,\\
  (0,s(\omega)^{-1}),&r(\omega)=0.
 \end{cases}
\]
The element \(w=w_0+c_1r+c_2s\in(r,s)\) is \(1\) on \(E\) and
agrees with \(w_0\) elsewhere.  It is therefore zero-free, hence regular
by (a), proving \eqref{eq:A2-contrapositive}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:elementary-geometry}
The following facts hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item The sets of polynomials in \(A\), ordered pairs in \(A^2\), and
curves through \(o\) are countable.
\item A proper closed subset of an irreducible affine curve is finite,
and an affine curve over \(k\) has infinitely many \(k\)-points.
\item For every \(0\neq a\in A\), infinitely many lines through \(o\)
are not contained in \(V(a)\).
\item If \(f,g\in A\) are nonzero and have greatest common divisor
\(d\), write \(f=df_0\), \(g=dg_0\).  Then
\[
 W(f,g)=V(f_0,g_0)
 \tag{4.1}\label{eq:residual-locus}
\]
is either empty or a finite union of irreducible affine curves.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The field \(\overline{\mathbb Q}\) is countable, hence so are \(A\) and
\(A^2\).  Since \(A\) is Noetherian, every ideal is finitely generated;
there are consequently only countably many ideals and only countably
many curves through \(o\).  This proves (i).

A proper closed subset of an irreducible one-dimensional Noetherian
space has dimension zero and therefore consists of finitely many
closed points.  Noether normalization gives a finite dominant morphism
from any affine curve to \(\mathbb A^1_k\).  Its image is both dense and
closed, hence it is surjective.  Every fiber over a \(k\)-point
contains a closed point, whose residue field is \(k\) because \(k\)
is algebraically closed.  As \(k\) is infinite, the curve has
infinitely many \(k\)-points.  This proves (ii).

Write \(a=\sum_{r=0}^N a_r\), where the \(a_r\) are homogeneous, and
choose \(r\) with \(a_r\neq0\).  Every direction
\([v]\in\mathbb P^2(k)\) satisfying \(a_r(v)\neq0\) determines a
line on which \(a\) is not identically zero.  These directions form a
nonempty Zariski-open subset of \(\mathbb P^2_k\), hence an infinite
set.  This proves (iii).

For (iv), \(A\) is a unique factorization domain and
\(\gcd(f_0,g_0)=1\).  Let \(\mathfrak p\) be a prime minimal over
\((f_0,g_0)\).  Krull's height theorem gives
\(\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak p\leq2\).  Height zero is impossible when
the common zero set is nonempty, because \(A\) is a domain and
\((f_0,g_0)\neq0\).  A height-one prime in the UFD \(A\) is generated
by an irreducible element; if it contained both \(f_0\) and \(g_0\),
that element would be a common divisor.  Thus every minimal prime has
height two.  The irreducible components of \(W(f,g)\) consequently
have dimension \(3-2=1\), and there are finitely many of them.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:thin}
There is a countable set
\[
  X=\{p_1,p_2,\ldots\}\subseteq\mathbb A^3_k\setminus\{o\}
\]
with the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textnormal{(T\arabic*)},ref=T\arabic*]
\item\label{T:dense}
\(X\) is Zariski dense in \(\mathbb A^3_k\).
\item\label{T:thin}
Every curve through \(o\) meets \(X\) in a nonempty finite set.
\item\label{T:pair-hit}
If \(f,g\in\mathfrak m\), then \(f\) and \(g\) have a common zero in \(X\).
\item\label{T:coder}
For every \(f,g\in A\), there is \(t\in A\) such that
\[
  Z_X(t)=Z_X(f)\cap Z_X(g).
  \tag{4.2}\label{eq:T-coder}
\]
\item\label{T:escape}
If \(f,g\in A\) have no common zero on \(X\), there are \(u,v\in A\)
such that \(uf+vg\) is zero-free on \(X\).
\end{enumerate}

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Enumerate the curves through \(o\) without repetition as
\[
 C_1,C_2,\ldots,
\]
enumerate the nonzero polynomials as \(a_1,a_2,\ldots\), and enumerate
all ordered pairs of polynomials as
\[
  (f_1,g_1),(f_2,g_2),\ldots.
  \tag{4.3}\label{eq:enumerations}
\]
For every index \(j\) with \(f_jg_j\neq0\), form the residual locus
\(W_j=W(f_j,g_j)\) from \eqref{eq:residual-locus}.  By
\cref{lem:elementary-geometry}(iv), the components of \(W_j\) that
contain \(o\) occur among finitely many of the \(C_i\).  Let
\(\ell(j)\) be the largest of their indices, with \(\ell(j)=0\) when
there is no such component, and set
\[
  \nu(j)=\max\{j,\ell(j)\}.
  \tag{4.4}\label{eq:activation}
\]
When \(f_jg_j=0\), define \(\nu(j)=j\).
For a fixed \(n\), only finitely many indices satisfy \(\nu(j)=n\),
because \(\nu(j)\geq j\).

We recursively choose \(p_n\in C_n\setminus\{o\}\) and maintain a
finite family \(\mathcal F_n\) of permanently forbidden closed sets.
The induction invariant is
\[
 \text{no member of \(\mathcal F_n\) contains \(C_r\) for any \(r>n\)}.
 \tag{4.5}\label{eq:invariant}
\]
Start with \(\mathcal F_0=\varnothing\).  Assume all choices through
stage \(n-1\) have been made.  On \(C_n\), avoid \(o\), every member
of \(\mathcal F_{n-1}\), and, for each \(j\leq n\) for which
\(C_n\nsubseteq V(a_j)\), avoid \(V(a_j)\).  By
\eqref{eq:invariant}, every member of \(\mathcal F_{n-1}\) meets
\(C_n\) properly; the same holds for each \(V(a_j)\) by the stated
condition \(C_n\nsubseteq V(a_j)\).  There are finitely many of these
sets, and each
intersection is finite by \cref{lem:elementary-geometry}(ii).
Since \(C_n(k)\) is infinite, a point \(p_n\) remains.  Add \(C_n\)
to the forbidden family.  Notice that the \(p_n\) are distinct:
after stage \(i\), every later point avoids \(C_i\), which contains
\(p_i\).

After choosing \(p_n\), we impose the following requirements.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Intersections of zero sets.}
For every \(j\) with \(\nu(j)=n\), do the following.  If
\(f_j=g_j=0\), set \(t_j=0\).  If exactly one is zero,
let \(t_j\) be the other polynomial.  Suppose now that both are
nonzero.  Write
\[
 f_j=d_jf_j',\qquad g_j=d_jg_j',
 \qquad \gcd(f_j',g_j')=1,
\]
and let \(W_j=V(f_j',g_j')\).  The maximal ideals of the distinct
points \(o,p_1,\ldots,p_n\) are pairwise comaximal.  The Chinese
remainder theorem therefore gives a polynomial \(q_j\in A\) such that
\[
  q_j(o)=1,\qquad
  q_j(p_i)=
  \begin{cases}
    0,&p_i\in W_j,\\
    1,&p_i\notin W_j
  \end{cases}
  \quad(1\leq i\leq n).
  \tag{4.6}\label{eq:q-interpolation}
\]
Set \(t_j=d_jq_j\), and add \(W_j\) and \(V(q_j)\) to the forbidden
family.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Linear-combination requirement.}
Consider the single pair \((f_n,g_n)\).  If \(f_n,g_n\in\mathfrak m\), make no
additional choice.  If at least one is outside \(\mathfrak m\), but the two
polynomials already have a common zero among \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\), again
make no additional choice.  Otherwise, at every point of
\(\{o,p_1,\ldots,p_n\}\), at least one of \(f_n,g_n\) is nonzero.
Set \(p_0=o\), and choose scalars \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\ink\) such that
\[
 \alpha_i f_n(p_i)+\beta_i g_n(p_i)=1.
\]
Apply the Chinese remainder theorem separately to the prescribed
values \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_i\).  This produces \(u_n,v_n\in A\)
for which
\[
 h_n=u_nf_n+v_ng_n
 \quad\text{satisfies}\quad
 h_n(o)=h_n(p_1)=\cdots=h_n(p_n)=1.
 \tag{4.7}\label{eq:h-interpolation}
\]
Add \(V(h_n)\) to the forbidden family.

This completes stage \(n\).  We verify the invariant.  The newly
forbidden curve \(C_n\) contains no different curve through \(o\).  The sets
\(V(q_j)\) and \(V(h_n)\) contain no curve through \(o\) because their
defining polynomials take the value \(1\) at \(o\).  If \(W_j\) is
added at stage \(n=\nu(j)\), every curve through \(o\) contained in \(W_j\)
is an irreducible component of \(W_j\), by
\cref{lem:elementary-geometry}(iv), and its index is at most
\(\ell(j)\leq n\).  Thus \(W_j\) contains no curve \(C_r\) with \(r>n\).
This proves \eqref{eq:invariant} and hence completes the recursion.

We now establish the five asserted properties.  Fix \(0\neq a_j\in A\).
By \cref{lem:elementary-geometry}(iii), infinitely many lines through \(o\)
are not contained in \(V(a_j)\).  Their indices in the enumeration of
the \(C_i\) are unbounded.  For every such index \(n\geq j\), the
choice rule gives \(a_j(p_n)\neq0\).  This proves property
\ref{T:dense}.  If \(C=C_j\), then \(p_j\in C\), while every
\(p_i\) with \(i>j\) avoids the permanently forbidden set \(C_j\).
Thus
\[
  \varnothing\neq X\cap C_j\subseteq\{p_1,\ldots,p_j\},
\]
which proves property \ref{T:thin}.

For property \ref{T:pair-hit}, let \(f,g\in\mathfrak m\).  We first show that
some curve through \(o\) is contained in \(V(f,g)\).  Work in the
three-dimensional local domain \(A_\mathfrak m\), and let \(P\) be a prime
minimal over \((f,g)A_\mathfrak m\).  Krull's height theorem gives
\(\operatorname{ht}P\leq2\).  Contract \(P\) to a prime
\(\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak m\) of \(A\); localization preserves its height.
If \(\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak p=2\), then
\(V(\mathfrak p)\) is the required curve through \(o\).  If
\(\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak p=1\), the UFD property gives
\(\mathfrak p=(\pi)\) for an irreducible \(\pi\).  Choose
\(s\in\mathfrak m\setminus\mathfrak p\), and in \(A_\mathfrak m\) choose a prime minimal
over \((\pi,s)A_\mathfrak m\).  Its contraction \(\mathfrak q\) is contained in
\(\mathfrak m\) and strictly contains \(\mathfrak p\), so
\(\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak q\geq2\).  Krull's height theorem applied
to the two generators \(\pi,s\) gives
\(\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak q\leq2\).  Hence
\(\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak q=2\), and
\(V(\mathfrak q)\subseteq V(f,g)\) is a curve through \(o\).  Finally, if
\(\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak p=0\), then
\(\mathfrak p=0\); the injectivity \(A\hookrightarrow A_\mathfrak m\) gives
\(f=g=0\), and \(V(x,y)\) is a curve through \(o\) in \(V(f,g)\).
By property \ref{T:thin}, the resulting curve through \(o\) meets \(X\),
giving a common zero of \(f,g\).  This proves property
\ref{T:pair-hit}.

To prove property \ref{T:coder}, consider the pair \((f_j,g_j)\),
processed at stage \(n=\nu(j)\).  The cases in which a polynomial
is zero are immediate.  In the remaining case,
\[
 Z_X(f_j)\cap Z_X(g_j)
 =Z_X(d_j)\cup\bigl(X\cap W_j\bigr).
 \tag{4.8}\label{eq:gcd-zero}
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:q-interpolation} gives
\(q_j(p_i)=0\) exactly when \(p_i\in W_j\) for \(i\leq n\).
Every later point avoids both \(W_j\) and \(V(q_j)\).  Consequently
\[
  Z_X(q_j)=X\cap W_j.
\]
Since \(t_j=d_jq_j\), \eqref{eq:gcd-zero} gives
\(Z_X(t_j)=Z_X(f_j)\cap Z_X(g_j)\), as required.

Finally, suppose \(f_j,g_j\) have no common zero on \(X\).
Property \ref{T:pair-hit} shows that they cannot both belong to \(\mathfrak m\).
They had no common zero among the points present at stage \(j\), so
the recursion constructed \(h_j=u_jf_j+v_jg_j\) as in
\eqref{eq:h-interpolation}.  It is nonzero at the points already
chosen, and every later point avoids the permanently forbidden set
\(V(h_j)\).  Thus \(h_j\) is zero-free on \(X\), proving property
\ref{T:escape}.

The ring \(R\) is reduced by \cref{prop:dictionary}(a).
Property \ref{T:coder} and \cref{prop:dictionary}(b) give (a.c.);
property \ref{T:escape} and \cref{prop:dictionary}(c) give \((A_2)\).

Consider the ideal
\[
  I=(\iota(x),\iota(y),\iota(z))R.
\]
Its generators have no common zero in \(\Omega\), since the only common
zero of \(x,y,z\) in \(\mathbb A^3_k\) is \(o\), which does not belong to
\(X\).  Thus \cref{prop:dictionary}(a) gives \(\operatorname{Ann}_R(I)=0\).

For an arbitrary \(q\in I\), write
\[
  q=\iota(x)r_1+\iota(y)r_2+\iota(z)r_3,
  \qquad r_i=\iota(f_i)+d_i,\quad f_i\in A,\ d_i\in D.
\]
Because \(D\) is an ideal, this has the form \(q=\iota(F)+d\), where
\[
  F=xf_1+yf_2+zf_3\in\mathfrak m,\qquad d\in D.
\]
Property \ref{T:pair-hit}, applied to \((F,0)\), gives \(p\in X\)
with \(F(p)=0\).  Some coordinate of the infinite fiber
\(\{p\}\times\mathbb N\) lies outside the finite set \(\operatorname{supp}(d)\), and \(q\)
vanishes there.  By \cref{prop:dictionary}(a), \(q\) is a zero-divisor.
We have proved
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bad-ideal}
  I\subseteq Z(R),\qquad \operatorname{Ann}_R(I)=0.
\end{equation}
This three-generated ideal witnesses the failure of \((A_n)\) for
every \(n\geq3\).  Since \((A_1)\) is automatic and \((A_2)\) holds,
\(R\) satisfies \((A_n)\) exactly for \(n=1,2\), and is not McCoy.

Finally, \(k=\overline{\mathbb Q}\), \(A\), and \(\Omega\) are
countable.  There are countably many finite subsets of \(\Omega\),
and countably many \(k\)-valued functions supported on each.
Hence \(D\), and therefore \(R=\iota(A)+D\), is countable.
