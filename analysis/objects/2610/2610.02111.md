<!-- question-1: body_md -->
Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be arbitrary monic polynomials. What is the discriminant of the composition $f(g(x))$ in terms of their coefficients and exponents?
<!-- end question-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{Discriminant}
Let $F$ be a field and $f(x) \in F[x]$ be a monic polynomial of degree $n$ and  $h(x) = x^m + a x^k + b \in F[x]$, where $\gcd(m, k) = t$, $m = m_1 t$, $k = k_1 t$ and $m - k = t$. Suppose that $f\circ h$ is irreducible over $F$. Then the discriminant of the composition  $f\circ h$ is given by
\[
\mathfrak{D}_{f \circ h} = (-1)^{\,\frac{nm(nm+2k+n-4)}{2}} \, \mathfrak{D}_f^{m} \,f(b)^{k - 1} \, m^{nm} \, f\left(G\left(\frac{-a k_1}{m_1}\right)\right)^t,
\]
where $G(x)=x^{m_1}+ax^{k_1}+b$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- question-2: body_md -->
Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be monic polynomials in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Under what conditions are both $f(x)$ and $f(g(x))$ monogenic?
<!-- end question-2 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:2.1}
    Let $f(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be a monic polynomial of degree $n$ and $h(x) = x^m + a x^{m-1} + b \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be a monic polynomial of degree $m \geq 2$ such that $f(h(x^t))$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.                                     
    Then $f(h(x^t))$ is monogenic if and only if: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item [\textup{(1)}] $f(x)$ is monogenic;
        \item  [\textup{(2)}] $f(b)$ is squarefree whenever $m>2$. Further, if $m=2$ then $p^2 \nmid f(b)$ whenever $p$ divides both $ a f'(b)$ and $\mathfrak{D}_{f(h(x^t))}$;
        \item [\textup{(3)}] $p^2\nmid m^{mn}\,f\left(h\left(\frac{-a(m-1)}{m}\right)\right)$ for every $p\nmid \gcd(a,m)$;
        \item [\textup{(4)}] $p$ does not divide index of $f(x^p+b)$ for every $p\mid \gcd (a, m)$ and $p^2\mid a$;
        \item[\textup{(5)}] For $p\mid \gcd(a,m)$ with $p\mid \mid a$, write $m=p^u \ell$ with $p\nmid \ell$. Then $f(x^{\ell}+b)$ and $\frac{f(x^m+b)-f(x^{\ell}+b)^{p^u}}{p}+\frac{a}{p}x^{m-1}f'(x^m+b)$ are coprime modulo $p$;
        \item [\textup{(6)}] $p$ does not divide index of $f(h(x^p))$ for every $p\mid t$.
    \end{itemize}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
In the above theorem, we assume $m\geq 2$ since the case $m=1$ is immediate. Indeed, when $m=1$, we have $h(x)=x+c\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$, and the monogeneity of $f(x)$ and that of $f(h(x))=f(x+c)$ are equivalent. Further, for $r>1$, the monogeneity of $f(h(x^r))=f(x^r+c)$ can be obtained as a special case of the above theorem by taking $m=r$, $a=0$, $t=1$, and $b=c$.
<!-- end remark-1 -->

However, as the applications over $\mathbb{Q}$ are easy to understand and the proofs in the general setting would be essentially repetitive, we state and prove the below results only over $\mathbb{Q}$.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{analytic}
Let $q$ be a prime. Let $n \ge 2$ and $m \ge 3$ be integers and  $a$ be a nonzero integer such that $q \mid a$ and $\gcd(a,m)=1$. Put $\epsilon = (-1)^{m+1}a^m(m-1)^{m-1}$ and, for $b \in \mathbb{Z}$, define \( h_b(x) = x^m + ax^{m-1} + b \).
Let $f(x) = x^n + c_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \dots + c_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be a monic, $q$-Eisenstein, and monogenic polynomial. Set
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{N}_f(b) = \sum_{j=0}^n c_j(m^m b + \epsilon)^j m^{m(n-j)} \in \mathbb{Z}[b]; \quad R_f(y) = \frac{f(qy)}{q} \cdot \frac{\mathcal{N}_f(qy)}{q} \in \mathbb{Q}[y].
\end{equation*}
Suppose that for every prime $p \le 2n$ such that $p \neq q$ and $p \nmid m$, we have $p^2 \nmid R_f(0)$. If the $abc$-conjecture is true, then
\begin{equation*}
    \#\{1 \le b \le Y : q \mid b \text{ and } f(h_b(x)) \text{ is monogenic}\} \ge (\lambda + o(1))\left(\frac{Y}{q}\right),
\end{equation*}
where $\lambda = \prod_{p}\left(1 - \frac{\omega(p)}{p^2}\right) > 0$ and $\omega(p) = \#\{1 \le \alpha \le p^2 : R_f(\alpha) \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^2) \}$. In particular, $f(x)$ and $\mathcal{F}_b(x) = f(h_b(x))$ are simultaneously monogenic for a positive proportion of the integers $b$.
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
Note that, in the above theorem, the polynomial $\mathcal{F}_b(x)$ obtained after the composition is again monic, $q$-Eisenstein, and monogenic. Therefore, the same construction can be applied repeatedly. More precisely, starting with $\mathcal{F}_{b_0}(x)=f(x)$, one can construct a sequence of integers $b_k$ such that $\mathcal{F}_{b_k}(x)=\mathcal{F}_{b_{k-1}}\bigl(h_{b_k}(x)\bigr)$
and each polynomial $\mathcal{F}_{b_k}(x)$ remains monic, $q$-Eisenstein, and monogenic. This provides a recursive construction of monogenic polynomials of arbitrarily large degree and, consequently, of monogenic number fields of arbitrarily large degree.
<!-- end remark-4 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\cite[Proposition 12.9]{Morandi96}\label{norm-compute} Let $f(x) \in F[x]$ be monic and irreducible polynomial with $\deg(f)=n.$ Let $K=F(\alpha)$ be a field extension of $F$ where $\alpha$ is a root of $f(x)$. Then
	$$ \mathfrak{D}_f=(-1)^{\binom{n}{2}}\,\mathcal{N}_{K/F} (f'(\alpha)).$$
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\cite[Theorem~1.20]{SKM}\label {norm in extension} Let $K/F$ be an extension of degree $n$ and $\alpha$  be an element of $K$ with $[F(\alpha):F]=d$. Then $$ \mathcal{N}_{K/F}(\alpha)=\left( \mathcal{N}_{F(\alpha)/F}(\alpha)\right)^{n/d}. $$
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $\theta$ be a root of $f\circ h$ and $K=F(\theta)$. Using Lemma~\ref{norm-compute}, we have
\begin{align*}
    \mathfrak{D}_{f\circ h } &= (-1)^{nm(nm-1)/2} \, \mathcal{N}_{K/F}\left((f(h(\theta)))'\right) \\
    &= (-1)^{nm(nm-1)/2} \, \mathcal{N}_{K/F} \left(f'(h(\theta))\right) \cdot \mathcal{N}_{K/F} \left(h'(\theta)\right) \quad \textit{(since norm is multiplicative)}.
\end{align*}
Now, applying Lemma~\ref{norm-compute} to $f(x)$ and substituting $h'(\theta) = \theta^{k-1}(m\theta^t + ak)$ into the expression above, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
    \mathfrak{D}_{f\circ h } =(-1)^{\frac{nm(nm-1)+nm(n-1)}{2}}\, \mathfrak{D}_f^{m} \, \mathcal{N}_{K/F}(\theta^{k-1}(m\theta^t + ak)).
\end{equation*}
Using multiplicative property of Norm, we have
\begin{equation}\label{disc f.h}
    \mathfrak{D}_{f\circ h } =(-1)^{\frac{nm(nm+n-2)}{2}} \,\mathfrak{D}_f^m \, \mathcal{N}_{K/F}(\theta)^{k-1}\,\mathcal{N}_{K/F}(m\theta^t + ak).
\end{equation}
Since the minimal polynomial of $\theta$ is $f(h(x))$, it follows that
\begin{equation} \label{norm of theta}
    \mathcal{N}_{K/F}(\theta) = (-1)^{nm} \, f(b).
\end{equation}
Next, we compute $\mathcal{N}_{K/F}(m\theta^t + ak)$. Let us denote $z = m\theta^t + ak$ and  $f(x) = x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1x + a_0$. Since $f(h(\theta)) = 0$, we substitute $\theta^t = \frac{z - ak}{m}$ to obtain
\begin{equation}
    f\left(\left(\frac{z - ak}{m}\right)^{m_1} + a\left(\frac{z - ak}{m}\right)^{k_1} + b\right) = \left(\left(\frac{z - ak}{m}\right)^{m_1} + a\left(\frac{z - ak}{m}\right)^{k_1} + b\right)^n + \cdots + a_0 = 0.
\end{equation}
Therefore, we see that $z$ is a root of the polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{minimal poly for z}
    h_1(x) = \left(G_1(x)\right)^n + a_{n-1} m^{m_1} \left(G_1(x)\right)^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 m^{m_1n},
\end{equation}
where $G_1(x) = (x - ak)^{m_1} + am(x - ak)^{k_1} + bm^{m_1}$.
We now show that $h_1(x)$ is the minimal polynomial of $z$ over $F$.
 It is easy to verify that $F(z)=F(\theta^{t})$. Hence, to establish that the degree of the minimal polynomial of $z$ over $F$ is $m_1n$, it is sufficient to prove that $[F(\theta^t):F]=m_1n$. Now define a polynomial 
\[g(x)=[x^{m_1}+ax^{k_1}+b]^n+a_{n-1}[x^{m_1}+ax^{k_1}+b]^{n-1}+\cdots+ a_1[x^{m_1}+ax^{k_1}+b]+a_0\in F[x].\]
We will show that $g(x)$ is minimal polynomial for $\theta^t$, i.e., $g(x)$ is irreducible and $g(\theta^t)=0$. We have
\[ g(\theta^t)=[(\theta^t)^{m_1}+a(\theta^t)^{k_1}+b]^n+a_{n-1}[(\theta^t)^{m_1}+a(\theta^t)^{k_1}+b]^{n-1}+\cdots+ a_0=f(h(\theta))=0. \]
  Now suppose $g(x)$ is reducible say $g(x)=g_1(x)\, g_2(x)\in F[x]$ with degree $\deg(g_1),~ \deg (g_2) \geq 2$. Clearly $g(x^t)=f(h(x))$. Using this we obtain that $f(h(x))=g_1(x^t)\cdot g_2(x^t)$. This contradicts the irreducibility of $f(h(x)).$ Hence, $g(x)$ is irreducible. This proves that $h_1(x)$ is minimal polynomial for $z$. Thus, 
  \[\mathcal{N}_{K(z)/F}=(-1)^{m_1n}h_1(0).\] Using Lemma \ref{norm in extension}, we get
  \[\mathcal{N}_{K/F}(m\theta^t+ak)=\left((-1)^{m_1n}h_1(0)\right)^t.\]
Substituting value of $h_1(0)$ in above equation, we have
\begin{equation}\label{norm of z}
    \mathcal{N}_{K/F}\,(m\theta^t+ak)=(-1)^{\,mn}\,m^{\,mn}\,f\left(\left(\frac{- ak}{m}\right)^{m_1} + a\left(\frac{- ak}{m}\right)^{k_1} + b\right)^t.
\end{equation}
Therefore, the theorem follows from Lemma~\ref{norm-compute}, together with Equations~\eqref{disc f.h}, \eqref{norm of theta} and~\eqref{norm of z}.

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Let $K$ be a field and let $\Gamma$ be a totally ordered abelian group, written additively. A \emph{Krull valuation} on $K$ is a surjective map
\( v:K^\times\longrightarrow\Gamma \)
satisfying
\begin{enumerate}
\item [\textup{(1)}] $v(xy)=v(x)+v(y)$;
\item [\textup{(2)}] $v(x+y)\ge \min\{v(x),v(y)\}$ whenever $x+y\neq0$.
\end{enumerate}
The valuation is extended to all of $K$ by setting $v(0)=\infty$, where $\infty$ is a symbol satisfying $\gamma<\infty$ and $\gamma+\infty=\infty$ for every $\gamma\in\Gamma$. The ordered abelian group $\Gamma$ is called the \emph{value group} of $v$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- head-1: body_md -->
Throughout this section, $R$ is an integral domain (with unity) and $F$ is the field of fraction of $R$. $v$ denotes a Krull valuation of arbitrary rank on a field $F$, with valuation ring $R_v$, maximal ideal $M_v$, value group $\Gamma_v$ and residue field \( k_v=R_v/M_v. \) For an element $\xi \in R_v$, we write $\overline{\xi}$ for its residue class in $k_v$. If $g(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_i x^i\in R_v[x],$ then $\overline g(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\overline a_i x^i\in k_v[x]$ denotes the polynomial obtained by reducing each coefficient of $g(x)$ modulo $M_v$.
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
Let $v$, $R_v, M_v$ defined as above. Let $G(x)\in R_v[x]$ be a monic irreducible polynomial having a root
$\theta$ in its splitting field, and suppose that
\( \overline{G}(x) = \overline{g}_1(x)^{e_1} \cdots \overline{g}_t(x)^{e_t} \) is the factorization of $\overline{G}(x)$ into powers of distinct irreducible polynomials over $R_v/M_v$, where each $g_i(x)\in R_v[x]$ is monic. Then $R_v[\theta]$ is integrally closed if and only if one of the following holds:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}]
$e_i=1$ for every $1\le i\le t$;

\item[\textup{(ii)}]
There exists an index $1\le j\le t$ such that $e_j>1$.
In this case, $M_v$ is a principal ideal, say
$M_v=(\pi)$ and
$\overline{g}_j(x)$ does not divide
$\overline{Y}(x)$, where
\[ Y(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \Bigl( G(x)-g_1(x)^{e_1}\cdots g_t(x)^{e_t} \Bigr) \in R_v[x]. \]
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{valuation dede}
Let $v$, $R_v$, $M_v$, $\theta$, $G(x)$ and $g_i(x)$ be as in the notations. Then $R_v[\theta]$ is integrally closed if and only if $G(x) \notin \langle M_v, g_i(x)\rangle ^2 \  \text{in } R_v[x] \  \text{for any } i,\ 1 \le i \le t.$
<!-- end theorem-8 -->
<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
Many results on monogenic extensions are established under the assumption that the underlying valuation ring is a Dedekind\footnote{An integral domain $R$ where the localization $R_v$ is a discrete valuation ring (DVR) for valuation $v$ is called a Dedekind or Noetherian Prüfer domain.}. In contrast, our results are formulated for Krull valuations of arbitrary rank, allowing the valuation ring to be non-Noetherian. The only additional hypothesis needed in our arguments is local in nature: whenever the polynomial under consideration has a repeated root modulo $M_v$ and the discriminant lies in $M_v$, we assume that the maximal ideal $M_v$ is principal. This condition is sufficient for the discriminant arguments developed later and extends the classical Dedekind domain framework to a broader class of Pr\"{u}fer domains.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:equivalences}
Let $v$ be a non-trivial Krull valuation of a field $F$ with valuation ring $R_v$. Then the following statements are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $R_v$ is a discrete valuation ring.
\item $R_v$ is Noetherian.
\item $R_v$ is a principal ideal domain.
\item The valuation $v$ has rank one and its value group is isomorphic to $\mathbb Z$.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, whenever any of these conditions hold, the maximal ideal $M_v$ is principal.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{pro3.7}
                                                                                                
Let $f(x) \in R_v[x]$ be a monic irreducible polynomial of degree $n$, and let $K = F(\theta)$ where $\theta$ is a root of $f(x)$. Suppose $k, a \in R_v$ are elements such that $v(k) = 0$. Then, $R_v[\theta]$ is integrally closed if and only if $R_v[k\theta + a]$ is integrally closed.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Set $\alpha=k\theta+a$. Since $k,a\in R_v$, we have $\alpha\in R_v[\theta]$. Hence $R_v[\alpha]\subseteq R_v[\theta]$. Moreover, $v(k)=0$ implies that $k$ is a unit of  $R_v$. Thus $k^{-1}\in R_v$. From $\alpha=k\theta+a$, we obtain $\theta=k^{-1}(\alpha-a)\in R_v[\alpha]$. Consequently, $R_v[\theta]\subseteq R_v[\alpha]$. Therefore, $R_v[\theta]=R_v[\alpha]=R_v[k\theta+a]$.

Hence, the two rings are identical and so $R_v[\theta]$ is integrally closed if and only if $R_v[k\theta+a]$ is integrally closed. This completes the proof.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:ideal_split}
Let $M_v=\langle\pi\rangle$ be a maximal ideal in $R_v$ and $g(x) \in R_v[x]$ be a monic polynomial that is irreducible modulo $M_v$. Then  $\langle M_v, g(x) \rangle^2 = \langle M_v^2, g(x) \rangle \cap \langle M_v, g(x)^2 \rangle.$
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The containment $\langle M_v, g(x) \rangle^2 \subseteq \langle M_v^2, g(x) \rangle \cap \langle M_v, g(x)^2 \rangle$ follows from the definition of the product of ideals. For the reverse inclusion, let $r(x)$ be a polynomial belonging to the intersection $\langle M_v^2, g(x) \rangle \cap \langle M_v, g(x)^2 \rangle$. Then there exist polynomials $a(x), b(x), c(x), d(x) \in R_v[x]$ such that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:lem_ident}
\pi^2 a(x) + g(x) b(x) = r(x) = \pi c(x) + g^2(x) d(x).
\end{equation}
By above relation, we obtain that $\pi(c(x)-\pi a(x))\in\langle g(x)\rangle$. As $\pi\not\in\langle g(x) \rangle$, implies that $c(x)\equiv\pi a(x) \ (\mathrm{mod}\ g(x)) $. Write $c(x)=\pi a(x)+g(x)t(x)$ for some $t(x)\in R_v[x]$. Now substituting this value in second relation of \eqref{eq:lem_ident}, we obtain 
\[r(x)=\pi(\pi a(x)+g(x)t(x))+g^2(x)d(x).\]
Thus, $r(x) \in \langle M_v, g(x) \rangle^2$, which completes the proof.

<!-- theorem-9: body_md -->
\label{Thm:4.1}
 Let $f(x)$, $h(x)$ and $g(x)$ be monic polynomials in $R_v[x]$ such that $g(x)$ is irreducible modulo $M_v=\langle\pi \rangle$. Suppose that $f(x) \in \langle \pi,\, g(x)^2 \rangle$. Then $f(x) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x) \rangle^2$ if and only if $f(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,\,g(h(x)) \rangle^2$.
<!-- end theorem-9 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By hypothesis, $f(x) \in \langle \pi,\, g(x)^2 \rangle$. Since $g(x)$ is monic, we can use the division algorithm in $R_v[x]$ to write  $f(x) = g(x)^2 q(x) + \pi r(x)$ for some $q(x), r(x) \in R_v[x]$.  
Applying the division algorithm again to divide $r(x)$ by $g(x)$, we have  
\[
    r(x) = g(x) s(x) + t(x),
\]
where $\deg t(x) < \deg g(x)$.  
Substituting this expression for $r(x)$ back into our first equation yields  
\begin{equation}\label{eq3.1}
  f(x) = g(x)^2 q(x) + \pi\, g(x) s(x) + \pi \,t(x).  
\end{equation}
Notice that the first two terms on the right-hand side belong to $\langle \pi,\, g(x) \rangle^2$. Therefore, by above equation we have 
\begin{equation} \label{eq3.3}
    f(x) \in \langle \pi,~g(x) \rangle^2 \text{ if and only if } \pi \, t(x) \in \langle \pi,~g(x) \rangle^2.
\end{equation}
                                                                                                       
Now, replacing $x$ with the polynomial $h(x)$ in \eqref{eq3.1}, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
    f(h(x)) = g(h(x))^2 \, q(h(x)) + \pi \, g(h(x)) \, s(h(x)) + \pi\, t(h(x)).
\end{equation*} 
By the same reasoning as for $f(x)$,                                                                           
we deduce
\begin{equation}\label{eq3.4}
    f(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,\,g(h(x)) \rangle^2 \text{ if and only if } \pi\,t(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,\,g(h(x)) \rangle^2.
\end{equation} 
In view of \eqref{eq3.3} and \eqref{eq3.4}, the theorem reduces to showing that
\begin{equation}\label{eq3.5}
    \pi \, t(x) \in \langle \pi,~g(x) \rangle^2 \text{ if and only if } \pi \, t(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,~g(h(x)) \rangle^2.
\end{equation} 
If $\pi \, t(x) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x) \rangle^2$, then substituting $h(x)$  gives $\pi\, t(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,\,g(h(x)) \rangle^2$. To prove the converse, assume that $\pi \, t(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,~g(h(x)) \rangle^2$. This means there exist polynomials, $h_1(x), h_2(x), h_3(x) \in R_v[x]$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eqns:3.6}
    \pi\,t(h(x)) = \pi^2\, h_1(x) + \pi\,g(h(x)) \, h_2(x) + g(h(x))^2\, h_3(x).
\end{equation}
Reducing this equation modulo $M_v$, the terms multiplied by $\pi$ vanish, leaving
\[
    g(h(x))^2\, h_3(x) \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) .
\]
Since $g(x)$ and $h(x)$ are monic, the composition $g(h(x))$ is also monic and thus non-zero modulo $M_v$. Since $(R_v/M_v)[x]$ is an integral domain, it follows that $h_3(x) \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) $. We can therefore write $h_3(x) = \pi h_4(x)$ for some $h_4(x) \in R_v[x]$. Substituting $h_3(x) = \pi h_4(x)$ into \eqref{eqns:3.6} and dividing throughout by $\pi$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
  t(h(x)) &= \pi\, h_1(x) + g(h(x)) \, h_2(x) + g(h(x))^2\, h_4(x),\\  
  &= \pi\, h_1(x) + g(h(x)) \big[h_2(x) + g(h(x))\, h_4(x)\big].
\end{align*}
Reducing this equation modulo $M_v$ yields
\[
    t(h(x)) \equiv g(h(x)) \big[h_2(x) + g(h(x))\, h_4(x)\big] \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) .
\]
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that $h_2(x) + g(h(x))\, h_4(x) \not\equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) $ and for $j \geq 0$, let $b_jx^j$ be its leading non-zero term modulo $M_v$. Then using $g(h(x))$ is monic, right-hand side of the congruence contains a term $b_j x^{\deg(g\circ h)+j}$ which is non-zero modulo $M_v$. But we have $\deg(t) < \deg(g)$ and hence $\deg(t \circ h) < \deg(g \circ h)$, this means that every non-zero term modulo $M_v$ on left-hand side has degree less than $\deg(g \circ h)$, which is a contradiction.
                                                                                            
Thus, we have $ t(h(x)) \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) .$
Finally, we show that this forces $t(x) \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) $. Let $t(x) = a_d x^d + a_{d-1} x^{d-1} + \cdots + a_0$. Evaluating at $h(x)$ gives
\[
    t(h(x)) = a_d h(x)^d + a_{d-1} h(x)^{d-1} + \cdots + a_0 \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) .
\]
Since $h(x)$ is monic, the leading term of this expansion is exactly $a_d x^{d \cdot \deg(h)}$. For $t(h(x))$ to be the zero polynomial modulo $M_v$, its leading coefficient must be zero, so $a_d \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) $. Continuing this process inductively for the remaining coefficients, we see that every coefficient of $t(x)$ must be zero modulo $M_v$. 
Thus, $t(x) \equiv 0 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ M_v) $, which means $t(x) = \pi t_1(x)$ for some polynomial $t_1(x) \in R_v[x]$. Multiplying by $\pi$, we obtain $\pi t(x) = \pi^2 t_1(x)$, .i.e., $\pi t(x)$ lies in the ideal $\langle \pi \rangle^2 \subseteq \langle \pi, g(x) \rangle^2$. This proves the reverse implication of \eqref{eq3.5} which completes the proof of the theorem.

<!-- corollary-4: body_md -->
\label{Cor:4.2}
 Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be monic polynomials in $R_v[x]$ such that $g(x)$ is irreducible modulo $M_v=\langle\pi \rangle$. Suppose that $f(x) \in \langle \pi,\, g(x)^2 \rangle$ and $p$ is a prime such that $v(p)>0$. Then $f(x) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x) \rangle^2 $ if and only if $f(x^p) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x) \rangle^2.$
<!-- end corollary-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Applying Theorem \ref{Thm:4.1} with $h(x)=x^p$ to the polynomial $f(x)$, we have
    \begin{equation*}
        f(x) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x) \rangle^2 \text{ if and only if } f(x^p) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x^p) \rangle^2.
    \end{equation*}
    We wish to point out that the irreducibiliy of $g(x)$ was only required for the above equivalence. The statement proved below follows for any polynomial $g(x)$. To prove the corollary, we only need to show that $f(x^p) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x^p) \rangle^2 \text{ if and only if } f(x^p) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x) \rangle^2$. Let $t(x)$ be the polynomial such that $g(x^p) = g(x)^p + p t(x)$.

    \noindent Now assume, $f(x^p) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x^p) \rangle^2 $, i.e., there exists polynomials $h_1(x), h_2(x), h_3(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ such that
    \begin{align*}
        f(x^p) &= \pi^2h_1(x) + \pi g(x^p) h_2(x) + g(x^p)^2 h_3(x), \\
        &= \pi^2 h_1(x) + \pi g(x)^ph_2(x) + \pi p t(x) + \big[ g(x)^{2p} + p g(x)^p \times 2 + p^2 t(x)^2 \big] h_3(x), \\
        &= \pi^2 h_4(x) + \pi g(x) h_5(x) + g(x)^2 h_6(x)
    \end{align*}
    for some $h_4(x), h_5(x), h_6(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, where the last relation is clubbed using the fact $v(p)>0$. This implies $f(x^p) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x) \rangle^2$. Using similar manipulations, we obtain that other implication. This completes the proof of the corollary.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:5.14}
Let $v$ be a Krull valuation on a field $F$ with valuation ring $R_v$ and its maximal ideal is principal, say $M_v=(\pi)$. Suppose that $\overline{g}(y)$ is an irreducible repeated factor of $\overline{f}(y)$ in $k_v[y]$ and  $\overline{g}_1(x)$ be an irreducible factor of $\overline{g}(\overline{h}(x))$. If \( f(h(x)) \notin \langle \pi,g(h(x)) \rangle^2, \) then \( f(h(x)) \notin \langle \pi,g_1(x) \rangle^2. \)
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We prove the contrapositive. Assume that
$f(h(x))\in\langle\pi,g_1(x)\rangle^2$.
Since $\overline g(y)$ is a repeated factor of $\overline f(y)$, there exists $\overline Q(y)\in k_v[y]$ such that
$\overline f(y)=\overline g(y)^2\overline Q(y)$.
Choosing a lift $Q(y)\in R_v[y]$ of $\overline Q(y)$, we have
$f(y)-g(y)^2Q(y) \in M_v = \langle \pi \rangle$ and hence there exists $A(y)\in R_v[y]$ such that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:lifting}
f(y) = g(y)^2 Q(y) + \pi A(y).
\end{equation}
Substituting $y=h(x)$ into \eqref{eq:lifting} gives
\begin{equation} \label{eq:composition}
f(h(x)) = g(h(x))^2Q(h(x)) + \pi A(h(x)).
\end{equation}
Since $\overline g_1(x)\mid\overline g(\overline h(x))$, we have
$g(h(x))\in\langle\pi,g_1(x)\rangle$ and therefore
$g(h(x))^2\in\langle\pi,g_1(x)\rangle^2$. Hence \eqref{eq:composition} implies
$\pi A(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,g_1(x) \rangle^2$. Thus, there exists polynomials $U(x), V(x), W(x) \in R_v[x]$ satisfying the relation
\[
\pi A(h(x)) =
\pi^2U(x) +\pi g_1(x)V(x) + g_1(x)^2W(x).
\]
Reducing modulo $\pi$, the above equation yields $\overline g_1(x)^2\overline W(x)=0$. Since $k_v[x]$ is an integral domain and $\overline g_1(x) \neq 0$, we obtain $\overline W(x)=0$. So, $W(x)=\pi\widetilde W(x)$ for some $\widetilde W(x)\in R_v[x]$. Cancelling the factor $\pi$ yields
\[
A(h(x)) = \pi U(x)+g_1(x)V(x)+ g_1(x)^2 \widetilde W(x).
\]
This implies that
$A(h(x))\in\langle\pi,g_1(x)\rangle$. Let $\alpha$ be a root of $\overline g_1(x)$ in an algebraic closure of $k_v$ and put $\beta=\overline h(\alpha)$. Since $\overline g_1(x)\mid\overline A(\overline h(x))$, we have $\overline A(\beta)=0$. Moreover, $\overline g_1(x)\mid\overline g(\overline h(x))$ implies $\overline g(\beta)=0$. As $\overline g(y)$ is irreducible over $k_v$, it is the minimal polynomial of $\beta$ and hence $\overline g(y)\mid\overline A(y)$. Therefore,
$A(y)=g(y)B(y)+\pi C(y)$ for some $B(y),C(y)\in R_v[y]$.
Substituting $y=h(x)$ into this identity and then into \eqref{eq:composition}, we obtain
\begin{align}
f(h(x)) &= g(h(x))^2Q(h(x)) + \pi g(h(x))B(h(x)) + \pi^2C(h(x)),
\end{align}
which shows that
$f(h(x))\in\langle\pi,g(h(x))\rangle^2$.
This proves the contrapositive.

<!-- head-2: body_md -->
The significance of Lemma \ref{lem:5.14} is that, when applying Dedekind's criterion to the polynomial $f(h(x))$, one does not need to factor $g(h(x))$ further. More precisely, suppose that if every irreducible factor $g(x)$ of $\overline{f}(x)$ is a repeated factor of $\overline{f}(x)$. Then, in order to ensure that
$R_v[\theta]$ is integrally closed, where $\theta$ is a root of the irreducible polynomial $f(h(x))$, it is enough to verify that 
\( f(h(x))\notin \langle \pi,g(h(x))\rangle^2 \)
for every irreducible factor $g(x)$ of $\overline{f}(x)$. Indeed, Lemma \ref{lem:5.14} shows that this condition automatically implies 
\( f(h(x))\notin \langle \pi,g_1(x)\rangle^2 \)
for every irreducible factor $g_1(x)$ of $\overline{g(h(x))}$. Thus, no further factorization of $g(h(x))$ is required in the application of Dedekind's criterion.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lemma:3.6}
 Assume that the residue field $k_v$ has prime characteristic $p > 0$ and $M_v = \langle \pi \rangle$. Let $f(x)$, $h(x)$ and $g(x)$ be monic polynomials in $R_v[x]$ such that $g(x)$ is irreducible modulo $M_v$. If $f(x) \in \langle \pi,\, g(x)^2 \rangle$, then
$f(x^p) \in \langle \pi,\,g(x) \rangle^2 \,\, \text{if and only if} \,\, f(h(x^p)) \in \langle \pi,\,g(h(x)) \rangle^2.$
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Using the assumption $f(x) \in \langle \pi,\, g(x)^2 \rangle$ by Corollary~\ref{Cor:4.2}, we have $f(x^p) \in \langle \pi,\, g(x) \rangle^2$ if and only if $f(x) \in \langle \pi,\, g(x) \rangle^2$.
By Theorem~\ref{Thm:4.1}, this latter condition holds if and only if $f(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,\, g(h(x)) \rangle^2$. To prove the result, it only remains to show that $f(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,\, g(h(x)) \rangle^2$ if and only if $f(h(x^p)) \in \langle \pi,\, g(h(x)) \rangle^2$. In view of $v(p)>0$ and the arguments analogues to those used in Corollary \ref{Cor:4.2}, proving the above equivalence is same as proving $f(h(x)) \in \langle \pi,\, g(h(x)) \rangle^2$ if and only if $f(h(x^p)) \in \langle \pi,\, g(h(x^p)) \rangle^2$.

If $f(h(x)) \in \langle \pi, g(h(x)) \rangle^2$ then trivially we have $f(h(x^p)) \in \langle \pi, g(h(x^p)) \rangle^2$. Now suppose $f(h(x^p)) \in \langle \pi, g(h(x^p)) \rangle^2$ and let $g_1(x)$ be the irreducible factor of $g(h(x))$ modulo $M_v$. Then we have $(f\circ h)(x^p) \in \langle \pi, g_1(x^p) \rangle^2$. As $g_1(x)$ is an irreducible repeated factor of $f\circ h$ modulo $M_v$, Theorem \ref{Thm:4.1} with $f = f \circ h$ and $h(x) = x^p$ yields $(f \circ h)(x) \in \langle \pi, g_1(x) \rangle^2$. Now since $g(x)$ is an irreducible repeated factor of $f(x)$ and $g_1(x)$ is an irreducible factor of $g \circ h$, applying Lemma \ref{lem:5.14} yields $f(h(x)) \in \langle \pi, g(h(x)) \rangle^2$. This completes the proof of the lemma.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{pro:5.13}
Let $M_v=\langle\pi \rangle$ be maximal ideal and  $f(x), h(x) \in R_v[x]$ be monic polynomials such that the composition $\mathcal{F}(x) = f(h(x))$ is irreducible  over $F$. Let $\alpha$ be a root of $f(x)$ and $\theta$ be a root of $\mathcal{F}(x)$. If $R_v[\alpha]$ is not integrally closed, then $R_v[\theta]$ is also not integrally closed.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
If $R_v[\alpha]$ is not integrally closed, then by Theorem~\ref{valuation dede}, there exists a monic polynomial $g(x) \in R_v[x]$ such that its reduction $\overline{g}(x)$ is an irreducible repeated factor of $f(x)$ modulo $M_v$. This implies that $f(x) \in \langle \pi, g(x) \rangle^2.$ By substituting $h(x)$ for $x$ in this ideal relation, it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eqn;513}
     \mathcal{F}(x) \in \langle M_v, g(h(x)) \rangle^2.
\end{equation}
  Let $\overline{g_1}(x)$ be an irreducible factor of $\overline{g}(\overline{h}(x))$ in the residue field $k_v$, and let $g_1(x) \in R_v[x]$ be a monic lift of this factor. As $g(x)$ was a repeated factor of $f(x)$ modulo $M_v$, it is clear that $g(h(x))$ is a repeated factor of $\mathcal{F}(x)$ modulo $M_v$. Thus, we have $g_1(x)$ is a repeated factor of $\mathcal{F}(x)$ modulo $M_v$. Now using $\overline{g_1}(x)$ divides $\overline{g}(\overline{h}(x))$, we have
\[
    \langle M_v, g(h(x)) \rangle \subseteq \langle M_v, g_1(x) \rangle.
\]
The square of both sides of above inclusion yields $\langle M_v, g(h(x)) \rangle^2 \subseteq \langle M_v, g_1(x) \rangle^2$. By \eqref{eqn;513} and the above inclusion, we have  $\mathcal{F}(x)\in \langle M_v, g_1(x) \rangle^2$. Thus, we have proved that $\mathcal{F}(x)$ has a repeated factor $g_1(x)$ modulo $M_v$ such that $\mathcal{F}(x)\in \langle M_v, g_1(x) \rangle^2$. Applying Theorem~\ref{valuation dede}, we conclude that $R_v[\theta]$ is not integrally closed. This completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{pro:5.12}
 Let $f(x) \in R_v[x]$ be a monic polynomial of degree $n$ and  $h(x) = x^m + a x^{m-1} + b \in R_v[x]$. Suppose that $f(h(x))$ is irreducible over $F$ and $v(m)=0$. Let $\theta$ be a root of $f(h(x))$, and set $L = F(\theta)$. Furthermore, assume that 
\[
m^{mn} f\left(h\left(\frac{-a(m-1)}{m}\right)\right) \in M_v^2.
\]
Then the simple ring extension $R_v[\theta]$ is not integrally closed.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Define $z := m\theta + a(m-1)$. Since $\theta$ is a root of $f(h(x))$, the minimal polynomial of $z$ over $F$ is given by
\[
    H(x) = m^{mn} f\left(h\left(\frac{x - a(m-1)}{m}\right)\right).
\]
The constant term of $H(x)$ is
\[
    H(0) = m^{mn} f\left(h\left(\frac{-a(m-1)}{m}\right)\right).
\]
By our hypothesis, $H(0) \in M_v^2$. Next, we compute the derivative of $H(x)$ at $x = 0$ to determine the multiplicity of $x$ modulo $M_v$. By the chain rule, we have
\[
    H'(x) = m^{mn-1} f'\left(h\left(\frac{x - a(m-1)}{m}\right)\right) h'\left(\frac{x - a(m-1)}{m}\right).
\]
Using $h'(x) = x^{m-2}(mx + a(m-1))$, we have
\[
    h'\left(\frac{-a(m-1)}{m}\right) =  0.
\]
Consequently, $H'(0) = 0$. Since $\overline{H}(0) = \overline{0}$ and $\overline{H}'(0) = \overline{0}$ in the residue field $R_v/M_v$, $x$ is a repeated irreducible factor of $\overline{H}(x)$. Therefore, in $R_v[x]$, $H(x)$ can be expanded as $x^2 Q(x) + c_1 x + H(0)$ for some $Q(x) \in R_v[x]$, where the linear coefficient is $c_1 = H'(0) = 0$. Assume that $q^2\mid\Phi_p(2j)$ for some prime $q$. Since $\Phi_p(x)=\frac{x^p-1}{x-1}$, we have $(2j)^p-1=(2j-1)\Phi_p(2j)$. Thus, $q^2\mid\Phi_p(2j)$ implies
\begin{equation}
(2j)^p\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) .
\end{equation}
Therefore, $2j$ is invertible modulo $q^2$, and its multiplicative order modulo $q^2$ divides $p$. Since $p$ is prime, the order of $2j$ modulo $q^2$ is either $1$ or $p$. Suppose to the contrary, that the order is $1$. Then $2j\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) $. Hence,
\begin{equation}
\Phi_p(2j)=(2j)^{p-1}+(2j)^{p-2}+\cdots+1\equiv p\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) .
\end{equation}
On the other hand, $q^2\mid\Phi_p(2j)$ gives $\Phi_p(2j)\equiv0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) $. Therefore, $p\equiv0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) $, which is not possible since $p$ is prime. Thus, the order of $2j$ modulo $q^2$ must be $p$. We now show that $p$ divides $q-1$. By Lagrange's theorem, the order of an element of $(\mathbb{Z}/q^2\mathbb{Z})^\times$ divides the order of the group. Since $|(\mathbb{Z}/q^2\mathbb{Z})^\times|=\phi(q^2)=q(q-1)$ and the order of $2j$ is $p$, we have $p\mid q(q-1).$ As $p$ is prime, it follows that either $p=q$ or $p\mid(q-1)$.

We now show that $p\neq q$. Suppose that $p=q$. Since the order of $2j$ modulo $q^2$ is $q$, we have $(2j)^q\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) $, and hence $2j\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q) $. Write $2j=1+kq$ for some integer $k$. Then

$$
\Phi_q(1+kq)=\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}(1+kq)^r\equiv q+kq^2\frac{q-1}{2}\equiv q\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) .
$$

But $q^2\mid\Phi_q(2j)$, so $\Phi_q(2j)\equiv0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) $, giving $q\equiv0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) $, a contradiction. Thus $p\neq q$, and consequently $p\mid(q-1)$. Since $p\mid(q-1)$, there exists an integer $k$ such that $q-1=kp$. From the initial divisibility assumption, we have $(2j)^p\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) $. Raising both sides to the $k$-th power gives
\begin{equation}
(2j)^{kp}\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) .
\end{equation}
Since $kp=q-1$, it follows that
\begin{equation}
(2j)^{q-1}\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q^2) .
\end{equation}
Thus, $q$ is a generalized Wieferich prime to base $2j$, proving condition (1).

\label{lem:6.2}
Let $p$ be an odd prime and  $\Phi_p(x)$ denote the $p$-th cyclotomic polynomial. Then
\[
\Phi_p(x)\mid\frac{\Phi_p(x^2)-\Phi_p(x)^2}{2}
\]
in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Since $p$ is odd, $\Phi_p(x)=1+x+\cdots+x^{p-1}$. Moreover,
$\Phi_p(x^2)=\frac{x^{2p}-1}{x^2-1}
=\Phi_p(x)\frac{x^p+1}{x+1}$.
Thus
\[
\Phi_p(x^2)-\Phi_p(x)^2
=\Phi_p(x)\left(\frac{x^p+1}{x+1}-\Phi_p(x)\right).
\]
Since $p$ is odd,
$\frac{x^p+1}{x+1}=x^{p-1}-x^{p-2}+\cdots-x+1$, and hence
$\frac{x^p+1}{x+1}-\Phi_p(x)
=-2(x^{p-2}+x^{p-4}+\cdots+x)$.
Therefore
\[
\frac{\Phi_p(x^2)-\Phi_p(x)^2}{2}
=-\Phi_p(x)(x^{p-2}+x^{p-4}+\cdots+x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x].
\]
Hence $\Phi_p(x)\mid\frac{\Phi_p(x^2)-\Phi_p(x)^2}{2}$. This completes the proof of lemma.

<!-- proposition-8: body_md -->
\label{pro:6.2}
Let $p$ be an odd prime and $j\geq1$ an integer. Let $\Phi_p(x)$ denote the $p$-th cyclotomic polynomial, which is  monogenic. Define the Misiurewicz trinomial $$h(x)=x^2-4jx+(4j^2+2j).$$ If $\Phi_p(2j)$ is squarefree and $j$ is odd. Then the infinite dynamical tower defined by $\mathfrak{F}_k(x)=\Phi_p(h^k(x))$ is monogenic for every $k\geq1$.
<!-- end proposition-8 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
By classical algebraic  number theory, the ring of integers of the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ is  $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]$. Hence, the base polynomial $f(x) = \Phi_p(x)$ is monogenic.



Now we verify condition~(ii) of Corollary~\ref{T2.1}. Since $m=2$ in our case, it suffices to show that $q^2\nmid\phi_p(b)$ whenever $q\mid a\phi_p'(b)$. There are two cases,  either $q\mid a(=2j)$ or $q\mid\phi_p'(b)$. First, suppose that $q\mid2j$. Since $b=2j(2j+1)$, we have $q\mid b$. Therefore, $\phi_p(b)=b^{p-1}+b^{p-2}+\cdots+b+1\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q) $. Hence $q\nmid\phi_p(b)$, and consequently $q^2\nmid\phi_p(b)$.

It remains to consider the case $q\mid\phi_p'(b)$. If $q\nmid\phi_p(b)$, then there is nothing to prove. Thus, suppose that $q\mid\phi_p(b)$ and $q\mid\phi_p'(b)$. From the identity $(x-1)\phi_p(x)=x^p-1,$ we obtain, upon differentiating, $\phi_p(b)+(b-1)\phi_p'(b)=pb^{p-1}$. It follows that $q\mid pb^{p-1}$. Since we have already proved that $q\nmid b$, we must have $q=p$.

We now show that $p^2\nmid\phi_p(b)$. First, we claim that $p\mid\phi_p(b)$ implies $b\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p) $. Indeed, from $(b-1)\phi_p(b)=b^p-1$ and Fermat's little theorem, $b^p\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p) $, we obtain $(b-1)\phi_p(b)\equiv b-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p) $, or equivalently, $(b-1)(\phi_p(b)-1)\equiv0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p) $. Since $p\mid\phi_p(b)$, we have $p\nmid(\phi_p(b)-1)$. Hence, $b\equiv1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p) $. Writing $b=1+kp$, we obtain
\[
\phi_p(b)=\frac{(1+kp)^p-1}{kp}
=p+\binom{p}{2}kp+\binom{p}{3}k^2p^2+\cdots+k^{p-1}p^{p-1}.
\]
Since $p\mid\binom{p}{r}$ for $1\le r\le p-1$, all terms after the first are divisible by $p^2$. Therefore, $\phi_p(b)\equiv p\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^2) $, and hence $p^2\nmid\phi_p(b)$. Since $q=p$, this proves that $q^2\nmid\phi_p(b)$. Thus condition~(ii) of Corollary~\ref{T2.1} is satisfied.

The critical point of $h(x)$ is $x=2j$. Evaluating $h$ at this point gives $h(2j)=2j.$ Thus, $2j$ is a fixed point of $h$, and hence $h^k(2j)=2j$ for every $k\geq1$. Therefore, along the critical orbit, we have $\Phi_p(h^k(2j))=\Phi_p(2j).$ By hypothesis, $\Phi_p(2j)$ is squarefree. Condition~(iii) of Corollary~\ref{thm:2.1} is satisfied.

Since $\gcd (a, m)=2$, so for condition $(iv)$, we need to show that $2$ does not divide index of $\phi_p(x^2+b)$. Using $2\mid b$, we have
\begin{align*}
    \phi_p(x^2+b)=& \sum_{i=1}^{p-1}(x^2+b)^i+1\\
    =& \sum_{i=1}^{p-1}(x^{2i}+ix^{2(i-1)}b)+1+4A(x)\\
    =& \phi_p(x^2)+b\phi'_p(x^2)+4B(x)
\end{align*}
for some $A(x), B(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Let $\phi_p(x)\equiv\prod g_i(x)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2) $. Write $\phi_p(x)=\prod g_i(x)+2H(x)$ for some $H(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Since $2\mid b$, we have $\phi_p(x^2+b) \equiv (\prod g_i(x))^2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2) $.  Using this and previous equation, we have
\[\frac{\phi_p(x^2+b)-(\prod g_i(x))^2}{2}=\frac{\phi_p(x^2)-\phi_p(x)^2}{2}+\frac{b}{2}\phi_p'(x^2)+2C(x)\]
for some $C(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Using Lemma~\ref{lem:6.2} and above equation, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:6.5}
\frac{\phi_p(x^2+b)-\left(\prod_i g_i(x)\right)^2}{2}
\equiv j\phi_p'(x^2)
\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \langle 2,\phi_p(x)\rangle) .
\end{equation}
Let $\alpha$ be a root of $\Phi_p(x)$ in $\overline{\mathbb F}_2$. Since
$p$ is odd, $\alpha$ is a primitive $p$-th root of unity. As $\gcd(2,p)=1$, $\alpha^2$ is also a primitive $p$-th root of unity,
and hence $\Phi_p(\alpha^2)=0$. Moreover, $x^p-1$ is separable over $\mathbb F_2$, since
$(x^p-1)'=px^{p-1}=x^{p-1}\neq0$. Thus $\Phi_p(x)$ is separable. Therefore $\Phi_p'(\alpha^2)\neq0$. Hence $\alpha$ is not a root of $\Phi_p'(x^2)$, and consequently
\[
\gcd\bigl(\Phi_p(x),\Phi_p'(x^2)\bigr)=1
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2[x].
\]
By Dedekind theorem and \eqref{eqn:6.5} we get desired result whenever $j$ is odd.
