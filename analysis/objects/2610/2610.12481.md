<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Let $\mathfrak{L}$ be a Lie algebra. The linear map $D:\mathfrak{L}\to\mathfrak{L}$ is called a derivation if
\[ D[x,y]=[D(x),y]+[x,D(y)]. \]
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
Let $\mathfrak{L}$ be a Lie algebra and $f:\mathfrak{L}\to\mathfrak{L}$ be a linear map. If there exist linear maps $f',f'':\mathfrak{L}\to\mathfrak{L}$ such that
\[ [f(x),y]+[x,f'(y)]=f''[x,y], \]
then $f$ is called a generalized derivation. If $f=f'$, then $f$ is called a quasi-derivation. If $f=f'=\delta f''$, we say that $f''$ is a $\delta$-derivation.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
Let $\delta$ be a fixed complex number. An algebra $(\mathfrak{P},\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])$ is defined to be a transposed $\delta$-Poisson algebra if $(\mathfrak{P},\cdot)$ is a commutative associative algebra, $(\mathfrak{P},[\cdot,\cdot])$ is a Lie algebra, and the following identity holds:
\begin{equation}\label{tdp}
x\cdot[y,z]=\delta\big([x\cdot y,z]+[y,x\cdot z]\big).
\end{equation}
A Lie algebra $(\mathfrak{L},[\cdot,\cdot])$ has a nontrivial transposed $\delta$-Poisson structure $\cdot$ if $(\mathfrak{L},\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])$ is a transposed $\delta$-Poisson algebra with both multiplications nonzero.
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem4}
Let $(\mathfrak{L},\cdot,[\cdot,\cdot])$ be a transposed $\delta$-Poisson algebra and $z$ an arbitrary element of $\mathfrak{L}$. Then the right multiplication $R_z$ in the associative commutative algebra $(\mathfrak{L},\cdot)$ is a $\delta$-derivation of the Lie algebra $(\mathfrak{L},[\cdot,\cdot])$. In particular, if $\mathfrak{Der}_{\delta}(\mathfrak{L})\subseteq\langle\mathrm{id}\rangle$, then there are no nontrivial transposed $\delta$-Poisson structures on $\mathfrak{L}$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Relation \eqref{main} only involves coefficients $a^{d,k}_{m,i}$ and $a^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,i}$ with the same difference $(d-m,k-i)$, i.e., the defining identity of a quasi-derivation is homogeneous with respect to the natural $\mathbb{Z}^2$-grading of $\mathcal{B}(q)$. Hence every homogeneous component $f_{(a,b)}(L_{m,i})=a^{m+a,i+b}_{m,i}L_{m+a,i+b}$ of a quasi-derivation $f$ is again a quasi-derivation, and it suffices to determine the homogeneous quasi-derivations. <!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{th7}
$\mathfrak{QDer}\big(\mathcal{B}(0)\big)=\langle\mathrm{ad}_{L_{d,k}}\mid d\neq0,\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\rangle\oplus\langle\mathrm{id}\rangle\oplus\langle p_k\rangle\oplus\langle h_{d,k}\rangle\oplus\mathrm{Ann}^{Q}\big(\mathcal{B}(0)\big)$.

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $f\in\mathfrak{QDer}\big(\mathcal{B}(0)\big)$. Then the relations \eqref{main} have the following form:
\begin{equation}\label{q0}
(nk-dj)a^{d-n,k-j}_{m,i}+(di-mk)a^{d-m,k-i}_{n,j}=(ni-mj)a^{\prime\,d,k}_{m+n,i+j}.
\end{equation}
Since linear combinations of quasi-derivations are quasi-derivations, we may replace $f$ with the normalized map
\[ \tilde f=f-\sum_{d\neq0}\beta_{d,k}\mathrm{ad}_{L_{d,k}}-\gamma\,\mathrm{id}-\sum_k\lambda_kp_k-\sum_d\mu_{d,k}h_{d,k}-\sum_{d,k}\sigma_{d,k}g_{d,k}. \]
By choosing the coefficients as follows:
\begin{align*}
\beta_{d\neq0,k} &= -a^{d,k+1}_{0,1}d^{-1};\\
\gamma &= \tfrac12\big(a^{1,0}_{1,0}+a^{-1,0}_{-1,0}\big);\\
\lambda_k &= \delta_{k,0}\big(a^{1,0}_{1,1}-a^{1,-1}_{1,0}\big)+\delta_{k,1}\big(a^{0,1}_{0,1}-\tfrac12(a^{1,0}_{1,0}+a^{-1,0}_{-1,0})\big)+\hat{\delta}_{k,0}\hat{\delta}_{k,1}a^{0,k}_{0,1};\\
\mu_{d,k} &= \delta_{d,0}\delta_{k,0}\big(a^{0,1}_{1,1}-a^{-1,1}_{0,1}\big)+\tfrac12\delta_{d,1}\delta_{k,0}\big(a^{1,0}_{1,0}-a^{-1,0}_{-1,0}\big)\\
&\quad+\delta_{d,1}\hat{\delta}_{k,0}a^{1,k}_{1,0}+\hat{\delta}_{d,1}\hat{\delta}_{(d,k),(0,0)}\Big(a^{d,k}_{1,0}+\frac{k}{d-1}a^{d-1,k+1}_{0,1}\Big);\\
\sigma_{d,k} &= \delta_{d,0}\delta_{k,0}\big(a^{0,0}_{0,0}-\tfrac12(a^{1,0}_{1,0}+a^{-1,0}_{-1,0})\big)+\delta_{d,0}\hat{\delta}_{k,0}\big(a^{0,0}_{0,k}-k\lambda_{1-k}\big)\\
&\quad+\hat{\delta}_{d,0}\big(a^{0,0}_{d,k}-d\mu_{1-d,-k}\big),
\end{align*}
we may assume without loss of generality that
\begin{equation}\label{n0}
a^{-1,0}_{-1,0}=a^{1,0}_{1,1}=a^{0,1}_{1,1}=a^{0,0}_{d,k}=a^{d,k}_{1,0}=a^{d,k}_{0,1}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad d,k\in\mathbb{Z}.
\end{equation}
We now show that \eqref{n0} implies $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i,d,k\in\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{description}
\item[Step 1.] First, we show that $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $d\neq m$ and $k\neq i$.

Taking $m=n=i=0$ and $j=1$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain $a^{d\neq0,k}_{0,0}=0$.

Taking $m=i=j=0$ and $n=1$ in \eqref{q0}, we get $a^{d,k\neq0}_{0,0}=0$, i.e., $a^{d,k}_{0,0}=0$ for all $d,k\in\mathbb{Z}$.

Taking $i=j=0$ and $n=1$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain $ka^{d-1,k}_{m,0}-mka^{d-m,k}_{1,0}=0$, i.e., $a^{d,k\neq0}_{m,0}=0$.

Taking $m=n=0$ and $j=1$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain $da^{d,k-1}_{0,i}-dia^{d,k-i}_{0,1}=0$, i.e., $a^{d\neq0,k}_{0,i}=0$.

Considering $n=0$ and $i=0$ in \eqref{q0}, for any $m$ and $j$, we get
\[ dja^{d,k-j}_{m,0}+mka^{d-m,k}_{0,j}=mja^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,j}, \]
which implies $a^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,j}=0$ for $m\notin\{0;d\}$ and $j\notin\{0;k\}$.

Setting $d=m$, $k=i$, $n\neq0$, and $j\neq0$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain
\[ (ni-mj)a^{m-n,i-j}_{m,i}=(ni-mj)a^{\prime\,m,i}_{m+n,i+j}. \]
By this equality, we have $a^{m-n,i-j}_{m,i}=0$ for $n\notin\{0;-m\}$, $j\notin\{0;-i\}$, and $ni\neq mj$.

Finally, setting $d=2m+n$ and $k=2i+j$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain that $a^{2m,2i}_{m,i}=0$ for $m,i\neq0$.

The previous two relations do not cover the cases $d=2m$, $k=2i$, or $di=mk$. All cases can be treated uniformly as follows. Taking $n=0$ in \eqref{q0} and using $a^{d\neq0,k}_{0,j}=0$, we get $dj\,a^{d,k-j}_{m,i}=mj\,a^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,i+j}$ for $d\neq m$; choosing $j\notin\{0;-i\}$ and using $a^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,i+j}=0$ for $m\notin\{0;d\}$, $i+j\notin\{0;k\}$, we obtain $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $d\neq0$, $m\notin\{0;d\}$, $k\neq i$. For $d=0$, taking $j=0$ in \eqref{q0} and using $a^{d-m,k-i}_{n,0}=0$ for $k\neq i$, we get $nk\,a^{d-n,k}_{m,i}=ni\,a^{\prime\,d,k}_{m+n,i}$; with $d=n\notin\{0;-m\}$ this gives $a^{0,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $m\neq0$, $i\neq0$, $k\notin\{0;i\}$. The remaining coefficients $a^{0,0}_{m,i}$, $a^{d,k}_{m,0}$ ($k\neq0$), and $a^{d,k}_{0,i}$ ($d\neq0$) vanish by \eqref{n0} and the relations above.
Thus, we conclude that $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $d\neq m$, $k\neq i$.

\item[Step 2.] Second, we show that $a^{d,i}_{m,i}=0$ for $d\neq m$.
We consider the relation \eqref{q0} with the restriction $k=i+j$. Taking $n=i=0$ and $k=j$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain
\[ dja^{d,0}_{m,0}+mja^{d-m,j}_{0,j}=mja^{\prime\,d,j}_{m,j}. \]
Since for $d\neq m$, we have $a^{d-m,j}_{0,j}=0$, it follows that $da^{d,0}_{m,0}=ma^{\prime\,d,j}_{m,j}$ for $d\neq m$ and $j\neq0$.
Then, setting $n=0$, $j=1$, and $k=i+1$ in \eqref{q0}, we get
\[ da^{d,i}_{m,i}-\big(di-m(i+1)\big)a^{d-m,1}_{0,1}=ma^{\prime\,d,i+1}_{m,i+1}. \]
Since $a^{d-m,1}_{0,1}=0$ by \eqref{n0}, we have $da^{d,i}_{m,i}=ma^{\prime\,d,i+1}_{m,i+1}$.
Similarly, setting $n=0$, $j=-1$, and $k=i-1$ in \eqref{q0}, we get $da^{d,i}_{m,i}=ma^{\prime\,d,i-1}_{m,i-1}$ for $d\neq m$.
From the previous three equalities, we derive
\begin{equation}\label{six}
a^{d,i}_{m,i}=a^{d,0}_{m,0}\quad\text{for}\quad d\notin\{0;m\}.
\end{equation}
(For $i\neq-1$ one uses the second equality, and for $i=-1$ the third one.)

In particular, in the case $m=1$, we get $a^{d,i}_{1,i}=a^{d,0}_{1,0}=0$ for $d\notin\{0;1\}$.
After that, by setting $n=j=1$, $i=m\neq1$, and $k=m+1$ in \eqref{q0}, for any $d\neq m+1$, we obtain
\[ (m+1-d)\big(a^{d-1,m}_{m,m}-ma^{d-m,1}_{1,1}\big)=0, \]
which implies $a^{d-1,m}_{m,m}=ma^{d-m,1}_{1,1}$ for $m\notin\{1;d-1\}$.
Since $a^{d,1}_{1,1}=0$ for $d\neq1$, we derive that $a^{d,m}_{m,m}=0$ for $d\neq m$.
Thus, using \eqref{six}, we derive $a^{d,0}_{m,0}=a^{d,m}_{m,m}=0$, which implies $a^{d,i}_{m,i}=0$ for $d\notin\{0;m\}$.

Putting $n=d=1$, $i=0$, and $k=j$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain
\[ mja^{1-m,j}_{1,j}=mja^{\prime\,1,j}_{m+1,j},\ \text{which implies } a^{\prime\,1,j}_{m+1,j}=0 \text{ for } j\neq0,\ m\notin\{0;1\}. \]
Setting $n=d=1$, $j=0$, and $k=i$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain
\[ ia^{0,i}_{m,i}=ia^{\prime\,1,i}_{m+1,i},\ \text{which yields } a^{0,i}_{m,i}=0 \text{ for } m\notin\{0;1\}. \]
Finally, taking $n=2$, $m=1$, $d=2$, $j=2i$, and $k=3i$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain
\[ 2a^{0,i}_{1,i}=a^{1,2i}_{2,2i},\ \text{which implies } a^{0,i}_{1,i}=0. \]

\item[Step 3.] Third, we show that $a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $k\neq i$.
First, setting $n=0$, $i=0$, and $d=m$ in \eqref{q0}, we get
\[ mja^{m,k-j}_{m,0}+mka^{0,k}_{0,j}=mja^{\prime\,m,k}_{m,j}. \]
Consequently, since $a^{m,k-j}_{m,0}=0$ for $k\neq j$, we obtain $ka^{0,k}_{0,j}=ja^{\prime\,m,k}_{m,j}$ for $m\neq0$, $k\neq j$.
Then, considering $n=1$, $j=0$, and $d=m+1$ in \eqref{q0}, by \eqref{n0}, we have
\[ ka^{m,k}_{m,i}=ia^{\prime\,m+1,k}_{m+1,i}. \]
By combining the last two relations, we obtain the following identity:
\[ a^{m,k}_{m,i}=a^{0,k}_{0,i},\quad m\neq-1,\ k\notin\{0;i\};\ \text{i.e., if } i=1, \text{ we get } a^{m,k}_{m,1}=0 \text{ for } k\notin\{0;1\},\ m\neq-1. \]
Next, by setting $n=j=1$, $i=m$, and $d=m+1$ in \eqref{q0}, for any $k\neq m+1$, we obtain
\[ (m+1-k)\big(a^{m,k-1}_{m,m}-ma^{1,k-m}_{1,1}\big)=0,\ \text{which implies } a^{m,k-1}_{m,m}=ma^{1,k-m}_{1,1}. \]
Since $a^{1,k}_{1,1}=0$ for $k\neq1$, we derive that $a^{m,k}_{m,m}=0$ for $k\neq m$.
Thus, using $a^{0,k}_{0,i}=a^{i,k}_{i,i}=0$ (for $i\neq-1$), we conclude that $a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $k\notin\{0;i\}$, $m\neq-1$, $i\neq-1$.

The cases $m=-1$ or $i=-1$ are treated as follows. Relation \eqref{q0} with $n=0$, $d=m$, $j=1$ gives, by \eqref{n0}, $a^{m,k-1}_{m,i}=a^{\prime\,m,k}_{m,i+1}$ for $m\neq0$. Using this, relation \eqref{q0} with $n=0$, $d=m$ (respectively, with $m=0$, $d=n$; respectively, with $m=-1$, $n=2$, $d=1$) yields
\begin{align*}
& ja^{m,k-j}_{m,i}+(k-i)a^{0,k-i}_{0,j}=ja^{m,k-1}_{m,i+j-1}, && m\neq0,\\
& (k-j)a^{0,k-j}_{0,i}+ia^{n,k-i}_{n,j}=ia^{n,k-1}_{n,i+j-1}, && n\neq0,\\
& (2k-j)a^{-1,k-j}_{-1,i}+(i+k)a^{2,k-i}_{2,j}=(2i+j)a^{1,k-1}_{1,i+j-1}.
\end{align*}
Taking $i=-1$ and $|j|$ large enough in the first relation, we get $a^{m,K}_{m,-1}=0$ for $m\notin\{0;-1\}$, $K\neq-1$; taking $i=-1$, $n=1$, and $|j|$ large enough in the second one, we get $a^{0,K}_{0,-1}=0$ for $K\notin\{0;-1\}$; finally, taking $|j|$ large enough in the third relation, we get $a^{-1,K}_{-1,i}=0$ for all $K\neq i$. In all three cases, the other coefficients in the relation vanish by the cases already proved.

After that, putting $n=0$, $i=k=1$, and $d=m$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain
\[ mja^{m,1-j}_{m,1}=mja^{\prime\,m,1}_{m,j+1},\ \text{which implies } a^{\prime\,m,1}_{m,j+1}=0 \text{ for } m\neq0,\ j\notin\{0;1\}. \]
Setting $n=0$, $j=k=1$, and $d=m$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain $ma^{m,0}_{m,i}=ma^{\prime\,m,1}_{m,i+1}$, which yields
\[ a^{m,0}_{m,i}=0\quad\text{for}\quad i\notin\{0;1\}. \]
Finally, taking $i=1$, $j=2$, $k=2$, $n=2m$, and $d=3m$ in \eqref{q0}, we obtain
\[ 2a^{m,0}_{m,1}=a^{2m,1}_{2m,2},\ \text{which implies } a^{m,0}_{m,1}=0. \]

\item[Step 4.] Finally, we show that $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i\in\mathbb{Z}$.
Substituting $d=m+n$ and $k=i+j$ into \eqref{q0}, the equation becomes
\begin{align}
(ni-mj)a^{m,i}_{m,i}+(ni-mj)a^{n,j}_{n,j} &= (ni-mj)a^{\prime\,m+n,i+j}_{m+n,i+j}, \quad\text{i.e.,}\notag\\
a^{m,i}_{m,i}+a^{n,j}_{n,j} &= a^{\prime\,m+n,i+j}_{m+n,i+j}\quad\text{for}\quad ni\neq mj. \label{seven}
\end{align}
Substituting successively $(n,j)=(0,1)$, $(n,j)=(1,0)$, and $(n,j)=(-1,0)$ into \eqref{seven}, and taking into account the conditions \eqref{n0}, we obtain the following relations:
\begin{align}
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m,i+1}_{m,i+1}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq0, \label{eight}\\
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m+1,i}_{m+1,i}\quad\text{for}\quad i\neq0, \label{nine}\\
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m-1,i}_{m-1,i}\quad\text{for}\quad i\neq0. \label{ten}
\end{align}
By analyzing the index shifts in \eqref{eight} and \eqref{nine}, we can establish $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=a^{1,i+m-1}_{1,i+m-1}$.
Furthermore, by combining \eqref{nine} and \eqref{ten}, we get $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=a^{m+2,i}_{m+2,i}$.
From these two equalities, it is not difficult to obtain $a^{1,k}_{1,k}=a^{1,k+2}_{1,k+2}$ for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.
Taking into account $a^{1,0}_{1,0}=0$, we get that $a^{1,k}_{1,k}=0$ for all even $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.
Now, setting $n=m=i=1$ and $j=3$ in \eqref{seven}, we get $a^{1,1}_{1,1}+a^{1,3}_{1,3}=a^{\prime\,2,4}_{2,4}$.
Since $a^{\prime\,2,4}_{2,4}=a^{1,4}_{1,4}=0$, it yields $a^{1,3}_{1,3}=-a^{1,1}_{1,1}$, and taking into account $a^{1,3}_{1,3}=a^{1,1}_{1,1}$, we deduce $a^{1,1}_{1,1}=0$, which implies $a^{1,k}_{1,k}=0$ for all odd $k\in\mathbb{Z}$; i.e., $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i\in\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{description}

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{th8}
If $q\notin\mathbb{Z}$, then $\mathfrak{QDer}\big(\mathcal{B}(q)\big)=\langle\mathrm{ad}_{L_{d,k}}\mid d\neq0,\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\rangle\oplus\langle\mathrm{id}\rangle\oplus\langle p_k\rangle\oplus\langle h_{d,k}\rangle$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $f\in\mathfrak{QDer}\big(\mathcal{B}(q)\big)$. Since linear combinations of quasi-derivations are quasi-derivations, we may replace $f$ with the normalized map
\[ \tilde f=f-\sum_{d\neq0}\beta_{d,k}\mathrm{ad}_{L_{d,k}}-\gamma\,\mathrm{id}-\sum_k\lambda_kp_k-\sum_d\mu_{d,k}h_{d,k}. \]
By choosing the coefficients as follows:
\begin{align*}
\beta_{d\neq0,k} &= -a^{d,k}_{0,0}d^{-1}q^{-1};\\
\gamma &= a^{0,0}_{0,0}-q\big(a^{0,1}_{0,1}-a^{0,0}_{0,0}\big);\\
\lambda_k &= \delta_{k,1}\big(a^{0,1}_{0,1}-a^{0,0}_{0,0}\big)+\hat{\delta}_{k,1}a^{0,k-1}_{0,0}q^{-1};\\
\mu_{d,k} &= \delta_{d,1}\big(a^{1,k}_{1,0}-a^{0,k}_{0,0}\big)+\hat{\delta}_{d,1}\Big(a^{d,k}_{1,0}+\frac{k-(d-2)q}{(d-1)q}a^{d-1,k}_{0,0}\Big),
\end{align*}
we may assume without loss of generality that
\begin{equation}\label{n8}
a^{0,1}_{0,1}=a^{d,k}_{0,0}=a^{d,k}_{1,0}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad d,k\in\mathbb{Z}.
\end{equation}
We now show that \eqref{n8} implies $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i,d,k\in\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{description}
\item[Step 1.] First, we show that $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $d\neq m$.

Substituting $n=j=0$ into \eqref{main}, using $a^{d,k}_{0,0}=0$ and $q\notin\mathbb{Z}$, we obtain
\[ da^{d,k}_{m,i}=ma^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,i}, \text{ which in the case of } m=0 \text{ implies } a^{d,k}_{0,i}=0 \text{ for } d\neq0. \]
Letting $n=i=0$ in \eqref{main}, and using $a^{d-m,k}_{0,j}=0$ for $d\neq m$, we have
\[ da^{d,k-j}_{m,0}=ma^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,j} \text{ for } d\neq m. \]
Combining these two equalities, we obtain $a^{d,k}_{m,j}=a^{d,k-j}_{m,0}$ for $d\notin\{0;m\}$.
Hence, substituting $m=1$ in this relation, we have $a^{d,k}_{1,j}=a^{d,k-j}_{1,0}=0$ for $d\notin\{0;1\}$.
Now, letting $n=1$, $j=0$ in \eqref{main}, we get
\[ (k+2q-dq)a^{d-1,k}_{m,i}=(i+q-mq)a^{\prime\,d,k}_{m+1,i},\ \text{which implies} \]
\[ (m+1)(k+2q-dq)a^{d-1,k-i}_{m,0}=d(i+q-mq)a^{d,k-i}_{m+1,0}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq-1,\ d\notin\{0;1;m+1\}. \]
For $i=0$, $k=t$ and for $i=1$, $k=t+1$, these relations give us the following system of equations:
\[ \begin{cases}
(m+1)(t+2q-dq)a^{d-1,t}_{m,0} = d(q-mq)a^{d,t}_{m+1,0},\\
(m+1)(t+1+2q-dq)a^{d-1,t}_{m,0} = d(1+q-mq)a^{d,t}_{m+1,0}.
\end{cases} \]
Solving this system of equations, we have $\big(t+q(m+1-d)\big)a^{d-1,t}_{m,0}=0$, which implies
\[ a^{d-1,k}_{m,0}=0 \text{ for } m\neq-1,\ d\notin\{1;m+1\};\ \text{i.e., we have } a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0 \text{ for } m\neq-1,\ d\notin\{0;m\}. \]
By choosing $n=d\notin\{0;-1\}$ and $m\notin\{0;n\}$ in \eqref{main}, we derive $a^{0,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $m\notin\{0;-1\}$.
Next, substituting $n=-1$ and $m=2$ into \eqref{main}, we have
\[ \big((d-2)(i+q)-2(k-i+q)\big)a^{d-2,k-i}_{-1,j}=0\quad\text{for}\quad d\neq1. \]
By choosing $i$ such that $\big((d-2)(i+q)-2(k-i+q)\big)\neq0$, we have $a^{d,k}_{-1,j}=0$ for $d\neq-1$.
Hence we deduce $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $d\neq m$.

\item[Step 2.] Now, we show that $a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $k\neq i$.
Substituting $n=j=0$ and $d=m$ in identity \eqref{main} and taking into account \eqref{n8}, we can derive
\[ a^{m,k}_{m,i}=a^{\prime\,m,k}_{m,i}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq0. \]
Setting $n=1$, $j=0$, and $d=m+1$ in \eqref{main}, we get
\[ \big((m-1)q-k\big)a^{m,k}_{m,i}=\big((m-1)q-i\big)a^{\prime\,m+1,k}_{m+1,i}. \]
From these two equalities, we get that
\begin{equation}\label{twelve}
\big((m-1)q-k\big)a^{m,k}_{m,i}=\big((m-1)q-i\big)a^{m+1,k}_{m+1,i}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq-1.
\end{equation}
By using \eqref{twelve} with $i=0$, for particular values of $m$, we get
\[ a^{3,k}_{3,0}=\frac{q-k}{q}a^{2,k}_{2,0}\quad\text{and}\quada^{5,k}_{5,0}=\frac{(3q-k)(2q-k)(q-k)}{6q^3}a^{2,k}_{2,0}. \]
Then, substituting the indices $n=3$, $j=0$, $m=2$, $i=0$, and $d=5$ into \eqref{main}, we derive that
\[ (3k+q)a^{2,k}_{2,0}+(q-2k)a^{3,k}_{3,0}=qa^{5,k}_{5,0},\ \text{which implies } \frac{(k+q)(k+2q)(k+3q)}{6q^2}a^{2,k}_{2,0}=0. \]
Since $q\notin\mathbb{Z}$, the factor $k+q\neq0$, and we have $a^{2,k}_{2,0}=0$ for $k\notin\{-2q;-3q\}$.
Consider the following three subcases:
\begin{description}
\item[$(2q,3q\notin\mathbb{Z})$] If $2q\notin\mathbb{Z}$ and $3q\notin\mathbb{Z}$, then $a^{2,k}_{2,0}=0$ for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.
\item[$(2q\in\mathbb{Z})$] If $2q\in\mathbb{Z}$, then setting $i=0$ and $k=-2q$ in \eqref{twelve}, we have
\[ (m+1)a^{m,-2q}_{m,0}=(m-1)a^{m+1,-2q}_{m+1,0} \text{ for } m\neq-1, \text{ it follows that} \]
\[ a^{m,-2q}_{m,0}=\tfrac{m(m-1)}{2}a^{2,-2q}_{2,0} \text{ for } m\geq0;\qquad a^{m,-2q}_{m,0}=\tfrac{m(m-1)}{2}a^{-1,-2q}_{-1,0} \text{ for } m\leq-1. \]
Putting $n=-1$, $j=0$, $m=2$, $i=0$, and $d=1$, $k=-2q$ in \eqref{main}, we get
\[ a^{-1,-2q}_{-1,0}=a^{2,-2q}_{2,0};\ \text{i.e., } a^{m,-2q}_{m,0}=\tfrac{m(m-1)}{2}a^{2,-2q}_{2,0} \text{ for all } m\in\mathbb{Z}. \]
Next, setting $n=1$, $j=0$, $i=2q$, $k=0$, and $d=m+1$ in \eqref{main}, we get
\[ (1-m)a^{m,0}_{m,2q}=(3-m)a^{m+1,0}_{m+1,2q} \text{ for } m\neq-1, \text{ which implies} \]
\begin{equation}\label{thirteen}
a^{3,0}_{3,2q}=0,\quad a^{5,0}_{5,2q}=3a^{4,0}_{4,2q}\quad\text{and}\quada^{6,0}_{6,2q}=6a^{4,0}_{4,2q}.
\end{equation}
Then, putting $n=2$, $i=2q$, $j=0$, $k=0$, and $d=m+2$ in \eqref{main}, we get
\[ (2-m)a^{m,0}_{m,2q}+(6+m)a^{2,-2q}_{2,0}=(6-m)a^{m+2,0}_{m+2,2q} \text{ for } m\neq-2, \text{ which yields} \]
\begin{equation}\label{fourteen}
3a^{2,-2q}_{2,0}=a^{5,0}_{5,2q}\quad\text{and}\quad 5a^{2,-2q}_{2,0}-a^{4,0}_{4,2q}=a^{6,0}_{6,2q}.
\end{equation}
Combining the relations \eqref{thirteen} and \eqref{fourteen}, we have $a^{2,-2q}_{2,0}=0$.
\item[$(3q\in\mathbb{Z})$] If $3q\in\mathbb{Z}$, then setting $i=0$ and $k=-3q$ in \eqref{twelve}, we have
\[ (m+2)a^{m,-3q}_{m,0}=(m-1)a^{m+1,-3q}_{m+1,0} \text{ for } m\neq-1. \]
Similarly to the previous subcase, putting $n=-2$, $j=0$, $m=3$, $i=0$, $d=1$, and $k=-3q$ in \eqref{main}, we get $a^{-2,-3q}_{-2,0}=-a^{2,-3q}_{2,0}$. Consequently,
\[ a^{m,-3q}_{m,0}=\tfrac{(m-1)m(m+1)}{6}a^{2,-3q}_{2,0} \text{ for all } m\in\mathbb{Z}. \]
Next, setting $n=1$, $j=0$, $i=3q$, $k=0$, and $d=m+1$ in \eqref{main}, we obtain
\[ (1-m)a^{m,0}_{m,3q}=(4-m)a^{m+1,0}_{m+1,3q} \text{ for } m\neq-1. \]
Then, setting $n=2$, $i=3q$, $j=0$, $k=0$, and $d=m+2$ in \eqref{main}, we get
\[ (2-m)a^{m,0}_{m,3q}+2(4+m)a^{2,-3q}_{2,0}=(8-m)a^{m+2,0}_{m+2,3q} \text{ for } m\neq-2. \]
Using these two identities for appropriate values of $m$, we obtain
\[ a^{4,0}_{4,3q}=0;\ a^{6,0}_{6,3q}=4a^{5,0}_{5,3q};\ a^{7,0}_{7,3q}=10a^{5,0}_{5,3q};\ 4a^{2,-3q}_{2,0}=a^{6,0}_{6,3q};\ 6a^{2,-3q}_{2,0}-a^{5,0}_{5,3q}=a^{7,0}_{7,3q}. \]
Consequently, it follows that $a^{2,-3q}_{2,0}=0$.
\end{description}
Thus, we have established that $a^{2,k}_{2,0}=0$ for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.
After that, putting $n=-1$, $j=0$, $i=0$, $m=2$, and $d=1$ (or $m=3$ and $d=2$) in \eqref{main}, we obtain
\[ (2k+3q)a^{-1,k}_{-1,0}=0 \text{ and } (3k+4q)a^{-1,k}_{-1,0}=0, \text{ which implies } a^{-1,k}_{-1,0}=0 \text{ for all } k\in\mathbb{Z}. \]
Substituting $n=-1$, $j=0$, and $d=m-1$ into the main identity \eqref{main} yields
\begin{equation}\label{fifteen}
\big((m+1)q+k\big)a^{m,k}_{m,i}=\big((m+1)q+i\big)a^{m-1,k}_{m-1,i}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq1.
\end{equation}
Considering \eqref{twelve} and \eqref{fifteen} as a linear system of equations, we obtain
\[ a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0 \text{ for } m\notin\{-1;0\},\ k\neq-i-3q. \]
Next, setting $k=j-i-3q$, $d=m+n\neq0$, and $m,n\notin\{-1;0\}$, $j\notin\{0;i\}$ in \eqref{main}, we have $a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $k=-i-3q$.
Finally, considering $m=0$, $n\neq0$ and $m=-1$, $n\notin\{0;1\}$ in \eqref{main}, we conclude that $a^{0,k}_{0,i}=0$ and $a^{-1,k}_{-1,i}=0$. Thus, we have $a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m\in\mathbb{Z}$, $k\neq i$.

\item[Step 3.] Finally, we show that $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i\in\mathbb{Z}$.
Substituting $d=m+n$ and $k=i+j$ into \eqref{main}, the equation becomes
\[ \big(n(i+q)-m(j+q)\big)a^{m,i}_{m,i}+\big(n(i+q)-m(j+q)\big)a^{n,j}_{n,j}=\big(n(i+q)-m(j+q)\big)a^{\prime\,m+n,i+j}_{m+n,i+j}. \]
Hence, whenever $n(i+q)-m(j+q)\neq0$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{sixteen}
a^{m,i}_{m,i}+a^{n,j}_{n,j}=a^{\prime\,m+n,i+j}_{m+n,i+j}\quad\text{for}\quad n(i+q)\neq m(j+q).
\end{equation}
Substituting successively $(n,j)=(0,0)$, $(n,j)=(1,0)$, and $(n,j)=(0,1)$ into \eqref{sixteen} and taking into account the conditions \eqref{n8}, we obtain the following relations:
\begin{align}
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m,i}_{m,i}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq0; \label{seventeen}\\
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m+1,i}_{m+1,i}\quad\text{for}\quad (m,i)\neq(1,0); \label{eighteen}\\
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m,i+1}_{m,i+1}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq0. \label{nineteen}
\end{align}
Since $a^{1,0}_{1,0}=0$, using \eqref{seventeen} and \eqref{nineteen}, we obtain $a^{1,i}_{1,i}=0$ for all $i\in\mathbb{Z}$.
Moreover, from \eqref{eighteen} and \eqref{nineteen}, we get $a^{2,0}_{2,0}=a^{\prime\,2,1}_{2,1}=a^{1,1}_{1,1}=0$.
By analyzing the index shifts in \eqref{seventeen} and \eqref{eighteen}, we can establish
\[ a^{m,0}_{m,0}=a^{2,0}_{2,0}=0;\qquad a^{m,i}_{m,i}=a^{1,i}_{1,i}=0 \text{ for } m\geq0;\qquad a^{m,i}_{m,i}=a^{-1,i}_{-1,i} \text{ for } m\leq-1. \]
Finally, putting $m=-1$, $n=2$, and $j=0$ in \eqref{sixteen}, we obtain
\[ a^{-1,i}_{-1,i}+a^{2,0}_{2,0}=a^{\prime\,1,i}_{1,i},\ \text{which implies } a^{-1,i}_{-1,i}=0 \text{ for } 2i+3q\neq0. \]
If $2i+3q=0$ (which is possible only if $3q\in\mathbb{Z}$), then $3i+4q=-\frac{q}{2}\neq0$, and putting $m=-1$, $n=3$, and $j=0$ in \eqref{sixteen}, we obtain $a^{-1,i}_{-1,i}+a^{3,0}_{3,0}=a^{\prime\,2,i}_{2,i}=a^{2,i}_{2,i}=0$, i.e., again $a^{-1,i}_{-1,i}=0$. Hence $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i\in\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{description}

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{th9}
If $q\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}$, then
\[ \mathfrak{QDer}\big(\mathcal{B}(q)\big)=\langle\mathrm{ad}_{L_{d,k}}\mid d\neq0,\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\rangle\oplus\langle\mathrm{id}\rangle\oplus\langle p_k\rangle\oplus\langle h_{d,k}\rangle\oplus\mathrm{Ann}^{Q}\big(\mathcal{B}(q)\big). \]
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $f\in\mathfrak{QDer}\big(\mathcal{B}(q)\big)$. Since linear combinations of quasi-derivations are quasi-derivations, we may replace $f$ with the normalized map
\[ \tilde f=f-\sum_{d\neq0}\beta_{d,k}\mathrm{ad}_{L_{d,k}}-\gamma\,\mathrm{id}-\sum_k\lambda_kp_k-\sum_d\mu_{d,k}h_{d,k}-\sum_{d,k}\sigma_{d,k}g_{d,k}, \]
by choosing the coefficients as follows:
\begin{align*}
\beta_{d\neq0,k} &= -a^{d,k}_{0,0}d^{-1}q^{-1};\\
\gamma &= a^{0,0}_{0,0}-q\big(a^{1,1}_{1,1}-a^{1,0}_{1,0}\big);\\
\lambda_k &= \delta_{k,1}\big(a^{1,1}_{1,1}-a^{1,0}_{1,0}\big)+\delta_{k,1-q}\big(a^{1,1-q}_{1,1}-a^{1,-q}_{1,0}\big)+\hat{\delta}_{k,1}\hat{\delta}_{k,1-q}a^{0,k-1}_{0,0}q^{-1};\\
\mu_{d,k} &= \delta_{d,1}\delta_{k,-q}\big((1+q)a^{1,-q}_{1,0}-qa^{1,1-q}_{1,1}\big)+\delta_{d,0}\delta_{k,-q}\Big(a^{0,1-q}_{1,1}-\frac{(q+1)a^{-1,-q}_{0,0}}{q}\Big)\\
&\quad+\delta_{d,1}\hat{\delta}_{k,-q}\big(a^{1,k}_{1,0}-a^{0,k}_{0,0}\big)+\hat{\delta}_{d,1}\hat{\delta}_{(d,k),(0,-q)}\Big(a^{d,k}_{1,0}+\frac{k-(d-2)q}{(d-1)q}a^{d-1,k}_{0,0}\Big);\\
\sigma_{d,k} &= \delta_{d,0}\delta_{k,0}\big(a^{0,-q}_{0,0}-q\lambda_{1-q}\big)+\delta_{d,0}\delta_{k,-q}\big(a^{0,-q}_{0,-q}-\gamma\big)\\
&\quad+\delta_{d,0}\hat{\delta}_{k,0}\hat{\delta}_{k,-q}\big(a^{0,-q}_{0,k}-(k+q)\lambda_{1-k-q}\big)+\hat{\delta}_{d,0}\big(a^{0,-q}_{d,k}-dq\beta_{-d,-k-q}-d\mu_{1-d,-k-q}\big);
\end{align*}
we may assume without loss of generality that
\begin{equation}\label{n9}
a^{1,1}_{1,1}=a^{0,1-q}_{1,1}=a^{1,1-q}_{1,1}=a^{d,k}_{0,0}=a^{d,k}_{1,0}=a^{0,-q}_{d,k}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad d,k\in\mathbb{Z}.
\end{equation}
We now show that \eqref{n9} implies $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i,d,k\in\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{description}
\item[Step 1.] First, we show that $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $d\neq m$.
Substituting $n=j=0$ into \eqref{main} yields
\begin{equation}\label{twentyone}
da^{d,k}_{m,i}=ma^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,i}.
\end{equation}
Substituting $m=0$ into this relation implies that $a^{d,k}_{0,i}=0$ for $d\neq0$.
Letting $n=i=0$ in \eqref{main}, and using $a^{d,k}_{0,i}=0$, we get $d(j+q)a^{d,k-j}_{m,0}=m(j+q)a^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,j}$, which implies
\begin{equation}\label{twentytwo}
da^{d,k-j}_{m,0}=ma^{\prime\,d,k}_{m,j}\quad\text{for}\quad d\neq m,\quad j\neq-q.
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{twentyone} and \eqref{twentytwo}, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{twentythree}
a^{d,k}_{m,i}=a^{d,k-i}_{m,0}\quad\text{for}\quad d\notin\{0;m\},\quad i\neq-q.
\end{equation}
Substituting $m=1$ into \eqref{twentythree}, we obtain $a^{d,k}_{1,i}=a^{d,k-i}_{1,0}=0$ for $d\notin\{0;1\}$, $i\neq-q$.
Next, by substituting $n=1$ and $j=0$ into \eqref{main}, using $a^{d,k}_{1,0}=0$ and relations \eqref{twentytwo} and \eqref{twentythree}, for $m\neq-1$, $d\notin\{0;1;m+1\}$, $i\neq-q$, we have
\[ (m+1)(k+2q-dq)a^{d-1,k-i}_{m,0}=d(i+q-mq)a^{d,k-i}_{m+1,0}. \]
For $i=0$, $k=t$ and for $i=1$, $k=t+1$, these relations give us the following system of equations:
\[ \begin{cases}
(m+1)(t+2q-dq)a^{d-1,t}_{m,0} = d(q-mq)a^{d,t}_{m+1,0},\\
(m+1)(t+1+2q-dq)a^{d-1,t}_{m,0} = d(1+q-mq)a^{d,t}_{m+1,0}.
\end{cases} \]
Solving this system of equations, we have that
\[ a^{d-1,t}_{m,0}=a^{d,t}_{m+1,0}=0 \text{ for } t+q(m+1-d)\neq0, \]
which implies $a^{d,k}_{m,0}=0$ for $m\neq-1$, $d\notin\{0;m\}$. Thus, we have that
\[ a^{d,k}_{m,i}=a^{d,k-i}_{m,0}=0 \text{ for } m\neq-1,\ d\notin\{0;m\},\ i\neq-q. \]
Choosing $d=n\notin\{0;-1\}$ and $m\notin\{0;n\}$ in \eqref{main}, it follows that
\[ a^{0,k}_{m,i}=0 \text{ for } m\notin\{0;-1\},\ i\neq-q. \]
Substituting $n=-1$, $m=2$ into \eqref{main}, then applying \eqref{twentytwo}, we obtain
\[ \big((d-2)(i+q)-2(k-i+q)\big)a^{d-2,k-i}_{-1,j}=0, \]
and by choosing $i$ such that the coefficient $(d-2)(i+q)\neq2(k-i+q)$, we obtain
\[ a^{d-2,k-i}_{-1,j}=0 \text{ for } d\neq1. \]
Consequently, $a^{d,k}_{-1,j}=a^{d,k-j}_{-1,0}=0$ for $d\neq-1$, $j\neq-q$.
Next, setting $i=-q$ and $j=2q$ in \eqref{main}, we derive
\[ \big(n(k-q)-3q(d-n)\big)a^{d-n,k-2q}_{m,-q}=0 \text{ for } d\neq m+n. \]
By choosing $n$ such that $n(k-q)\neq3q(d-n)$, we obtain $a^{d-n,k-2q}_{m,-q}=0$ for $d\neq m$.
Hence, we deduce $a^{d,k}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $d\neq m$.

\item[Step 2.] Second, we show that $a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0$ for $k\neq i$.
The proof of this case is similar to that of Step 2 in Theorem \ref{th8}; we highlight the key differences below. First, considering $n=j=0$ and $n=1$, $j=0$ in \eqref{main}, we get
\[ \big((m-1)q-k\big)a^{m,k}_{m,i}=\big((m-1)q-i\big)a^{m+1,k}_{m+1,i} \text{ for } m\neq-1. \]
Considering $i=0$, for particular values of $m$, we express $a^{3,k}_{3,0}$ and $a^{5,k}_{5,0}$ in terms of $a^{2,k}_{2,0}$. Then, substituting $n=3$, $j=0$, $m=2$, $i=0$, and $d=5$ into \eqref{main}, we obtain
\[ \frac{(k+q)(k+2q)(k+3q)}{6q^2}a^{2,k}_{2,0}=0. \]
Similarly to the proof of Theorem \ref{th8}, this yields the relation
\[ a^{2,k}_{2,0}=0 \text{ for } k\notin\{-q;-2q;-3q\}. \]
The cases $k=-2q$ and $k=-3q$ are treated literally as the subcases $(2q\in\mathbb{Z})$ and $(3q\in\mathbb{Z})$ in the proof of Theorem \ref{th8}.
Since $q\in\mathbb{Z}$, unlike in the proof of Theorem \ref{th8}, it is necessary to explicitly show that $a^{2,-q}_{2,0}=0$. For this purpose, setting $i=0$ and $k=-q$ in \eqref{twelve}, we have
\[ ma^{m,-q}_{m,0}=(m-1)a^{m+1,-q}_{m+1,0} \text{ for } m\neq-1. \]
It follows that
\[ a^{m,-q}_{m,0}=(m-1)a^{2,-q}_{2,0} \text{ for } m\geq1;\qquad a^{m,-q}_{m,0}=\tfrac{1-m}{2}a^{-1,-q}_{-1,0} \text{ for } m\leq-1. \]
Putting $n=-1$, $j=0$, $m=2$, $i=0$, $d=1$, and $k=-q$ in \eqref{main}, we get $a^{-1,-q}_{-1,0}=-2a^{2,-q}_{2,0}$. Hence, we obtain $a^{m,-q}_{m,0}=(m-1)a^{2,-q}_{2,0}$ for all $m\in\mathbb{Z}$.
Then, putting $m=4$, $n=2$, $i=q$, $j=0$, $k=0$, and $d=6$ in \eqref{main}, we get $a^{4,0}_{4,q}=0$.
Next, setting $m=2$, $n=2$, $i=q$, $j=0$, $k=0$, and $d=4$ in \eqref{main}, we get $a^{2,-q}_{2,0}=0$.
Thus, we have established that $a^{2,k}_{2,0}=0$ for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.
Proceeding analogously to the proof of Theorem \ref{th8}, by evaluating the main identity \eqref{main} for suitable choices of indices such as $n=-1$, $j=0$, $i=0$, and $m=2$, $d=1$ (or $m=3$, $d=2$) and combining the resulting relations, we obtain
\[ (2k+3q)a^{-1,k}_{-1,0}=0 \text{ and } (3k+4q)a^{-1,k}_{-1,0}=0, \text{ which implies } a^{-1,k}_{-1,0}=0 \text{ for all } k\in\mathbb{Z}. \]
Substituting $n=1$, $j=0$, $d=m+1$ and $n=-1$, $j=0$, $d=m-1$ into the main identity \eqref{main} yields
\begin{align*}
\big((m-1)q-k\big)a^{m,k}_{m,i} &= \big((m-1)q-i\big)a^{m+1,k}_{m+1,i}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq-1,\\
\big((m+1)q+k\big)a^{m,k}_{m,i} &= \big((m+1)q+i\big)a^{m-1,k}_{m-1,i}\quad\text{for}\quad m\neq1.
\end{align*}
Considering these equations as a linear system of equations, we obtain
\[ a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0 \text{ for } m\notin\{-1;0\},\ k\neq-i-3q. \]
Similarly, for $k=j-i-3q$, by applying the same argument as in the proof of the above theorem, we obtain $a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0$.
Finally, substituting $m=0,n\neq0$ and $m=-1,n\neq0,1$ into \eqref{main} yields $a^{0,k}_{0,i}=0$ and $a^{-1,k}_{-1,i}=0$. Hence, we obtain $a^{m,k}_{m,i}=0$ for any $m\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $k\neq i$.

\item[Step 3.] As a final step, we show that $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i\in\mathbb{Z}$.
Substituting $d=m+n$ and $k=i+j$ into \eqref{main}, the equation becomes
\[ a^{m,i}_{m,i}+a^{n,j}_{n,j}=a^{\prime\,m+n,i+j}_{m+n,i+j} \text{ for } ni-mj+(n-m)q\neq0. \]
Substituting successively $(n,j)=(0,0)$, $(n,j)=(1,0)$, and $(n,j)=(1,1)$ into this equation, we obtain
\begin{align*}
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m,i}_{m,i} &&\text{for } m\neq0;\\
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m+1,i}_{m+1,i} &&\text{for } i\neq q(m-1);\\
a^{m,i}_{m,i} &= a^{\prime\,m+1,i+1}_{m+1,i+1} &&\text{for } i\neq q(m-1)+m.
\end{align*}
As established at the beginning of the proof, we have $a^{0,-q}_{0,-q}=0$. Proceeding as in Step 3 of the proof of Theorem \ref{th8}, we obtain $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=0$ for all $i\neq q(m-1)$.
It remains to consider the case $i=q(m-1)$, $m\neq0$. 

(The argument via the third relation above does not work for $q=1$, since then $\big(m+1,q(m-1)+1\big)=\big(m+1,qm\big)$ is again an exceptional pair, and for $m=-1$.) If $m\neq-2$, we take $(n,j)=(2,0)$: then $ni-mj+(n-m)q=qm\neq0$, and since $a^{2,0}_{2,0}=0$ and $a^{\prime\,m+2,q(m-1)}_{m+2,q(m-1)}=a^{m+2,q(m-1)}_{m+2,q(m-1)}=0$ (because $q(m-1)\neq q(m+1)$), we obtain $a^{m,q(m-1)}_{m,q(m-1)}=0$. If $m=-2$, we take $(n,j)=(3,0)$: then $ni-mj+(n-m)q=-4q\neq0$, and $a^{3,0}_{3,0}=0$, $a^{\prime\,1,-3q}_{1,-3q}=a^{1,-3q}_{1,-3q}=0$ give $a^{-2,-3q}_{-2,-3q}=0$.
Therefore, we conclude that $a^{m,i}_{m,i}=0$ for all $m,i\in\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{description}

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor11}
If $q\notin\mathbb{Z}$, then $\mathcal{B}(q)$ does not admit nontrivial $\delta$-derivations.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor12}
If $q\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $\varphi$ is a nontrivial $\delta$-derivation of $\mathcal{B}(q)$, then $\varphi\in\langle D_5\rangle$ if $\delta\neq\frac12$ and $\varphi\in\langle\mathrm{id},D_5\rangle$ if $\delta=\frac12$, where
\[ D_5(L_{0,-2q})=L_{0,-q}. \]

<!-- definition-5: body_md -->
A bilinear map $\varphi$ on a Lie algebra $(\mathfrak{L},[\cdot,\cdot])$ is called a $\delta$-biderivation if
\begin{align*}
\varphi\big(x,[y,z]\big) &= \delta\big([\varphi(x,y),z]+[y,\varphi(x,z)]\big),\\
\varphi\big([x,y],z\big) &= \delta\big([\varphi(x,z),y]+[x,\varphi(y,z)]\big).
\end{align*}
A nonzero $\delta$-biderivation $\varphi$ is called nontrivial if $\delta\notin\{0;1\}$ and $\varphi\neq\lambda[\cdot,\cdot]$, where $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$.
<!-- end definition-5 -->
<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
If $\varphi$ is a nontrivial $\delta$-biderivation on $\mathcal{B}(q)$, then $q\in\mathbb{Z}$ and
\[ \varphi\big(L_{0,-2q},L_{0,-2q}\big)=\mu L_{0,-q},\quad \mu\in\mathbb{C}^{*}, \]
and $\varphi$ vanishes on all other pairs of basis elements.
<!-- end corollary-3 -->
<!-- corollary-4: body_md -->
\label{cor15}
If $\cdot$ defines a nontrivial transposed $\delta$-Poisson structure on $\mathcal{B}(q)$ with $\delta\notin\{0;1\}$, then $q\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $L_{0,-2q}\cdot L_{0,-2q}=\mu L_{0,-q}$, $\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{*}$, and all other products of basis elements are zero. In particular, up to isomorphism, there is only one nontrivial transposed $\delta$-Poisson structure $\cdot$ on $\mathcal{B}(q)$, $q\in\mathbb{Z}$, $\delta\notin\{0;1\}$; it is given by $L_{0,-2q}\cdot L_{0,-2q}=L_{0,-q}$.
<!-- end corollary-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem4}, every right multiplication $R_z$ is a $\delta$-derivation, so the statement follows from Corollaries~\ref{cor11} and~\ref{cor12} and the commutativity of $\cdot$ (associativity is not needed for this part). Conversely, since $L_{0,-q}$ is central and $L_{0,-2q}\notin[\mathcal{B}(q),\mathcal{B}(q)]$, the product $L_{0,-2q}\cdot L_{0,-2q}=\mu L_{0,-q}$ satisfies \eqref{tdp} for every $\delta$. For $q\neq0$, the automorphism $\theta\colon L_{m,i}\mapsto b^{i}L_{m,i}$ of $\mathcal{B}(q)$ with $b^{3q}=\mu^{-1}$ satisfies $\theta(x\cdot_\mu y)=\theta(x)\cdot_1\theta(y)$, where $\cdot_\mu$ denotes the structure with parameter $\mu$; for $q=0$, the same is done by the automorphism $L_{0,0}\mapsto\mu L_{0,0}$, $L_{m,i}\mapsto L_{m,i}$ for $(m,i)\neq(0,0)$ (recall that $L_{0,0}$ is central and $L_{0,0}\notin[\mathcal{B}(0),\mathcal{B}(0)]$).
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The restriction $\delta\notin\{0;1\}$ in Corollary~\ref{cor15} cannot be removed. For example, let $\delta=0$ and $2q=n\in\mathbb{Z}$, and put $e=L_{0,-n}$. Then $e\notin[\mathcal{B}(q),\mathcal{B}(q)]$, so the product $e\cdot e=e$ (all other products of basis elements being zero) is commutative and associative and satisfies $x\cdot[y,z]=0$, i.e., it is a nontrivial transposed $0$-Poisson structure on $\mathcal{B}(q)$. For $q=\frac12$ this contradicts the conclusion $q\in\mathbb{Z}$, and for $q\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}$ this structure is not of the form $L_{0,-2q}\cdot L_{0,-2q}=\mu L_{0,-q}$. For $\delta=1$ (i.e., Poisson structures), Lemma~\ref{lem4} together with the description of derivations of $\mathcal{B}(q)$ would be needed instead of Corollaries~\ref{cor11} and~\ref{cor12}.
