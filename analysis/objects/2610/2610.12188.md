<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:rings}
The class $\mathbf R_n$, in the semiring similarity type, is defined
by the additional identities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ring-basis}
 x^n\approx x,\qquad (m+1)x\approx x,\qquad mx\approx my.
\end{equation}
Its nontrivial subdirectly irreducible members are precisely
the finite fields $\mathbb F_q$ for which $q-1$ divides $n-1$.
Every member of $\mathbf R_n$ is a subdirect product of such fields.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
In a ring satisfying $x^n\approx x$, the equality
$2a=(2a)^n=2^na$ gives $ma=0$, verifying
\eqref{eq:ring-basis}. Conversely, the last two identities
in \eqref{eq:ring-basis} give a common additive zero $mx$ and
an additive inverse $(m-1)x$ for $x$. They make the additive
reduct a group; the first identity gives membership in $\mathbf R_n$.

Let $R$ be a nontrivial subdirectly irreducible member of $\mathbf R_n$,
and let $J$ be the nonzero ideal corresponding to its monolith.
Choose $0\ne a\in J$ and put $h=a^{n-1}$. Then $h^2=h$,
$ha=a$, and $h\in J$. Since $R$ is commutative, $x\mapsto hx$
is an idempotent ring endomorphism. Its kernel does not contain
$a$, and hence is zero. An injective idempotent map is the
identity, so $h$ is the identity element of $R$.
Containing the identity, the ideal satisfies $J=R$.
This makes $R$ simple; being commutative
with identity, it is a field.

Every element of this field is a root of $X^n-X$.
The bound on the number of roots makes $R$ finite, say
$R\cong\mathbb F_q$. Its multiplicative group
is cyclic~\cite[Chapter~2]{LN}, so $q-1\mid n-1$.
Conversely, each such field belongs
to $\mathbf R_n$ and is simple. The last assertion follows from the
subdirect representation theorem~\cite[Chapter~II]{BS}.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:skeleton}
Let $\mathbf W\leq\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$. A semiring $S$ belongs to $\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$
if and only if it satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:malcev-basis}
 x^n\approx x,\qquad (m+1)x\approx x,\qquad
 mu\approx mv\quad(u\approx v\in\operatorname{Id}(\mathbf W)).
\end{equation}
For every such $S$, the map
\[
 \varepsilon:S\longrightarrow E^+(S),\qquad \varepsilon(a)=ma
\]
is a retraction with kernel $\mathcal H^{+}$. For its image one has
$I=E^+(S)\in\mathbf W$, and each set
\[
 R_i=\varepsilon^{-1}(i)\qquad(i\in I)
\]
is a ring in $\mathbf R_n$ with zero element $i$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $\rho$ witness $S\in\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$. Each $\rho$-class is a
ring, so $(S,+)$ is a semilattice of abelian groups and hence
a commutative Clifford semigroup. Since the quotient is an
additive semilattice, $\mathcal H^{+}\subseteq\rho$. Conversely, every
$\rho$-class is a subgroup of $(S,+)$ and is contained in a
maximal subgroup, so $\rho\subseteq\mathcal H^{+}$ and $\rho=\mathcal H^{+}$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:rings}, the first two identities of
\eqref{eq:malcev-basis} hold, and $ma$ is the zero of the ring
component containing $a$, giving
$E^+(S)=\{ma:a\in S\}$. Distributivity and
$(m+1)x\approx x$ give
\[
 m(a+b)=ma+mb,\qquad (ma)(mb)=m^2ab=mab.
\]
These identities make $\varepsilon$ a homomorphism. It fixes every
additive idempotent and has kernel $\mathcal H^{+}$. The isomorphism
$E^+(S)\cong S/\mathcal H^{+}\in\mathbf W$ establishes the remaining identities
in \eqref{eq:malcev-basis}.

Conversely, suppose that \eqref{eq:malcev-basis} holds.
The identity $(m+1)x\approx x$ makes each cyclic additive
subsemigroup a group with identity $mx$, making $(S,+)$
a commutative Clifford semigroup. The preceding calculation
again shows that $\varepsilon(a)=ma$ is a retraction onto $E^+(S)$.
For every semiring term $u$,
\[
 u(ma_1,\ldots,ma_k)=m\,u(a_1,\ldots,a_k).
\]
The displayed equality shows that $E^+(S)$ satisfies all
identities of $\mathbf W$. Its multiplication is idempotent, so
each $\varepsilon$-fiber is closed under multiplication. Such a fiber
has an additive group and satisfies $x^n\approx x$, making it
a ring in $\mathbf R_n$. The congruence $\ker\varepsilon$ therefore witnesses
$S\in\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:connecting}
Let $S\in\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$ and use the notation of
Lemma~\ref{lem:skeleton}. For every $e\in I$, the set
$S_{\leq e}=\bigcup_{i\leq e}R_i$ is a subsemiring, and
\[
 \alpha_e:S_{\leq e}\longrightarrow R_e,\qquad
 \alpha_e(a)=a+e
\]
is a homomorphism. For $i\leq j$,
\[
 \phi_{i,j}:R_i\longrightarrow R_j,\qquad a\longmapsto a+j
\]
is a ring homomorphism, and
$\phi_{j,k}\phi_{i,j}=\phi_{i,k}$ whenever $i\leq j\leq k$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For $i,j\leq e$, the inequalities $i+j\leq e$ and
$ij\leq e^2=e$ show that $S_{\leq e}$ is closed under
both operations.
For $a\in R_i$, the element $ae$ is additively idempotent,
since $e+e=e$. Its support is $ie$, so $ae=ie$.
The right-handed calculation gives $eb=ej$ for $b\in R_j$.
These equalities yield
\[
 (a+e)(b+e)=ab+ie+ej+e=ab+e.
\]
Additive compatibility and the composition formula follow from \eqref{eq:order}.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:extension}
Let $A,B\in\mathbf R_n$, and consider a ring homomorphism
$\alpha:A\to B$. Suppose that $\chi:A\to K$ is a nonzero
homomorphism into an algebraically closed field. If
$\ker\alpha\subseteq\ker\chi$, then there is a homomorphism
$\psi:B\to K$ such that $\psi\alpha=\chi$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The map $\chi$ induces a homomorphism $\bar\chi:C\to K$,
where $C=\alpha(A)\leq B$. Choose $c\in C$ with
$\bar\chi(c)\ne0$ and put $h=c^{n-1}$. Then $h^2=h$ and
$\bar\chi(h)=1$. The inclusion $hC\subseteq hB$ is an
inclusion of commutative rings with common identity $h$.
It is integral, since every element of $hB$ satisfies the
monic polynomial $X^n-X$ over $hC$.

The image of $hC$ in $K$ is a finite field by
Lemma~\ref{lem:rings}; let $\mathfrak p$ be the kernel of
this map. By the lying-over theorem for integral extensions
\cite[Chapter~5]{AM}, there is a prime ideal
$\mathfrak q$ of $hB$ with
$\mathfrak q\cap hC=\mathfrak p$. The domain
$hB/\mathfrak q$ satisfies $x^n\approx x$, so it is a finite
field. Its embedding of $hC/\mathfrak p$ extends to an
embedding into $K$, because $K$ is algebraically closed.
This gives a homomorphism $\eta:hB\to K$ extending
$\bar\chi|_{hC}$. Set $\psi(b)=\eta(hb)$. For $c\in C$,
\[
 \psi(c)=\bar\chi(hc)=\bar\chi(c).
\]
This restriction gives $\psi\alpha=\chi$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:separation}
Let $S\in\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$ have finite skeleton $I$. For distinct
$a,b$ in the same ring component, there is a field $F$,
a homomorphic image of a ring component of $S$, for which
some algebra $Q\in\operatorname{HS}(F\times I)$ admits a homomorphism
$\eta:S\to Q$ satisfying $\eta(a)\ne\eta(b)$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Suppose that $a,b\in R_k$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:rings}, there
is a homomorphism $\chi_0:R_k\to K$ into an algebraically
closed field such that $\chi_0(a)\ne\chi_0(b)$. Since $I$
is finite, choose a maximal $e\geq k$ for which there is a
homomorphism $\chi:R_e\to K$ satisfying
$\chi\phi_{k,e}=\chi_0$. For $i\leq e$, put
\[
 \chi_i=\chi\phi_{i,e}.
\]
We claim that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product-separation}
 ij\leq e\ \hbox{and}\ \chi_{ij}\ne0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad i,j\leq e.
\end{equation}
Suppose otherwise, and set $g=ij$ and $f=e+i+j$.
Then $f>e$. Maximality of $e$ implies that $\chi$ does not
extend along $\phi_{e,f}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:extension},
there is $c\in R_e$ such that
\[
 c+f=f,\qquad \chi(c)\ne0.
\]
Choose $d\in R_g$ such that $\chi(d+e)\ne0$.

In any band, $g=ij$ implies $gig=gjg=g$.
Combining this with the implication from $g\leq e$ to
$g\leq geg\leq e$ gives
\[
 gfg=geg+gig+gjg=geg.
\]
Multiplying $c+f=f$ on both sides by $d$, we obtain
$dcd+dfd=dfd$. Both products belong to the ring $R_{geg}$,
and the additive idempotence of $dfd$ makes $dfd$ the
zero of that ring, giving $dcd=geg$. Applying the homomorphism
$\chi\alpha_e$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:connecting} gives
\[
 \chi(d+e)^2\chi(c)=0,
\]
contradicting the choices of the two nonzero factors.

Put
\[
 D=\mathord{\downarrow} e,\qquad
 U=\{i\leq e:\chi_i\ne0\},\qquad
 V=\mathord{\uparrow} U,\qquad F=\chi(R_e),
\]
where $\mathord{\uparrow} U=\{j\in I:i\leq j\text{ for some }i\in U\}$.
The set $U$ is upward closed within $D$: for
$i\leq j\leq e$, the equality $\chi_i=\chi_j\phi_{i,j}$
expresses the homomorphism at the smaller index as a
composition through the larger one.
It is also closed under multiplication: if $i,j\in U$,
choose $u\in R_i$ and $v\in R_j$ with
$\chi_i(u)\chi_j(v)\ne0$. Then
\[
 \chi_{ij}(uv)=\chi_i(u)\chi_j(v)\ne0.
\]
These closure properties give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:support-filter}
 V\cap D=U,\qquad VV\subseteq V,\qquad V+I\subseteq V.
\end{equation}
The nonzero image $F$ is a finite field in $\mathbf R_n$.

Consider the subsemiring
\[
 T=\{(r,i)\in F\times I:i\in V\text{ or }r=0\}.
\]
Closure follows from \eqref{eq:support-filter}. Define an
equivalence relation $\theta$ on $T$ by retaining all
elements of each fiber over $D$, and collapsing each fiber
over $I\setminus D$ to one point, without identifying
different indices. Addition cannot send an index outside
$D$ into $D$, so it preserves $\theta$.
For multiplication, only an index $i\in V\setminus D$
and a factor with nonzero first coordinate require
consideration. The latter factor has index $j\in V$.
If $ij\in D$, then \eqref{eq:support-filter} gives
$ij\in U$, contradicting \eqref{eq:product-separation}.
The argument also applies to $ji$. When the other first
coordinate is zero, both product coordinates are zero,
which gives the remaining compatibility case. These cases
establish $\theta\in\operatorname{Con}(T)$.

Let $Q=T/\theta$ and define, for $s\in R_i$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eta}
 \eta(s)=
 \begin{cases}
  [(\chi_i(s),i)]_\theta,&i\leq e,\\
  [(0,i)]_\theta,&i\not\leq e.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This is well defined, since $\chi_i=0$ when $i\in D\setminus U$.
For addition, the condition $i+j\in D$ forces $i,j\in D$,
where Lemma~\ref{lem:connecting} gives compatibility.
Outside this case the target fiber is collapsed.
Multiplicative compatibility for $ij\in D$ and $i,j\in D$
comes from the same lemma. When $ij\in D$ but one of
$i,j$ lies outside $D$, \eqref{eq:product-separation}
implies $\chi_{ij}=0$, so both sides of the required equality
have first coordinate zero. The case $ij\notin D$ has a
collapsed target fiber.

The fiber over $k$ remains uncollapsed and $\chi_k=\chi_0$,
so $\eta(a)\ne\eta(b)$.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:join}
For every $\mathbf W\leq\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:join}
 \mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W=\mathbf R_n\vee\mathbf W.
\end{equation}
For $S\in\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$ with skeleton $I$ and ring
components $R_i$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:individual-variety}
 \operatorname{Var}(S)=
 \left(\bigvee_{i\in I}\operatorname{Var}(R_i)\right)\vee\operatorname{Var}(I).
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Assume that $I$ is finite. The map $\varepsilon:S\to I$
separates elements from different ring components.
For a pair in a common component, Lemma~\ref{lem:separation}
supplies a separating homomorphism whose field is a
homomorphic image of a ring component.
Taking the product of these separating homomorphisms embeds
$S$ in a product of algebras belonging to the right-hand
side of \eqref{eq:individual-variety}. The reverse
inclusion follows because every $R_i$ and $I$ are
subsemirings of $S$.

Let $I$ be arbitrary. A finitely generated subsemiring
$S_0$ of $S$ has finitely generated skeleton
$I_0=\varepsilon(S_0)$. Since $\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$ is locally finite, $I_0$ is
finite. Its ring components are subrings of the corresponding
components of $S$, and $I_0\leq I$. The finite-skeleton
argument therefore puts every such $S_0$ in the right-hand
side of \eqref{eq:individual-variety}. Since identities
can be checked on finitely generated subalgebras,
\eqref{eq:individual-variety} follows in general.

Equation~\eqref{eq:individual-variety} gives
$\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W\subseteq\mathbf R_n\vee\mathbf W$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:skeleton} makes $\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$ a variety
containing both $\mathbf R_n$ and $\mathbf W$, which gives the reverse
inclusion in \eqref{eq:join}.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:ambient-basis}
The variety $\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$ is defined, relative to the
semiring axioms, by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ambient-basis}
 x^n\approx x,\qquad (m+1)x\approx x,\qquad mx^2\approx mx.
\end{equation}
For $n=2$, the two identities $x^2\approx x$ and $3x\approx x$
suffice.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Under $(m+1)x\approx x$, the map $x\mapsto mx$ is the
retraction onto the additive idempotents. The last identity
of \eqref{eq:ambient-basis} makes its image multiplicatively
idempotent, so Lemma~\ref{lem:skeleton} identifies the
defined variety. If $n=2$, the last identity is
a consequence of $x^2\approx x$.
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:corners}
Every member of $\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner}
 t^{n-1}xt^{n-1}yt^{n-1}
 \approx t^{n-1}xyt^{n-1}.
\end{equation}
For every $c\in S$, this identity makes the map
\[
 \pi_h:S\longrightarrow S,\qquad \pi_h(x)=hxh,
 \qquad h=c^{n-1},
\]
an idempotent endomorphism.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
In $\mathbf R_n$, commutativity and the multiplicative
idempotence of $t^{n-1}$ establish the identity.
Its restriction to $\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$ is \eqref{eq:regular-band}.
Theorem~\ref{thm:join} gives \eqref{eq:corner}.
Distributivity proves additive compatibility of $\pi_h$,
and
\[
 (hxh)(hyh)=hxh yh=hxyh
\]
proves multiplicative compatibility. The equality $h^2=h$
gives $\pi_h^2=\pi_h$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:one-field}
Let $S\in\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$ be subdirectly irreducible and not
additively idempotent. Then $S$ has a multiplicative identity,
and its monolith is $\mathcal H^{+}$. Exactly one ring component of
$S$ is nontrivial. This component is a finite field
$\mathbb F_q$, where $q-1\mid n-1$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let $\mu$ be the monolith of $S$. Since $\mathcal H^{+}$ is
nontrivial, $\mu\subseteq\mathcal H^{+}$. Choose $a\ne b$ with
$a\mathrel\mu b$. These elements belong to a common
component $R_e$. In that ring, put
$c=a-b\ne e$ and $h=c^{n-1}$. Since $hch=c$, the
endomorphism $\pi_h$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:corners}
separates $a$ and $b$, so $\mu$ is not contained in
$\ker\pi_h$. Subdirect irreducibility gives
$\ker\pi_h=\Delta_S$. An injective idempotent endomorphism
is the identity, so $hxh=x$ for every $x\in S$.
Multiplying this equality by $h$ on either side gives
$hx=xh=x$.

From $a\mathrel\mu b$, addition of $-b$ in $R_e$
gives $c\mathrel\mu e$. Taking $(n-1)$st powers yields
$h\mathrel\mu e$. Since $e=mh$, every $s\in S$ satisfies
\[
 s=sh\mathrel\mu se=s(mh)=ms=\varepsilon(s).
\]
This gives $\mathcal H^{+}\subseteq\mu$; together with the inclusion
established at the start of the proof, it yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:monolith}
 \mu=\mathcal H^{+}.
\end{equation}

Suppose that $R_f$ is any nontrivial ring component and
choose $d\ne f$ in $R_f$. The endomorphism
$\pi_{d^{n-1}}$ separates $d$ and $f$. By
\eqref{eq:monolith}, its kernel cannot be nontrivial.
As before, $d^{n-1}$ is a multiplicative identity of $S$,
and uniqueness gives $d^{n-1}=h$. Taking supports yields
$f=e$, so $R_e$ is the unique nontrivial ring component.

Every nonzero $d\in R_e$ satisfies $d^{n-1}=h$,
which makes $R_e$ a field. Lemma~\ref{lem:rings}
identifies it as $R_e\cong\mathbb F_q$ with $q-1\mid n-1$.

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:construction}
Let $F\in\mathbf R_n$ be a finite field, and let $I\in\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$
have a multiplicative identity $e$. Define $A(F,I,e)$
on the set $F\cup I$, with $F\cap I=\{0_F\}=\{e\}$.
Retain the operations on $F$ and $I$, and prescribe
the mixed operations by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-product}
 ri=ir=i\qquad(r\in F,\ i\in I)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-sum}
 r+i=i+r=
 \begin{cases}
  r,&i\leq e,\\
  e+i,&i\not\leq e.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:construction}
The operations in Definition~\ref{def:construction} make
$A(F,I,e)$ a semiring in $\operatorname{HS}(F\times I)$. Its skeleton
is $I$, its unique nontrivial ring component is $F$,
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:construction-variety}
 \operatorname{Var}(A(F,I,e))=\operatorname{Var}(F)\vee\operatorname{Var}(I).
\end{equation}
The multiplicative identity of $A(F,I,e)$ is $1_F$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Consider
\[
 T=(F\times\mathord{\uparrow} e)\ \cup\ (\{0_F\}\times I).
\]
The order is compatible with multiplication and $e^2=e$,
so $\mathord{\uparrow} e$ is closed under multiplication and is upward
closed under addition. These closure properties give
$T\leq F\times I$.
On $T$, collapse each fiber with index different from $e$
to one point, retaining every element of the fiber over $e$.

This equivalence is a congruence. A fiber which is
nontrivially collapsed has index $i>e$, and addition cannot
send this index back to $e$. For multiplication, a band with
identity $e$ satisfies the implication from $ij=e$ to $i=j=e$:
$i=i(ij)=i^2j=ij=e$, and then $j=e$.
Multiplication cannot distinguish elements of a collapsed
fiber in the fiber over $e$. All other target
fibers are singletons.

The resulting quotient is the algebra in
Definition~\ref{def:construction}. The two definitions
of its operations agree at the common element $e=0_F$.
The embedded copies of $F$ and $I$ give one inclusion in
\eqref{eq:construction-variety}; membership in
$\operatorname{HS}(F\times I)$ gives the other.
Equations~\eqref{eq:mixed-product} and \eqref{eq:mixed-sum}
identify the skeleton, the ring component, and the
multiplicative identity.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:reconstruction}
Every subdirectly irreducible semiring $S\in\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$
that is not additively idempotent is isomorphic to $A(F,I,e)$
for some
$F=\mathbb F_q$, $q-1\mid n-1$, and $I\in\mathbf W$ with multiplicative
identity $e$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
By Proposition~\ref{prop:one-field}, $S$ has a unique
nontrivial ring component $F=R_e$ and a multiplicative
identity $h=1_F$. All other ring components are singletons.
Its underlying set is $F\cup I$, with
$F\cap I=\{e\}$. For $i\in I$,
\[
 ei=(mh)i=m(hi)=mi=i,
\]
and the calculation on the other side gives $ie=i$.
Together these identify $e$ as the multiplicative identity
of $I$.

For $r\in F$ and $i\in I$, the product $ri$ is
additively idempotent with support $ei=i$, which gives
$ri=i$. Reversing the multiplication gives $ir=i$.
If $i\leq e$, then
$r+i=r+e+i=r+e=r$. Otherwise $r+i$ belongs to the
singleton component with support $e+i\ne e$, so
$r+i=e+i$.
<!-- end proof-11 -->
<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:syntactic}
The relation $\equiv_D$ is the largest congruence of $I$
for which $D$ is a union of congruence classes.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
The relation defined by the polynomial condition is an
equivalence relation.
The identity polynomial ensures that it saturates $D$,
and composition preserves it under every unary polynomial.
For a basic binary operation, replacing the two arguments
one at a time, with the other fixed as a parameter,
establishes compatibility, making $\equiv_D$ a congruence.
Any congruence $\tau$ saturating $D$ preserves membership
after polynomial evaluation: $i\mathrel\tau j$ implies
$p(i)\mathrel\tau p(j)$ for every unary polynomial $p$,
and hence $i\equiv_D j$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:criterion}
Let $S=A(F,I,e)$, where $F\in\mathbf R_n$ is a finite field and
$I\in\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$ has identity $e$. Put $D=\mathord{\downarrow} e$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:si-criterion}
 S\text{ is subdirectly irreducible}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \equiv_D\,=\,\Delta_I.
\end{equation}
When these equivalent conditions hold, the monolith
has $F$ as its unique nonsingleton class, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:congruence-lattice}
 \operatorname{Con}(S)=\{\Delta_S\}\ \cup\
 \{\varepsilon^{-1}(\tau):\tau\in\operatorname{Con}(I)\},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon^{-1}(\tau)=
\{(a,b):(\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b))\in\tau\}$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Let $H=\ker\varepsilon$. Its only nonsingleton class is $F$.
A congruence $\theta$ that is nontrivial on $F$ identifies
all of $F$ by simplicity of the field, so $H\subseteq\theta$.

Suppose instead that $\theta|_F=\Delta_F$.
No $r\in F\setminus\{e\}$ can be $\theta$-equivalent to
an $i\in I$. Otherwise application of the unary term
$mx$ gives $e\mathrel\theta i$, and hence
$r\mathrel\theta e$, a contradiction. It follows from this
exclusion that
\[
 \theta=\tau\ \cup\ \Delta_{F\setminus\{e\}}
\]
for a congruence $\tau$ on $I$. This congruence must
saturate $D$. For if $i\mathrel\tau j$ with $i\in D$
and $j\notin D$, adding $1_F$ gives
$1_F\mathrel\theta e+j$, again identifying a nonzero
field element with an element of $I$.

Conversely, let $\tau\in\operatorname{Con}(I)$ saturate $D$.
Extend it by singleton classes on $F\setminus\{e\}$.
For $i\mathrel\tau j$, mixed multiplication preserves
the relation by \eqref{eq:mixed-product}. For addition
of $r\in F$, if $i,j\in D$ both sums equal $r$; if
$i,j\notin D$, the sums $e+i$ and $e+j$ are
$\tau$-equivalent. The extension is a congruence
of $S$ whose intersection with $H$ is $\Delta_S$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:syntactic}, there is no nontrivial
congruence of this second kind exactly when
$\equiv_D=\Delta_I$. In that case every nontrivial
congruence contains $H$, so $H$ is the monolith.
If a nontrivial congruence of the second kind exists,
its intersection with $H$ is trivial, so $S$ is not
subdirectly irreducible, establishing both directions of
\eqref{eq:si-criterion}. Formula~\eqref{eq:congruence-lattice}
then follows from the correspondence theorem
\cite[Chapter~II]{BS} applied to $S/H\cong I$.
<!-- end proof-13 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:classification}
Let $\mathbf W\leq\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$. Up to isomorphism, the subdirectly
irreducible members of $\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$ are precisely:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item the subdirectly irreducible members of $\mathbf W$;
 \item the semirings $A(\mathbb F_q,I,e)$, where $q-1\mid n-1$,
 $I\in\mathbf W$ has multiplicative identity $e$, and for all
 distinct $i,j\in I$ there is a $p\in\operatorname{Pol}_1(I)$ such that
 exactly one of $p(i),p(j)$ belongs to $\mathord{\downarrow} e$.
\end{enumerate}
There is no finiteness restriction on $I$.
The parameters in {\rm(2)} are unique up to isomorphism:
$A(\mathbb F_q,I,e)\cong A(\mathbb F_{q'},I',e')$ if and only if
$q=q'$ and $I\cong I'$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
An additively idempotent member of $\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$ coincides
with its skeleton and hence belongs to $\mathbf W$.
For all other members, necessity follows from
Lemma~\ref{lem:reconstruction} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}. Conversely,
Lemma~\ref{lem:construction} puts every algebra in
(2) in $\mathbf R_n\vee\mathbf W$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion} makes it subdirectly
irreducible.

An isomorphism preserves additive idempotents and ring
components, so it preserves both $I$ and the unique
nontrivial component $\mathbb F_q$. Conversely, isomorphisms
of the two parameters agree at their identified zero
and identity elements, and extend to an isomorphism
by \eqref{eq:mixed-product} and \eqref{eq:mixed-sum}.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:lattice}
For every $\mathbf W\leq\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$, the map
\[
 L(\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W)\longrightarrow L(\mathbf R_n)\times L(\mathbf W),
 \qquad
 \mathbf V\longmapsto(\mathbf V\cap\mathbf R_n,\mathbf V\cap\mathbf W)
\]
is a lattice isomorphism. Its inverse sends
$(\mathbf A,\mathbf B)$ to $\mathbf A\vee\mathbf B$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
Let $\mathbf V\leq\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$. For each $S\in\mathbf V$,
all its ring components belong to $\mathbf V\cap\mathbf R_n$, and
its skeleton belongs to $\mathbf V\cap\mathbf W$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:individual-variety} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:subvariety-decomp}
 \mathbf V=(\mathbf V\cap\mathbf R_n)\vee(\mathbf V\cap\mathbf W).
\end{equation}

It remains to recover an arbitrary pair
$\mathbf A\leq\mathbf R_n$, $\mathbf B\leq\mathbf W$ from $\mathbf A\vee\mathbf B$.
If $u\approx v$ holds in $\mathbf A$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ring-lift}
 u+mv\approx mu+v
\end{equation}
holds in both $\mathbf A$ and every member of $\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$.
On $\mathbf R_n$, this identity is equivalent to $u\approx v$.
These identities recover $(\mathbf A\vee\mathbf B)\cap\mathbf R_n=\mathbf A$.
For an identity $u\approx v$ of $\mathbf B$, the equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:idempotent-lift}
 mu\approx mv
\end{equation}
holds in $\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf R_n$ and reduces to $u\approx v$
on $\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$, recovering $(\mathbf A\vee\mathbf B)\cap\mathbf W=\mathbf B$.
The maps are inverse order-preserving bijections
and are lattice isomorphisms.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:ring-lattice}
There is a lattice isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ring-lattice}
 L(\mathbf R_n)\cong\prod_{p\ {\rm prime}}\mathcal O(T_p(n)).
\end{equation}
The coordinate associated with $\mathbf A\leq\mathbf R_n$ at $p$ is
$\{t:\mathbb F_{p^t}\in\mathbf A\}$.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:rings}, each subvariety of $\mathbf R_n$
is generated by the finite fields which it contains.
For a fixed characteristic, these fields form a
downward closed set, because
$\mathbb F_{p^s}\leq\mathbb F_{p^t}$ if and only if $s\mid t$
\cite[Chapter~2]{LN}.

To realize prescribed downward closed sets without adding
fields, choose $D_p\subseteq T_p(n)$ and put
$P=\{p:D_p\ne\varnothing\}$. Assume $P\ne\varnothing$. Put $d=\prod_{p\in P}p$, and let
$M_p$ be the set of maximal elements of $D_p$.
The variety generated by the selected fields satisfies
the ring identities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:field-selection}
 dx=0,\qquad
 \frac{d}{p}\prod_{s\in M_p}(x^{p^s}-x)=0
 \quad(p\in P).
\end{equation}
Here ring notation is an abbreviation:
within $\mathbf R_n$, replace $-x$ by $(m-1)x$ and zero by $mx$;
the first identity may be written $(d+1)x\approx x$.

Let $\mathbb F_{p^t}\in\mathbf R_n$ satisfy \eqref{eq:field-selection}.
The first identity forces $p\in P$.
Choose a generator $a$ of its multiplicative group.
The scalar $d/p$ is nonzero in characteristic $p$,
so one factor in the second identity vanishes at $a$.
For some $s\in M_p$,
$a^{p^s}=a$, whence $p^t-1\mid p^s-1$.
This is equivalent to $t\mid s$, and therefore $t\in D_p$.
No unselected field can belong to the generated variety.
If $P$ is empty, take the trivial variety.
Lemma~\ref{lem:rings} proves bijectivity; inclusion of the
selected fields also preserves the order.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:finite-lattice}
A finitely based variety with finitely many subvarieties
is hereditarily finitely based.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Let $\mathbf V$ have a finite identity basis $\Sigma$, and
let $\Upsilon\leq\mathbf V$. For each $\mathbf U\leq\mathbf V$ with
$\mathbf U\not\subseteq\Upsilon$, choose an identity
holding in $\Upsilon$ and failing in $\mathbf U$.
There are only finitely many choices. Adjoin them
to $\Sigma$. The resulting variety contains $\Upsilon$
and is contained in $\mathbf V$. If it were not contained in
$\Upsilon$, one of the chosen identities would exclude
it, a contradiction.

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:absorption}
If $\mathbf W\leq\mathbf N^+$, then the subdirectly irreducible
members of $\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf W$ are precisely the finite fields
$\mathbb F_q$ with $q-1\mid n-1$ and the subdirectly

universal relation, and it is equality only when

of subdirectly irreducible members, and group the
field factors and the idempotent factors separately.

respectively, and give the required subdirect product.
An empty group of factors is replaced by the trivial
semiring.

<!-- corollary-4: body_md -->
\label{cor:semilattice}
The subdirectly irreducible members of
$\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf{Sl}$ are the two-element semilattice
semiring and the semirings $\mathbb F_q$ and $\mathbb F_q^\infty$,

A unary polynomial which depends on its variable is

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:large}
There is a four-element semiring $B$ in
$\mathbf R_2\circ\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$ such that $\operatorname{Var}(B)$ is
residually large. The variety $\mathbf R_n\circ\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$ is
residually large for every $n\geq2$.
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
On the chain $K=\{0,e,t\}$ with $0<e<t$, define addition
by maximum. Multiplication has absorbing element $0$ and
agrees with maximum on $\{e,t\}$.
These operations are associative, and multiplication
is order preserving on a chain, hence distributes
over maximum. This gives $K\in\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$, with $e$ as its
multiplicative identity.

For a nonempty set $J$, put
\[
 I_J=K^J,\qquad e_J=(e)_{j\in J},\qquad
 D_J=\mathord{\downarrow} e_J=\{0,e\}^J.
\]
For the separation condition in
Theorem~\ref{thm:classification}, take distinct
$a,b\in I_J$ and choose $j$ with $a_j\ne b_j$.
Let $c$ be zero at every coordinate other than $j$.
If $\{a_j,b_j\}=\{0,e\}$, put $c_j=t$.
Then the $j$th coordinates of $ac$ and $bc$ are
$0$ and $t$, in some order. If one of $a_j,b_j$
is $t$, put $c_j=e$. Again exactly one of the two
products has coordinate $t$ at $j$, and all their
other coordinates are zero. The unary polynomial
$p(x)=xc$ sends precisely one of $a,b$ into $D_J$.

The separation criterion makes
\[
 S_J=A(\mathbb F_2,K^J,e_J)
\]
subdirectly irreducible. By
\eqref{eq:construction-variety},
\[
 \operatorname{Var}(S_J)\subseteq\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb F_2)\vee\operatorname{Var}(K)
 =\operatorname{Var}\bigl(A(\mathbb F_2,K,e)\bigr).
\]
Set $B=A(\mathbb F_2,K,e)$. It has four elements.
For $|J|=k<\infty$, the algebra $S_J$ has $3^k+1$
elements; for infinite $J$, it has $3^{|J|}$ elements.
The absence of a uniform bound on these cardinalities makes
$\operatorname{Var}(B)$ residually large.
The membership $\mathbb F_2\in\mathbf R_n$ for every $n\geq2$ also
gives $B\in\mathbf R_n\vee\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$ for every such $n$.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:no-two-factor}
The semiring $B$ generating the variety in the preceding
corollary is subdirectly irreducible and is neither a ring nor
additively idempotent.
If it admitted a subdirect embedding into a product of a ring
in $\mathbf R_n$ and an idempotent semiring, the coordinate kernels
would have trivial intersection. Subdirect irreducibility forces
one kernel to be trivial. The corresponding projection is
surjective, so $B$ would be isomorphic to that factor, a
contradiction. This counterexample shows that the join equality
of Theorem~\ref{thm:join} does not imply the two-factor
decomposition of Corollary~\ref{cor:absorption} for arbitrary
$\mathbf W\leq\mathbf S_{\ell}^{+}$.
