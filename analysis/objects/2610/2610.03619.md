<!-- thmstar-1: body_md -->
Let $\beta\in(1,2)$ be such that the lexicographically largest point of the
beta-shift of $\beta$ is a Sturmian sequence.  Then the beta-shift does not
have TPO, with respect to any of its standard metrics, and neither $T_\beta$
nor $U_\beta$ has TPO in any H\"older space $C^{0,\gamma}([0,1])$,
$0<\gamma\le1$.  In each case there is a non-empty open set of functions
whose unique maximizing measure is the Sturmian measure.

<!-- defn-1: body_md -->
Let $X$ be a subshift equipped with a metric $d_\theta$.  A function
$f\in{\rm Lip}(X,d_\theta)$ has the \emph{periodic optimization property} if
$\mathcal M_{\max}(f)=\{\mu\}$ for some periodic orbit measure $\mu$, and $\mathcal P(X)$
will denote the set of such $f$.  The subshift $X$ has \emph{typical periodic
optimization}, or \emph{TPO}, if $\mathcal P(X)$ is dense in ${\rm Lip}(X,d_\theta)$;
equivalently, by \cite[Cor.~6.6]{HJXZ}, if there is an open dense subset of
${\rm Lip}(X,d_\theta)$ all members of which have the periodic optimization
property.
<!-- end defn-1 -->
<!-- defn-2: body_md -->
For irrational $\alpha\in(0,1)$, the \emph{maximal Sturmian sequence of
rotation number $\alpha$} is $\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}(\alpha):=(a_i)_{i\ge0}\in\Sigma$,
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a}
  a_i:=\lceil(i+1)\alpha\rceil-\lceil i\alpha\rceil ,
\end{equation}
so that $a_i=1$ if and only if the interval $[i\alpha,(i+1)\alpha)$ contains
an integer.  The \emph{Sturmian subshift of rotation number $\alpha$} is defined to be the
orbit closure $X_\alpha:=\overline{\{\sigma^n\mathbf{a}:n\ge0\}}$.

<!-- defn-3: body_md -->
\label{def:functions}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational. Let $X_\alpha$
be the
Sturmian subshift,
and $Y_\alpha$ the Sturmian beta-shift, of rotation number $\alpha$,
and let $\theta\in(0,1)$.  Define\footnote{These functions are defined on the whole of $\Sigma$ because the
Birkhoff sums of $\Phi$ are used on $\Sigma$ in Section \ref{sec:cob};
elsewhere we only consider their restrictions to $Y_\alpha$ and to $X_\alpha$,
which we denote by the same symbols.}
$\Phi,\Psi,F:\Sigma\to\mathbb R$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:functions}
  \Phi(x):=x_0,\qquad  \Psi(x)=\Psi_\theta(x):=d_\theta(x,X_\alpha),\qquad
  F:=\Phi-\Psi .
\end{equation}
We call $\Phi$ the \emph{digit function}, so that $\int\Phi\,d\nu$ is the
frequency of the digit $1$ under $\nu$; we call $\Psi$ the \emph{distance
function}, which vanishes precisely
on $X_\alpha$; and we call their difference $F$ the
\emph{digit-distance difference}.
<!-- end defn-3 -->
<!-- rem-1: body_md -->
All three functions $\Phi$, $\Psi$ and $F$ are Lipschitz: if $\ell$ is the
smallest index at which $x$ and $y$ differ, so that
$d_\theta(x,y)=\theta^{\ell}$, then $|\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)|=|x_0-y_0|$ equals $1$
when $\ell=0$ and $0$ otherwise, while $\Psi$ is a distance function and
$F=\Phi-\Psi$; so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:FLip}
  |\Phi|_{{\rm Lip}(\Sigma)}=1,\qquad
  |\Psi|_{{\rm Lip}(\Sigma)}\le1,\qquad
  |F|_{{\rm Lip}(\Sigma)}\le2 .
\end{equation}
The same bounds hold for the restrictions of these functions to $Y_\alpha$,
with $|\Phi|_{{\rm Lip}(Y_\alpha)}=1$ since $0^\infty$ and $\mathbf{a}$ both lie in
$Y_\alpha$.

\label{thm:A}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational and let $\theta\in(0,1)$.  Then the
Sturmian beta-shift $Y_\alpha$, equipped with the metric $d_\theta$, does
not have typical periodic optimization.
Indeed, the digit-distance difference function $F$ belongs to ${\rm Lip}(Y_\alpha,d_\theta)$, and with
$C_\theta:=1+2(1-\theta)^{-3}$ the set
\[
  U:=\bigl\{f\in{\rm Lip}(Y_\alpha,d_\theta):
  |f-F|_{{\rm Lip}(Y_\alpha)}<C_\theta^{-1}\bigr\}
\]
is a non-empty open subset of ${\rm Lip}(Y_\alpha,d_\theta)$, and all $f\in U$
satisfy $\mathcal M_{\max}(f)=\{\mu_\alpha\}$, where the Sturmian measure $\mu_\alpha$ is not
a periodic orbit measure.

There exist shift spaces with the specification property which do not have

\label{thm:C}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $\theta\in(0,1)$, let $Y_\alpha$ be
the Sturmian beta-shift of rotation number $\alpha$ equipped with the metric
$d_\theta$, with Sturmian measure $\mu_\alpha$ and Wasserstein distance
$W_\theta$,
and let $F\in{\rm Lip}(Y_\alpha,d_\theta)$ be
the digit-distance difference function.
 Then, with
$C_\theta:=1+2(1-\theta)^{-3}$,
\[
  \int F\,d\mu_\alpha-\int F\,d\nu\ \ge\ C_\theta^{-1}\,
  W_\theta(\nu,\mu_\alpha)\qquad\text{for all }\nu\in\mathcal M(Y_\alpha,\sigma).
\]

<!-- thmx-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:D}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $\theta\in(0,1)$, let $X_\alpha$ be
the Sturmian subshift of rotation number $\alpha$ equipped with the metric
$d_\theta$, with Sturmian measure $\mu_\alpha$, and let the digit function
$\Phi$ be as in \eqref{eq:functions}.  Then for all
$f\in{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$ there is a unique constant $c_f\in\mathbb R$, and a
function $\tilde f\in{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$ which is unique up to an additive
constant, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cohomology}
  f-\int f\,d\mu_\alpha=c_f\,(\Phi-\alpha)+\tilde f\circ\sigma-\tilde f .
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c-bound}
  |c_f|\le\frac{|f|_{{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha)}}{(1-\theta)^{2}}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:u-bound}
  |\tilde f|_{{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha)}
  \le\frac{\theta\,|f|_{{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha)}}{(1-\theta)^{3}} ,
\end{equation}
where the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:u-bound} does not depend on the choice
of $\tilde f$.

Consequently the quotient of ${\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$ by the subspace spanned
by the constants and the Lipschitz coboundaries is one-dimensional, and is
spanned by the equivalence class of the digit function $\Phi$.  The functional
$f\mapsto c_f$ is the unique linear functional on ${\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$
which vanishes on the constants and on the Lipschitz coboundaries and
satisfies $c_\Phi=1$.
<!-- end thmx-3 -->
<!-- rem-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:uniqueness}
The uniqueness of $c_f$ in Theorem \ref{thm:D} is equivalent to the
fact that $\Phi-\alpha$ is not a Lipschitz coboundary on $X_\alpha$.
To see this, note that if
$\Phi-\alpha$
were a Lipschitz coboundary, with $\Phi-\alpha=u\circ\sigma-u$ for some
$u\in{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$, then \eqref{eq:cohomology} would remain
valid after replacing $c_f$ by $c_f+t$ and $\tilde f$ by $\tilde f-tu$, for
all $t\in\mathbb R$.
Conversely,
if $c_f$ were non-unique then
there would exist constants $c\ne c'$ and Lipschitz transfer functions $h$ and $h'$, such that
$f-\int f\,d\mu_\alpha=c\,(\Phi-\alpha)+h\circ\sigma-h$
and
$f-\int f\,d\mu_\alpha=c'\,(\Phi-\alpha)+h'\circ\sigma-h'$,
yielding
$\Phi-\alpha=w\circ\sigma-w$ with
$w:=(c-c')^{-1}(h'-h)\in{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$.

Although $\Phi-\alpha$ is not a Lipschitz coboundary on $X_\alpha$, it is a
continuous coboundary there: by Proposition \ref{prop:rigidity}, the function
$\tau:=\sup_{r\ge0}S_r(\Phi-\alpha)$ is continuous, takes values in $[0,1]$,
and satisfies $\Phi-\alpha=\tau-\tau\circ\sigma$.  That some continuous
transfer function exists also follows from the classical criterion recalled in
Remark \ref{rem:linsine}, since the Birkhoff sums of $\Phi-\alpha$ are
uniformly bounded, Sturmian sequences being balanced (Lemma
\ref{lem:sturmian}(iv)).  The function $\tau$ is not H\"older continuous: for
all $k\in\mathbb N$ it maps each cylinder set $[w]_{X_\alpha}$, $w\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$,
onto an interval of length $\mu_\alpha([w]_{X_\alpha})$, and since
$\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$ has $k+1$ elements, one of these cylinder sets has measure at
least $(k+1)^{-1}$, whereas its $d_\theta$-diameter is at most $\theta^{k}$,
for all $\theta\in(0,1)$ (Proposition \ref{prop:rigidity}(iii)).

On a hyperbolic system this could not happen, since by Liv\v{s}ic's theorem
\cite{Liv71,Livsic} a H\"older function which is a continuous coboundary is a
H\"older coboundary.  Corollary \ref{cor:livsic} describes how this fails on
$X_\alpha$: if $f\in{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$ satisfies $\int f\,d\mu_\alpha=0$,
then the continuous transfer functions for $f$ are the functions
$\tilde f-c_f\tau+c$, $c\in\mathbb R$.  So the failure of Liv\v{s}ic regularity is
confined to the single direction $\mathbb R\tau$, along which the transfer function
has coefficient $-c_f$.

\label{thm:E}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $\beta\in(1,2)$ be such that
$d_\beta(1)$ is the maximal Sturmian sequence of rotation number $\alpha$,
and let $\gamma\in(0,1]$.  Then neither $T_\beta$ nor $U_\beta$ has typical
periodic optimization with respect to $C^{0,\gamma}(I)$.  More precisely,
let $\Lambda_\alpha$ be the closure of the $U_\beta$-orbit of $1$, and define
$\widehat F\in C^{0,\gamma}(I)$ by
\[
  \widehat F(t):=(\beta-1)t-\operatorname{dist}(t,\Lambda_\alpha)^\gamma .
\]
Then there is exactly one $\widehat\mu_\alpha\in\mathcal M(I,U_\beta)$ with
$\widehat\mu_\alpha(\Lambda_\alpha)=1$, namely
$\widehat\mu_\alpha=\mu_\alpha\circ h_\beta^{-1}$, it is not a periodic orbit
measure,
and there exists $\delta>0$ such that all $\psi\in C^{0,\gamma}(I)$ with
$|\psi-\widehat F|_\gamma<\delta$ satisfy
\[
  \mathcal M_{\max}(U_\beta,\psi)=\mathcal M_{\max}(T_\beta,\psi)=\{\widehat\mu_\alpha\} .
\]

<!-- thmx-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:F}
Suppose $\beta\in(1,2)$ is not a simple beta-number. Let $\gamma\in(0,1]$ and
$\theta:=\beta^{-\gamma}$.  Let $K\subseteq S_\beta$ be a non-empty closed
set with $\sigma(K)\subseteq K$, such that for some $N\in\mathbb N$ the word $0^N$
occurs in no element of $K$, and let $\Lambda:=h_\beta(K)$.  Then the
following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] some non-empty open $\mathcal V\subseteq{\rm Lip}(S_\beta,d_\theta)$ is such
that each $f\in\mathcal V$ has a maximizing measure $\mu$ with $\mu(K)=1$;
\item[(b)] some non-empty open $\mathcal V'\subseteq C^{0,\gamma}(I)$ is such that
each $g\in\mathcal V'$ has some $\nu\in\mathcal M_{\max}(U_\beta,g)$ with $\nu(\Lambda)=1$.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, $\mathcal V$ and $\mathcal V'$ can then be chosen such that all maximizing
measures of all $f\in\mathcal V$ give full measure to $K$, and all members of
$\mathcal M_{\max}(U_\beta,g)=\mathcal M_{\max}(T_\beta,g)$, $g\in\mathcal V'$, give full measure to
$\Lambda$.  If $K$ contains no periodic orbit and (a) or (b) holds, then
$S_\beta$ does not have TPO with respect to $d_\theta$, and neither $U_\beta$
nor $T_\beta$ has TPO with respect to $C^{0,\gamma}(I)$.

<!-- corx-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:G}
Let $\beta\in(1,2)$, $\gamma\in(0,1]$, and equip the beta-shift
$S_\beta$ with the metric $d_{\beta^{-\gamma}}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] If $S_\beta$ has TPO, then $U_\beta$ has TPO with respect to
$C^{0,\gamma}(I)$, and so does $T_\beta$ unless $\beta$ is a simple
beta-number.
\item[(ii)] If $U_\beta$ has TPO with respect to $C^{0,\gamma}(I)$, then
$S_\beta$ has TPO, provided that $\beta$ is a beta-number, or
$d^*_\beta(1)$ does not belong to its own $\omega$-limit set, or this
$\omega$-limit set contains no periodic orbit.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, if $\beta$ is emergent, then $S_\beta$ has TPO if and only if
$U_\beta$ has TPO with respect to $C^{0,\gamma}(I)$.

<!-- rem-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:markov-boundary}
Theorem \ref{thm:A} may be compared with the structural theory of
\cite{HJXZ}, in which the obstruction to TPO for a shift space $X$ is
located in its \emph{Markov boundary} $\partial_MX$, a subshift of $X$
introduced by Thomsen \cite{Thomsen06} (see \cite[Def.~8.7]{HJXZ}), which is
empty if and only if $X$ is sofic \cite[Lem.~8.9]{HJXZ}.  An open dense
subset of ${\rm Lip}(X)$ consists of functions having a maximizing measure which
is either periodic or supported by $\partial_MX$ \cite[Thm.~9.13]{HJXZ}, and
consequently $X$ has TPO whenever
$\partial_MX$ has TPO \cite[Thm.~1.3]{HJXZ}.  Thomsen
\cite[Thm.~2.8]{Thomsen05} showed that the derived shift space of a
two-sided beta-shift which is not of finite type is the natural extension of
the $\omega$-limit set of the quasi-greedy expansion of $1$, and the
corresponding statement holds for the Markov boundary of a one-sided
beta-shift which is not sofic;\footnote{To see this, suppose that $\mathbf e\in\Sigma$ satisfies the
hypotheses of Definition \ref{def:graph} and is not eventually periodic;
then $Y(\mathbf e)$ (see \eqref{eq:Y(a)}) is a beta-shift which is not
sofic, and all non-sofic beta-shifts arise in this way
\cite{Par60,Bertrand86,blanchard}.  The statement can be checked directly
from \cite[Def.~8.7]{HJXZ}.  For a word $v$ occurring in $Y(\mathbf e)$, let
$\ell(v)$ be the length of the longest suffix of $v$ which is a prefix of
$\mathbf e$.
Let $x\in Y(\mathbf e)$.  As $v$ occurs in $Y(\mathbf e)$, each suffix of $v$
which is not a prefix of $\mathbf e$ is lexicographically smaller than the
prefix of $\mathbf e$ of the same length, so $vx\in Y(\mathbf e)$ if and only
if $x\le_\mathrm{lex}\sigma^m\mathbf e$ for all $m\ge1$ such that the suffix of $v$
of length $m$ is a prefix of $\mathbf e$.  For such $m$ we have
$m\le\ell(v)$, and $\sigma^{\ell(v)}\mathbf e\le_\mathrm{lex}\sigma^m\mathbf e$
because $\sigma^{\ell(v)-m}\mathbf e\le_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf e$ and these two
sequences have the same first $m$ symbols.  Thus $vx\in Y(\mathbf e)$ if
and only if $x\le_\mathrm{lex}\sigma^{\ell(v)}\mathbf e$. So the follower set of $v$,
that is, the set of words $u$ such that $vu$ occurs in $Y(\mathbf e)$,
depends only on $\ell(v)$, while the follower sets of the prefixes of
$\mathbf e$ are pairwise distinct, since the sequences $\sigma^n\mathbf e$
are.  Hence the words $uw$ occurring in $Y(\mathbf e)$ have infinitely many
distinct follower sets if and only if $w$ occurs in $\mathbf e$ at
arbitrarily large positions (if all occurrences of $w$ in $\mathbf e$ start
before position $B$, then $\ell(uw)<B+|w|$), and therefore
$\partial_MY(\mathbf e)$ is the $\omega$-limit set of $\mathbf e$.  The two
notions differ for sofic beta-shifts which are not of finite type: the
derived shift space is then a periodic orbit \cite[Prop.~2.6]{Thomsen05},
whereas the Markov boundary is empty \cite[Lem.~8.9]{HJXZ}.}
since $X_\alpha$ is minimal and contains $\mathbf{a}$, the $\omega$-limit set of
$\mathbf{a}$ is $X_\alpha$, so that
\[
  \partial_MY_\alpha=X_\alpha .
\]
Since $X_\alpha$ contains no periodic orbit, it does not have TPO, so
\cite[Thm.~1.3]{HJXZ} does not apply to $Y_\alpha$; and Theorem
\ref{thm:A} shows that the set of Lipschitz functions having a maximizing
measure supported by $\partial_MY_\alpha$ has non-empty interior, that is,
$Y_\alpha$ is not \emph{fragile} in the sense of \cite[Def.~10.2]{HJXZ}.

The failure of TPO is not determined by the Markov boundary alone: all
beta-shifts $S\ne Y_\alpha$ with $\partial_MS=X_\alpha$ have TPO, with
respect to all metrics $d_\theta$.  To see this, let $\beta\in(1,2)$ be such
that $S=S_\beta$.  As $\partial_MS$ is non-empty, $S$ is not sofic, so
$d^*_\beta(1)$ is not eventually periodic \cite{Bertrand86,blanchard}, and
its $\omega$-limit set is $\partial_MS=X_\alpha$.  In particular
$\mathbf{a}\in S$, so $\mathbf{a}\le_\mathrm{lex} d^*_\beta(1)$, and the inequality is strict as
$S\ne Y_\alpha$.  The quasi-greedy expansion $d^*_{\beta'}(1)$ increases to
$d^*_\beta(1)$ as $\beta'$ increases to $\beta$
\cite[Prop.~3.9(xiii),(xiv)]{HHJL}, and simple beta-numbers are dense in
$(1,\infty)$ \cite{Par60}, so there is a simple beta-number $\beta'<\beta$
with $\mathbf{a}\le_\mathrm{lex} d^*_{\beta'}(1)$.  Then $X_\alpha\subseteq Y_\alpha
\subseteq S_{\beta'}\subseteq S$, where $S_{\beta'}$ is a shift of finite
type, as $d^*_{\beta'}(1)$ is periodic (see e.g.~\cite{blanchard}), and
therefore has TPO \cite[Lem.~9.4]{HJXZ}; so $S$ has TPO by
\cite[Prop.~9.14]{HJXZ}.  For example, if $\mathbf{a}$ begins with $1^k0$ then
$S:=Y(1\mathbf{a})$ (cf.~Definition \ref{def:graph}) is such a beta-shift, and
one may take for $\beta'$ the simple beta-number with
$d_{\beta'}(1)=1^{k+1}0^\infty$, whose beta-shift is the set of sequences in
which the word $1^{k+1}$ does not occur.  The parameters $\beta$ of these
beta-shifts $S$ are non-emergent \cite[Prop.~5.3]{HHJL}, as the orbit
closure of $d^*_\beta(1)$ properly contains the closed invariant set
$X_\alpha$, so the beta-transformations $T_\beta$ and $U_\beta$ also have
TPO \cite[Thm.~J]{HHJL}; by contrast the parameter of $Y_\alpha$ is
emergent, and neither $T_\beta$ nor $U_\beta$ has TPO (Theorem
\ref{thm:E}).

<!-- lem-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:stability}
Let $X$ be a subshift equipped with a metric $d_\theta$, let
$f\in{\rm Lip}(X,d_\theta)$, let $\mu\in\mathcal M(X,\sigma)$, and let $\delta>0$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
  \int f\,d\mu-\int f\,d\nu\ \ge\ \delta\,W_\theta(\nu,\mu)
  \qquad\text{for all }\nu\in\mathcal M(X,\sigma).
\end{equation}
Then $\mathcal M_{\max}(f+g)=\{\mu\}$ for all $g\in{\rm Lip}(X,d_\theta)$ with
$|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}<\delta$.
<!-- end lem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}<\delta$ and let $\nu\in\mathcal M(X,\sigma)\setminus\{\mu\}$, so
that $W_\theta(\nu,\mu)>0$.  If $g$ is constant then
$\int g\,d\mu-\int g\,d\nu=0$, and otherwise applying \eqref{eq:wass} to
$g/|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}$ gives
$\int g\,d\mu-\int g\,d\nu\ge-|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}W_\theta(\nu,\mu)$; in either
case, by \eqref{eq:gap},
\[
  \int(f+g)\,d\mu-\int(f+g)\,d\nu
  \ \ge\ \bigl(\delta-|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}\bigr)W_\theta(\nu,\mu)\ >\ 0 .
\]
So $\mu$ is the unique maximizing measure for $f+g$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- rem-4: body_md -->
\label{rem:converse}
The converse of Lemma \ref{lem:stability} also holds: if
$\mu\in\mathcal M_{\max}(f+g)$ for all $g\in{\rm Lip}(X,d_\theta)$ with
$|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}<\delta$, then \eqref{eq:gap} holds.  Indeed, suppose
\eqref{eq:gap} fails for some $\nu$ (necessarily with $\nu\ne\mu$ and hence
$W_\theta(\nu,\mu)>0$), and choose $\delta'\in[0,\delta)$ with
$\int f\,d\mu-\int f\,d\nu<\delta'W_\theta(\nu,\mu)$.  By \eqref{eq:wass}
there is a function $h$ with $|h|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}\le1$ such that
$\int h\,d\nu-\int h\,d\mu$ is close enough to $W_\theta(\nu,\mu)$ that
$g:=\delta'h$ satisfies $\int(f+g)\,d\nu>\int(f+g)\,d\mu$; then
$|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}<\delta$ and $\mu\notin\mathcal M_{\max}(f+g)$.  So the stability of
$\mu$ as a maximizing measure is equivalent to an inequality of the form
\eqref{eq:gap}.

<!-- lem-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:coding}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $\mathbf{a}$ be the maximal Sturmian
sequence \eqref{eq:a} of rotation number $\alpha$, let $\mathbf{b}$ be the Sturmian coding
(cf.~Definition \ref{def:coding}), and let $I_0:=[0,1-\alpha)$ and
$I_1:=[1-\alpha,1)$, regarded as subsets of $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$.  Then for all
$t\in\mathbb R$ and $i\ge0$, the digit $\mathbf{b}(t)_i$ equals $1$ if
$t+i\alpha\in I_1$ modulo $1$, and equals $0$ otherwise.  Moreover $\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}^{-}(0)$, and $a_i=\mathbf{b}(0)_i$
for all $i\ge1$, whereas $a_0=1$ and $\mathbf{b}(0)_0=0$.  Finally,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shiftcoding}
  \sigma\mathbf{b}(t)=\mathbf{b}(R_\alpha t),\qquad
  \sigma\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t)=\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(R_\alpha t).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Writing $s:=\{t+i\alpha\}$, \eqref{eq:coding} gives
$\mathbf{b}(t)_i=\lfloor s+\alpha\rfloor$, which equals $1$ if $s+\alpha\ge1$ and
$0$ otherwise, since $0\le s+\alpha<2$.  As $s+\alpha\ge1$ if and only if
$s\ge1-\alpha$, i.e.~if and only if $t+i\alpha\in I_1$ modulo $1$, the
first statement follows; and \eqref{eq:shiftcoding} is immediate from
\eqref{eq:coding}.  For $i\ge1$
neither $i\alpha$ nor $(i+1)\alpha$ is an integer, so
$\lceil j\alpha\rceil=\lfloor j\alpha\rfloor+1$ for $j\in\{i,i+1\}$ and
hence $a_i=\mathbf{b}(0)_i$, whereas $a_0=\lceil\alpha\rceil=1$ and
$\mathbf{b}(0)_0=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor=0$.  Finally, given $n\in\mathbb N$, choose $\eta>0$ smaller
than the distance from each of $\alpha,2\alpha,\ldots,n\alpha$ to the
nearest integer; then $\lfloor-\eta+i\alpha\rfloor=\lceil
i\alpha\rceil-1$ for $i\le n$, so that
$\mathbf{b}(-\eta)_i=a_i$ for $i<n$; letting $\eta\downarrow0$ and $n\to\infty$
gives $\mathbf{b}^{-}(0)=\mathbf{a}$.

\label{not:cuts}
For $k\in\mathbb N$ and $v\in\{0,1\}^k$ set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Iv}
  I_v:=\{t\in[0,1):\mathbf{b}(t)_{[0,k)}=v\},
\end{equation}
and for
$j\ge0$ set
\[
  c_j:=\{-j\alpha\} .
\]
We call $c_0,\ldots,c_k$ the \emph{cuts of level $k$}, i.e.~the
first $k+1$ terms of the single sequence $(c_j)_{j\ge0}$.
When $I_v$ is the argument of an
indicator function on $\mathbb R$, it is identified with the $1$-periodic set
$I_v+\mathbb Z$.

<!-- lem-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:cuts}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $k\in\mathbb N$, and let the intervals
$I_v$, $v\in\{0,1\}^k$, and the cuts $c_j$ be as in Notation
\ref{not:cuts}.  Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] the cuts of level $k$ are $k+1$ distinct points of $[0,1)$, one of
which is $c_0=0$,
and they divide $[0,1)$ into $k+1$ intervals of positive length, each of the
form $[c,c')$ with $c$ a cut of level $k$ and $c'$ either a cut of level $k$
or $1$;
\item[(ii)] each of these $k+1$ intervals equals $I_v$ for exactly one
$v\in\{0,1\}^k$, distinct intervals corresponding to distinct words, and
$I_v=\emptyset$ for all other $v$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Summing \eqref{eq:coding} over $i<j$ gives
$\sum_{i<j}\mathbf{b}(t)_i=\lfloor t+j\alpha\rfloor$ for $t\in[0,1)$, so the word
$\mathbf{b}(t)_{[0,k)}$ is determined by the $k$ integers
$\lfloor t+j\alpha\rfloor$, $1\le j\le k$.  As $t$ increases through
$[0,1)$, the $j$th of these is non-decreasing, and increases by one when
$t$ passes $c_j$.  Since $\alpha$ is irrational the points
$c_0,\ldots,c_k$ are distinct, which gives (i).
Each of these $k$ integers is a right continuous step function of $t$, so all
of them are constant on each of the intervals $[c,c')$ in (i), and hence so is
$\mathbf{b}(t)_{[0,k)}$;
and if $t<t'$ lie in different intervals then some $c_j$ with $1\le j\le k$
separates them, so that $\lfloor t+j\alpha\rfloor<\lfloor t'+j\alpha\rfloor$
and the two words differ,
thus giving (ii).

<!-- lem-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:sturmian}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, with $\mathbf{a}$ the maximal Sturmian
sequence \eqref{eq:a} of rotation number $\alpha$, and let $X_\alpha$ and $Y_\alpha$ be the
Sturmian subshift, and Sturmian beta-shift, of rotation number $\alpha$.
Let $\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}_\alpha$ be the Sturmian coding
(cf.~Definition \ref{def:coding}),
let the intervals $I_v$ be as in Notation
\ref{not:cuts}, and let $\Phi$ be
the digit function (cf.~Definition \ref{def:functions}).   Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $X_\alpha=\{\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t):t\in\mathbb R\}$, and $X_\alpha$ is minimal,
non-periodic and uniquely ergodic, with invariant measure
$\mu_\alpha=\mathbf{b}_*\mathrm{Leb}$;
\item[(ii)] $\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)=\{v\in\{0,1\}^k:I_v\ne\emptyset\}$ has exactly
$k+1$ elements, and $\mu_\alpha([v]_{X_\alpha})=|I_v|$ for all
$v\in\{0,1\}^k$, with the convention $|I_v|=0$ when $I_v=\emptyset$;
\item[(iii)] $\int\Phi\,d\mu_\alpha=|I_1|=\alpha$;
\item[(iv)] all $v\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$ satisfy
$\lfloor k\alpha\rfloor\le|v|_1\le\lceil k\alpha\rceil$, and consequently
$|S_k\Phi(x)-k\alpha|<1$ for all $x\in X_\alpha$ and $k\in\mathbb N_0$;
\item[(v)] $\mathbf{a}=\max_\mathrm{lex} X_\alpha$ and $\sigma^j\mathbf{a}<_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{a}$ for all
$j\ge1$; consequently there is a unique $\beta\in(1,2)$ with
$\mathbf{a}=d_\beta(1)$, the set $Y_\alpha$ of \eqref{eq:Yalpha} is the beta-shift
of that $\beta$, and $X_\alpha\subseteq Y_\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lem-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
These are standard; see \cite{MH40} and \cite[Ch.~2]{Lothaire}, or
\cite[Ch.~6]{Fogg}.  For the first equality in (i), note that
\eqref{eq:coding} exhibits $\mathbf{b}(t)$ as the lower mechanical word of slope
$\alpha$ and intercept $t$, while
$\mathbf{b}^{-}(t)_i=\lceil t+(i+1)\alpha\rceil-\lceil t+i\alpha\rceil$ exhibits
$\mathbf{b}^{-}(t)$ as the upper mechanical word of the same slope and intercept;
so $\{\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t):t\in\mathbb R\}$ is the set of all mechanical words of slope
$\alpha$, which by the theorem of Morse \& Hedlund
\cite[Thm.~2.1.13]{Lothaire} is the set of all Sturmian words of slope
$\alpha$.  The factors of such a word depend only on its slope
\cite[Prop.~2.1.18]{Lothaire}, so that set is the subshift generated by any
one of its elements, which is $X_\alpha$.  Part (ii) is the count of
the $k+1$ intervals in Lemma \ref{lem:cuts} together with unique ergodicity
of $R_\alpha$ applied to the intervals $I_v$.  Part (iv) follows from
$|v|_1=\lfloor t+k\alpha\rfloor-\lfloor t\rfloor$ for $t\in I_v$.  For (v),
the identity $\sum_{i<j}\mathbf{b}(t)_i=\lfloor t+j\alpha\rfloor$ shows that for
$0\le t<s<1$, at the first index where the partial sums of $\mathbf{b}(t)$ and
$\mathbf{b}(s)$ differ the digit of $\mathbf{b}(t)$ is the smaller; hence
$\mathbf{b}(t)\le_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{b}(s)$, and letting $s\uparrow1$ and using (i) gives
$\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}^{-}(0)=\max_\mathrm{lex} X_\alpha$.  As $X_\alpha$ is non-periodic the
inequality $\sigma^j\mathbf{a}\le_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{a}$ is strict for $j\ge1$, so Parry's
characterisation \cite{Par60} of the sequences arising as $d_\beta(1)$
applies; and $X_\alpha\subseteq Y_\alpha$ because $\sigma^jx\le_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{a}$
for all $x\in X_\alpha$ and $j\ge0$.

\label{def:graph}
Let $\mathbf{a}=(a_i)_{i\ge0}\in\Sigma$ be such that $a_0=1$ and
$\sigma^j\mathbf{a}\le_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{a}$ for all $j\ge1$.  We define the \emph{graph of
$\mathbf{a}$} to mean the directed labelled graph $G(\mathbf{a})$ with vertex set
$\mathbb N_0$ whose edges are, for each $r\in\mathbb N_0$, an edge from $r$ to $r+1$
labelled $a_r$, together with an edge from $r$ to $0$ labelled $0$ in the
case $a_r=1$.  A \emph{first return loop} of $G(\mathbf{a})$ is a path from the
vertex $0$ to itself, of length at least $1$, visiting the vertex $0$ only
at its two endpoints; we identify such a path with the word formed by its
labels.  Finally set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Y(a)}
  Y(\mathbf{a}):=\{x\in\Sigma:\sigma^jx\le_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{a}\ \text{ for all }j\ge0\} .
\end{equation}
<!-- end defn-5 -->
<!-- rem-5: body_md -->
\label{rem:general}
By Parry's criterion \cite{Par60} the hypotheses of Definition
\ref{def:graph} are satisfied by $\mathbf{a}=d_\beta(1)$ whenever $\beta\in(1,2)$
is such that $d_\beta(1)$ is not eventually zero, and $Y(\mathbf{a})$ is then the
beta-shift of $\beta$.  The remaining $\beta\in(1,2)$ are covered as well,
after replacing $d_\beta(1)$ by the quasi-greedy expansion $d^{*}_\beta(1)$,
which is periodic and satisfies the same two hypotheses.  So Lemma
\ref{lem:presentation} below is a statement about all beta-shifts.  If
$\mathbf{a}$ is not eventually periodic then $G(\mathbf{a})$ is the Fischer automaton of
$Y(\mathbf{a})$ in the sense of Blanchard \& Hansel \cite{BH86}, and in general the
presentation is standard; see
\cite[Sect.~5]{PS05}, \cite[Sect.~5.1]{Tho12} and \cite[Sect.~3.2]{GK}.  The short
proof of Lemma \ref{lem:presentation} is included because the precise form
of $G(\mathbf{a})$ is used at various points below.

<!-- lem-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:presentation}
Let $\mathbf{a}=(a_i)_{i\ge0}\in\Sigma$ be such that $a_0=1$ and
$\sigma^j\mathbf{a}\le_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{a}$ for all $j\ge1$, and let $G(\mathbf{a})$ and $Y(\mathbf{a})$
be as in Definition \ref{def:graph}.  Then $Y(\mathbf{a})$ is the set of
$x\in\Sigma$ labelling an infinite path in $G(\mathbf{a})$ that starts at the
vertex $0$.  Moreover, the first return loops of $G(\mathbf{a})$ are precisely the
words
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rn}
  R_n:=a_0a_1\cdots a_{n-2}\,0\qquad
  \bigl(n\in\mathcal N(\mathbf{a}):=\{n\ge1:a_{n-1}=1\}\bigr),
\end{equation}
the word $R_n$ having length $n$; in particular $1\in\mathcal N(\mathbf{a})$ and
$R_1=0$.
<!-- end lem-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
For $r\in\mathbb N_0$ let $\mathcal Q_r$ denote the set of $y\in\Sigma$ labelling an
infinite path in $G(\mathbf{a})$ starting at the vertex $r$, and let
$(s_j)_{j\ge0}$, with $s_0=r$, be the vertex sequence of that path.  We
claim that $y\in\mathcal Q_r$ implies $y\le_\mathrm{lex}\sigma^r\mathbf{a}$.  If $s_j\ne0$ for
all $j\ge1$ then inductively $s_j=r+j$ and $y_j=a_{r+j}$, so
$y=\sigma^r\mathbf{a}$.  Otherwise let $h\ge1$ be the smallest integer such that $s_h=0$; then
$y_j=a_{r+j}$ for $j\le h-2$, while the $h$th edge runs from $s_{h-1}=r+h-1$
to $0$ and is therefore labelled $0$, so $y_{h-1}=0<1=a_{r+h-1}$ and
$y<_\mathrm{lex}\sigma^r\mathbf{a}$.

If $x$ labels an infinite path starting at $0$ then
$\sigma^jx\in\mathcal Q_{r_j(x)}$, so $\sigma^jx\le_\mathrm{lex}\sigma^{r_j(x)}\mathbf{a}
\le_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{a}$ by hypothesis, and $x\in Y(\mathbf{a})$.  Conversely, let
$x\in Y(\mathbf{a})$ and suppose $r_j(x)$ were undefined for some smallest $j$,
necessarily $j\ge1$.  Writing $\rho:=r_{j-1}(x)$, no edge labelled
$x_{j-1}$ leaves the vertex $\rho$.  The labels of the edges leaving $\rho$
are $a_\rho$, and also $0$ in the case $a_\rho=1$; so $a_\rho=0$, since
otherwise both labels occur, and consequently $x_{j-1}=1>0=a_\rho$.  As
$a_0=1$ we have $\rho\ge1$, so $\rho=r_{j-2}(x)+1$ and
$x_{j-2}=a_{r_{j-2}(x)}$; iterating downwards gives
$r_{j-1-\ell}(x)=\rho-\ell$ and $x_{j-1-\ell}=a_{\rho-\ell}$ for
$1\le\ell\le\rho$.  Setting $p:=j-1-\rho$ this gives $x_{p+i}=a_i$ for
$0\le i<\rho$, while $x_{p+\rho}=x_{j-1}>a_\rho$, so that
$\sigma^p x>_\mathrm{lex}\mathbf{a}$, contradicting that $x\in Y(\mathbf{a})$.

For the last statement,
following the edges labelled
$a_0,\ldots,a_{n-2}$ from the vertex $0$
leads to the vertex $n-1$ without returning to $0$, and a final symbol $0$
then returns to $0$
if and only if $a_{n-1}=1$; all first return loops
arise in this way.  Since $a_0=1$ we have $1\in\mathcal N(\mathbf{a})$ and $R_1=0$.

<!-- lem-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:spec}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $\mathbf{a}$ be the maximal Sturmian
sequence \eqref{eq:a} of rotation number $\alpha$, and let $Y_\alpha$ be the
Sturmian beta-shift of rotation number $\alpha$.  Then there is $N\in\mathbb N$ such
that the word $0^N$ does not occur in $\mathbf{a}$.  For all such $N$, and all words
$u,v$ occurring in $Y_\alpha$, the word $u0^Nv$ occurs in $Y_\alpha$, and
$(u0^N)^\infty\in Y_\alpha$.  In particular $Y_\alpha$ has the specification
property.
<!-- end lem-6 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
If $0^N$ occurred in $\mathbf{a}$ for all $N\in\mathbb N$ then $0^\infty$ would lie in
$X_\alpha$, and $X_\alpha$ would equal $\{0^\infty\}$ by minimality; this proves
the first statement.  Now fix such an $N$.  All words occurring in
$Y_\alpha$ are prefixes of points of $Y_\alpha$, since
$\sigma(Y_\alpha)\subseteq Y_\alpha$, so by Lemma \ref{lem:presentation}
each of them labels a path in $G_\alpha$ starting at the vertex $0$.  An edge labelled $0$
leaves each vertex $r$: it runs to $0$ if $a_r=1$, and to $r+1$ if $a_r=0$;
in particular the edge labelled $0$ leaving the vertex $0$ is a loop.  A path
labelled $0^N$ which never visits the vertex $0$ uses only edges of the form
$r\to r+1$, so its label is a word occurring in $\mathbf{a}$, which is impossible;
hence all paths labelled $0^N$ end at the vertex $0$.  So if $u$ labels a path
starting at $0$ then $u0^N$ labels a path from $0$ to $0$, and the sequences
$u0^Nv0^\infty$ and $(u0^N)^\infty$ label infinite paths starting at the
vertex $0$; by Lemma \ref{lem:presentation} they lie in $Y_\alpha$.  (The
specification property of $Y_\alpha$ can also be deduced from the theorem of
Bertrand-Mathis \cite{Bertrand86,Bertrand88} that a beta-shift has the
specification property if and only if the strings of zeros in $d_\beta(1)$
have bounded length; see also \cite[Prop.~3.5]{Sch97} and

\label{lem:returnwords}

\end{equation}

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:Phimax}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $Y_\alpha$ be the Sturmian
beta-shift of rotation number $\alpha$, with Sturmian measure $\mu_\alpha$,
and let $\Phi$ be the digit function (cf.~Definition \ref{def:functions}).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform}
  S_n\Phi(x)=\sum_{i<n}x_i\ \le\ n\alpha+1
  \qquad\text{for all }x\in Y_\alpha\text{ and all }n\in\mathbb N .
\end{equation}
Consequently $\int\Phi\,d\nu\le\alpha$ for all $\nu\in\mathcal M(Y_\alpha,\sigma)$,
with equality for $\nu=\mu_\alpha$, so that $\mu_\alpha\in\mathcal M_{\max}(\Phi)$.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $x\in Y_\alpha$.
By Lemma \ref{lem:presentation}, whose hypotheses hold for $\mathbf{a}$ as noted above, $x$ labels
an infinite path in $G_\alpha$ from the
vertex $0$, with vertex sequence $(r_j(x))_{j\ge0}$.  Fix $n\in\mathbb N$ and let
$0=q_0<q_1<\cdots<q_s\le n$ be an enumeration of those $j\le n$ with $r_j(x)=0$, a
finite and non-empty list.  For $1\le i\le s$ the segment
$x_{[q_{i-1},q_i)}$ is a first return loop, so
$x_{[q_{i-1},q_i)}=R_{n_i}$ with $n_i:=q_i-q_{i-1}\in\mathcal N$ by
Lemma \ref{lem:presentation}.

For the final segment, set $m:=n-q_s\ge0$.  For $q_s<j\le n$ we have
$r_j(x)\ne0$, and the only edge from a vertex $h$ which does not lead to
$0$ is the one labelled $a_h$ leading to $h+1$; so inductively
$r_{q_s+h}(x)=h$ and $x_{q_s+h}=a_h$ for $0\le h\le m-1$, that is
$x_{[q_s,n)}=a_{[0,m)}$, whose number of digits $1$ is
$\lceil m\alpha\rceil$ by \eqref{eq:prefixsum}.

Combining these with \eqref{eq:defect},
\[
  \sum_{i<n}x_i=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lfloor n_i\alpha\rfloor+\lceil m\alpha\rceil
  \le\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_i\alpha+m\alpha+1=n\alpha+1 ,
\]
which is \eqref{eq:uniform}.

Now let $\nu\in\mathcal M(Y_\alpha,\sigma)$.  Integrating \eqref{eq:uniform} and
using invariance of $\nu$ gives
$n\int\Phi\,d\nu=\int S_n\Phi\,d\nu\le n\alpha+1$, so
$\int\Phi\,d\nu\le\alpha+1/n$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$, and therefore
$\int\Phi\,d\nu\le\alpha$.  Finally $\mu_\alpha\in\mathcal M(Y_\alpha,\sigma)$ by
Lemma \ref{lem:sturmian}(v), and $\int\Phi\,d\mu_\alpha=\alpha$ by Lemma
\ref{lem:sturmian}(iii).

\label{not:cob}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $X_\alpha$ be the Sturmian subshift
of rotation number $\alpha$, let $\Phi$ be as in Definition \ref{def:functions}, let the
cuts $c_j$ and the intervals $I_v$ be as in Notation \ref{not:cuts}, and
let $k\in\mathbb N$.
By Lemmas \ref{lem:cuts}
and \ref{lem:sturmian}(ii),
each $v\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$ determines an interval
$I_v$ whose endpoints are cuts of level $k$, and we write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoints}
  I_v=[c_i,c_j),
\end{equation}
where $i=i(v)$ and $j=j(v)$ satisfy $0\le i,j\le k$ and $i\ne j$, and where
the right endpoint is to be taken as $1$ in the case $j=0$.

\[
  \gamma_{v,r}:=
  \begin{cases}

  \end{cases}
  \qquad(0\le r\le k-1),

\end{equation}

$$\phi_v:=\mathbbm{1}_{I_v}-|I_v|,$$

$v\in\{0,1\}^k\setminus\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$ then we set $B_v:=0$, $|I_v|:=0$,
$\gamma_{v,r}:=0$, and $\phi_v:=0$.

$H_r(t):=\{t+r\alpha\}$, define functions on $\Sigma$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kr}
  K_0:=0,\qquad K_r:=S_r(\alpha-\Phi)\quad(r\ge1),
\end{equation}
and set
\[
  G_v:=\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{v,r}H_r,\qquad
  V_v:=\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{v,r}K_r .
\]

\label{lem:Bvbounds}
Let $\alpha$, $k$ and $v$ be as in Notation \ref{not:cob}, and let $B_v$,
$K_r$, $G_v$ and $V_v$ be as defined there.  If $v\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$ then
the non-zero $\gamma_{v,r}$ all have the same sign, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bvalue}
  B_v=j-i,\qquad |B_v|\le k .
\end{equation}
For all $v\in\{0,1\}^k$ and $r\in\mathbb N_0$, the function $K_r$ depends on the
coordinates $x_0,\ldots,x_{r-1}$ only, the function $V_v$ depends on the
coordinates $x_0,\ldots,x_{k-2}$ only, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Krbound}
  \|K_r\|_{C(\Sigma)}\le r\max(\alpha,1-\alpha)\le r ,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Vvbound}
  \|V_v\|_{C(\Sigma)}\le\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}r=\tfrac12k(k-1) .
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let $v\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$.  Since $i\ne j$, at most one of the first two
cases in the definition of $\gamma_{v,r}$ arises for any given $r$, and
only one of the two arises at all, so the non-zero $\gamma_{v,r}$ all have

$|B_v|\le k$ because $0\le i,j\le k$.

For the remaining statements, $K_r$ is by \eqref{eq:Kr} a sum of $r$ terms

statements about $V_v$ follow, since $V_v$ is a linear combination of
$K_0,\ldots,K_{k-1}$ with coefficients in $\{-1,0,1\}$.

\label{lem:coboundary}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $X_\alpha$ be the Sturmian subshift
of rotation number $\alpha$, let $k\in\mathbb N$, let $v\in\{0,1\}^k$, and let
$\phi_v$, $H_r$ and $G_v$ be as in Notation \ref{not:cob}.  If
$v\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$, with $i$ and $j$ as in \eqref{eq:endpoints}, then
\[
  \phi_v=H_j-H_i=G_v\circ R_\alpha-G_v .
\]
If $v\notin\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$ then $\phi_v=G_v=0$.
<!-- end lem-9 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
The second statement is immediate from Notation \ref{not:cob}, so let
$v\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$.  For $0\le p<q\le1$ and $t\in[0,1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:indicator}
  \mathbbm{1}_{[p,q)}(t)-(q-p)=\{t-q\}-\{t-p\} ,
\end{equation}
as is checked separately on the three intervals $[0,p)$, $[p,q)$ and
$[q,1)$, on which the right-hand side takes the values $p-q$, $1-q+p$ and
$p-q$ respectively.  Note that $c_i<c_j$ whenever $j\ne0$, since $I_v$ is a
non-empty interval, and that $c_j$ is taken as $1$ when $j=0$; so we may
take $p=c_i$ and $q=c_j$ in \eqref{eq:indicator}.  Using
$c_i\equiv-i\alpha$ and $c_j\equiv-j\alpha$ modulo $1$ gives
$\phi_v=H_j-H_i$; here the case $j=0$ is covered because
$\{t-1\}=\{t\}=H_0(t)$.

For the second equality, $H_r(R_\alpha t)=\{t+(r+1)\alpha\}=H_{r+1}(t)$, so
by Notation \ref{not:cob},
\[
  G_v\circ R_\alpha-G_v=\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{v,r}(H_{r+1}-H_r) .
\]
If $i<j$ then $\gamma_{v,r}=1$ exactly for $i\le r<j$ and the sum is
$H_j-H_i$; if $j<i$ then $\gamma_{v,r}=-1$ exactly for $j\le r<i$ and the
sum is $-(H_i-H_j)$, which is again $H_j-H_i$.

\label{rem:brs}
Lemma \ref{lem:coboundary} is a special case of a classical result.  An
interval whose length lies in $\mathbb Z\alpha+\mathbb Z$ is a \emph{bounded remainder
set} for $R_\alpha$, meaning that its centred indicator function is an
$R_\alpha$-coboundary with bounded transfer function; this is due to Hecke
\cite{Hecke} and Ostrowski \cite{Ost27,Ost30},
while Kesten \cite{Kesten}
proved the converse, confirming a conjecture of Erd\H{o}s \& Sz\"usz.  The
intervals $I_v$ satisfy the condition, since
$|I_v|=c_j-c_i\equiv(i-j)\alpha$ modulo $1$.
What is needed below, however, is not the boundedness of a transfer
function but the explicit form of $G_v$, which, up to sign, is the sum of
the functions $H_r$ with $\min\{i,j\}\le r<\max\{i,j\}$: it is this form
which transports to $X_\alpha$ in Proposition \ref{prop:centred-cylinder}.
For more on bounded
remainder sets, including transfer functions constructed in this way, see
\cite{GL}.

\label{prop:centred-cylinder}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $X_\alpha$ be the Sturmian subshift
of rotation number $\alpha$, with Sturmian measure $\mu_\alpha$, let $\Phi$
be the digit function (cf.~Definition \ref{def:functions}), let $k\in\mathbb N$ and $v\in\{0,1\}^k$, let $B_v$ and
$V_v$ be as in Notation \ref{not:cob}, and set $C:=[v]_{X_\alpha}$.  Then
the centred indicator function of $C$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centred-cylinder}
  \mathbbm{1}_{C}-\mu_\alpha(C)=-B_v(\Phi-\alpha)+V_v\circ\sigma-V_v
  \qquad\text{on }X_\alpha .
\end{equation}
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
We may assume $v\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$, since otherwise both sides vanish.
The key identity is \eqref{eq:Gvpullback} below, which expresses the circle
transfer function $G_v$ of Lemma \ref{lem:coboundary}, evaluated at
$t^{\pm}$, as the sum of $V_v(\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t))$ and the multiple
$B_vH_0(t^{\pm})$.
The first term yields the coboundary
$V_v\circ\sigma-V_v$ of \eqref{eq:centred-cylinder}, and the second
yields the multiple of $\Phi-\alpha$, since $H_0$ evaluated in this way is
a transfer function for $\alpha-\Phi$.

Let $t\in\mathbb R$.  By Lemma \ref{lem:Krcoding},
$H_r(t^{\pm})=K_r(\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t))+H_0(t^{\pm})$ for $0\le r<k$; multiplying
by $\gamma_{v,r}$, summing over $r$, and using Notation \ref{not:cob} and
\eqref{eq:Bv}, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gvpullback}
  G_v(t^{\pm})=V_v\bigl(\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t)\bigr)+B_vH_0(t^{\pm}) .
\end{equation}
Since \eqref{eq:Gvpullback} holds for all $t\in\mathbb R$, it holds in particular
with $R_\alpha t$ in place of $t$; moreover
$\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(R_\alpha t)=\sigma\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t)$ by \eqref{eq:shiftcoding}.
Writing $x:=\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t)$, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gvpullbackshift}
  G_v\bigl((R_\alpha t)^{\pm}\bigr)
  =V_v(\sigma x)+B_vH_0\bigl((R_\alpha t)^{\pm}\bigr) ,
\end{equation}
and subtracting \eqref{eq:Gvpullback} from \eqref{eq:Gvpullbackshift} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gvdifference}
  G_v\bigl((R_\alpha t)^{\pm}\bigr)-G_v(t^{\pm})
  =\bigl(V_v(\sigma x)-V_v(x)\bigr)
   +B_v\bigl(H_0\bigl((R_\alpha t)^{\pm}\bigr)-H_0(t^{\pm})\bigr) .
\end{equation}
The left-hand side of \eqref{eq:Gvdifference} is $\phi_v(t^{\pm})$, by
Lemma \ref{lem:coboundary} together with the fact that $R_\alpha$ is
increasing, so that if $s\to t^{\pm}$ then $R_\alpha s\to(R_\alpha t)^{\pm}$.
Using that
$H_1=H_0\circ R_\alpha$,
together with
Lemma
\ref{lem:Krcoding} and \eqref{eq:Kr},
we see that
\[
H_0\bigl((R_\alpha t)^{\pm}\bigr)-H_0(t^{\pm})
=
H_1(t^{\pm})-H_0(t^{\pm})
=K_1(x)= (\alpha-\Phi)(x),
\]
so
\eqref{eq:Gvdifference} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phiv}
  \phi_v(t^{\pm})
  =\bigl(V_v(\sigma x)-V_v(x)\bigr)+B_v(\alpha-\Phi)(x) .
\end{equation}
Finally, $\phi_v(t^{\pm})=\mathbbm{1}_{I_v}(t^{\pm})-|I_v|=\mathbbm{1}_C(x)-\mu_\alpha(C)$,
by Notation \ref{not:cob}, \eqref{eq:Iv} and Lemma \ref{lem:sturmian}(ii),
so \eqref{eq:phiv} gives
\[
  \mathbbm{1}_C(x)-\mu_\alpha(C)
  =-B_v(\Phi-\alpha)(x)+(V_v\circ\sigma-V_v)(x) .
\]
By Lemma \ref{lem:sturmian}(i) all points of $X_\alpha$ are of the form
$\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t)$ for some $t\in\mathbb R$, so this proves
\eqref{eq:centred-cylinder}.

\label{rem:intrinsic}
For $\alpha$ and $k$ as in Proposition \ref{prop:centred-cylinder}, and
$v\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$, the integer $B_v$ and the measure $\mu_\alpha([v]_{X_\alpha})$ are
determined by the Birkhoff sums $K_0,\ldots,K_k$ on $[v]$, in the following way.
Note that $K_0,\ldots,K_k$ are constant on $[v]$, since $K_r$ depends only on
$x_0,\ldots,x_{r-1}$.  For $t\in I_v$, Lemma \ref{lem:Krcoding} gives
$K_r(\mathbf{b}(t))=H_r(t)-H_0(t)$, which equals $-c_r$ if $c_r\le t$ and $1-c_r$ if
$c_r>t$.  These $k+1$ values are distinct, those of the first kind lying in
$(-1,0]$ and those of the second kind in $(0,1)$.  No cut of level $k$ lies in
the interior of $I_v$, so the smallest value is attained only at $r=i$ and the
greatest only at $r=j$ (the greatest value being $0$, attained at $r=0$, if
$j=0$), and their difference is $1-|I_v|$.
Writing $r_-(v)$ and $r_+(v)$ for the
indices in $\{0,1,\ldots,k\}$ at which $K_r$ is smallest and greatest on
$[v]$, we therefore have
\[
  B_v=r_+(v)-r_-(v),\qquad
  \mu_\alpha\bigl([v]_{X_\alpha}\bigr)
  =1-\Bigl(\max_{0\le r\le k}K_r-\min_{0\le r\le k}K_r\Bigr),
\]
the second of which is the formula of Bousch \& Mairesse
\cite[Prop.~1.2]{BM02} for the measure of a cylinder set under a Sturmian
measure, in the form given in
\cite[Lem.~3.13]{AJ}.

<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Set $L:=|f|_{{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha)}$.  The proof is in five steps.  Steps 1 and 2
expand $f$, up to an additive constant, as a series of centred indicator
functions of cylinder sets, with coefficients decaying like $\theta^{n}$;
this is where the Sturmian hypothesis is used, through
the fact that $\mathbb{W}_n(X_\alpha)$ has exactly $n+1$ elements.  Step 3 applies
Proposition \ref{prop:centred-cylinder} to each term of that series and sums the results,
which proves \eqref{eq:cohomology} and \eqref{eq:c-bound}.
Steps 4 and 5 rewrite the transfer function as a series in
$(\alpha-\Phi)\circ\sigma^{h}$ and bound its Lipschitz seminorm, which
gives \eqref{eq:u-bound}.
Once the uniqueness statements are available,
\eqref{eq:c-bound} and \eqref{eq:u-bound} are upgraded from statements
about the particular $c_f$ and $\tilde f$ constructed here to statements
about invariants of $f$.

\emph{Step 1: a refining sequence of partitions, and representatives.}
Set $\mathcal A_0:=\{X_\alpha\}$ and, for $n\in\mathbb N$,
\[
  \mathcal A_n:=\bigl\{[v]_{X_\alpha}:v\in\mathbb{W}_n(X_\alpha)\bigr\},
\]
a partition of $X_\alpha$ into $|\mathbb{W}_n(X_\alpha)|$ non-empty clopen sets.  We
claim that, for all $n\in\mathbb N_0$, exactly one atom of $\mathcal A_n$ is the union of
two atoms of $\mathcal A_{n+1}$, while all other atoms of $\mathcal A_n$ are themselves
atoms of $\mathcal A_{n+1}$.

Indeed, this is immediate for $n=0$, since $\mathcal A_0$ has the single atom
$X_\alpha$ and $\mathcal A_1$ the two atoms $[0]_{X_\alpha}$ and $[1]_{X_\alpha}$.
For $n\in\mathbb N$, each $w\in\mathbb{W}_{n+1}(X_\alpha)$ has a unique prefix of length
$n$, lying in $\mathbb{W}_n(X_\alpha)$; and each $v\in\mathbb{W}_n(X_\alpha)$ has at least
one extension $vb\in\mathbb{W}_{n+1}(X_\alpha)$, since $v$ occurs in some
$y\in X_\alpha$, say $v=y_{[i,i+n)}$, and then $y_{[i,i+n+1)}$ is such an
extension.  Grouping the words of length $n+1$ according to their prefix
therefore expresses $|\mathbb{W}_{n+1}(X_\alpha)|$ as a sum over
$v\in\mathbb{W}_n(X_\alpha)$ of terms equal to $1$ or $2$; these two numbers being
$n+2$ and $n+1$ by Lemma \ref{lem:sturmian}(ii), exactly one term equals
$2$.  For all other $v$, with unique extension $vb$, all points of
$[v]_{X_\alpha}$ have $(n+1)$st digit $b$, so $[v]_{X_\alpha}=[vb]_{X_\alpha}$
and this atom of $\mathcal A_n$ is also an atom of $\mathcal A_{n+1}$; the claim follows.

We choose a representative point in each atom of each partition $\mathcal A_n$,
inductively.  The single atom of $\mathcal A_0$ is $X_\alpha$; let its
representative be any point of $X_\alpha$.  Suppose representatives have
been chosen for the atoms of $\mathcal A_n$, let $A$ be the unique atom of $\mathcal A_n$
that is the union of two atoms of $\mathcal A_{n+1}$, and let $C_n$ be whichever
of those two atoms does not contain the representative of $A$; the other
one contains it, and retains it as its own representative.  All atoms
of $\mathcal A_{n+1}$ other than $C_n$ then already contain a representative,
which we retain; as the representative of $C_n$ we choose a new point.
Since $C_n$ is an atom of $\mathcal A_{n+1}$, there is a unique
$v_n\in\mathbb{W}_{n+1}(X_\alpha)$ with $C_n=[v_n]_{X_\alpha}$.

\emph{Step 2: the expansion of $f$ into centred indicator functions.}
Let $f_n:X_\alpha\to\mathbb R$ be constant on each atom of $\mathcal A_n$, with value
there the value of $f$ at the chosen representative of that atom.
Since all atoms of $\mathcal A_{n+1}$ other than $C_n$ retain the representative
of the atom of $\mathcal A_n$ containing it, $f_{n+1}$ and $f_n$ agree off $C_n$,
and there is $d_n\in\mathbb R$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:successive-difference}
  f_{n+1}-f_n=d_n\mathbbm{1}_{C_n} .
\end{equation}
Here $d_n$ is the difference of the values of $f$ at the representative of
$C_n$ and at the representative of $A$.  These two points both lie in $A$,
an atom of $\mathcal A_n$, so they agree on their coordinates $0,1,\ldots,n-1$ and
are therefore at distance at most $\theta^{n}$, giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dn}
  |d_n|\le L\theta^{n} .
\end{equation}
Similarly, each $x\in X_\alpha$ agrees on its first $n$ coordinates with the
representative of the atom of $\mathcal A_n$ containing it, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fn-uniform}
  \|f-f_n\|_{C(X_\alpha)}\le L\theta^{n} .
\end{equation}

Summing the differences \eqref{eq:successive-difference} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:partial-sum}
  f_N=f_0+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}d_n\mathbbm{1}_{C_n}\qquad(N\ge1) .
\end{equation}
Since $\mathcal A_0$ has a single atom, $f_0$ is constant; integrating
\eqref{eq:partial-sum} with respect to $\mu_\alpha$, and subtracting this
from \eqref{eq:partial-sum},
we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expression-minus-integral}
  f_N-\int f_N\,d\mu_\alpha
  =\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}d_n\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{C_n}-\mu_\alpha(C_n)\bigr) .
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:fn-uniform}, the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:expression-minus-integral} converges uniformly to
$f-\int f\,d\mu_\alpha$ as $N\to\infty$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centered-expansion}
  f-\int f\,d\mu_\alpha
  =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_n\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{C_n}-\mu_\alpha(C_n)\bigr)
\end{equation}
uniformly on $X_\alpha$.

\emph{Step 3: decomposition of each term.}
The word $v_n$ has length $n+1$, so Proposition \ref{prop:centred-cylinder},
applied with $k=n+1$ and $C=C_n$, gives an integer $B_n:=B_{v_n}$ and a
function $V_n:=V_{v_n}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cn-decomp}
  \mathbbm{1}_{C_n}-\mu_\alpha(C_n)=-B_n(\Phi-\alpha)+V_n\circ\sigma-V_n
  \qquad\text{on }X_\alpha,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{n,r}:=\gamma_{v_n,r}$ and, by Notation \ref{not:cob},
\eqref{eq:Bvalue} and \eqref{eq:Vvbound},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BnVn}
  |B_n|\le n+1,\qquad V_n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\gamma_{n,r}K_r,\qquad
  \gamma_{n,r}\in\{-1,0,1\},\qquad
  \|V_n\|_{C(\Sigma)}\le\tfrac12n(n+1) .
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cfuf}
  c_f:=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_nB_n,\qquad
  \tilde f:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_nV_n .
\end{equation}
The first series converges absolutely, by \eqref{eq:dn} and
\eqref{eq:BnVn}, and
\[
  |c_f|\le L\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)\theta^{n}=\frac{L}{(1-\theta)^{2}},
\]
which is \eqref{eq:c-bound}.  The second series converges
absolutely in $C(\Sigma)$, since by \eqref{eq:dn} and \eqref{eq:BnVn}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uf-absolute}
  \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|d_n|\,\|V_n\|_{C(\Sigma)}
  \le\frac L2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n(n+1)\theta^{n}<\infty ,
\end{equation}
so $\tilde f$ is a well defined continuous function on $\Sigma$, and in
particular $\sum_nd_n(V_n\circ\sigma)$ converges uniformly to
$\tilde f\circ\sigma$, uniform convergence being preserved by composition
with $\sigma$.

Now substitute \eqref{eq:Cn-decomp} into \eqref{eq:centered-expansion}.
Each of the three resulting series, namely those with terms
$-d_nB_n(\Phi-\alpha)$, $d_nV_n\circ\sigma$ and $-d_nV_n$, converges
absolutely and uniformly on $X_\alpha$ by \eqref{eq:dn}, \eqref{eq:BnVn} and
\eqref{eq:uf-absolute}, so they may be separated and summed individually.
This gives
\[
  f-\int f\,d\mu_\alpha
  =\Bigl(-\sum_{n\ge0}d_nB_n\Bigr)(\Phi-\alpha)
  +\Bigl(\sum_{n\ge0}d_nV_n\Bigr)\circ\sigma-\sum_{n\ge0}d_nV_n
  =c_f(\Phi-\alpha)+\tilde f\circ\sigma-\tilde f
\]
on $X_\alpha$, which is \eqref{eq:cohomology}.  It remains to bound
$|\tilde f|_{{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha)}$, and in particular to show that $\tilde f$ is
Lipschitz.

\emph{Step 4: rewriting $\tilde f$ as a series in
$(\alpha-\Phi)\circ\sigma^{h}$.}
By \eqref{eq:BnVn} and \eqref{eq:Kr},
\[
  V_n=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\gamma_{n,r}K_r
  =\sum_{r=1}^{n}\gamma_{n,r}\sum_{h=0}^{r-1}(\alpha-\Phi)\circ\sigma^{h} .
\]
 Substituting this into the
definition \eqref{eq:cfuf} of $\tilde f$ gives a triple sum whose terms
have supremum norm at most $|d_n|$, by \eqref{eq:Krbound}, and which has at
most $\tfrac12n(n+1)$ terms for each $n$; by \eqref{eq:uf-absolute} it
therefore converges absolutely in $C(\Sigma)$, and may be summed in any
order.  Gathering the coefficient of each $(\alpha-\Phi)\circ\sigma^{h}$,
and noting that $d_n\gamma_{n,r}$ contributes to the index $h$ exactly when
$r>h$, we see that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bh-expansion}
  \tilde f=-\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}b_h(\Phi-\alpha)\circ\sigma^{h},\qquad
  b_h:=\sum_{r=h+1}^{\infty}\ \sum_{n=r}^{\infty}d_n\gamma_{n,r} .
\end{equation}
Since $|\gamma_{n,r}|\le1$, two applications of \eqref{eq:dn} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bh-bound}
  |b_h|\le L\sum_{r=h+1}^{\infty}\ \sum_{n=r}^{\infty}\theta^{n}
  =\frac{L}{1-\theta}\sum_{r=h+1}^{\infty}\theta^{r}
  =\frac{L\theta^{h+1}}{(1-\theta)^{2}} .
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 5: the Lipschitz bound.}
Let $x,y\in X_\alpha$ with $x\ne y$, and let $\ell\ge0$ be the least index
with $x_\ell\ne y_\ell$, so that $d_\theta(x,y)=\theta^{\ell}$.  Since
$(\Phi-\alpha)(\sigma^{h}z)=z_h-\alpha$, the $h$th terms of
\eqref{eq:bh-expansion} at $x$ and at $y$ differ by
$|b_h|\,|x_h-y_h|$, which vanishes for $h<\ell$ and is at most $|b_h|$ for
$h\ge\ell$.  So by \eqref{eq:bh-bound},
\[
  |\tilde f(x)-\tilde f(y)|\le\sum_{h=\ell}^{\infty}|b_h|
  \le\frac{L}{(1-\theta)^{2}}\sum_{h=\ell}^{\infty}\theta^{h+1}
  =\frac{\theta L}{(1-\theta)^{3}}\,d_\theta(x,y) ,
\]
and taking the supremum over $x\ne y$ gives
$\tilde f\in{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$ together with \eqref{eq:u-bound}.

\label{prop:rigidity}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be irrational, let $X_\alpha$ be the Sturmian subshift
of rotation number $\alpha$, with Sturmian measure $\mu_\alpha$,
let $\mathbf{b}$ be the Sturmian coding
(cf.~Definition \ref{def:coding}), let the intervals $I_v$ be as in Notation
\ref{not:cuts}, let $\Phi$ be as in Definition \ref{def:functions}, and let $H_0$ be as in
Notation \ref{not:cob}.  Define
\[
  \tau:=\sup_{r\ge0}S_r(\Phi-\alpha)\qquad\text{on }X_\alpha .
\]
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $\tau(\mathbf{b}^{\pm}(t))=H_0(t^{\pm})$ for all $t\in\mathbb R$; in
particular $0\le\tau\le1$;
\item[(ii)] $\alpha-\Phi=\tau\circ\sigma-\tau$ on $X_\alpha$;
\item[(iii)] for all $k\in\mathbb N$ and $w\in\mathbb{W}_k(X_\alpha)$ the image
$\tau\bigl([w]_{X_\alpha}\bigr)$ is a closed interval of length
$\mu_\alpha([w]_{X_\alpha})$; consequently $\tau$ is continuous on
$X_\alpha$, but $\tau\notin{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$ for all
$\theta\in(0,1)$;
\item[(iv)] for all $\theta\in(0,1)$ there is no
$u\in{\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$ with $\Phi-\alpha=u\circ\sigma-u$ on
$X_\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, for all $\theta\in(0,1)$, the two uniqueness statements of
Theorem \ref{thm:D} hold,
the functional $f\mapsto c_f$ is as described there, and the quotient of
${\rm Lip}(X_\alpha,d_\theta)$ by the constants and the Lipschitz coboundaries
is one-dimensional, spanned by the equivalence class of $\Phi$.

  \alpha-\int\Phi\,d\nu\ \ge\ 0 ,
\end{equation}

absolute values in \eqref{eq:integrated-residual}, and using
\eqref{eq:cu-bounds} on the first term and \eqref{eq:residual-distance} on
the second,
\[
  \Bigl|\int f\,d\nu-\int f\,d\mu_\alpha\Bigr|
  \le\frac{L}{(1-\theta)^{2}}\Bigl(\alpha-\int\Phi\,d\nu\Bigr)
  +C_\theta L\int\Psi\,d\nu .
\]
Since $C_\theta>2(1-\theta)^{-3}>(1-\theta)^{-2}$, and since
$\alpha-\int\Phi\,d\nu\ge0$ by \eqref{eq:nonneg}, the first term on the
right is at most $C_\theta L\bigl(\alpha-\int\Phi\,d\nu\bigr)$, so
\begin{align*}
  \Bigl|\int f\,d\nu-\int f\,d\mu_\alpha\Bigr|
  &\le C_\theta L\Bigl(\Bigl(\alpha-\int\Phi\,d\nu\Bigr)
     +\int\Psi\,d\nu\Bigr)\\
  &=C_\theta L\Bigl(\alpha-\int(\Phi-\Psi)\,d\nu\Bigr)
   =C_\theta L\Bigl(\alpha-\int F\,d\nu\Bigr),
\end{align*}
using that $F=\Phi-\Psi$
(cf.~\eqref{eq:functions}).  Finally, $\Psi$ vanishes on $X_\alpha$, which is the support
of $\mu_\alpha$, so $\int F\,d\mu_\alpha=\int\Phi\,d\mu_\alpha=\alpha$ by
Lemma \ref{lem:sturmian}(iii), and the right-hand side is
$C_\theta L(\int F\,d\mu_\alpha-\int F\,d\nu)$.  This proves
\eqref{eq:test-function}.

Let $f\in{\rm Lip}(Y_\alpha,d_\theta)$ satisfy $|f|_{{\rm Lip}(Y_\alpha)}\le1$.  By
the computation above, $\int F\,d\mu_\alpha-\int F\,d\nu$ equals
$\bigl(\alpha-\int\Phi\,d\nu\bigr)+\int\Psi\,d\nu$, which is non-negative

\eqref{eq:test-function} is at most
$C_\theta\bigl(\int F\,d\mu_\alpha-\int F\,d\nu\bigr)$, which does not
depend on $f$.  Taking the supremum over all such $f$ gives
\[

  \ \le\ C_\theta\Bigl(\int F\,d\mu_\alpha-\int F\,d\nu\Bigr),
\]
and the left-hand side is $W_\theta(\nu,\mu_\alpha)$ by the definition
\eqref{eq:wass}.  As $\nu\in\mathcal M(Y_\alpha,\sigma)$ was arbitrary, this
completes the proof.

\label{rem:magic-morse}
The magic Morse shift of \cite[\S 12]{HJXZ}, mentioned in the
Introduction, is synchronized but does not have the specification property.
To recall its definition, let $\mathbf m=0110100110010110\ldots$ be the
Morse sequence, that is, the fixed point beginning with $0$ of the
substitution $0\mapsto01$, $1\mapsto10$, and for $k\ge0$ let $p_k$ be its
prefix of length $2^k$, so that $p_{k+1}=p_k\overline{p_k}$, where
$\overline{v_1\cdots v_n}:=\bar{v}_1\cdots\bar{v}_n$, with $\bar{0}:=1$ and
$\bar{1}:=0$.
The magic Morse shift is the closure $X$
in $\{0,1,\flat\}^{\mathbb N_0}$ of the set of all sequences
$\sigma^n(p_{k_1}\flat\,p_{k_2}\flat\,p_{k_3}\flat\cdots)$ with
$n,k_1,k_2,\ldots\ge0$.  It is readily checked that $X$ is transitive, and
the symbol $\flat$ is a synchronizing word for $X$ \cite[\S 12]{HJXZ}, so
$X$ is synchronized.

To see that $X$ does not have specification, let $N\in\mathbb N$.  All words occurring in $X$ occur in the sequences
$\sigma^n(p_{k_1}\flat\,p_{k_2}\flat\cdots)$, so in such a word each maximal
block of symbols $0$ and $1$ that is immediately followed by $\flat$ is a
suffix of some $p_k$.  Hence if $u$ is a word occurring in $\mathbf m$,
and $uw\flat$ occurs in $X$ for some word $w$ of length at most $N$, then,
considering the first symbol $\flat$ after $u$, we see that $u$ occurs in
some $p_k$, ending within distance $N$ of the end of $p_k$.  Since
$p_{k+1}=p_k\overline{p_k}$, for all $\ell\in\mathbb N$ the words $p_k$ with
$2^k\ge\ell$ have at most two distinct suffixes of length $\ell$; so for all
$L\in\mathbb N$ at most $2(N+1)$ words $u$ of length $L$ arise in this way.  On the
other hand the orbit closure of $\mathbf m$ is infinite, so the number of
words of length $L$ occurring in $\mathbf m$ tends to infinity with $L$.
Hence for some $L$ there is a word $u$ of length $L$ occurring in $X$ such
that $uw\flat$ occurs in $X$ for no word $w$ of length at most $N$.  As $N$
was arbitrary, $X$ does not have the specification property.

\label{lem:key}
Let $K\subseteq S_\beta$ be a non-empty closed set with $\sigma(K)\subseteq

$\partial_MS_\beta$ contains no periodic orbit, each of these two conditions
is equivalent to TPO in the corresponding setting \cite[Cor.~10.1]{HJXZ},
\cite[Thm.~5.22]{HHJL}.  In the Sturmian case, Theorem \ref{thm:A} shows
directly that $Y_\alpha$ is not fragile (Remark \ref{rem:markov-boundary}),
and Theorems \ref{thm:A} and \ref{thm:F}, together with the unique
ergodicity of $X_\alpha$, give Theorem \ref{thm:E}, apart from the explicit
form of the open set.

Finally, Corollary \ref{cor:G} concerns only the metrics $d_\theta$ with
$\theta\in[\beta^{-1},1)$, which are those corresponding to H\"older classes
on $I$, and the exception in Corollary \ref{cor:G}(i) is necessary: if
$\beta$ is a simple beta-number, then $S_\beta$ is of finite type, so has
TPO \cite{Con16}, whereas $T_\beta$ does not have TPO
\cite[Rem.~5.21]{HHJL}.
