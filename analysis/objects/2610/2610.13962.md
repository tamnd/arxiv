<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:polygon}
Let \(P\) be a convex \(m\)-gon of area \(a\) containing a disk of
radius \(r>0\). If \(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_m\) are its exterior angles,
then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polygon-area}
 a\ge r^2\sum_{j=1}^m\tan\frac{\beta_j}{2}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Translate the disk centre to the origin. Write the supporting
half-planes of \(P\) as \(\langle n_j,x\rangle\le d_j\), with unit
outward normals \(n_j\). Containment gives \(d_j\ge r\). Replacing
every half-plane by \(\langle n_j,x\rangle\le r\) produces a polygon
\(P^{\rm tan}\subset P\). Since consecutive collinear sides were
removed, consecutive normal directions differ by an angle in
\((0,\pi)\); hence every new tangent line supports an actual side and
\(P^{\rm tan}\) has the same exterior angles.

At a vertex of exterior angle \(\beta_j\), each tangent segment to the
two adjacent contact points has length \(r\tan(\beta_j/2)\). Thus the
semiperimeter is \(r\sum_j\tan(\beta_j/2)\). A tangential polygon has
area equal to its inradius times its semiperimeter, proving
\eqref{eq:polygon-area}.

The first mean is at least \(\pi/4\), while the second is at most

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:euler-budget}
For a generic diagram of \(n\) nonempty cells,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler-budget}
 M=6n-2-T.
\end{equation}
Thus for \(n=26\), \(M=154-T\).
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let \(I\) be the number of interior vertices and \(E\) the number of
edges, including boundary edges. The connected planar graph has
\(I+T+4\) vertices and \(n+1\) faces, so Euler gives
\[
 E=I+T+n+3.
\]
Interior and open-side transition vertices have degree three, while
the four square corners have degree two. Hence
\[
 2E=3I+3T+8.
\]
Eliminating \(I\) gives \(E=3n+1\). There are \(T+4\) boundary edges.
An interior edge is counted twice in \(M\), and a boundary edge once;
therefore \(M=2E-(T+4)=6n-2-T\).

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:boundary-runs}
At least three distinct cells meet \(\partial Q\). If \(T>0\), the
transition points divide the boundary circle into exactly \(T\) open
runs, each with a constant owner. Consequently there are at most \(T\)
boundary cells, and the number of transition endpoints incident with
any fixed boundary cell is even.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Deleting \(T\) points from the circle \(\partial Q\) gives \(T\) runs.
Each run has two endpoints, so a cell is incident with twice its number
of runs.

One boundary cell would contain all four corners and hence their convex
hull \(Q\), leaving no room for another full-dimensional cell. Suppose
there were exactly two boundary cells \(A,B\). Their disjoint interiors
admit a weakly separating line \(L\), with \(A\subset H_A\) and
\(B\subset H_B\). Every point of \(\partial Q\) strictly in \(H_A\)
belongs to \(A\); by closure, so do the endpoints of \(L\cap Q\).
The polygon \(Q\cap H_A\) is the convex hull of these boundary points
and chord endpoints, hence lies in \(A\). Similarly
\(Q\cap H_B\subset B\). Thus \(A\cup B=Q\), again excluding the
remaining full-dimensional cells.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:g-concavity}
The function \(g(x)=1/[x\tan(\pi/x)]\) is strictly increasing and
strictly concave for \(x\ge3\). Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:g-affine}
 g_m\le A+Bm\qquad(m\ge3).
\end{equation}
For a fixed number of integer side counts \(m_j\ge3\) and an upper
bound on their sum, \(\sum_jg_{m_j}\) is maximized by exhausting the
side budget and making the \(m_j\)'s differ by at most one.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Set \(u=\pi/x\). Then \(g(x)=u\cot u/\pi\), and \(g'(x)>0\) is
equivalent to \(u-\sin u\cos u>0\). Writing
\[
 h(u)=u^2\bigl(u\csc^2u-\cot u\bigr),
 \qquad g'(x)=\frac{h(u)}{\pi^2},
\]
direct differentiation gives
\[
 h'(u)=\frac{2u}{\sin^2u}
 \bigl(2u-u^2\cot u-\sin u\cos u\bigr)>0.
\]
Indeed, \(\tan u>u\) gives \(u^2\cot u<u\), and
\(\sin u\cos u<u\), for \(0<u\le\pi/3\). Since
\(du/dx=-u^2/\pi<0\), we have \(g''(x)<0\).

The line \(A+Bx\) passes through \((5,g_5)\) and \((6,g_6)\).
Decreasing first differences place every integer point \((m,g_m)\)
below its extension, proving \eqref{eq:g-affine}. If \(b\ge a+2\),
decreasing differences give
\(g_{a+1}+g_{b-1}\ge g_a+g_b\). Repeated smoothing, together with
monotonicity, proves the balancing assertion.

 p'(s)=\frac{s^2}{32}-\frac54<0.
\]
Also \(p(0)>0\), while
\(p(3\pi/2)=1-9\pi/8+9\pi^3/256<0\), using
\(3<\pi<22/7\). Hence \(p\) has one zero \(s_*\). The derivative of
\[
 \psi(s)=\frac1{D(s)}+\frac{s-\pi}{50}
\]
has the sign of \(p(s)\), so \(\psi\) is maximized at \(s_*\). For
\(s_0=23/8\), \(\pi>333/106\) gives
\[
 p(s_0)=\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac{115321}{49152}>0,
\]
so \(s_*>s_0\). At the critical point,
\[
 \psi(s_*)=\frac4{5s_*}+\frac{s_*-\pi}{50}.
\]
Its derivative is \(1/50-4/(5s^2)\), which is negative for
\(0<s\le3\pi/2\). Therefore
\[
 H_m\le\psi(s_*)<
 \frac4{5(23/8)}+\frac{23/8-\pi}{50}<\frac{273}{1000},
\]
again using \(\pi>333/106\). The four bounds imply
\eqref{eq:H-envelope}. At \(m=3,4,5\), its right-hand side is,
respectively,
\[
 0.2418,\qquad 0.2524,\qquad 0.2630.
\]
For every \(m\ge6\), its right-hand side is \(0.2736\).

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:T5-no-four-marks}
No cell has four marks when \(T=5\).
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
A cell with four marks has \(m=4\), by the angle sum following
\eqref{eq:ck-definition}. If it owns a square corner, then, because
the four corners have distinct owners, it owns exactly one corner and
its other three marks are transition vertices. Its total number of
incident transition endpoints is even by Lemma~\ref{lem:boundary-runs},
and hence is at least four. Together with the corner, these give at
least five distinct polygon vertices, contradicting \(m=4\).

If the cell owns no corner, its four marks are transition endpoints and
require at least two boundary runs. Each of the four distinct corner
owners requires a separate run, since a run cannot contain corners
with different owners. This requires at least six runs, whereas \(T=5\)
gives exactly five.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:generic-density}
Every packing of finitely many positive-radius disks is the limit of
strictly feasible packings whose clipped power diagrams satisfy the four
genericity conditions above.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Shrink every radius by a common factor \(1-\varepsilon\), keeping the
centres fixed. For every pair \(i\ne j\), the new pairwise slack is at
least \(\varepsilon(r_i+r_j)>0\), and the new wall slack is at least
\(\varepsilon r_i>0\). Strict feasibility is therefore open near the
shrunken parameter vector.

After removing the common term \(|x|^2\), the power diagram is the lower
envelope of
\[
 \ell_i(x)=-2\langle z_i,x\rangle+|z_i|^2-r_i^2.
\]
For \(r_i>0\), the map
\[
 (z_{i,1},z_{i,2},r_i)\longmapsto
 (-2z_{i,1},-2z_{i,2},|z_i|^2-r_i^2)
\]
has Jacobian determinant \(-8r_i\ne0\). It is a local diffeomorphism,
so arbitrary small perturbations of the affine coefficients arise from
arbitrary small perturbations of centres and positive radii.

For finitely many affine functions clipped by four fixed lines, a
nongeneric lower envelope satisfies one of finitely many determinant
equalities: four affine functions agree at one point; three agree at a
point of a prescribed wall; two agree at a square corner; a
pair-equality line coincides with a wall; or comparison lines coincide.
These conditions also contain every collapsed edge or boundary run:
collapsing an interior edge produces a four-function equality, while
collapsing a boundary edge produces a three-function wall equality or
a corner tie. Each determinant is a nonzero polynomial in the affine
coefficients (varying one intercept shows it is not identically zero).
A finite union of proper real algebraic zero sets has empty interior.
Thus generic coefficient vectors are dense. Pull one back through the
local diffeomorphism, choosing it within the open strict-feasibility
neighbourhood. Equation~\eqref{eq:disk-in-power-cell} ensures every
perturbed cell remains full-dimensional and nonempty.

<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Let \(\mathcal D\) be arbitrary. Choose
\(\varepsilon_\nu\downarrow0\), shrink its radii by
\(1-\varepsilon_\nu\), and use Lemma~\ref{lem:generic-density} to make
a further generic perturbation small enough to preserve strict
feasibility and converge to \(\mathcal D\). The generic result gives
\(S(\mathcal D_\nu)\le U\) for every \(\nu\). Continuity of the sum of
radii yields \(S(\mathcal D)\le U\) in the limit.
