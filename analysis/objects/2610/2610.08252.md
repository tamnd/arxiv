<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For every $n\geq7$ there exists a nonzero polynomial class $F_n=\Delta_n P_n\in(J_n)_{d(n)}$
not proportional to the Hodges numerator. More precisely:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item for $n=7$, one may take $\Delta_n=\Delta_{2,1}$ on six labels and a one-point completion as described in \Cref{sec:seven};
\item for $n=2k\geq8$, one may take $\Delta_n=\Delta_{k-1,1}$ on all $n$ labels;
\item for $n=2k+1\geq9$, one may take $\Delta_n=\Delta_{k-2,1}$ on the first $n-3$ labels.
\end{enumerate}
In each case, $P_n\in Q_n$ is chosen suitably.
Consequently, $\dim_\mathbb C(J_n)_{d(n)}\geq2$ for every $n\geq7$.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Let $V=\mathbb C^n$ with its standard basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ and its standard nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form.
    We look at the product of two Grassmannians $\mathrm{Gr}(2,n)\times \mathrm{Gr}(2,n)$. A point of the first Grassmannian is a $2$-dimensional plane $S\subset V$,  with Pl\"{u}cker coordinates $p_{ij}$. A point of the second Grassmannian is another $2$-dimensional plane $M\subset V$,  with Pl\"{u}cker coordinates $q_{ij}$.

    Choose $2\times n$ matrices $X$ and $Y$ whose row spans are $S$ and $M$.
    Then the momentum-conservation equations are expressed by the matrix equation $XY^T=0$.
    Equivalently, every vector in $S$ is orthogonal to every vector in $M$.
    Letting $W=M^\perp$, the condition $S\perp M$ becomes $S\subset W$.
    Hence a spinor-helicity point is identified with a two-step flag $S\subset W\subset V$, where $\dim S=2$ and $\dim W=n-2$.
    One can show that this identification yields an isomorphism of algebraic varieties between the spinor-helicity variety and the two-step flag variety $\mathbb{F}(2,n-2;n)$ in $V=\mathbb C^n$ \cite{SH}. In particular, $Q_n$ is an integral domain. Given $n$ spinors $\lambda_i$ and $\widetilde{\lambda}_i$, one has a pair of $2\times n$ matrices
    \[
     \Lambda:=(\lambda_1\ \cdots\ \lambda_n),
     \qquad
     \widetilde\Lambda:=(\widetilde\lambda_1^T\ \cdots\
     \widetilde\lambda_n^T).
    \]
    Then, each point of $\mathrm{SH}(2,n,0)$ can be represented by a pair of matrices $(\Lambda, \widetilde{\Lambda})$, where both have full rank and $\Lambda\widetilde{\Lambda}^T=0$. Thus, any such pair defines an evaluation homomorphism $Q_n\longrightarrow \mathbb C$ by taking the minors of $\Lambda$ and $\widetilde \Lambda$.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:Hodges-alternating}
We shall use repeatedly that the Hodges numerator $N_n$ is alternating under
permutations of the labels. More precisely, for every
$\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_n$,
\[
\sigma\cdot N_n=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,N_n.
\]
Equivalently, $N_n$ spans a copy of the sign representation of
$\mathfrak{S}_n$ inside $(J_n)_{d(n)}$. In particular, if a nonzero element
$F\in (J_n)_{d(n)}$ is invariant under some transposition, then
$F$ cannot be proportional to $N_n$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:segre-basic}
    The polynomial $\Delta_{a,b}(I)$ is invariant under the action of
    $\operatorname{SL}(U)\times\operatorname{SL}(\widetilde U)$ on $S_n$.
                                                   
    It defines a nonzero class in the Pl\"ucker ring $R_n$ of spinor multidegree
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-general}
     \deg\Delta_{a,b}(I)
     =\left(\frac{am}{2},\frac{bm}{2};(a-b)^{m},0^{n-m}\right).
    \end{equation}
    Here the label coordinates are ordered with those in $I$ first.
    Moreover, for every pair of distinct indices $i,j\in I$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:segre-pair}
     \Delta_{a,b}(I)\in(p_{ij},q_{ij})\subset R_n.
    \end{equation}
    Finally, if $\sigma$ is a permutation of the labels in $I$, then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:segre-sign}
     \sigma\cdot\Delta_{a,b}(I)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\Delta_{a,b}(I).
    \end{equation}

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We divide the proof into five steps.

    \smallskip
    \noindent\emph{Step 1: the group action.}
    Let $G:=\operatorname{SL}(U)\times\operatorname{SL}(\widetilde U)$.
    For each index $i$, the degree-one polynomial variables $x_i,y_i$ span a copy $U_i\simeq U$, while $u_i,v_i$ span a copy $\widetilde U_i\simeq\widetilde U$.  The group $G$ acts diagonally on
    $\bigoplus_{i=1}^n U_i\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^n\widetilde U_i$,
    and this action extends multiplicatively to the polynomial ring $S_n$.  In particular, $G$ acts on $W_{a,b}=\operatorname{Sym}^aU\otimes\operatorname{Sym}^b\widetilde U$.
    Let $\rho_{a,b}(g,h)$ be the matrix of this irreducible representation in the monomial basis
    \[
     x^{a-r}y^r u^{b-s}v^s,
     \qquad 0\leq r\leq a,\quad 0\leq s\leq b.
    \]
    The action on the monomials in each row of $M_{a,b}(I)$ is given by right multiplication by the transpose of $\rho_{a,b}(g,h)$.
    More succinctly,
    \[
        (g,h)\bullet M_{a,b}(I) = M_{a,b}(I) \left(\rho_{a,b}(g,h)\right)^T.
    \]
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            
        
                              
                                   
        
                                                                                                                  
        
                                   
                                           
                          
        
    It remains to compute the determinant
                                   
    of this matrix.

    For a two-dimensional complex vector space $E$ and $T\in\operatorname{GL}(E)$, one has $\det(\operatorname{Sym}^aT)=(\det T)^{a(a+1)/2}$.
    Indeed, if $T$ has eigenvalues $\alpha,\beta$, then $\operatorname{Sym}^aT$ has eigenvalues $\alpha^a,\alpha^{a-1}\beta,\ldots,\alpha\beta^{a-1},\beta^a$,
    whose product is $(\alpha\beta)^{a(a+1)/2}$.  The identity for arbitrary $T$ follows because both sides are polynomial characters on $\operatorname{GL}(E)$.  Hence, for $g\in\operatorname{SL}(U)$ and $h\in\operatorname{SL}(\widetilde U)$,
    $\det(\operatorname{Sym}^ag)=\det(\operatorname{Sym}^bh)=1$.
    Using the property of the determinant of the Kronecker product of two matrices, $\det(A\otimes B)=\det(A)^{\dim B}\det(B)^{\dim A}$,
    we obtain
    \[
     \det\rho_{a,b}(g,h)
     =\det(\operatorname{Sym}^ag)^{b+1}\det(\operatorname{Sym}^bh)^{a+1}=1.
    \]
    Thus $\Delta_{a,b}(I)$ is invariant under $G=\operatorname{SL}(U)\times\operatorname{SL}(\widetilde U)$.

    \smallskip
    \noindent\emph{Step 2: from spinor to Pl\"ucker coordinates.}
    Let
    \[
     A_I:=\operatorname{Sym}(U^{\oplus I}),\qquad
     B_I:=\operatorname{Sym}(\widetilde U^{\oplus I}).
    \]
    Then the polynomial ring on the supporting spinors is $S_I=A_I\otimes B_I$.  The first factor of $G$, i.e. $\operatorname{SL}(U)$ acts only on $A_I$, and the second factor $\operatorname{SL}(\widetilde U)$ acts only on $B_I$.  Taking invariants, we have $S_I^G
     =A_I^{\operatorname{SL}(U)}\otimes B_I^{\operatorname{SL}(\widetilde U)}$.
    The First Fundamental Theorem for $\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$ states that $A_I^{\operatorname{SL}(U)}$ is generated by the $2\times2$ determinants $p_{ij}$, $i,j\in I$, and that the only relations among these generators are the Pl\"ucker relations; the same statement holds for the $q_{ij}$ on the second factor.  Thus, by \cite[Chapter 11, \S1.2, Theorem 1]{Procesibook},
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:FFT-product}
     S_I^G\cong
     \frac{\mathbb C[p_{ij}:i<j,\ i,j\in I]}{(p\text{-Pl\"ucker relations})}
     \otimes
     \frac{\mathbb C[q_{ij}:i<j,\ i,j\in I]}{(q\text{-Pl\"ucker relations})}.
    \end{equation}
    The right-hand side is the tensor product Pl\"ucker ring on the labels $I$, naturally embedded in $R_n$.  Since {\it Step 1} shows that $\Delta_{a,b}(I)\in S_I^G$, the determinant is a well-defined polynomial class in $R_n$.  We use the same symbol for this class.

    \smallskip
    \noindent\emph{Step 3: nonvanishing.}
    We prove that the determinant polynomial is not identically zero.  Choose pairwise distinct complex numbers
    $t_0,\ldots,t_a, \ell_0,\ldots,\ell_b$. Consider the $m=(a+1)(b+1)$ pairs $(t_r,\ell_s)$ with $0\le r\le a$ and $0\le s\le b$. For each such pair $(t_r,{\ell_s})$, define
    \[
     \lambda_{r,s}=(1,t_r)^T,\qquad
     \widetilde\lambda_{r,s}=(1,\ell_s).
    \]
    With columns ordered by the monomials $x^{a-r'}y^{r'}u^{b-s'}v^{s'}$, the corresponding Segre matrix is, up to simultaneous row and column reordering, the Kronecker product \linebreak $V_a(t_0,\ldots,t_a)\otimes V_b(\ell_0,\ldots,\ell_b)$, where $V_a$ and $V_b$ are the usual Vandermonde matrices.  Hence
    $\det M_{a,b}
     =\det(V_a)^{b+1}\det(V_b)^{a+1}\neq0$.
    Therefore $\Delta_{a,b}(I)$ is a nonzero polynomial in $S_I$, and, by the invariant-ring identification \eqref{eq:FFT-product}, a nonzero class in the product Pl\"ucker ring.

    \smallskip
    \noindent\emph{Step 4: the spinor multidegree.}
    The determinant is separately multihomogeneous in the spinors attached to every label $i$.  Indeed, every term in the determinant expansion uses exactly one entry from the row indexed by $i$.  Every entry in that row is homogeneous of degree $a$ in the coordinates $x_i,y_i$ of $\lambda_i$ and of degree $b$ in the coordinates $u_i,v_i$ of $\widetilde\lambda_i$.  Thus, in the polynomial ring $S_I$,
    \[
     \deg_{\lambda_i}\Delta_{a,b}=a,\qquad
     \deg_{\widetilde\lambda_i}\Delta_{a,b}=b
     \qquad(\mbox{ for every } i\in I),
    \]
    and both degrees are zero for labels outside $I$.

    Under the isomorphism \eqref{eq:FFT-product}, a factor $p_{ij}$ has degree one in the $L$-coordinates of label $i$ and one in those of label $j$, while a factor $q_{ij}$ has degree one in the $\widetilde L$-coordinates of labels $i$ and $j$.  Therefore the total number of $p$-factors of every monomial in $p_{ij},q_{ij}$ of an expansion of $\Delta_{a,b}$ in such variables is
    \[
     \deg_p\Delta_{a,b}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in I}a=\frac{am}{2},
    \]
    and similarly
    \[
     \deg_q\Delta_{a,b}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in I}b=\frac{bm}{2},
    \]
    where $m=|I|$.
    (These are integers because $a(a+1)$ and $b(b+1)$ are even.)  Finally, the $i$th label component of the spinor multidegree is, by definition, the number of $p$-occurrences of $i$ minus the number of $q$-occurrences of $i$.  Hence this component is $a-b$ for every $i\in I$, and $0$ outside $I$.  This proves \eqref{eq:degree-general}.

    \smallskip
    \noindent\emph{Step 5: ideal memberships.}
    Fix distinct $i,j\in I$ and work first in the polynomial ring $S_I$.  Consider the ideal
    \[
     K_{ij}:=(p_{ij},q_{ij})S_I.
    \]
    We claim that $K_{ij}$ is a prime ideal of $S_I$.  Geometrically, $p_{ij}=0$ is the irreducible determinantal hypersurface on which the two vectors $\lambda_i,\lambda_j$ are linearly dependent, and $q_{ij}=0$ is the analogous irreducible determinantal hypersurface for $\widetilde\lambda_i,\widetilde\lambda_j$; since the two equations involve disjoint sets of coordinates, their common zero set is the product of these two irreducible determinantal varieties with an affine space for the remaining labels, hence is irreducible.  Equivalently, $S_I/K_{ij}$ is a domain.

    On the zero locus $V(K_{ij})$, both pairs of spinors are linearly dependent.  Thus, up to exchanging $i$ and $j$, there are scalars $c,d$ such that $\lambda_j=c\lambda_i,
     \widetilde\lambda_j=d\widetilde\lambda_i$.
    The $j$th evaluation row of $M_{a,b}(I)$ is then $c^ad^b$ times the $i$th row. Hence $\Delta_{a,b}(I)$ vanishes identically on $V(K_{ij})$.  Since $K_{ij}$ is prime, Hilbert's Nullstellensatz yields
    $\Delta_{a,b}(I)\in K_{ij}$.
    Thus there exist $A,B\in S_I$ such that
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:pair-decomposition-polynomial}
     \Delta_{a,b}(I)=p_{ij}A+q_{ij}B.
    \end{equation}
    At this stage $A$ and $B$ need not be invariant. Now, the algebraic group $G=\operatorname{SL}(U)\times\operatorname{SL}(\widetilde U)$ is linearly reductive.  Hence there exists a Reynolds operator
    \[
     \mathcal R:S_I\longrightarrow S_I^G
    \]
    which is the identity on $S_I^G$ and is $S_I^G$-linear; see \cite[Chapters 8 and 14]{Procesibook}. (Such a Reynolds operator may be realized by integration over a maximal compact subgroup equipped with a suitable measure.)   The three polynomials $\Delta_{a,b}(I)$, $p_{ij}$ and $q_{ij}$ are $G$-invariant.  Applying $\mathcal R$ to \eqref{eq:pair-decomposition-polynomial} therefore gives
    \[
     \Delta_{a,b}(I)
     =p_{ij}\mathcal R(A)+q_{ij}\mathcal R(B),
    \]
    with $\mathcal R(A),\mathcal R(B)\in S_I^G$.  Via the First Fundamental Theorem isomorphism \eqref{eq:FFT-product}, these invariant coefficients are classes in the Pl\"ucker ring $R_n$.  Consequently $\Delta_{a,b}(I)\in(p_{ij},q_{ij})$ in $R_n$.  This proves \eqref{eq:segre-pair}.

    Finally, a permutation $\sigma$ of the labels in $I$ simply permutes the rows of the evaluation matrix $M_{a,b}(I)$.  Hence its determinant is multiplied by $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$, which proves \eqref{eq:segre-sign} and completes the proof.

\label{rem:five-six}
    Segre geometry already appears in the two multiplicities for which uniqueness was proved in \cite{Gravity}.

    For $n=5$, one has $d(5)=(2,2;0^5)$,
    which is the spinor multidegree of $\Delta_{1,1}$ on any four labels.  Let $M$ be the $5\times4$ matrix whose $i$th row is $(x_i u_i,\ x_i v_i,\ y_i u_i,\ y_i v_i)$.
    Momentum conservation $\Lambda\widetilde\Lambda^T=0$ is exactly the statement that the sum of the five rows $R_i$ of $M$ is zero.  For $r\in[5]$, let $D_r$ be the determinant of the $4\times4$ matrix obtained by deleting the $r$th row.  So, momentum conservation gives the row relation $R_1+R_2+R_3+R_4+R_5=0$. For $r<5$, substituting
    $R_5=-\sum_{j=1}^4R_j$ into the computation of the determinant $D_r$ implies that each determinant is $\pm D_5$.
    Thus, in $Q_5$, these determinants have, up to scaling, a common class, denoted $\Delta_5$.  By \Cref{prop:segre-basic}, $\Delta_5\in (p_{ij},q_{ij})$ and has degree $d(5)$.  It is easy to see that it must be nonzero. Five-point uniqueness from \cite{Gravity} then implies that $\Delta_5$ is proportional to the five-point Hodges numerator.  Thus the first two unique MHV numerators themselves admit a direct Segre-determinantal interpretation.

    For $n=6$, one has $d(6)=(6,3;1^6)$. Taking $(a,b)=(2,1)$ gives $m=6$, and \Cref{prop:segre-basic} yields $\deg\Delta_{2,1}(1,\ldots,6)=(6,3;1^6)=d(6)$.
    The same proposition shows that $\Delta_{2,1}$ belongs to every ideal $(p_{ij},q_{ij})$ with $1\leq i<j\leq6$.  Its image in $Q_6$ is nonzero.  Indeed, take $\lambda_i=(1,i-1)^T$ for $1\leq i\leq6$, and set
    \[
     \widetilde\lambda_1=(1,0),\quad
     \widetilde\lambda_2=(-2,0),\quad
     \widetilde\lambda_3=(1,0),
    \]
    \[
     \widetilde\lambda_4=(0,1),\quad
     \widetilde\lambda_5=(0,-2),\quad
     \widetilde\lambda_6=(0,1).
    \]
    One checks that $\Lambda\widetilde\Lambda^T=0$. After grouping the $u$- and $v$-columns, the Segre matrix is block diagonal with two nonzero $3\times3$ Vandermonde blocks.  Hence $\Delta_{2,1}\neq0$ in $Q_6$, and therefore
    $0\neq\Delta_{2,1}\in(J_6)_{d(6)}$.
    Since \cite{Gravity} proves that this space is one-dimensional, $\Delta_{2,1}$ is proportional to the six-point Hodges numerator.

The monomial $\Psi_r$ is defined via a graph.
    Fix $r\geq0$ and let $N=2r+6$ and $V=\{1,\ldots,N\}$.
    Let $A=\{1,\ldots,r+1\}$ and $B=V\setminus A$, so $|B|=r+5$.
    Then, let $C_r$ be the graph on $B$ constructed as follows.
    Identify the vertices in $B$ with elements of $\mathbb Z/(r+5)\mathbb Z$.

    \begin{enumerate}[
        leftmargin=6em,
        labelwidth=4em,
        labelsep=1em,
        align=left
    ]
        \item[\textbf{\boldmath $r$ even}]
        Then $|B|$ is odd, and $i,j\in\mathbb Z/|B|\mathbb Z$ form an edge
        of $C_r$ if and only if
        $j-i\equiv\pm1,\ldots,\pm r/2$ (modulo $|B|$).

        \item[\textbf{\boldmath $r$ odd}]
        Then $|B|$ is even, and $i,j\in\mathbb Z/|B|\mathbb Z$ form an edge
        of $C_r$ if and only if
        $j-i\equiv\pm1,\ldots,\pm(r-1)/2,\pm|B|/2$
        (modulo $|B|$).
    \end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:Psi}
The spinor multidegree of $\Psi_r$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Psi-degree}
 \deg\Psi_r=\bigl(r(r+4),r;r^N\bigr).
\end{equation}
Moreover, if $r\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Psi-sign}
 (1\,2)\Psi_r=-\Psi_r.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The $r$-regular graph $G_r$ has $Nr/2=(r+3)r$ edges.
For the total $p$-degree, each edge of $G_r$ contributes one $p_{ij}$; moreover, the matching contributes $r$ $p_{ij}$'s. Therefore the total $p$-degree is $(r+3)r+r=r(r+4)$. For the total $q$-degree, only the matching contributes $r$.

At an endpoint $i$ of the matching, the additional occurrences in the $p$- and $q$-factors cancel, so the difference between $p$-occurrences and $q$-occurrences is $r$ for each matched vertex. At a vertex outside the matching, there is no change, so there are only $r$ $p$-occurrences and zero $q$-occurrences, hence their difference is again $r$.   This proves the multidegree \eqref{eq:Psi-degree}.

When $r\geq 1$, the set of edges of $G_r$ is invariant under $(1\,2)$, because $1,2$ appear as vertices in the complete graph $K_A$. This graph contains the edge $\{1,2\}$, which contributes one sign because $p_{21}=-p_{12}$; all other edges incident to $1$ or $2$ are exchanged in pairs.  The circulant part and the matching avoid the labels $1,2$.  Hence \eqref{eq:Psi-sign}.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:even}
For $n=2k\geq8$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Feven}
 F_{2k}=\Delta_{k-1,1}(1,\ldots,2k)\,\Psi_{k-3}.
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 0\neq F_{2k}\in(J_{2k})_{d(2k)}, \mbox{ and } (1\,2)F_{2k}=F_{2k}.
\]
In particular, $F_{2k}$ is not proportional to the Hodges numerator.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Using \Cref{lem:Psi}, the spinor multidegrees add to
\begin{align*}
\deg_pF_{2k}
 &=k(k-1)+(k-3)(k+1)=2k^2-3k-3,\\
\deg_qF_{2k}
 &=k+(k-3)=2k-3,
\end{align*}
and every label component is
$(k-2)+(k-3)=2k-5$.
This is precisely $d(2k)$.  Since the Segre determinant is supported on all labels, \eqref{eq:segre-pair} shows that it belongs to every ideal $(p_{ij},q_{ij})$.

We next check the non-vanishing of the Segre factor in $Q_{2k}$.  Split the labels into two sets $I_1,I_2$ of size $k$.  Choose $k$ distinct points $[\lambda_i]\in\mathbb P^1$ on each set, with nonzero representatives $\lambda_i\in L$.  The corresponding degree-$(k-1)$ Veronese evaluation matrices are invertible.  Since $k\geq4$, one may choose nonzero scalars $c_i$ on $I_1$ and $d_i$ on $I_2$ with
\[
 \sum_{i\in I_1}c_i\lambda_i=0,\qquad
 \sum_{i\in I_2}d_i\lambda_i=0.
\]

We check the previous statement. Since the points $[\lambda_i]\in\mathbb P^1$, $i\in I_1$, are
pairwise distinct, the $2\times k$ matrix having the $\lambda_i$ as
columns has rank $2$, and hence its kernel $K$ has dimension $k-2$.
Moreover, $K$ is not contained in any coordinate hyperplane
$\{c_i=0\}$. Indeed, fixing $i$ and choosing distinct
$j,\ell\neq i$, the vectors $\lambda_j,\lambda_\ell$ form a basis of
$\mathbb C^2$, so $\lambda_i=a\lambda_j+b\lambda_\ell$,
with $a,b\neq0$, giving a kernel vector whose $i$th coordinate is
nonzero. Thus each $K\cap\{c_i=0\}$ is a proper linear subspace of
$K$. Since $\mathbb C$ is infinite, a finite union of proper linear
subspaces cannot cover $K$. Hence there exists
$(c_i)_{i\in I_1}\in K$ with $c_i\neq0$ for every $i$, that is,
\[
\sum_{i\in I_1} c_i\lambda_i=0,
\qquad c_i\neq0\quad\text{for all }i\in I_1.
\]

Set $\widetilde\lambda_i=c_i(1,0)$ on $I_1$ and $\widetilde\lambda_i=d_i(0,1)$ on $I_2$.  Momentum conservation holds, while after grouping the columns according to their $u$- and $v$-factors the Segre matrix is block diagonal with two nonzero Veronese determinants.  Hence $\Delta_{k-1,1}$ is nonzero in $Q_{2k}$.  Since $Q_{2k}$ is a domain, each Pl\"ucker coordinate is a nonzero class, and $\Psi_r$ is a monomial in these variables, so $F_{2k}\neq0$.

Finally, both $\Delta_{k-1,1}$ and $\Psi_r$ change sign under $(1\,2)$, by \Cref{prop:segre-basic,lem:Psi}.  Their product is therefore invariant.  The Hodges numerator is alternating, so the nonzero $F_{2k}$ cannot be proportional to it.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:odd}
For $n=2k+1\geq9$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fodd}
 F_{2k+1}=\Delta_{k-2,1}(S)\,\Psi_{k-4}(S)\,\Theta_k.
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 0\neq F_{2k+1}\in(J_{2k+1})_{d(2k+1)}.
\]
If $n\geq11$, then $(1\,2)F_{2k+1}=F_{2k+1}$, hence $F_{2k+1}$ is not proportional to the Hodges numerator.  The same conclusion holds for $n=9$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The spinor multidegrees in \eqref{eq:odd-core-degree}, \eqref{eq:Psi-degree}, and \eqref{eq:Theta-degree} add as follows:
\begin{align*}
\deg_pF_{2k+1}
 &=(k-2)(k-1)+k(k-4)+(6k-6)=2k^2-k-4,\\
\deg_qF_{2k+1}
 &=(k-1)+(k-4)+3=2k-2.
\end{align*}
For $i\in S$, the label component is $(k-3)+(k-4)+3=2k-4$,
while for $t\in T$ it is already $2k-4$ from $\Theta_k$.  Thus $\deg F_{2k+1}=d(2k+1)$.

Pairs inside $S$ are covered by the Segre determinant.  Every pair joining $S$ to $T$ occurs as a $p$-factor in $\Theta_k$, and every pair inside $T$ occurs as a $q$-factor.  Hence $F_{2k+1}\in J_{2k+1}$.

We next prove that $F_{2k+1}$ is nonzero in $Q_{2k+1}$.
We first show that the Segre factor $\Delta_{k-2,1}(S)$
defines a nonzero class in $Q_{2k+1}$.
By \Cref{prop:segre-basic}, there exists a choice of the spinor pairs
$(\lambda_i,\widetilde\lambda_i), i\in S$,
for which $\Delta_{k-2,1}(S)\neq 0$.
These spinors need not satisfy momentum conservation.  Let $P_S:=\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_i\widetilde\lambda_i$
be their total momentum matrix.  This is an arbitrary $2\times2$ complex
matrix.  Since every $2\times2$ matrix is a sum of at most two rank-one
matrices, we may write
\[
-P_S=
\lambda_{2k-1}\widetilde\lambda_{2k-1}
+
\lambda_{2k}\widetilde\lambda_{2k}.
\]
If, say, $-P_S=
\begin{pmatrix}
a&b\\
c&d
\end{pmatrix},
$
one may take
\[
\lambda_{2k-1}=
\binom{a}{c},
\qquad
\widetilde\lambda_{2k-1}=
(1,0),
\qquad
\lambda_{2k}=
\binom{b}{d},
\qquad
\widetilde\lambda_{2k}=
(0,1).
\]
For the last label we may take, for instance, $\lambda_{2k+1}=0$,
and choose $\widetilde\lambda_{2k+1}$ arbitrarily.  Its contribution to
the momentum matrix is then zero.  Consequently,
$\sum_{i=1}^{2k+1}\lambda_i\widetilde\lambda_i
=
P_S+
\lambda_{2k-1}\widetilde\lambda_{2k-1}
+
\lambda_{2k}\widetilde\lambda_{2k}
=0$.
Thus the resulting $(2k+1)$-tuple satisfies the momentum-conservation
relations defining $Q_{2k+1}$.

Since $\Delta_{k-2,1}(S)$ depends only on the labels in $S$, its value at
this momentum-conserving configuration is the same nonzero value with
which we started.  Hence
$\Delta_{k-2,1}(S)\neq0$ in $Q_{2k+1}$.

Finally, $Q_{2k+1}$ is the homogeneous coordinate ring of the
spinor-helicity variety, equivalently of the two-step flag variety
described above, and in particular it is an integral domain.  Each
Pl\"ucker coordinate appearing in the monomials
$\Psi_{k-4}(S)$ and $\Theta_k$ is a nonzero class in $Q_{2k+1}$.
Therefore $\Psi_{k-4}(S)\Theta_k\neq0$ in
$Q_{2k+1}$, and hence $F_{2k+1}\neq0$.

If $k\geq5$, equivalently $n\geq11$, then $r=k-4\geq1$.  The Segre determinant and $\Psi_r$ are both anti-invariant under $(1\,2)$, while $\Theta_k$ is invariant.  Therefore $(1\,2)F_{2k+1}=F_{2k+1}$, so the element is not proportional to the Hodges numerator.

For $n=9$ one has $k=4$ and $\Psi_0=1$, so the preceding argument cannot be applied.  An exact momentum-conserving witness is recorded in \Cref{app:witnesses}; at that point  $F_9=0$ and  $((6\,7)F_9)\neq0$.
Hence $(6\,7)F_9$ is not equal to $-F_9$, and $F_9$ is not alternating.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:S7-new}
The class of $\mathcal S_7^{ab}$ in $Q_7$ is independent of $\{a,b\}$.  Denote the common class by $\mathcal S_7$.  Moreover,
\[
 \mathcal S_7\in(p_{i7},q_{i7})Q_7\qquad(1\leq i\leq6).
\]
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
For distinct $a,b,c\in[6]$, the Pl\"ucker relation
\[
 p_{bk}p_{c7}-p_{ck}p_{b7}=p_{bc}p_{k7}
\]
gives, after collecting the two terms $k=b,c$,
\[
 \mathcal S_7^{ab}-\mathcal S_7^{ac}
 =p_{bc}\!\prod_{\ell\in[6]\setminus\{a,b,c\}}p_{\ell7}
 \sum_{r\in[6]\setminus\{a\}}p_{ar}q_{r7}.
\]
The last sum is the momentum relation $M_{a7}$, hence the difference is zero in $Q_7$.  This proves independence of $\{a,b\}$.

For the pair $(i,7)$ choose a representative $\mathcal S_7^{ab}$ with $i\notin\{a,b\}$.  Its $k=i$ summand contains $q_{i7}$; every other summand contains $p_{i7}$.  Thus the common class lies in $(p_{i7},q_{i7})$.
