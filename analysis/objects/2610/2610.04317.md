<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $D$ be a division ring of finite dimension over its center $F$. Then the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $D$ is an Amitsur--Small{} ring;
\item either $D=F$, or $F$ is real closed and
\[
D\cong (-1,-1)_F.
\]
\end{enumerate}
If $D$ is noncommutative, these conditions are also equivalent to $D$ being left algebraically closed (equivalently, right algebraically closed). Moreover, if $D$ is noncommutative and does not satisfy the second condition, then there exists a maximal left ideal $M$ of $D[x,y]$ such that $M\cap D[x]$ is not maximal in $D[x]$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Let $D$ be a division ring. We say that $D$ is an \emph{Amitsur--Small{} ring} if, for every $n\ge 2$, every $1\le k<n$, and every maximal left ideal
\[
M\subset D[x_1,\ldots,x_n],
\]
the contraction
\[
M\cap D[x_1,\ldots,x_k]
\]
is a maximal left ideal of $D[x_1,\ldots,x_k]$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:standard}
Let $D$ be a finite-dimensional central division algebra over a field $F$, and write $d=\deg D$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item There exists a finite separable extension $K/F$ such that $D_K$ is split.
\item The Artin--Schreier Theorem: If an algebraic closure of $F$ has finite degree over $F$, then $F$ is either algebraically closed or real closed. In the latter case its algebraic closure is $F(\sqrt{-1})$ and has degree two over $F$. 
\item The algebra $D$ contains a maximal subfield $K$ which is separable over $F$. Every such $K$ satisfies $[K:F]=d$ and is a splitting field of $D$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:NJB}
Let $D$ be a noncommutative division ring of finite dimension over its center $F$. The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item every nonconstant polynomial in $F[x]$ has a root in $D$;
\item $D$ is right algebraically closed;
\item $D$ is left algebraically closed;
\item $F$ is real closed and
\[
D\cong(-1,-1)_F.
\]
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:nullstellensatz}
Let $D$ be a division ring.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $D$ is a Nullstellensatz ring if and only if $D$ is centrally algebraically closed.
\item If $D$ is right algebraically closed, then
\[
\begin{aligned}
D\text{ is Amitsur--Small{}}
&\Longleftrightarrow D\text{ is a Nullstellensatz ring}\\
&\Longleftrightarrow D\text{ is centrally algebraically closed}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:pullback}
Let $D$ be a central division algebra over $F$, and let
\[
F\subseteq K=F(\theta)\subseteq E=K(\alpha)
\]
be a tower of finite field extensions. Consider the epimorphisms
\[
\Phi:D[x,y]\longrightarrow D_E,
\qquad x\longmapsto\theta,
\qquad y\longmapsto\alpha,
\]
and
\[
\Psi:D[x]\longrightarrow D_K,
\qquad x\longmapsto\theta.
\]
Let $N$ be a left ideal of $D_E$, and put $M=\Phi^{-1}(N)$. Then
\[
M\cap D[x]=\Psi^{-1}(N\cap D_K).
\]
Consequently, if $N$ is maximal in $D_E$ and $N\cap D_K$ is not maximal in $D_K$, then $M$ is maximal in $D[x,y]$ and $M\cap D[x]$ is not maximal in $D[x]$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The maps are surjective because $K=F(\theta)$ and $E=K(\alpha)$, and the variables are mapped to central elements. Moreover, the restriction of $\Phi$ to $D[x]$ is the composition of $\Psi$ with the natural inclusion $D_K\subseteq D_E$. Thus, for $f\in D[x]$,
\[
f\in M\Longleftrightarrow \Psi(f)\in N\cap D_K,
\]
which proves the equality.

If $N$ is maximal, then $M$ is maximal because $\Phi$ is surjective. Finally,
\[
D[x]/(M\cap D[x])\cong D_K/(N\cap D_K)
\]
as left $D[x]$-modules, where the $D[x]$-module structure on the right is induced by the surjection $\Psi:D[x]\to D_K$. Hence the $D[x]$-submodules of $D_K/(N\cap D_K)$ are exactly its $D_K$-submodules, and therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
M\cap D[x]\text{ is maximal in }D[x]
&\Longleftrightarrow D[x]/(M\cap D[x])\text{ is simple}\\
&\hspace{2.4em}\text{as a left }D[x]\text{-module}\\
&\Longleftrightarrow D_K/(N\cap D_K)\text{ is a simple left }D_K\text{-module}\\
&\Longleftrightarrow N\cap D_K\text{ is maximal in }D_K.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last equivalence proves the asserted nonmaximality of $M\cap D[x]$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:annihilator}
Let $A$ be a split central simple algebra of degree $d\geq 2$ over a field $K$, let $V$ be a simple left $A$-module, and let $E=K(\alpha)$ be a proper finite extension. Choose linearly independent elements $u_1,u_2\in V$, put
\[
A_E=A\otimes_K E,
\qquad
V_E=V\otimes_K E,
\qquad
w=u_2\otimes 1-u_1\otimes\alpha,
\]
and let
\[
N=\operatorname{Ann}_{A_E}(w)=\{a\in A_E:aw=0\}.
\]
Then $N$ is a maximal left ideal of $A_E$, and
\[
N\cap A=\operatorname{Ann}_A(u_1,u_2)
=\{a\in A:au_1=au_2=0\}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
A/(N\cap A)\cong V\oplus V
\]
as left $A$-modules. In particular, $N\cap A$ is not maximal and the quotient has composition length two. Finally, $N=A_E\pi$ for an idempotent $\pi\in A_E$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The left $A$-module structure on $V$ defines a $K$-algebra homomorphism
\[
A\longrightarrow\operatorname{End}_K(V),
\qquad
 a\longmapsto(v\longmapsto av).
\]
Since $A$ is split and $V$ is simple, the Wedderburn--Artin theorem identifies this homomorphism with an isomorphism
\[
A\cong\operatorname{End}_K(V).
\]
After extending scalars from $K$ to $E$, the induced left $A_E$-module structure on $V_E$ similarly gives
\[
A_E\cong\operatorname{End}_E(V_E),
\]
and $V_E$ is a simple left $A_E$-module. These standard facts about split simple algebras and their simple modules are contained in \cite[Theorem~3.3]{Lam}; see also \cite[Chapter~1, Sections~2--3]{Lam} for the Wedderburn--Artin theory.

The vector $w$ is nonzero, since the linearly independent vectors $u_1,u_2$ remain linearly independent after extending scalars. Give $A_E$ its left regular module structure and $V_E$ the left $A_E$-module structure just described. Then the evaluation map
\[
A_E\longrightarrow V_E,
\qquad a\longmapsto aw,
\]
is a nonzero homomorphism of left $A_E$-modules. Its image is a nonzero submodule of the simple module $V_E$, and is therefore all of $V_E$. Its kernel is $N$, so $N$ is maximal.

For $a\in A$, one has
\[
aw=au_2\otimes 1-au_1\otimes\alpha.
\]
Since $1$ and $\alpha$ are linearly independent over $K$, this vanishes if and only if $au_1=au_2=0$. Hence
\[
N\cap A=\operatorname{Ann}_A(u_1,u_2).
\]
The map
\[
A\longrightarrow V\oplus V,
\qquad a\longmapsto(au_1,au_2),
\]
is surjective: prescribed images of the linearly independent vectors $u_1,u_2$ extend to a $K$-linear endomorphism of $V$, and the isomorphism $A\cong\operatorname{End}_K(V)$ shows that every such endomorphism is induced by an element of $A$. Its kernel is $N\cap A$, which gives the asserted isomorphism. Since $V$ is simple, the quotient has composition length two.

Choose a decomposition $V_E=Ew\oplus U$, and let $\pi$ be the projection onto $U$ along $Ew$. Then $\pi^2=\pi$ and $\pi(w)=0$. An endomorphism $a$ of $V_E$ annihilates $w$ if and only if $a=a\pi$. Therefore
\[
N=A_E\pi.
\]
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:obstruction}
Let $D$ be a central division algebra of degree $d>1$ over $F$, and let $K/F$ be a finite separable splitting field of $D$. If $K$ is not algebraically closed, then $D$ is not an Amitsur--Small{} ring. Moreover, a counterexample may be chosen in $D[x,y]$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By the primitive element theorem, we may write $K=F(\theta)$. Since $K$ is not algebraically closed, there is a proper finite extension $E=K(\alpha)$. Put $A=D_K$, and choose a simple left $A$-module $V$. Since $A$ is split of degree $d>1$, the $K$-space $V$ has dimension $d$, so it contains linearly independent elements $u_1,u_2$.

Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:annihilator} to $A$, $V$ and $E/K$. Under the natural identification $A_E\cong D_E$, it gives a maximal left ideal $N$ of $D_E$ such that $N\cap D_K$ is not maximal in $D_K$. Lemma~\ref{lem:pullback} now gives a maximal left ideal $M$ of $D[x,y]$ whose contraction to $D[x]$ is not maximal.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:generators}
The construction may also be described by generators. Let $p(X)\in F[X]$ be the minimal polynomial of $\theta$. Let $q(Y)\in K[Y]$ be the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$, and choose a polynomial $\widetilde q(X,Y)\in F[X,Y]$ whose image in
\[
F[X,Y]/(p(X))\cong K[Y]
\]
is $q(Y)$. The homomorphism $F[X,Y]\to E$ given by $X\mapsto\theta$ and $Y\mapsto\alpha$ has kernel $(p(X),\widetilde q(X,Y))$: after quotienting by $p(X)$, it becomes the map $K[Y]\to E$ with kernel $(q(Y))$. Tensoring the resulting exact sequence over $F$ with the $F$-vector space $D$, and using $D[x,y]\cong D\otimes_F F[X,Y]$ and $D_E=D\otimes_F E$, gives
\[
\ker\Phi=D[x,y]p(x)+D[x,y]\widetilde q(x,y).
\]

The ideal $N$ in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:obstruction} is principal by the last assertion of Lemma~\ref{lem:annihilator}. Write
\[
N=D_E\pi
\]
for the idempotent $\pi$ supplied there, and choose $g\in D[x,y]$ with $\Phi(g)=\pi$. Since $\Phi$ is surjective,
\[
\Phi^{-1}(N)=D[x,y]g+\ker\Phi.
\]
Consequently, the maximal left ideal constructed above is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threegen}
M=D[x,y]g+D[x,y]p(x)+D[x,y]\widetilde q(x,y).
\end{equation}
Thus the counterexample can always be taken to be a three-generated left ideal.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:splitting-closed}
Let $D$ be a noncommutative centrally finite Amitsur--Small{} division ring. Then every finite separable splitting field of $D$ is algebraically closed. In particular, the center of $D$ has a finite algebraic closure.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The first claim follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:obstruction}. A finite separable splitting field exists by Theorem~\ref{thm:standard}(a), and this field is algebraic over the center. If it is algebraically closed, it is an algebraic closure of the center.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:centralizer-one}
Let $F$ be real closed, let $D=(-1,-1)_F$, and let $a\in D\setminus F$. Then
\[
\mathrm{C}_D(a)=F(a),
\]
and this field is algebraically closed.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Quaternionic conjugation gives
\[
a^2-(a+\overline a)a+a\overline a=0,
\]
whose coefficients lie in $F$. Since $a\notin F$, its minimal polynomial over $F$ has degree two, and hence $[F(a):F]=2$. By the double-centralizer theorem,
\[
\dim_F\mathrm{C}_D(a)=\frac{\dim_F D}{[F(a):F]}=2;
\]
see \cite[Section~12.7]{Pierce}. Since $F(a)\subseteq\mathrm{C}_D(a)$ and both have dimension two over $F$, they are equal. Finally, a proper finite extension of a real closed field is its quadratic algebraic closure, so Theorem~\ref{thm:standard}(b) shows that $F(a)$ is algebraically closed.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:centrally-closed}
Let $F$ be real closed and $D=(-1,-1)_F$. Then $D$ is centrally algebraically closed.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $a_1,\ldots,a_r$ be pairwise commuting elements of $D$. If all of them lie in $F$, then their common centralizer is $D$, which is right algebraically closed by Theorem~\ref{thm:NJB}. Otherwise choose $a_i\notin F$. Lemma~\ref{lem:centralizer-one} gives
\[
\mathrm{C}_D(a_i)=F(a_i).
\]
Every $a_j$ belongs to this commutative field because it commutes with $a_i$. Consequently, every element of $F(a_i)$ commutes with all the $a_j$, and hence
\[
\mathrm{C}_D(a_1,\ldots,a_r)=F(a_i).
\]
This field is algebraically closed by Lemma~\ref{lem:centralizer-one}. Thus every common centralizer of a commuting tuple is right algebraically closed.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:positive}
Let $F$ be real closed and let
\[
D=(-1,-1)_F.
\]
Then $D$ is an Amitsur--Small{} ring.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
By Theorem~\ref{thm:NJB}, the division ring $D$ is right algebraically closed. By Lemma~\ref{lem:centrally-closed}, it is centrally algebraically closed. Theorem~\ref{thm:nullstellensatz}(b), which combines \cite[Theorem~4.2]{ChapmanAuthor} with Aryapoor's central Nullstellensatz, now implies that $D$ is Amitsur--Small{}.
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
For the application of \cite[Theorem~4.2]{ChapmanAuthor}, one-sided algebraic closedness must be supplemented by the Nullstellensatz property. In the quaternionic case this additional condition follows from the centralizer computation above and \cite[Section~4]{Aryapoor}.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
If $D=F$, then $D$ is Amitsur--Small{} by the commutative case. If $F$ is real closed and $D\cong(-1,-1)_F$, then $D$ is Amitsur--Small{} by Proposition~\ref{prop:positive}.

Conversely, suppose that $D$ is Amitsur--Small{}. If $D$ is commutative, then $D=F$. Suppose that $D$ is noncommutative, and choose a maximal subfield $K\subseteq D$ which is separable over $F$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:standard}(c), the field $K$ is a finite separable splitting field of $D$. Corollary~\ref{cor:splitting-closed} therefore shows that $K$ is algebraically closed. Every nonconstant polynomial in $F[x]$ consequently has a root in $K\subseteq D$. Theorem~\ref{thm:NJB}, in Baer's central-root form, now gives
\[
F\text{ real closed},
\qquad D\cong(-1,-1)_F.
\]
The equivalence with left and right algebraic closedness follows from the remaining parts of Theorem~\ref{thm:NJB}.

It remains to prove the final assertion. Suppose that $D$ is noncommutative and is not of the displayed form. Choose a maximal subfield $K\subseteq D$ which is separable over $F$. If $K$ were algebraically closed, every nonconstant polynomial in $F[x]$ would have a root in $K\subseteq D$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:NJB} would imply that $F$ is real closed and $D\cong(-1,-1)_F$, a contradiction. Thus $K$ is not algebraically closed. Since $K$ is a finite separable splitting field by Theorem~\ref{thm:standard}(c), Proposition~\ref{prop:obstruction} supplies a maximal left ideal $M\subseteq D[x,y]$ such that $M\cap D[x]$ is not maximal in $D[x]$.

<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:constructible}
Let $F=\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{constr}}$ be the field of real constructible numbers, and put
\[
D=(-1,-1)_F.
\]
Recall that every element of $F$ lies in the top field of a finite tower of quadratic extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$. Since $F\subseteq\mathbb{R}$, it is formally real. Moreover, if $a,b\in F$, then $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ is constructible, so $F$ is Pythagorean. In particular, the reduced norm of $D$ is anisotropic and $D$ is a division algebra. It is not real closed, since $\sqrt[3]{2}\notin F$. Hence $D$ is not Amitsur--Small{} by Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

We now write down an explicit counterexample. Let
\[
K=F(\varepsilon),
\qquad \varepsilon^2=-1,
\qquad \alpha=\sqrt[3]{2},
\qquad E=K(\alpha).
\]
The element $\alpha$ does not belong to $K$: every element of $K$ is contained in a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ of degree a power of two, whereas $T^3-2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by Eisenstein's criterion and hence $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]=3$. The same argument applies to every root of $T^3-2$, so the polynomial has no root in $K$. Since it has degree three, it is irreducible over $K$.

Since $F$ is formally real, $-1$ is not a square in $F$. Thus $[F(i):F]=2=\deg D$, so $F(i)$ is a maximal subfield of $D$ and hence splits $D$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:standard}(c). Let
\[
\sigma:F(i)\longrightarrow K
\]
be the $F$-isomorphism determined by $\sigma(i)=\varepsilon$. The induced isomorphism
\[
\operatorname{id}_D\otimes\sigma:
D\otimes_F F(i)\longrightarrow D\otimes_F K
\]
shows that $K$ also splits $D$. Put $B=D_K$ and
\[
e=\frac{1-\varepsilon i}{2}\in B.
\]
Since $(\varepsilon i)^2=1$, the element $e$ is an idempotent. If $z=\varepsilon i$, then $zj=-jz$. Using the $K$-basis $1,z,j,zj$ of $B$, one obtains
\[
Be=Ke\oplus Kje,
\qquad eBe=Ke.
\]
Since $eBe$ is a division ring, $e$ is primitive; equivalently, $V=Be$ is a minimal nonzero left ideal of the simple Artinian ring $B$, and hence is a simple left $B$-module. See \cite[Chapter~1, Sections~2--3]{Lam}. It has $K$-basis
\[
u_1=e,
\qquad u_2=-je.
\]
The annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}_B(V)$ is a two-sided ideal of the simple ring $B$. Since $V\neq0$, this annihilator is proper and therefore zero. Thus $V$ is faithful.

In $V_E=V\otimes_K E$, set
\[
w=u_2\otimes1-u_1\otimes\alpha=-(j+\alpha)e
\]
and
\[
N=\operatorname{Ann}_{D_E}(w).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:annihilator}, the left ideal $N$ is maximal. Since $u_1,u_2$ form a basis of the faithful $B$-module $V$, Lemma~\ref{lem:annihilator} gives
\[
N\cap B=0.
\]

Let
\[
h=(1-\varepsilon i)(1+\alpha j)=2e(1+\alpha j)\in D_E.
\]
The relation $eje=0$ follows from $zj=-jz$, and hence
\[
hw
= -2e(1+\alpha j)(j+\alpha)e
= -2(1+\alpha^2)eje
=0.
\]
Therefore $D_Eh\subseteq N$. The element $1+\alpha j$ is invertible, with inverse
\[
\frac{1-\alpha j}{1+\alpha^2}.
\]
Here $1+\alpha^2\ne 0$, since $T^3-2$ is the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $K$. Right multiplication by $1+\alpha j$ therefore identifies $D_Ee$ with $D_Eh$, so $D_Eh$ has dimension two over $E$. The evaluation sequence in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:annihilator} gives $D_E/N\cong V_E$, and hence $N$ also has dimension two over $E$. Consequently,
\[
N=D_Eh.
\]

Let $R=D[s,t]$, and consider the left ideal
\begin{equation}\label{eq:explicit}
M=R(s^2+1)+R(t^3-2)+R(1-si)(1+tj).
\end{equation}
Under the epimorphism
\[
\Phi:R\longrightarrow D_E,
\qquad s\longmapsto\varepsilon,
\qquad t\longmapsto\alpha,
\]
the first two generators of \eqref{eq:explicit} vanish and the third maps to $h$. Remark~\ref{rem:generators} gives
\[
\ker\Phi=R(s^2+1)+R(t^3-2).
\]
Since $N=D_Eh$, it follows that $M=\Phi^{-1}(N)$, and hence $M$ is maximal.

The image of $D[s]$ is $B=D_K$. Since $N\cap B=0$, Lemma~\ref{lem:pullback} gives
\[
M\cap D[s]
=
\ker\bigl(D[s]\longrightarrow D_K\bigr)
=
D[s](s^2+1).
\]
Finally,
\[
D[s]/D[s](s^2+1)\cong D_K=B.
\]
The zero left ideal of $B$ is not maximal, since $Be$ is a nonzero proper left ideal. Thus the displayed contraction is not maximal.
