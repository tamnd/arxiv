We say $S \subset \mathbb N$ is \emph{large} if any finite coloring of $\mathbb N$ has arbitrarily long monochromatic arithmetic progressions with common difference in $S$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
We say $S \subset \mathbb N$ is \emph{$r$-large} if any $r$-coloring of $\mathbb N$ has arbitrarily long monochromatic arithmetic progressions with common difference in $S$.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- bglconjecture-1: body_md -->
Every $2$-large set is large.

<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:Bohr}
    A set $B \subset \mathbb N$ is a \emph{Bohr neighborhood of $0$} if there exist $\alpha_1, \cdots, \alpha_k, \varepsilon \in \mathbb R^{+}$ so that $B = \{n \in \mathbb{N}\ |\ ||n\alpha_i||_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}} < \epsilon\ \forall\ 1 \le i \le k\}$.
    If $S \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ intersects every Bohr neighborhood of $0$, then $S$ is a \emph{set of Bohr recurrence}.
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
If $S$ is $2$-large, then there do not exist $\alpha, \varepsilon \in \mathbb R^{+}$ so that $||s \alpha||_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z}>\varepsilon$ for all $s \in S$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->

<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\label{def:NilBohr}
    The set $S \subset \mathbb N$ is a \emph{nil-Bohr neighborhood}\footnote{This gives the same notion of nil-Bohr recurrence as \cite{HSY}, by Theorems B and 4.2.11.} \emph{of $0$} if there exist special generalized polynomials $P_1, \cdots, P_k$ and an $\varepsilon > 0$ for which $S = \{n \in \mathbb{N}\ |\ ||P_i(n)||_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z}<\varepsilon\ \forall\ 1 \le i \le k\}$.
    If $S$ intersects every nil-Bohr neighborhood of $0$, then $S$ is a set of \emph{nil-Bohr recurrence}.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:omitted}
For every $r\geq2$ and $m,D\geq1$, there are constants $C=C(r,m,D)>0$ and $k_0=k_0(r,m,D)\geq2$ with the following property.
For every family of special generalized polynomials $P_1,\ldots,P_m$ of degrees $1\leq d_i\leq D$, there is an $r$-coloring $c$ of $\mathbb N$ such that, for all $a,d\in\mathbb N$ and all $k\geq k_0$, if the progression $\{a, a+d, \cdots, a+(k-1)d\}$ does not contain elements of all $r$ colors, then $||P_i(d)||_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z} \le Ck^{-d_i}$ for $1 \le i \le m$.

The coloring is fixed independently of $k$, and the constants are independent of all the real coefficients.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:extremal}
Fix $\varepsilon,c>0$ and $m,D\geq1$. There is
$k_0=k_0(\varepsilon,c,m,D)$ such that, for every family $P_1,\ldots,P_m$ of
special generalized polynomials of degrees $1\leq d_i\leq D$, every
$k\geq k_0$, and every finite $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb N$, the set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bk}
 B_k(c)=\{d\in\mathbb N:\lVert P_i(d)\rVert_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z}\leq ck^{-d_i}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq m\}
\end{equation}
satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:extremal}
 r_k(\Omega)\leq r_k(\Omega;B_k(c))
 \leq(1+\varepsilon)r_k(\Omega).
\end{equation}

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:PolychromaticRamseyTheory}
    The passage from Theorem~\ref{thm:omitted} to its partition and density consequences uses a standard principle from the theory of polychromatic colorings.
Such a coloring partitions the vertices of a hypergraph into transversals, each meeting every edge; see \cite[Section~1.1]{BollobasEtAl2013}. Consequently, merging the
colors into two nonempty groups gives a coloring with no monochromatic edge, while the complement of each original color class is independent.
In the finite setting, deleting a smallest color class retains at least a proportion \(1-1/r\) of the vertices;
this observation is recorded explicitly in \cite[Observation~4]{Palvolgyi2024}.
A corresponding density argument for translates of a fixed finite subset of \(\mathbb Z\) appears in \cite[Lemma~1]{AxenovichEtAl2019}, where polychromatic colorings yield blocking sets of small density.
Our upper-density deduction above applies the same complement-of-one-color principle to the family of progressions whose differences violate the prescribed approximation conditions.

Let $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d,\varepsilon\in\mathbb R^{+}$
and let $S$ be the set of $x$ such that
$\max_{1\le i\le d}\|\alpha_i x\|_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z}>\varepsilon$. We will pick a sufficiently large integer $K$ as a function of $d$ and $\varepsilon$, and then pick some $L$ divisible by all integers up to $M^d$ for some sufficiently large function $M$ of $K$. In particular, $M=K^2$ and $L=(M^d)!$ suffices. As in \cite{AlweissBohr}, we define a coloring of
$(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z)^d$ (actually, in \cite{AlweissBohr} we color $(\mathbb R/L\mathbb Z)^d$, but this normalization will be more convenient for the general case), then pull it back to $\mathbb Z$ via the map $h=g \circ f: \mathbb Z \to \mathbb R^d \to (\mathbb R/\mathbb Z)^d$, where the first map $f$ sends
$n\mapsto(\alpha_1n/L,\ldots,\alpha_dn/L)$
and the second map $g$ is the natural quotient map
by $\mathbb Z^d$, with fundamental domain $[-1/2,1/2)^d$.

However, rather than splitting this into $M^d$ randomly colored cells as in
\cite{AlweissBohr}, we color the point
$(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\in[-1/2,1/2)^d$ by the parity of $\left\lfloor K\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_i^2\right\rfloor$, so that if this floor is even then the point is red and if it is odd then the point is blue.

        

                 
          
                                  
                                      
                                       
                 

                                                   
                                                         
                                                     

               
                                      
                                      
                                                     
                                               
                     
                                                     
           
                                                      
         
     
             
                 

                                                           

            

   

For $y \in S$, consider the image of $x, x+y, \cdots, x+ky$ under the map $h$. We will prove that when $k$ is sufficiently large and divisible by a sufficiently large factorial, this image contains points of both colors. We partition $(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z)^d$ into $M^d$ boxes of side length $1/M$ in the obvious way.
By pigeonhole, there is some $0 \le a<b \le M^d \ll k$ so that $h(x+ay)$ and $h(x+by)$ are in the same box. Hence $h(x+ay)$ and $h(x+by)$ differ by at most $1/M$ in each coordinate, and on $(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z)^d$ the same is true of $h(x)$ and $h(x+cy)$ where $1 \le c=b-a \le M^d$. Let $v\in[-1/M,1/M]^d$ be a representative of $h(cy)$.

Now, some coordinate $y'$ of $f(y)$ satisfies
$\|Ly'\|_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z}>\varepsilon$, and the same coordinate of $f(cy)$ is $cy'$.

$|\eta|>c\varepsilon/L$.

\[

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:algebra}
The class $\mathcal H$ is closed under multiplication, inversion, and affine
changes of parameter. The map $\delta_u$ is an automorphism of $G$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau-algebra}
 \tau(AB)=\tau(A)\tau(B),\qquad
 \tau\bigl(A(b+qt)\bigr)=\delta_q\tau(A)\quad(q>0).
\end{equation}
Moreover, $\tau(A)=I$ if $A$ is constant.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For upper-unitriangular matrices,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-product}
 (AB)_{ij}=A_{ij}+B_{ij}+\sum_{i<k<j}A_{ik}B_{kj}.
\end{equation}
Additivity of the weights bounds the degree of every term by $w_{ij}$.
The inverse of $I+V$, with $V$ strictly upper triangular, is the finite
sum $I-V+V^2-\cdots$; every path of entries contributing to position
$(i,j)$ again has total weight $w_{ij}$. This proves the closure
assertions. The same additive identity proves that $\delta_u$ respects
multiplication; its inverse is $\delta_{1/u}$.

In a product $A_{ik}B_{kj}$, a term of degree
$w_{ij}=w_{ik}+w_{kj}$ can arise only from the terms of the respective
maximal allowed degrees. Taking this coefficient in
\eqref{eq:matrix-product} proves the first identity in
\eqref{eq:tau-algebra}. The second follows from the scalar identity
\[
 [t^w]f(b+qt)=q^w[t^w]f(t)\qquad(\deg f\leq w).
\]
Finally, constant entries contribute zero because their weights are
positive.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:rounding}
Let $M\geq D+2$, and partition $I^\nu$ into boxes whose coordinate
intervals have length at most $\xi$, where
$2^{D+1}\xi<1$. If $A\in\mathcal H$ and
$\operatorname{rep}(A(0)),\ldots,\operatorname{rep}(A(M-1))$ lie in one box, then there are
$Q,C\in\mathcal H$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounding}
 A(t)C(t)=Q(t)\quad(t\in\mathbb R),\qquad
 C(n)\in\Gamma\quad(n\in\mathbb Z),
\end{equation}
with $Q(n)=\operatorname{rep}(A(n))$ for $0\leq n<M$. Furthermore,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounding-factorial}
 w_{ij}!\,\tau(C)_{ij}\in\mathbb Z
\end{equation}
for every off-diagonal position.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
On the initial samples write
$A(n)\lambda(n)=r(n)=\operatorname{rep}(A(n))$, with $\lambda(n)\in\Gamma$.
We construct $C_{ij}$ and $Q_{ij}$ in increasing order of the gap,
maintaining $C_{ij}(n)=\lambda(n)_{ij}$ and $Q_{ij}(n)=r_{ij}(n)$
for $0\leq n<M$.

For the current position $(i,j)$ put $w=w_{ij}$ and
\[
 f_{ij}(t)=A_{ij}(t)+\sum_{i<k<j}A_{ik}(t)C_{kj}(t).
\]
The entries in the sum have already been constructed.
Their weighted degree bounds give $\deg f_{ij}\leq w$, while the induction hypothesis
and \eqref{eq:matrix-product} give
\[
 r_{ij}(n)-f_{ij}(n)=\lambda(n)_{ij}\in\mathbb Z
 \qquad(0\leq n<M).
\]
Thus $\Delta^{w+1}r_{ij}(n)$ is an integer wherever it is defined.
All the $r_{ij}(n)$ lie in an interval of length at most $\xi$.
Subtracting an endpoint of that interval does not change a positive-order
difference. Hence \eqref{eq:diff-formula} yields
\[
 |\Delta^{w+1}r_{ij}(n)|\leq2^{w+1}\xi<1.
\]
These integer differences therefore vanish exactly.

The finite interpolation observation gives a polynomial $Q_{ij}$ of
degree at most $w$ agreeing with all the $r_{ij}(n)$. Set
$C_{ij}=Q_{ij}-f_{ij}$. It has degree at most $w$ and is integer-valued
at $0,\ldots,w$. Formula~\eqref{eq:newton} makes it integer-valued at
every integer and gives \eqref{eq:rounding-factorial}. The equation
defining $C_{ij}$ is precisely the $(i,j)$ entry of $AC=Q$.
Induction through all gaps and blocks completes the construction.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:shell}
The minimum in \eqref{eq:shell} is attained. The function $F$ is
continuous, satisfies $0\leq F(A)\leq\nu/4$, and is invariant under
right multiplication by $\Gamma$. On every compact set of matrices,
only finitely many elements of $\Gamma$ can occur as minimizers.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The canonical representative gives a candidate with squared-coordinate
sum at most $\nu/4$. If $A$ ranges over a fixed compact set and
$\sum(A\gamma)_{ij}^2\leq\nu/4$, then $A\gamma$ lies in a bounded
coordinate box. The matrices $\gamma=A^{-1}(A\gamma)$ are therefore
bounded as well. Only finitely many integer matrices have this property.
Thus the minimum is attained, with a common finite set of candidates on
each compact set. Locally $F$ is consequently the minimum of finitely
many continuous functions. Right-lattice invariance is immediate from the definition.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:no-flat}
If $Q:\mathbb R\to G$ is a nonconstant map with ordinary polynomial entries,
then $F(Q(t))$ is not constant on any nondegenerate real interval.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
It is enough to consider a compact nondegenerate interval. Its image
admits only finitely many minimizing matrices $\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_s$.
For each of them,
\[
 f_a(t)=\sum_{i<j}(Q(t)\gamma_a)_{ij}^{\,2}
\]
is a nonconstant polynomial. Indeed, $Q(t)\gamma_a$ is nonconstant
because multiplication by $\gamma_a$ is invertible. If $b\geq1$ is
the maximal degree of its coordinates, then the coefficient of $t^{2b}$
in $f_a$ is the sum of the squares of their degree-$b$ coefficients
and is strictly positive.
If $F(Q(t))$ were a constant $c$, the finitely many equations
$f_a(t)=c$ would cover the interval. Each has only finitely many solutions,
which is impossible.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:interpolation}
\begin{enumerate}
\item For fixed $D,T_0$ and $\rho>0$, there is an integer $M\geq D+2$
such that every real polynomial $f$ of degree at most $D$ with
$|f(n)|\leq1/2$ for $0\leq n<M$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:initial-motion}
 |f(t)-f(0)|<\rho\qquad(0\leq t\leq T_0).
\end{equation}
\item If $\deg f\leq w$, $w\geq1$, and $|f(t)|\leq B$ on $[0,J]$,
where $J>0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-bound}
 |[t^w]f(t)|\leq b_w B J^{-w},\qquad
 b_w=\frac{2^ww^w}{w!}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
For \textup{(i)}, let
\[
 \lambda_j(u)
 =
 \prod_{\substack{0\leq m\leq D\\m\neq j}}
 \frac{u-m}{j-m},
 \qquad 0\leq j\leq D,
\]
be the Lagrange basis for the nodes \(0,\ldots,D\), and choose
\[
 B_D>\frac12\sum_{j=0}^D\sup_{0\leq u\leq1}|\lambda_j'(u)|.
\]
Choose an integer
\[
 H>\max\{1,T_0,B_DT_0/\rho\},
 \qquad M=DH+1.
\]
If \(\deg f\leq D\) and \(|f(n)|\leq1/2\) for \(0\leq n<M\), then
Lagrange interpolation applied to \(\widetilde f(u)=f(Hu)\) gives
\[
 \widetilde f(u)=\sum_{j=0}^D f(jH)\lambda_j(u),
 \qquad
 \sup_{0\leq u\leq1}|\widetilde f'(u)|\leq B_D.
\]
Hence, for \(0\leq t\leq T_0\),
\[
 |f(t)-f(0)|
 =
 \left|\widetilde f(t/H)-\widetilde f(0)\right|
 \leq \frac{B_DT_0}{H}<\rho.
\]

For \textup{(ii)}, put \(s=J/w\). By the formula for an iterated
finite difference,
\[
 \Delta_s^w f(0)
 =
 \sum_{j=0}^w(-1)^{w-j}\binom{w}{j}f(js),
\]
and therefore
\[
 |\Delta_s^w f(0)|\leq 2^wB.
\]
On the other hand, \(\Delta_s^w\) annihilates every polynomial of
degree less than \(w\), while
\[
 \Delta_s^w(t^w)=w!s^w.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \Delta_s^w f(0)=w!s^w[t^w]f(t),
\]
and hence
\[
 |[t^w]f(t)|
 \leq \frac{2^wB}{w!s^w}
 =\frac{2^ww^w}{w!}\,BJ^{-w}
 =b_wBJ^{-w}.
\]

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:matrix-obstruction}
Let $p\in\mathcal H$ and $k\geq4W+2$. If
$\{\kappa(p(j)):0\leq j<k\}$ omits a color, there are
$\gamma\in\Gamma$ and $E\in G$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leading-matrix}
 \delta_L\tau(p)\,\gamma=E,
 \qquad
 |E_{ij}|\leq\frac34b_{w_{ij}}(2L)^{w_{ij}}k^{-w_{ij}}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $e$ be a color omitted by these samples. Apply the auxiliary grid
coloring to $p(0),\ldots,p(W-1)$. The choice of $W$ gives $b\geq0$
and $q\geq1$ with
\[
 b+(M-1)q\leq W-1
\]
such that the canonical representatives of
$p(b),p(b+q),\ldots,p(b+(M-1)q)$ lie in one grid box. In particular,
$b,q\leq W$. The rounding lemma, applied to $p(b+qt)$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polynomial-lift}
 p(b+qt)C(t)=Q(t),\qquad C(\mathbb Z)\subseteq\Gamma,
\end{equation}
where $Q,C\in\mathcal H$ and $Q(0),\ldots,Q(M-1)$ are canonical.
Put
\[
 J=\left\lfloor\frac{k-1-b}{q}\right\rfloor\geq M-1.
\]
For all integers $0\leq j\leq J$, right-lattice invariance gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:omitted-lift}
 \kappa(Q(j))=\kappa(p(b+qj))\neq e.
\end{equation}

We claim that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:no-escape}
 \lvert Q(t)-Q(0)\rvert_{\infty}<\rho\qquad(0\leq t\leq J).
\end{equation}
The first $M$ samples have all coordinates bounded by $1/2$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:interpolation}(i) therefore gives this displacement bound
up to time $T_0$. If \eqref{eq:no-escape} failed, continuity would give
a first real time $T\leq J$ when the displacement equals $\rho$.
Necessarily $T>T_0$. Up to that time the coordinates of $Q$ are bounded
by $1/2+\rho=3/4$, since $Q(0)$ is canonical. The rescaled curve
$\widetilde Q(u)=Q(Tu)$ belongs to $\mathcal K$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:sampling}, with mesh parameter $T$, says that the samples
\[
 \widetilde Q(j/T)=Q(j),\qquad0\leq j\leq\lfloor T\rfloor,
\]
receive all $r$ colors.
These samples occur in \eqref{eq:omitted-lift}, a contradiction.
This proves \eqref{eq:no-escape}.

Every coordinate of $Q$ is now bounded by $3/4$ on the entire real
interval $[0,J]$. Its individual degree bound and
Lemma~\ref{lem:interpolation}(ii) give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau-Q-small}
 |\tau(Q)_{ij}|\leq\frac34b_{w_{ij}}J^{-w_{ij}}.
\end{equation}
Applying $\tau$ to \eqref{eq:polynomial-lift} yields the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:extracted-lift}
 (\delta_q\tau(p))\tau(C)=\tau(Q).
\end{equation}
The matrix $\tau(C)$ need not belong to $\Gamma$, even though $C(n)$
does for every integer $n$. We clear its factorial denominators at the
same time as we remove the auxiliary step $q$.
Apply $\delta_{L/q}$ to \eqref{eq:extracted-lift} and define
\[
 \gamma=\delta_{L/q}\tau(C),\qquad E=\delta_{L/q}\tau(Q).
\]
By \eqref{eq:fixed-L}, $L/q$ is an integer multiple of $D!$.
Writing $\tau(C)_{ij}=z_{ij}/w_{ij}!$ with $z_{ij}\in\mathbb Z$, as permitted
by \eqref{eq:rounding-factorial}, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clearance}
 \gamma_{ij}=\frac{(L/q)^{w_{ij}}}{w_{ij}!}\,z_{ij}\in\mathbb Z.
\end{equation}
Thus $\gamma\in\Gamma$ and $\delta_L\tau(p)\gamma=E$.
Moreover, \eqref{eq:tau-Q-small} yields
\[
 |E_{ij}|\leq\frac34 b_{w_{ij}}\left(\frac{L}{qJ}\right)^{w_{ij}}.
\]
Finally,
$qJ\geq k-1-b-q\geq k-1-2W\geq k/2$, which proves
\eqref{eq:leading-matrix}.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:decode}
Suppose
$B(x_1,\ldots,x_\ell)\gamma=E$, where
$\gamma\in\operatorname{UT}_{\ell+1}(\mathbb Z)$ and
$E_{i,\ell+1} \in [-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})$ for $1\leq i\leq\ell$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decode}
 \mathsf L(x_1,\ldots,x_\ell)+\gamma_{1,\ell+1}=E_{1,\ell+1},
 \qquad
 \lVert \mathsf L(x_1,\ldots,x_\ell)\rVert_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z}=|E_{1,\ell+1}|.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Put $H_\ell=x_\ell$ and $H_i=x_i\langle H_{i+1}\rangle$ downward, so
$H_1=\mathsf L(x_1,\ldots,x_\ell)$. The last-column equation in row
$\ell$ is
\[
 E_{\ell,\ell+1}=x_\ell+\gamma_{\ell,\ell+1}.
\]
Since $E_{\ell,\ell+1} \in [-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})$, we have
$\gamma_{\ell,\ell+1}=-\langle H_\ell\rangle$ uniquely. For $i<\ell$, the
band structure gives
\[
 E_{i,\ell+1}=\gamma_{i,\ell+1}-x_i\gamma_{i+1,\ell+1}.
\]
If $\gamma_{i+1,\ell+1}=-\langle H_{i+1}\rangle$, this becomes
$E_{i,\ell+1}=\gamma_{i,\ell+1}+H_i$. Smallness again identifies the
integer correction exactly: $\gamma_{i,\ell+1}=-\langle H_i\rangle$.
Induction proves the top-row identity. Since its residual lies in
$[-1/2,1/2)$, distance to the integers is its absolute value.
