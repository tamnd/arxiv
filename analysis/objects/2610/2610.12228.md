\label{thm:symto}
Given unimodular complex numbers $\rho$ and $\gamma$, complex numbers
$u$ and $c$, and nonnegative real numbers $v$ and $s$ such
that $|u|^2 + v^2 = 1$ and $|c|^2 + s^2 = 1$, define the
following, 
\begin{equation*}
\begin{array}{l}
\begin{array}{lll}
p &=& -\rho(c + \overline{\gamma}\overline{c} u), \\
n &=& \sqrt{|p|^2 + v^2},
\end{array} \\
\begin{array}{llll}
\mathrm{if}\ n \neq 0: & & &\\
& \widehat{c} &=& p/n,\\
& \widehat{s} &=& v/n,\\
& \widehat{u} &=& \overline{\rho}(s^2u - \gamma c^2),\\
& \widehat{v} &=& s n\\
\mathrm{else}: & & &\\
& \widehat{c} &=& 0,\\
& \widehat{s} &=& 1,\\
& \widehat{u} &=& \overline{\rho} u,\\
& \widehat{v} &=& 0,\\
\end{array} \\
\begin{array}{lll}
\widehat{\gamma} &=& -\overline{\rho}\gamma.
\end{array}
\end{array}
\end{equation*}
Then, the following products are equivalent,
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
-\overline{\rho} \gamma c & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\rho} s  & \\
-\rho s & -\rho\overline{\gamma} \overline{c} & \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 &  u & -v           \\
 &  v & \phantom{-{}}\overline{u}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 c & -s           & \\
 s & \phantom{-{}}\overline{c} \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix}
= 
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 & \widehat{c} & -\widehat{s}           \\
 & \widehat{s} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\widehat{c}}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 \widehat{u} & -\widehat{v}           & \\
 \widehat{v} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\widehat{u}} \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 & -\overline{\rho}\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{c} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\rho}\widehat{s}           \\
 &  -\rho \widehat{s} & -\rho\overline{\widehat{\gamma}}\overline{\widehat{c}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Define \(H\) and \(\widehat{H}\) as follows
\[
H =
\begin{bmatrix}
-\overline{\rho} \gamma c & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\rho} s  & \\
-\rho s & -\rho\overline{\gamma} \overline{c} & \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 &  u & -v           \\
 &  v & \phantom{-{}}\overline{u}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 c & -s           & \\
 s & \phantom{-{}}\overline{c} \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\overline{\rho}(s^2u-\gamma c^2) & \overline{\rho} \gamma(c+\overline{\gamma}\overline{c} u)s & -\overline{\rho} vs\\
-\rho (c+\overline{\gamma}\overline{c} u)s & \rho(s^2 - \overline{\gamma}\overline{c}^2u) & \rho v\overline{\gamma}\overline{c} \\
vs & v\overline{c} & \overline{u}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
\[ 
\widehat{H} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 & \widehat{c} & -\widehat{s}           \\
 & \widehat{s} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\widehat{c}}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 \widehat{u} & -\widehat{v}           & \\
 \widehat{v} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\widehat{u}} \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 & -\overline{\rho}\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{c} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\rho}\widehat{s}           \\
 &  -\rho \widehat{s} & -\rho\overline{\widehat{\gamma}}\overline{\widehat{c}}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\widehat{u}
&
\overline{\rho}\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{c}\widehat{v}
&
-\overline{\rho}\widehat{s}\widehat{v}
\\
\widehat{c}\widehat{v}
&
\rho\widehat{s}^2-\overline{\rho}\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{c}^2\overline{\widehat{u}}
&
\overline{\rho}\widehat{c}\widehat{s}\overline{\widehat{u}}+\rho\overline{\widehat{\gamma}}\overline{\widehat{c}}\widehat{s}
\\
\widehat{s}\widehat{v}
&
-\overline{\rho}\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{c}\widehat{s}\overline{\widehat{u}}-\rho\overline{\widehat{c}}\widehat{s}
&
\overline{\rho}\widehat{s}^2\overline{\widehat{u}}-\rho\overline{\widehat{\gamma}}\overline{\widehat{c}}^2
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
It is enough to show that \(H=\widehat H\). Using \( p=-\rho(c+\overline{\gamma}\overline{c} u)
\) and \( \widehat{\gamma}=-\overline{\rho}\gamma, \) together with \(|\rho|=|\gamma|=1\) and \(s^2
= 1 - c\overline{c}\), \(H\) becomes
\[
H= 
\begin{bmatrix}
\overline{\rho}(s^2u-\gamma c^2) & \overline{\rho} \widehat{\gamma} ps & -\overline{\rho} vs\\
ps & \rho(s^2 - \overline{\gamma}\overline{c}^2u) & -v\overline{c}\overline{\widehat{\gamma}} \\
vs & v\overline{c} & \overline{u}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
In the case \(n\neq0\), define \(\widehat{Q}\) and \(\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}\) as follows,
\[ 
\widehat{Q} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 & \widehat{c} & -\widehat{s}           \\
 & \widehat{s} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\widehat{c}}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 & -\overline{\rho}\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{c} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\rho}\widehat{s} \\
 &  -\rho \widehat{s} & -\rho\overline{\widehat{\gamma}}\overline{\widehat{c}}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The matrices \(\widehat{Q}\) and \(\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}\) are designed to create zeros in the
$(3,1)$ and $(1,3)$ entries of $H$, respectively, so that 
\[
K = \widehat{Q}^* H \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}^* = 
\begin{bmatrix}
\widehat{u} & -\widehat{v} &  \\
\widehat{v} & \phantom{-{}}* & *  \\
    & \phantom{-{}}* & * 
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Assume that $s \neq 0$. Then $\widehat{v} \neq 0$ and, since $K$ is unitary,
$K(3,2) = K(2,3) = 0$. This implies that $K(2,2) = \overline{\widehat{u}}$; since $\det(H) =
\det(K) = 1$, we also have $K(3,3) = 1$. Therefore,
\[
K = 
\begin{bmatrix}
 \widehat{u} & -\widehat{v}           & \\
 \widehat{v} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\widehat{u}} \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix},
\]
and 
\[
H = 
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 & \widehat{c} & -\widehat{s}           \\
 & \widehat{s} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\widehat{c}}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 \widehat{u} & -\widehat{v}           & \\
 \widehat{v} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\widehat{u}} \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 1    &                & \\  
 & -\overline{\rho}\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{c} & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\rho}\widehat{s}           \\
 &  -\rho \widehat{s} & -\rho\overline{\widehat{\gamma}}\overline{\widehat{c}}
\end{bmatrix}
= \widehat{H}.
\]
For $s = 0$, notice that for $n \neq 0$, the entries of $H$ and $\widehat{H}$ are
continuous in $s$ as long as the phase of $c$ is unchanged,
\[
\lim_{s \rightarrow 0} |c| = 1, \qquad \frac{c}{|c|} = \textrm{constant}.
\] 
Since $H-\widehat{H} = 0$ for $s \neq 0$, by continuity, $\lim_{s \rightarrow 0} (H-\widehat{H}) = 0$.  

In the case \(n=0\), use $s^2 = 1 - c\overline{c}$ to rewrite $H$ as,
\[
H= 
\begin{bmatrix}
\overline{\rho}(u-\widehat{\gamma} c p) & \overline{\rho} \widehat{\gamma} ps & -\overline{\rho} vs\\
ps & \rho + p\overline{c}  & -v\overline{c}\overline{\widehat{\gamma}} \\
vs & v\overline{c} & \overline{u}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Now $n = 0$ implies that \(p = v = 0\) and the condition \(|u|^2+v^2=1\)
implies \(|u|=1\), so the expression for \(H\) reduces to
\[
H=
\begin{bmatrix}
\overline{\rho} u& & \\
 &\rho& \\
 & &\overline{u}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
By construction
\[
\widehat c=0,
\qquad
\widehat s=1,
\qquad
\widehat u=\overline{\rho} u,
\qquad
\widehat v=0,
\]
which gives
\[
\widehat H
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\widehat{u} & & \\
 &\rho & \\
 & & \overline{\rho}\overline{\widehat{u}}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\overline{\rho} u& & \\
 &\rho& \\
 & &\overline{u}
\end{bmatrix}
= H.
\]
This completes the proof.

\label{ex:p-instability}
Let $\mu=2^{-53}$ denote the unit roundoff for double precision and let
$\operatorname{fl}(\cdot)$ denote rounding to nearest. Define

$$
a=\operatorname{fl}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
  =0.86602540378443860
$$

and consider the floating-point input parameters

$$
u=i,\qquad
v=0,\qquad
c=\gamma=a-\frac{1}{2}i,\qquad
s=0,\qquad
\rho=1.
$$

Since

$$
|u|^2+v^2=1
$$

and

$$
|c|^2=|\gamma|^2
=1-1.1102230246251565\times10^{-16}
=1-\mu,
$$

the input parameters are unitary to working precision.  The corresponding input
product is diagonal and satisfies

$$
H=\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
Q
=
\begin{bmatrix}
 i & 0 & 0\\
 0 & 1 & 0\\
 0 & 0 & -i
\end{bmatrix}
+O(\mu).
$$

                            
 
   
  
  
                
                         
                                          
                               
                                       
       
               
   

Now compute the symmetric turnover in floating-point arithmetic using the
formulas in Theorem~\ref{thm:symto}.  The first intermediate quantity is

$$
p=-\rho\left(c+\overline{\gamma}\overline{c} u\right).
$$

Since $\gamma=c$, floating-point evaluation gives

$$
\gamma c
=
\left(\frac12-\mu\right)-ai
\qquad
\mathrm{and}
\qquad
\overline{\gamma}\overline{c} u
=
-a+\left(\frac12-\mu\right)i.
$$

Since

$$
c=a-\frac12 i,
\qquad
p=\mu i.
$$

It follows that

$$
n=\sqrt{|p|^2+v^2}=\mu,
\qquad
\widehat{c}=\frac{p}{n}=i,
\qquad
\widehat{s}=\frac{v}{n}=0.
$$

The remaining output parameters are

$$
\widehat{u}=\overline{\rho}(s^2u-\gamma c^2)
   =-\gamma c^2
   =i+O(\mu),
\qquad
\widehat{v}=sn=0,
\qquad
\widehat{\gamma}=-\overline{\rho}\gamma=-\gamma.
$$

The corresponding computed output product satisfies

$$
\widehat{H}=\widehat{Q}\widehat{U}\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
i & 0 & 0\\
0 & \displaystyle\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i & 0\\
0 & 0 & \displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac12i
\end{bmatrix}
+O(\mu).
$$

Consequently,

$$
\widehat{H}-H
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&0\\
0&
-\displaystyle\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i
&0\\
0&0&
-\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac12i
\end{bmatrix}
+O(\mu).
$$

Both nonzero diagonal entries of the leading term have modulus one, and hence

$$
\|\widehat{H}-H\|_2=1+O(\mu).
$$

                                    
 
   
               
       
            
          
   
 
                      

Thus, even though the input parameters represent core transformations that are
unitary to working precision, evaluation of the symmetric turnover in
Theorem~\ref{thm:symto} can produce an $O(1)$ error in the resulting product.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:symto}
Given unimodular complex numbers $\rho$, $\gamma$ and $\sigma$, a complex number $u$,
and nonnegative real numbers $v$, $c$ and $s$ such that $|u|^2 + v^2 = 1$ and
$c^2 + s^2 = 1$, define the following, 
\begin{equation*}
\begin{array}{l}
\begin{array}{lll}
t &=& \overline{\gamma}\overline{\sigma}^2 u,
\end{array} \\
\begin{array}{lll}
z &=&        
\begin{cases}
1+t, & \operatorname{Re}(t) \geq 0,\\
\frac{v^2 + 2\operatorname{Im}(t)i}{1-\overline{t}}, & \operatorname{Re}(t) < 0,
\end{cases}
\end{array} \\
\begin{array}{lll}
n &=& \sqrt{c^2|z|^2 + v^2},
\end{array} \\
\begin{array}{llll}
\mathrm{if}\ n \neq 0: & & &\\
& \widehat{c} &=& c|z|/n,\\
& \widehat{\sigma} &=& -\rho \sigma z/|z|,\\
& \widehat{s} &=& v/n,\\
& \widehat{u} &=& \overline{\rho}(s^2u - \gamma\sigma^2c^2),\\
& \widehat{v} &=& s n\\
\mathrm{else}: & & &\\
& \widehat{c} &=& 0,\\
& \widehat{\sigma} &=& 1,\\
& \widehat{s} &=& 1,\\
& \widehat{u} &=& \overline{\rho} u,\\
& \widehat{v} &=& 0,\\
\end{array} \\
\begin{array}{lll}
\widehat{\gamma} &=& -\overline{\rho}\gamma.
\end{array}
\end{array}
\end{equation*}
Then, 
\[
\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q = \widehat{Q} \widehat{U} \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}.
\]
<!-- end corollary-1 -->

The principal difference with the symmetric turnover formulas in
Theorem~\ref{thm:symto} is the addition of the variables $\sigma$ and
$\widehat{\sigma}$. In the $n \neq 0$ branch, the definition of $\widehat{\sigma}$ requires that
$z \neq 0$. For unitary inputs both formulas for $z$ give the same result.
Using the $\operatorname{Re}(t) \geq 0$ branch, notice that $z = 0 \implies |t| = |u| = 1
\implies v = 0$, thus $n = 0$. Therefore, $n \neq 0 \implies z \neq 0$. When
$n=0$, $\widehat{c} = 0$ and $\widehat{\sigma}$ is not uniquely defined, so it is set equal to 1
for simplicity. The rest of the proof follows directly from
Theorem~\ref{thm:symto} and the previously discussed changes of variables.

\label{thm:nearly}
Assume that the inputs of Corollary~\ref{cor:symto} are nearly-unitary for $0
\leq \epsilon < 1$. Then, for $\epsilon$ sufficiently small, the corresponding
outputs are also nearly-unitary with respect to the same $\epsilon$ and 
\[
\left\|\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q - \widehat{Q} \widehat{U} \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}} \right\| = O(\epsilon).
\]
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Since the inputs are nearly-unitary, their unitary defects are all real and
$O(\epsilon)$,
\[
|u|^2 + v^2 = 1 + \epsilon_{uv},
\quad
c^2 + s^2 = 1 + \epsilon_{cs},
\quad
|\sigma|^2 = 1 + \epsilon_{\sigma},
\quad
|\gamma|^2 = 1 + \epsilon_{\gamma},
\quad
|\rho|^2 = 1 + \epsilon_{\rho}.
\]
For $\epsilon$ sufficiently small, the unitary defects are all strictly less
than 1 in absolute value, so we can project the nearly-unitary inputs onto
nearby inputs that represent exactly unitary core transformations as follows,
\[
\underline{u} = \frac{u}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{uv}}},
\quad
\underline{v} = \frac{v}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{uv}}},
\quad
\underline{c} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{cs}}},
\quad
\underline{s} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{cs}}},
\]
\[
\underline{\sigma} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{\sigma}}},
\quad
\underline{\smash{\gamma}} = \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{\gamma}}},
\quad
\underline{\smash{\rho}} = \frac{\rho}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{\rho}}}.
\]
The same $\epsilon$ can be taken sufficiently small such that the projected
inputs also satisfy
\[
\underline{x} = x(1+O(\epsilon)),
\quad
x = u,v,c,s,\sigma,\gamma,\rho.
\]
Now let $\underline{\smash{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}$, $\underline{U}$ and $\underline{\smash{Q}}$ be the input core transformations to the
symmetric turnover defined in terms of the projected parameters $\underline{u}$, $\underline{v}$,
$\underline{c}$, $\underline{s}$, $\underline{\sigma}$, $\underline{\smash{\gamma}}$ and $\underline{\smash{\rho}}$. Then, it follows directly that 
\[
\left\|\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q - \underline{\smash{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}\,\underline{U}\,\underline{\smash{Q}} \right\| = O(\epsilon).
\]

Since $\underline{u}$, $\underline{v}$, $\underline{c}$, $\underline{s}$, $\underline{\sigma}$, $\underline{\smash{\gamma}}$ and $\underline{\smash{\rho}}$ represent
core transformations that are exactly unitary, applying the symmetric turnover
from Corollary~\ref{cor:symto} results in outputs $\underline{\widehat{u}}$, $\underline{\widehat{v}}$, $\underline{\widehat{c}}$,
$\underline{\widehat{s}}$, $\underline{\widehat{\sigma}}$ and $\underline{\smash{\widehat{\gamma}}}$ that correspond to the exactly unitary
core transformations $\underline{\smash{\widehat{Q}}}$, $\underline{\widehat{U}}$ and $\underline{\smash{\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}}}$ which satisfy
\[
\underline{\smash{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}\,\underline{U}\,\underline{\smash{Q}} = \underline{\smash{\widehat{Q}}}\,\underline{\widehat{U}}\,\underline{\smash{\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}}}.
\]

To finish the proof we must show that the outputs of applying the symmetric
turnover to the nearly-unitary inputs are also nearly-unitary, and that the
corresponding core transformations $\widehat{Q}$, $\widehat{U}$ and $\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}$ satisfy
\[
\left\|\underline{\smash{\widehat{Q}}}\,\underline{\widehat{U}}\,\underline{\smash{\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}}} - \widehat{Q}\widehat{U}\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}} \right\| = O(\epsilon).
\]
To show this, we apply the symmetric turnover from Corollary~\ref{cor:symto} to
the nearly-unitary inputs and track how the relative errors from projecting
them to the exactly unitary inputs propagate. The first step is to compute $t$, 
\[
t = \overline{\gamma} \overline{\sigma}^2 u = \underline{\smash{\overline{\gamma}}}
\overline{\underline{\sigma}}^2 \underline{u} (1+O(\epsilon)) = \underline{t}(1+O(\epsilon)).
\] 
This says that when $\epsilon$ is sufficiently small, the relative errors
introduced from projecting the nearly-unitary inputs translate into small
relative errors in the parameter $\underline{t}$ computed using the exactly unitary
inputs. What is not shown explicitly is that this relative error is real and
positive. That is because the projections were performed using real, positive
scalars. Since $t$ and $\underline{t}$ differ by a real positive mulitple, the sign of
$\operatorname{Re}(t)$ and $\operatorname{Re}(\underline{t})$ are the same, which preserves the branch structure.
When $\operatorname{Re}(t) \geq 0$ we have that
\[
z = 1 + t = 1 + \underline{t}(1+O(\epsilon)) = (1+\underline{t})(1+O(\epsilon)) = \underline{z}(1+O(\epsilon)),
\]
where we used the fact that there is no cancellation in $1+\underline{t}$. When 
$\operatorname{Re}(t) < 0$ we have
\[
z = \frac{v^2 + 2\operatorname{Im}(t) i}{1-\overline{t}} = \frac{\underline{v}^2(1+O(\epsilon)) + 2\operatorname{Im}(\underline{t}) i(1+O(\epsilon))}{1-\overline{\underline{t}}(1+O(\epsilon))}
= \underline{z}(1+O(\epsilon)),
\]
where we used the fact that $\underline{v}^2$ and $2\operatorname{Im}(\underline{t}) i$ are orthogonal and that
there is no cancellation in $1-\overline{\underline{t}}$. Although the relative errors from projecting
the inputs were real, the relative error between $z$ and $\underline{z}$ is, in general, complex. 

The last internal parameter is $n$,
\[
n = \sqrt{c^2|z|^2 + v^2} = \sqrt{\underline{c}^2|\underline{z}|^2(1+O(\epsilon)) + \underline{v}^2(1+O(\epsilon))} = \underline{n}(1+O(\epsilon)),
\]
where we used the fact that there is no cancellation when computing the sum of
squares and that the square-root preserves small relative errors for $\epsilon$
sufficiently small. 

Even though the relative errors in $z$ are complex, the relative error between
$n$ and $\underline{n}$ is real and positive, so the branch condition on $n$ is
preserved. Applying a similar analysis to the output variables in both branches
yields the following,
\[
\widehat{u} = \underline{\widehat{u}} + O(\epsilon),
\quad
\widehat{v} = \underline{\widehat{v}}(1+O(\epsilon)),
\quad
\widehat{c} = \underline{\widehat{c}}(1+O(\epsilon)),
\quad
\widehat{s} = \underline{\widehat{s}}(1+O(\epsilon)),
\]
\[
\widehat{\sigma} = \underline{\widehat{\sigma}}(1+O(\epsilon)),
\quad
\widehat{\gamma} = \underline{\smash{\widehat{\gamma}}}(1+O(\epsilon)).
\]
Apart from $\widehat{u}$, the relative errors introduced by projecting the
nearly-unitary inputs resulted in similar relative errors between these outputs
and the exactly unitary ones. Even with the absolute error in $\widehat{u}$, this shows
that for sufficiently small $\epsilon$, the outputs of the symmetric turnover
applied to nearly-unitary inputs are also nearly-unitary. Moreover, the
corresponding core transformations satisfy 
\[
\left\|\underline{\smash{\widehat{Q}}}\,\underline{\widehat{U}}\,\underline{\smash{\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}}} - \widehat{Q}\widehat{U}\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}} \right\| = O(\epsilon).
\]
Combining this with the previous results gives
\[
\left\|\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q - \widehat{Q} \widehat{U} \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}} \right\| = O(\epsilon),
\]
which completes the proof.

number $u$, and nonnegative real numbers $vv$, $cc$ and $ss$ such that
$|u|^2 + vv = 1$ and $cc + ss = 1$, define the following, 
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.00,y=-5mm,x=5mm]
\node at (0.0,0.0) [align=left, anchor=west] {$t$};
\node at (1.0,0.0) [align=left, anchor=west] {$=$};
\node at (2.0,0.0) [align=left, anchor=west] {$\overline{\omega} u,$};

\node at (0.0,2.0) [align=left, anchor=west] {$z$};
\node at (1.0,2.0) [align=left, anchor=west] {$=$};
\draw[
  decorate,
  decoration={brace, amplitude=6pt}
] (2.5,3.5) -- (2.5,0.5);
\node at (2.5,1.0) [align=left, anchor=west] {$1+t,$};
\node at (7.0,1.0) [align=left, anchor=west] {$\operatorname{Re}(t) \geq 0,$};

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:fstab}
Assume the matrices $\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
, $U$ and $Q$ are stored in floating point and
nearly-unitary with $O(\mu)$ defects, and let $\widehat{Q}'$, $\widehat{U}'$ and $\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}'$ be
the outputs of the symmetric turnover computed via Algorithm~\ref{alg:symto}.
Then,
\[
\left\|\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q - \widehat{Q}' \widehat{U}' \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}' \right\| = O(\mu).
\]
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The first step is to compute $t$ from the inputs $u$, $\sigma$ and $\gamma$.
Since this is done via complex multiplication, it follows that
\[
t' = \operatorname{fl}(t) = \operatorname{fl}(\overline{\gamma} \overline{\sigma}^2 u) = t(1+O(\mu)).
\]
Unlike Theorem~\ref{thm:nearly}, the relative error $(1+O(\mu))$ is complex in
general. So, even though the relative errors in $t'$ are small, the relative
errors in $\operatorname{Re}(t')$ and $\operatorname{Im}(t')$ may be large. This means that $t'$ does not
necessarily preserve the branch structure for computing $z$, except in the case
$t = 0$. 

To avoid this complication, the forward error in $t'$ can be interpreted as a
backward error for the input $\sigma$. Assume $t \neq 0$ and define $\sigma''$
as
\[
\sigma'' = \sigma \sqrt{\overline{t}'/\overline{t}}.
\]
Then, $\sigma'' = \sigma(1+O(\mu))$ and $t' = \overline{\gamma} \overline{\sigma}''^2 u$. Next, define
$\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
'$ and $Q''$ using $\sigma''$ and the other unperturbed inputs,
\[
\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
' = 
\begin{bmatrix}
-\overline{\rho} \gamma c \sigma'' & \phantom{-{}}\overline{\rho} s  & \\
-\rho s & -\rho\overline{\gamma} c\overline{\sigma}'' & \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
Q'' = 
\begin{bmatrix}
 c\sigma'' & -s           & \\
 s & \phantom{-{}}c\overline{\sigma}'' \\
     &                & 1 
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
It follows immediately that 
\[ 
\| \mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q - \mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
' U Q'' \| = O(\mu).
\]
Applying Algorithm~\ref{alg:symto} to the inputs $u$, $v$, $c$, $s$,
$\sigma''$, $\gamma$ and $\rho$ in exact arithmetic gives outputs $\widehat{u}''$,
$\widehat{v}''$, $\widehat{c}''$, $\widehat{s}''$, $\widehat{\sigma}''$ and $\widehat{\gamma}''$, and corresponding
matrices $\widehat{Q}''$, $\widehat{U}''$ and $\smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}''$ that satisfy
\[
\| \mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
' U Q'' - \widehat{Q}'' \widehat{U}'' \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}''\| = O(\mu).
\]
To complete the proof it is enough to show that 
\[
\widehat{u}' = \widehat{u}'' + O(\mu),
\quad
\widehat{v}' = \widehat{v}''(1+O(\mu)),
\quad
\widehat{c}' = \widehat{c}''(1+O(\mu)),
\]
\[
\widehat{s}' = \widehat{s}''(1+O(\mu)),
\quad
\widehat{\sigma}' = \widehat{\sigma}''(1+O(\mu)),
\quad
\widehat{\gamma}' = \widehat{\gamma}''(1+O(\mu)),
\]
from which it follows that
\[
\| \widehat{Q}'' \widehat{U}'' \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}'' - \widehat{Q}' \widehat{U}' \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}'\| = O(\mu).
\]
In exact arithmetic, the first step in the turnover using $\sigma''$ is
\[
t'' = \overline{\gamma} \overline{\sigma}''^2 u = t'.
\]
So using $\sigma''$ in exact arithmetic produces the same value for $t$ as
using $\sigma$ in floating-point arithmetic. This means that the branching
condition for $z$ is maintained. For $\operatorname{Re}(t') \geq 0$,
\[
z' = \operatorname{fl}(z) = \operatorname{fl}(1+t') = (1+t')(1+O(\mu)) = (1+t'')(1+O(\mu)) = z''(1+O(\mu)).
\] 
For the branch $\operatorname{Re}(t') < 0$, 
\[
z' = \frac{\operatorname{fl}(v^2) + \operatorname{fl}(2\operatorname{Im}(t'))i}{\operatorname{fl}(1-\overline{t}')} 
= \frac{v^2(1+O(\mu)) + 2\operatorname{Im}(t')(1+O(\mu)) i}{(1-\overline{t}')(1+O(\mu))}. 
\]
Since $v^2$ and $2\operatorname{Im}(t')$ are real, $v^2$ and $2\operatorname{Im}(t')i$ are orthogonal, so
their rounding errors are real and orthogonal and there is no cancellation.
Therefore, the rounding errors in $z'$ satisfy 
\[
z' = \frac{v^2 + 2\operatorname{Im}(t')i}{1-\overline{t}'}(1+O(\mu)) = z''(1+O(\mu)). 
\]
Applying a similar analysis to $n'$, $\widehat{v}'$, $\widehat{c}'$, $\widehat{s}'$ and $\widehat{\gamma}'$
gives
\[
n' = n''(1+O(\mu)),
\quad
\widehat{v}' = \widehat{v}''(1+O(\mu)),
\quad
\widehat{c}' = \widehat{c}''(1+O(\mu)),
\]
\[
\widehat{s}' = \widehat{s}''(1+O(\mu)),
\quad
\widehat{\gamma}' = \widehat{\gamma}''(1+O(\mu)),
\]
before renormalization. For $\widehat{u}'$ and $\widehat{\sigma}'$, extra care has to be taken
to account for the difference between $\sigma$ and $\sigma''$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\widehat{u}' &= \operatorname{fl}(\overline{\rho}(s^2u - \gamma \sigma^2 c^2))\\ 
&= \overline{\rho}(s^2u - \gamma \sigma^2 c^2) + O(\mu)\\
&= \overline{\rho}(s^2u - \gamma \sigma''^2(1+O(\mu)) c^2) + O(\mu)\\
&= \widehat{u}'' + O(\mu),
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
\[
\widehat{\sigma}' = \operatorname{fl}(-\rho \sigma z'/|z'|) 
= -\rho \sigma'' z''/|z''|(1+O(\mu)) = \widehat{\sigma}''(1+O(\mu)).
\]
Thus, before renormalization, the $'$ and $''$ variables are $O(\mu)$ close.
Since they are also nearly-unitary with $O(\mu)$ defects, the errors
introduced from renormalization, including the additional rounding errors, are
relative and $O(\mu)$, and the outputs remain within $O(\mu)$ of each other.

\label{ex:nearly}
For $0 < \epsilon < 1$, define the following nearly-unitary output parameters
of the symmetric turnover,
\[
\widehat{u} = 1,                 \quad
\widehat{v} = \sqrt{2\epsilon},  \quad
\widehat{c} = \widehat{s} = 1/\sqrt{2},  \quad
\widehat{\sigma} = 1,             \quad
\widehat{\gamma} = 1,             \quad
\rho = -1.
\]
These parameters are all nearly-unitary, so the following product is also
nearly-unitary,
\[
\widehat{Q} \widehat{U} \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -\sqrt{\epsilon} & \sqrt{\epsilon}\\
\sqrt{\epsilon} & 0 & -1\\
\sqrt{\epsilon} & 1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Suppose there exist input parameters $u$, $v$, $c$, $s$, $\sigma$ and $\gamma$
such that
\[
\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q = \widehat{Q} \widehat{U} \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}.
\]
Expanding the product of the input factors gives
\[
\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\overline{\rho}(s^2u-\gamma c^2\sigma^2)
&
\overline{\rho} cs(\gamma\sigma+\overline{\sigma} u)
&
-\overline{\rho} sv
\\[1ex]
-\rho cs(\sigma+\overline{\gamma}\overline{\sigma} u)
&
\rho s^2-\rho\overline{\gamma} c^2\overline{\sigma}^2u
&
\rho\overline{\gamma} c\overline{\sigma} v
\\[1ex]
sv
&
c\overline{\sigma} v
&
\overline{u}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Comparing the $(3,3)$ entries gives $\overline{u}=0$, and hence $u=0$. Comparing
the $(2,2)$ entries then gives $\rho s^2=0$. Since $\rho=-1$, this implies
$s=0$. However, comparing the $(1,3)$ entries gives
\[
-\overline{\rho} sv=\sqrt{\epsilon}.
\]
Since $0<\epsilon<1$, the right-hand side is nonzero, contradicting $s=0$.
Therefore there exist no input parameters such that
\[
\mathpalette 
  \ooalign{ 
    \hfil$\m@th Q$\hfil\cr
    \hidewidth\raisebox{-0.35ex}{$\m@th\widetilde{\phantom{Q}}$}\hidewidth\cr
  } 
 Q = \widehat{Q} \widehat{U} \smash{\widehat{\mathpalette\tQ@{}}}.
\]
In particular, the nearly-unitary output factors cannot, in general, be
interpreted as the exact symmetric turnover of nearby nearly-unitary input
core transformations.
