<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Assume
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-range}
 n\ge3,
 \qquad
 1<q<p<\frac{n+2}{n-2}.
\end{equation}
Then, for every integer $k\ge1$, problem \eqref{eq:PDE} possesses exactly
one radial $k$-node bound state $u_k=u_k(r)$ satisfying $u_k(0)>0$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
Let $n\ge2$ and assume that
\[
\begin{cases}
1\le q<p<\infty, \qquad& n=2,\\
1\le q<p<\dfrac{n+2}{n-2}, \qquad& n\ge3.
\end{cases}
\]
Then, for every integer $k\ge1$, the equation
\[
\Delta u-\lvertu\rvert^{q-1}u+\lvertu\rvert^{p-1}u=0
\quad\text{in }\mathbb R^n
\]
possesses exactly one radial $k$-node bound state satisfying $u(0)>0$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Existence follows from the prescribed-node existence theorem of \cite{MTW1990}. For $n=2$, uniqueness follows from \cite[Theorem~1.1]{CGHY2011}. For $n\ge3$, the cases $q>1$ and $q=1$ follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:main} and \cite{Tang2026}, respectively.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:nodal-geometry}
Let $u$ be a nontrivial solution of \eqref{eq:shoot}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item If $z_1$ is the first zero of $u$, then $\alpha=u(0)>\beta$ and
$u'(r)<0$ for $0<r< z_1$.
\item Between two consecutive zeros of $u$, there is exactly one critical point.
\item If $u$ is a bound state, then after its last zero, there is exactly one critical point; beyond it $u$ is monotone and converges to zero.
\item For a bound state, $E(r)>0$ for all $r>0$.
If
$c_-<c_+$ are consecutive critical points separated by a zero, then
$\lvertu(c_-)\rvert>\lvertu(c_+)\rvert>\beta$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Since $z_1$ is simple,
\[
 F(\alpha)=E(0)>E(z_1)=\frac12u'(z_1)^2>0.
\]
Thus $\alpha>\beta$, and radial regularity gives
$u''(0)=-f(\alpha)/n<0$.  Suppose that $u'$ has a first zero $c\in(0,z_1)$.
Then $u'<0$ on $(0,c)$, $u'(c)=0$ and $u''(c)\ge0$.  On the other hand,
$F(u(c))=E(c)>E(z_1)>0$, so $u(c)>\beta$ and
$u''(c)=-f(u(c))<0$.  This contradiction proves \textup{(i)}.

Rolle's theorem gives at least one critical point between consecutive zeros.  At any critical point $c$ preceding a later zero $z$, strict energy decay gives
\[
 F(u(c))=E(c)>E(z)=\frac12u'(z)^2>0,
\]
so $\lvertu(c)\rvert>\beta$. By $u''(c)=-f(u(c))$, a critical point with $u>0$ is therefore a strict local maximum, while one with $u<0$ is a strict local minimum.  Two critical points of the same type would force a critical point of the opposite type between them, which is impossible.  This proves \textup{(ii)}.

After the last zero of a bound state, $u$ has fixed sign and tends to zero.  Since $F(u(r))\to0$ and $E$ decreases, $E(r)$ has a limit $L\ge0$.  If $L>0$, then $u'(r)^2=2(E(r)-F(u(r)))$ is bounded away from zero for all large $r$, contradicting $u(r)\to0$.  Hence $L=0$ and $u'(r)\to0$.  The derivative immediately after the last zero points into the fixed-sign half-line, so it must vanish before the solution can return to zero at infinity.  At such a critical point $E(c)>0$, hence $\lvertu(c)\rvert>\beta$, and the same critical-point argument gives uniqueness.  This proves \textup{(iii)}.

Since $E$ is strictly
decreasing and $E(r)\downarrow0$ along the tail,
we obtain $E(r)>0$ for all $r>0$.  Finally, $E(c_+)<E(c_-)$ and $F$ is
strictly increasing as a function of $\lverts\rvert$ on $\lverts\rvert>1$.  Since both
amplitudes exceed $\beta>1$, statement \textup{(iv)} follows.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:Ppositive}
Let $u$ be a bound state of \eqref{eq:shoot}.
Then $P(r)>0$ and $P_2(r)>0$ at every positive radius belonging to a finite nodal phase of $u$.
Consequently, $P/r^n$ is strictly decreasing there.  On every subinterval where $\lvertu\rvert\geq1$, one also has $P_1>0$ and hence $\omega'>0$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
At any simple zero $z$ of $u$,
\[
 P(z)=P_2(z)=z^n u'(z)^2>0.
\]
At any critical point \(c>0\) such that \(u\) has at least one zero in $(c,\infty)$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:nodal-geometry} gives $|u(c)|>\beta$, and therefore
\[
 P(c)=2c^nF(u(c))>0,
 \qquad
 P_2(c)=\frac{n-2}{n}c^n u(c)f(u(c))>0.
\]
At the origin, $P(0)=P_2(0)=0$.

For  $t>0$, both functions
$h(t)$ and $h_2(t)$ have exactly one positive zero, are negative
below that zero, and positive above it.  Consider first a prezero
half-phase, on which $|u|$ decreases strictly.  The identities
\[
 P'=r^{n-1}h(|u|),\qquad
 P_2'=\frac{r^n|u'|}{n}h_2(|u|)
\]
show that both $P$ and $P_2$ can first increase and then
decrease, but cannot have an interior minimum.
Since they are nonnegative at the preceding endpoint
and strictly positive at the subsequent zero,
both remain strictly positive throughout the interior.

On a postzero half-phase, $uu'>0$, and the energy is strictly
positive by Lemma~\ref{lem:nodal-geometry}.  Consequently,
\[
 P=2r^nE+(n-2)r^{n-1}uu'>0.
\]
Moreover, $|u|$ is now increasing and the parity of $h_2$ yields
\[
 P_2'=-\frac{r^n|u'|}{n}h_2(|u|).
\]
Its derivative has the increase--decrease sign pattern.  Since
$P_2>0$ at both endpoints, it is positive throughout.  This proves
$P,P_2>0$ on every finite phase.  The identity for $(P/r^n)'$ now
implies its strict decrease.  Finally $h_1(u)>0$ for $|u|\ge1$,
so $P_1=P+r^nh_1(u)>0$ and $\omega'>0$ wherever $|u|\ge 1$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:Ptail}
Let $u$ be a bound state of \eqref{eq:shoot} and let $c$ be its last critical point.
If $c>0$, then $P(r)>0$ for $r\ge c$.  If $c=0$, then
$P(r)>0$ for $r>0$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
After reflection if necessary, assume $u>0$, $u'<0$, and $u\to0$ on
$(c,\infty)$.  Then $E(r)\downarrow0$ and $u'(r)\to0$.
For all sufficiently large $r$, one has $0<u(r)<1$ and $f(u)<0$.  Put
\[
 y(r):=-r^{n-1}u'(r)>0.
\]
Since $y'(r)=r^{n-1}f(u(r))<0$ on the small-amplitude tail, $y$ is bounded there.  From
\[
 P(r)=2r^nE(r)-(n-2)u(r)y(r)
\]
and $E(r)>0$, we obtain $P(r)>-(n-2)u(r)y(r)$,
so $\liminf_{r\to\infty}P(r)\ge0$.  On the other hand, $h(u)<0$ for all
sufficiently small $u$.  Hence $P'<0$ eventually.  The function $P$ cannot
become nonpositive on that final interval.  Indeed, if $P(r_0)\le0$ at a
sufficiently large point, then strict decrease gives
$P(r)<P(r_1)< P(r_0)\leq 0$ for every $r>r_1>r_0$.  This contradicts the preceding
lower limit.  Thus $P(r)>0$ for all sufficiently large $r$.

Along the whole monotone tail, $h(u(r))$ changes sign at most once, from
positive to negative.                                                
Hence the same increase--decrease argument as in Lemma~\ref{lem:Ppositive} shows that \(P\) remains positive on the whole monotone tail. This proves the claim.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:inflection}
Let $u$ be a monotone prezero half-phase, normalized so that $u>1$ and $u'<0$ between its initial critical point and the point where $u=1$. Assume $P_1>0$ on this interval. Then $u''$ vanishes exactly once there.  Before that point $u''<0$, and afterward $u''>0$.  In particular, $\lvertu'\rvert$ is strictly decreasing after the inflection point.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
At a positive-radius initial critical point, $u''=-f(u)<0$.  If the initial point is the origin, radial regularity gives $u''(0)=-f(\alpha)/n<0$.  At the level $u=1$, the equation gives $u''=-(n-1)u'/r>0$.  Hence a zero of $u''$ exists.  Let $r_0$ be the first one.  Then $u'''(r_0)\ge0$.  Differentiating the equation and using $u''(r_0)=0$ gives
\[
 u'''(r_0)=u'(r_0)
 \left(\frac{n-1}{r_0^2}-f'(u(r_0))\right),
\]
so, because $u'<0$,
\[
 \frac{n-1}{r_0^2}\le f'(u(r_0)).
\]
The inequality $u'''(r_0)\ge0$ is in fact strict. To see this, suppose
that $u'''(r_0)=0$ and put $u_0=u(r_0)>1$. Then
\[
 f'(u_0)=\frac{n-1}{r_0^2},\qquad
 f(u_0)=-\frac{n-1}{r_0}u'(r_0).
\]
Since $f$ is smooth near $u_0$, we may differentiate again. Since
\[
s\varpi'(s)
=1-\varpi(s)-\frac{f(s)f''(s)}{f'(s)^2},
\]
the strict positivity of $\varpi'(u_0)$ yields
\[
\frac{f(u_0)f''(u_0)}{f'(u_0)^2}
<1-\varpi(u_0)<1.
\]
Differentiating the equation twice and using
$u''(r_0)=u'''(r_0)=0$, we obtain
\[
u^{(4)}(r_0)
=-\frac{2(n-1)}{r_0^3}u'(r_0)
-f''(u_0)u'(r_0)^2=-\frac{u'(r_0)f'(u_0)}{r_0}
\left(
2-\frac{f(u_0)f''(u_0)}{f'(u_0)^2}
\right)>0.
\]
                                             
   
                                  
                   
   
              
   
              
                              
                                                    
   
Taylor's expansion yields
\[
u''(r)=\frac12u^{(4)}(r_0)(r-r_0)^2
+o((r-r_0)^2)>0
\]
for $r<r_0$ sufficiently close to $r_0$. This contradicts the negativity of $u''$ to the left of its first zero. Hence $u'''(r_0)>0$,
                                                                    
                                                             
               
and $u''$ crosses from negative to positive at $r_0$.

The equation at $r_0$ also yields
\[
 \omega(r_0)=-\frac{r_0u'(r_0)}{u(r_0)}
 =\frac{r_0^2 f(u(r_0))}{(n-1)u(r_0)}
 \geq \varpi(u(r_0)).
\]
Now $P_1>0$ implies that $\omega$ increases with $r$, while \eqref{eq:varpi} and the decrease of $u$ imply that $\varpi(u(r))$ decreases.  Thus
\[
 \omega(r)>\omega(r_0) \geq \varpi(u(r_0)) >\varpi(u(r)),\qquad r>r_0.
\]
At any later zero of $u''$, we would have
\[
 u'''=u'f'(u)\left(\frac{\varpi(u)}{\omega}-1\right)>0.
\]
If this were the first later zero, then $u''>0$ immediately to its left and hence $u'''\le0$ there.  This contradicts the displayed strict positivity.  Thus a second zero is impossible.  The final assertion follows from $u'<0$ and $u''>0$ after $r_0$.

\begin{equation*}

If $A=\beta$, then $g_2(A)=0$; if $A>\beta$, then $0<g_2(A)<2/(p-1)<2/(q-1)$.  Hence $C_A>0$ in either case.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:firstphase}

Let $u=u(\cdot,\alpha)$ be a bound state of \eqref{eq:shoot}.
Assume that $u$ has a first zero $z_1$ and a
first postzero critical point $c_1>z_1$.
Let $r_1\in(0,z_1)$ be the point at which $u(r_1)=1$.  Then the variation
$v=\partial_\alpha u$ has exactly one zero $\tau_1\in(0,r_1)$
on $[0,c_1]$.  More precisely,
\[
 v>0\quad\text{on }[0,\tau_1),
 \qquad
 v<0\quad\text{on }(\tau_1,c_1].
\]
In addition,
\begin{equation*}
 Q(c_1),\ M(c_1)>0,
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-Q12}
Q>0\quad\text{on }(0,r_1]\cup [\bar b_1,c_1],\qquad M,T_1>0\quad\text{on } (0,r_1],\qquad Q_1, Q_2>0
\quad \text{on } (0,c_1].
\end{equation}
Moreover
\begin{equation*}
  Q(\bar b_1)>Q(b_1)>0.
\end{equation*}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
From Lemma~\ref{lem:nodal-geometry}, we obtain $\alpha>\beta$, $u(c_1)<-\beta$ and $u'<0$ on $(0,c_1)$.
This implies that
all four level points below are well defined and unique:
\begin{equation*}
 \begin{gathered}
 u(b_1)=\beta,\qquad u(r_1)=1,
 \qquad 0<b_1<r_1<z_1,\\
 u(\bar r_1)=-1,\qquad u(\bar b_1)=-\beta,
 \qquad z_1<\bar r_1<\bar b_1<c_1.
 \end{gathered}
\end{equation*}
Moreover, by $u(c_1)<-\beta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:firstphase-energy-positive}
 E(r)>E(c_1)=F(u(c_1))>0,\qquad 0\le r< c_1.
\end{equation}

We divide the argument into five steps.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 1: the first zero occurs before the level $u=1$.}
If $v$ had no zero on $(0,r_1)$, then $v>0$ on $[0,r_1)$ and, by continuity, $v(r_1)\ge0$.  Since $u>1$, \eqref{eq:high-signs} and \eqref{eq:rhoprime} give $\rho'>0$ on $(0,r_1)$, hence $\rho(r_1)>\rho(0)=0$.  But $f(1)=0$, $f'(1)=p-q>0$, and $u'(r_1)<0$, so
\[
 \rho(r_1)=r_1^{n-1}f'(1)u'(r_1)v(r_1)\le0,
\]
a contradiction.  Thus $v$ has a first zero $\tau_1\in(0,r_1)$, and $v'(\tau_1)<0$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 2: there is no second zero while $u\geq1$.}
Before $\tau_1$, $f>0$, $uf'-f>0$, and $v>0$, so $Q',M'>0$.  Moreover
\[
 (r^{n-1}v')'=-r^{n-1}f'(u)v<0
\]
and $v'(0)=0$, hence $v'<0$.  Since $Q(0)=M(0)=T_1(0)=0$ and
\[
 T_1'=-g_1'(u)u'M,
\]
we obtain $Q,M,T_1>0$ on $(0,\tau_1]$.

Suppose that either $M$ ceases to be positive or $v$ has a second zero on
$(\tau_1,r_1]$, and let $s_*$ be the first such event.  At $\tau_1$, $M(\tau_1)>0$.
If $v$ had a second zero $\sigma\le r_1$ before $M$ vanished, then
$v'(\sigma)>0$ and
\[
 M(\sigma)=-\sigma^{n-1}u(\sigma)v'(\sigma)<0.
\]
Thus $M$ would already have vanished at an earlier point.  Hence the first
event must satisfy
\[
 M(s_*)=0,\qquad v(s_*)<0.
\]
On $(\tau_1,s_*)$, one has $M>0$, and therefore
$T_1'=-g_1'(u)u'M>0$.  It follows that
\[
 Q(s_*)=T_1(s_*)>0.
\]
The equality $M(s_*)=0$ gives $u'v=uv'$, and direct substitution yields
\[
 P_1(s_*)=Q(s_*)\frac{u(s_*)}{v(s_*)}.
\]
Here $u(s_*)\ge1$ and $v(s_*)<0$, while
$P_1(s_*)>0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:Ppositive}.
The right-hand side is negative.  This contradiction excludes both events.
Consequently
\[
 M>0,\qquad v<0\quad\text{on }(\tau_1,r_1].
\]
Using $T_1'=-g_1'(u)u'M>0$ again, we obtain $T_1>0$ on $(\tau_1,r_1]$. Thus $Q=T_1+g_1M>0$ on $(\tau_1,r_1]$.

At $r_1$, $g_1(1)=0$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qr1-positive}
 Q(r_1)=T_1(r_1)>0.
\end{equation}
Since $u'v>0$ there, also $Q_1(r_1)>0$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 3: the bridge $B_0$ rules out a second zero on $(r_1,z_1]$.}
Suppose that either $Q_1$ reaches zero or $v$ has a second zero on
$(r_1,z_1]$, and let $s_*$ be the first such event.  Before $s_*$,
\[
 v<0,\qquad Q_1>0,\qquad u'v>0,
\]
and hence $Q_n>Q_1>0$.  Since $F(u)<0$ on $(r_1,z_1)$,
\eqref{eq:B0prime} gives $B_0'>0$.  Also, by \eqref{eq:Qr1-positive},
\[
 B_0(r_1)=Q(r_1)-2F(1)\frac{r_1^{n-1}v(r_1)}{u'(r_1)}>0.
\]

The first event cannot be a zero of $v$.  Indeed, at a second zero, one has
$v'(s_*)>0$, and therefore
\[
 Q_1(s_*)=Q(s_*)=s_*^n u'(s_*)v'(s_*)<0.
\]
Thus $Q_1$ would have vanished strictly before $s_*$.
Consequently, the first event would have to satisfy
\[
 Q_1(s_*)=0,\qquad v(s_*)<0.
\]
Using $Q_1(s_*)=0$ in the definition of $B_0$, we obtain
\[
 B_0(s_*)=-2s_*^{n-1}\frac{v(s_*)}{u'(s_*)}E(s_*)<0,
\]
where \eqref{eq:firstphase-energy-positive} gives $E(s_*)>0$.  This contradicts
$B_0(s_*)>B_0(r_1)>0$.  Hence neither event occurs on $(r_1,z_1]$.
Consequently, $v$ has exactly one zero on $(0,z_1)$ and $v(z_1)<0$.

The same argument also records the $Q$-family signs.  On $(0,\tau_1]$, $Q_1',Q_2'>0$.  On $(\tau_1,r_1]$, $Q>0$ and $u'v>0$, hence $Q_2>Q_1>Q>0$.  On $(r_1,z_1]$, the bridge argument gives $Q_1>0$, and $Q_2>Q_1$ because $u'v>0$.  Thus
\begin{equation*}
 Q_1,Q_2>0\quad\text{on }(0,z_1].
\end{equation*}
In particular,
at $z_1$, the bridge identity $B_0(z_1)=Q(z_1)$ and the monotonicity from $r_1$ give
\begin{equation*}
Q(z_1)=B_0(z_1)>B_0(r_1)>0.
\end{equation*}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 4: passage through the postzero low-amplitude interval.}
On $(z_1,\bar b_1)$, one has $F<0$.  Suppose that either $Q_1$ reaches
zero or $v$ has a new zero on $(z_1,\bar b_1]$, and let $s_*$ be the first
event.  Before $s_*$, $v<0$, $Q_1>0$, and $u'v>0$,
so $Q_n>Q_1>0$.  Thus \eqref{eq:B0prime} gives $B_0'>0$ on $(z_1,\bar b_1)$.

If the first event were a zero of $v$, then $v'(s_*)>0$ and
\[
 Q_1(s_*)=Q(s_*)=s_*^n u'(s_*)v'(s_*)<0.
\]
Thus $Q_1$ would have reached zero earlier.  Hence the first event would have to satisfy $Q_1(s_*)=0$ with
$v(s_*)<0$.  As in Step~3,
\[
 B_0(s_*)=-2s_*^{n-1}\frac{v(s_*)}{u'(s_*)}E(s_*)<0,
\]
whereas $B_0(s_*)> B_0(z_1)=Q(z_1)>0$.  This contradiction proves
\[
 v<0,\qquad Q_2>Q_1>0\quad\text{on }(z_1,\bar b_1].
\]
In particular, since $F(-\beta)=0$,
\[
 Q(\bar b_1)=B_0(\bar b_1)>B_0(z_1)=Q(z_1)>0.
\]

We also record the comparison between the two points at the level $\lvertu\rvert=\beta$.  If $\tau_1\leq b_1$, then $u'v>0$ and $Q_n> Q_1>0$ on $(b_1,\bar b_1)$.  Since $F<0$ there, \eqref{eq:B0prime} gives $B_0'>0$.  Hence

\begin{equation}\label{eq:add}
 \frac{2(n-1)f(u(s_0))}{u(s_0)f'(u(s_0))}>\omega(s_0).
\end{equation}
At $b$, $F(u(b))=0$ and $P(b)>0$, we obtain $\omega(b)>n-2$ from

    0<P(b)=b^nu'^2+(n-2)b^{n-1}uu'=b^{n-1}|u u'|\cdot[\omega(b)-(n-2)].

 \bigl[2(n-1)-(n-2)p\bigr]x

\end{equation}

 \bigl[2(n-1)-(n-2)p\bigr](p+1)

 -(p-q)\bigl((n-2)(p+q)-n\bigr).
\end{equation}

 (4-p)x>4-q.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:residual}
Assume \eqref{eq:ind-data} and \eqref{eq:residual-range}. Consider a normalized phase with $b<\tau$.
Then for every $A\ge\beta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual-integral}
 \int_b^\tau
 u(r)^{q+1}
 \left[1-\left(\frac{u(r)}{A}\right)^{p-q}\right]
 \frac{Q_3(r)}{r u'(r)^2}\,\mathrm d r>0.
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
On $(b,\tau)$,
\[
 1<u< \beta,\qquad v>0,\qquad u'<0,\qquad v'<0,\qquad M>0.
\]
We organize the proof in seven steps.  They are the double-power
counterparts of the residual comparison                 
\cite[Lemma~5.4]{Tang2026}.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 1: logarithmic slopes and the decreasing ratio $\mathcal R$.}
Since $P(b)>0$ and $F(\beta)=0$, we have $\omega(b)>n-2=1$.
Moreover $P_1>0$ on $u>1$, so $\omega$ is strictly increasing.  From $M>0$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aomega}
 a(r):=-\frac{rv'(r)}{v(r)}>\omega(r)>\omega(b)>1.
\end{equation}
Set
\[
 \mathcal R(r):=\frac{f(u(r))v(r)}{u'(r)v'(r)}>0.
\]
For $x=u^d$,
\[
 \frac{3f(u)}{u f'(u)}=\frac{3(x-1)}{px-q}.
\]
The right-hand side increases with $x$, and $x< \beta^d=(p+1)/(q+1)$; hence
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{3f(u)}{u f'(u)}<\frac{3}{p+q+1}<1<\omega.
\end{equation*}
A direct differentiation using the equations for $u$ and $v$ gives
\begin{align*}
 u'v'\mathcal R'
 &=f'u'v+fv'
 +\mathcal R\left(\frac4r u'v'+fv'+f'u'v\right)\\
 &<f'u'v+fv'+\frac4r fv\\
 &=\frac{fv}{r}\left(\frac{rv'}v+1\right)
 +\frac{f'uv}{r}\left(\frac{3f}{uf'}-\omega\right)<0.
\end{align*}
Since $u'v'>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rdecrease}
 \mathcal R'(r)<0 \quad \text{on }(b,\tau).
\end{equation}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 2: the sign pattern of $Q_3$.}
For $n=3$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q3prime}
 Q_3'=r^2\bigl(3u'v'-fv\bigr)
 =r^2u'v'(3-\mathcal R).
\end{equation}
If $Q_3\ge0$ on $[b,\tau]$, by \eqref{eq:Qfamily-prep},
\[
 Q_3(\tau)=Q(\tau)>0.
\]
Moreover
$u(\tau)<\beta\le A$, so all other factors in the integrand of
\eqref{eq:residual-integral} are strictly positive in a left neighborhood
of $\tau$.  Hence the integral is strictly positive.  Suppose therefore
that $Q_3$ is negative somewhere on $(b,\tau)$.  Equation \eqref{eq:Rdecrease} shows that
$3-\mathcal R$ is strictly increasing.  Thus $Q_3'$ has at most one zero.
If it has one, its sign changes from negative to positive.  The zero is
therefore the absolute minimum point $r_0$ of $Q_3$.  At this point
$\mathcal R(r_0)=3$ (equivalently, $3u'(r_0)v'(r_0)=f(u(r_0))v(r_0)$), and
\begin{align*}
 \frac{Q_3(r_0)}{r_0^2}=&r_0\bigl(u'(r_0)v'(r_0)+f(u(r_0))v(r_0)\bigr)+4u'(r_0)v(r_0)
 \\=&4r_0u'(r_0)v'(r_0)+4u'(r_0)v(r_0)
 =4u'(r_0)v(r_0)
 \left(1+\frac{r_0v'(r_0)}{v(r_0)}\right)>0
\end{align*}
by \eqref{eq:aomega}.  This value is positive.  It contradicts the
assumed negative value of $Q_3$, because $r_0$ would be its absolute
minimum.  Hence $Q_3'$ has no zero.  Since
$Q_3'(\tau)=3\tau^2u'(\tau)v'(\tau)>0$, it follows that $Q_3'>0$

 -\frac{2u'(b)v(b)}{b}\le f(\beta)v(b).

\textbf{Step 4: quantitative separation of the positive and negative $Q_3$ regions.}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:S6general}
 3\bigl(u(\sigma)-u(\tau)\bigr)>\tau\lvertu'(\tau)\rvert.

\end{equation*}
The denominator is positive.  Lemma~\ref{lem:residual-parameter} gives
$\kappa\beta^{q-1}[3(\beta-1)-\kappa]<1$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-length}
 \frac{S-T}{\beta-S}

\end{equation}

quantitatively from the pure-power residual estimate in
\cite[Lemma~5.4]{Tang2026}: the factor $\beta^{q-1}$ is exactly what is
needed to absorb the additional weight below.

 &>\bigl(3-\mathcal R(\sigma)\bigr)\sigma^2\lvertv'(\sigma)\rvert

\end{align*}

\begin{equation*}
 Q_3(r^+)>\lvertQ_3(r^-)\rvert.
\end{equation*}

Define normalized points $\widetilde r_x\in[b,\sigma]$ and $\widehat r_x\in[\sigma,\tau]$ by
\[
 u(\widetilde r_x)=S+(\beta-S)x,

$(S-T)x$.  The drop paired with $\widetilde r_x$ is $(\beta-S)x$.  Since
$S-T>\beta-S$, $\widehat r_x$ lies strictly to the right of that
equal-amplitude partner.  The strict increase of $Q_3$ and the preceding
paired estimate give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized-pair}
 Q_3(\widehat r_x)>\lvertQ_3(\widetilde r_x)\rvert,\qquad

\end{equation}
                  
            
             
\textbf{Step 6: the weighted $Q_3$ mass and its quantitative margin.}

The changes of variable $\,\mathrm d r=\,\mathrm d u/u'$ give
\begin{align*}

 \lim_{s\downarrow0}y_2(s)=\lvertu'(Z_m(\alpha_2),\alpha_2)\rvert=0,
\]
a contradiction. Hence \(S_1>0\), and therefore $\alpha_1\in\mathcal F_m\subset\mathcal P_m$. This completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-18: body_md -->
\label{lem:ground-threshold}
Assume \eqref{eq:main-range}. Then there exists a unique $\alpha_0>1$ such that
\[
\mathcal G_1=\{\alpha_0\}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\mathcal P_1=(1,\alpha_0),
\qquad
\mathcal N_1=(\alpha_0,\infty).
\]
<!-- end lemma-18 -->
<!-- proof-27: body_md -->
Existence follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:external-hyp}, while uniqueness
follows from the Serrin--Tang criterion
\cite[Theorem~1]{SerrinTang2000}, since for $s>1$,
\[
K(s):=\frac{s f'(s)}{f(s)}
=\frac{p s^{d}-q}{s^{d}-1},
\qquad
K'(s)
=-\frac{(d)^2s^{d-1}}
       {(s^{d}-1)^2}<0.
\]
Thus $\mathcal G_1=\{\alpha_0\}$.

Since \(E(r,\alpha_0)\to0\) as \(r\to\infty\) and \(E\) is strictly decreasing, we have \(E(r,\alpha_0)>0\) for every finite \(r\).
Consequently
\(F(\alpha_0)=E(0)>0\), so \(\alpha_0>\beta\). On the other hand, for the shot with
\(\alpha=\beta\), one has  $E(0,\beta)=F(\beta)=0$.                   
Since \(E\) is decreasing,
this shot cannot reach \(u=0\), and therefore
\(\beta\in\mathcal P_1\).

By \cite[Proposition~2.2]{CGHY2011}, the sets
\(\mathcal P_1\) and \(\mathcal N_1\) are open and $(1,\infty)
 =\mathcal P_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}
  \mathcal G_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}\mathcal N_1$.
Since \(\beta\in(1,\alpha_0)\cap\mathcal P_1\), connectedness yields $\mathcal P_1=(1,\alpha_0)$.
Moreover, Lemma~\ref{lem:external-hyp} provides a \(1\)-node bound state, so \(\mathcal N_1\ne\varnothing\). Connectedness then gives $\mathcal N_1=(\alpha_0,\infty)$.

<!-- lemma-19: body_md -->
\label{lem:jumping}
Assume that $\mathcal N_{j-1}=
\mathcal P_j\mathbin{\dot\cup}
\mathcal G_j\mathbin{\dot\cup}
\mathcal N_j$, $j\ge2$,
where $\mathcal P_j$ and $\mathcal N_j$ are relatively open in
$\mathcal N_{j-1}$.
If $\mathcal N_{j-1}=(\alpha_{j-2},\infty)$
and $\mathcal G_j\neq\varnothing$, then there exists a unique
$\alpha_{j-1}>\alpha_{j-2}$ such that
\[
\mathcal G_j=\{\alpha_{j-1}\},
\qquad
\mathcal P_j=(\alpha_{j-2},\alpha_{j-1}),
\qquad
\mathcal N_j=(\alpha_{j-1},\infty).
\]
<!-- end lemma-19 -->
<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
Take $\gamma\in\mathcal G_j$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:key},
$\mathcal N_j$ contains a right neighborhood of $\gamma$.
We claim that $(\gamma,\infty)\subset\mathcal N_j$.
Otherwise, let
\[
b=\sup\{s>\gamma:(\gamma,s)\subset\mathcal N_j\}<\infty.
\]
Since $\mathcal N_j$ is relatively open, $b\notin\mathcal N_j$.
Also $b\notin\mathcal P_j$, because the openness of $\mathcal P_j$
would contradict $(\gamma,b)\subset\mathcal N_j$.
Hence $b\in\mathcal G_j$. The local shooting separation property at $b$ gives $(b-\delta,b)\subset\mathcal P_j$
for some $\delta>0$, again contradicting
$(\gamma,b)\subset\mathcal N_j$. Thus
\[
(\gamma,\infty)\subset\mathcal N_j.
\]
This immediately implies that $\gamma$ is the only point of
$\mathcal G_j$: if $\eta>\gamma$ belonged to $\mathcal G_j$, then
$\eta\in\mathcal N_j$; while if $\eta<\gamma$ belonged to
$\mathcal G_j$, applying the preceding argument to $\eta$ would give
$\gamma\in\mathcal N_j$.

Therefore
\[
\mathcal G_j=\{\gamma\}.
\]
Since
\[
(\alpha_{j-2},\gamma)
=
\bigl(\mathcal P_j\cap(\alpha_{j-2},\gamma)\bigr)
\mathbin{\dot\cup}
\bigl(\mathcal N_j\cap(\alpha_{j-2},\gamma)\bigr),
\]
and both sets are relatively open, the connectedness of
$(\alpha_{j-2},\gamma)$, together with the left-hand local shooting separation property,
yields
\[
(\alpha_{j-2},\gamma)\subset\mathcal P_j.
\]
Setting $\alpha_{j-1}=\gamma$ completes the proof.

<!-- proof-29: body_md -->
Lemma~\ref{lem:external-hyp} gives $\mathcal G_{k+1}\neq\varnothing$
for every \(k\ge1\), since a profile in \(\mathcal G_{k+1}\) has
exactly \(k\) zeros.
For \(k\ge1\), Proposition~\ref{prop:tail-v} provides the terminal
transversality
\[
v(r_*)<0,\qquad B_0(r_*)>0.
\]
Here Proposition~\ref{prop:tail-v} relies on the finite-phase induction
summarized in Proposition~\ref{cor:variation-finite}, which in turn
follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:firstphase},
Corollary~\ref{addcor2}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:phase}.
Consequently, Proposition~\ref{prop:key} yields the local shooting separation property
at every point of \(\mathcal G_j\), \(j\ge2\).

Lemma~\ref{lem:ground-threshold} gives the initial level \(j=1\).
Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:jumping} inductively, we conclude that $\mathcal G_{k+1}$
consists of exactly one shooting height for every \(k\ge1\).
Since the radial initial-value problem is uniquely determined by the
shooting height \(\alpha=u(0)>0\), there is at most one radial bound
state with exactly \(k\) nodes and positive value at the origin.
Together with the existence supplied by
Lemma~\ref{lem:external-hyp}, this proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\label{cor:shooting-structure}
Let $\alpha_k=u_k(0)$ for $k\ge1$, and let
$\alpha_0$ denote the ground-state shooting height.
Then
\[
\beta<\alpha_0<\alpha_1<\alpha_2<\cdots.

in $(0,\infty)$.

Lemmas~\ref{lem:ground-threshold}
and~\ref{lem:jumping}.
The exact zero count follows from
