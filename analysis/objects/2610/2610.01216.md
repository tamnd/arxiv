<!-- theorema-1: body_md -->
Let $n\ge3$. There is a neighbourhood of the regular $n$-simplex in the space of $n$-simplices up to similarity in which the regular simplex is the only simplex with regular Morley simplex. The derivative of the Morley map at the regular simplex has exactly two eigenvalues,
\[
\begin{gathered}
\lambda_1=-\frac{(n-2)(q_n-1)}{3n(1+nq_n)},\; \lambda_2=\frac{q_n-1}{3(q_n+1)},\\
q_n=2\cos\Bigl(\frac13\arccos\frac1n\Bigr).
\end{gathered}
\]
<!-- end theorema-1 -->
<!-- theoremb-1: body_md -->
Suppose that $6\le n\le200$, or that $n=\binom k2-1$ for some $k\ge9$. Then there is an $n$-simplex whose Morley simplex is regular and which has no hyperplane reflection symmetry. In particular $(G_n)$ is false for these $n$.
<!-- end theoremb-1 -->
<!-- theoremc-1: body_md -->
The classes $U_1$ and $U_2$ are given exactly by a root of an irreducible polynomial of degree $18$, respectively $8$, together with square roots. The Galois groups of the two polynomials are $S_{18}$ and $S_8$, and neither simplex can be expressed by radicals.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{sx:inertia}
The matrix $K$ is negative definite on $\mathbf 1^\perp$, its inertia is $(1,n)$, and $\operatorname{sign}\det K=(-1)^n$. In particular $K$ is invertible and $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is nondegenerate.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For $-1\le h\le1$ put $f(h)=2\cos(\frac13\arccos(-h))$. Then $f(1)=1$ and $q_{ij}=f(H_{ij})$ for all $i,j$. We claim that
\[
f(h)=\sqrt3-\sum_{m\ge1}c_mh^m,\; c_m>0,\; c_1=\frac13,
\]
with absolute convergence on $[-1,1]$. Put $y=\sqrt3-f(h)$. The equation $\varphi(f(h))=h$ becomes
\[
h=3y\Bigl(1-\frac{y}{\sqrt3}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{y}{2\sqrt3}\Bigr),
\]
and Lagrange inversion gives
\[
c_m=\frac{1}{m3^m}[u^{m-1}]
\Bigl(1-\frac{u}{\sqrt3}\Bigr)^{-m}
\Bigl(1-\frac{u}{2\sqrt3}\Bigr)^{-m}>0.
\]
The branch is analytic for $|h|<1$, because the only finite branch values of the cubic are $h=\pm1$. Letting $h\uparrow1$ gives $\sum_mc_m=\sqrt3-1$, so the series converges absolutely on $[-1,1]$.

Hence $K=\sqrt3\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T-\sum_{m\ge1}c_mH^{\circ m}$, where $H^{\circ m}$ is the $m$-th Hadamard power. It is the Gram matrix of the tensors $n_i^{\otimes m}$, hence positive semidefinite. For $0\ne z\perp\mathbf 1$,
\[
z^TKz=-\sum_{m\ge1}c_mz^TH^{\circ m}z\le-\frac13z^THz<0,
\]
because $\ker H=\mathbb{R} S$ with $S>0$, and $z$ is not a multiple of $S$. Since $\mathbf 1^TK\mathbf 1>0$, the inertia is $(1,n)$. The matrix of barycentric coordinates of $M_1,\dots,M_N$ is $\operatorname{diag}(t_i)K\operatorname{diag}(S_j)/(nV)$, which is invertible.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{sx:gram}
Let $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_N$ be the barycentric coordinates with respect to $\mathcal M(\Delta)$, let $T=\operatorname{diag}(t_i)$ and $B=K^{-1}HK^{-1}$. Then the Gram matrix of the gradients $\nabla\mu_i$ is $T^{-1}BT^{-1}$. Consequently, the Gram matrix of the inward unit normals of $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is
\begin{equation}\label{sx:morleymap}
H_\mathcal M=\Bigl(\frac{B_{ij}}{\sqrt{B_{ii}B_{jj}}}\Bigr)_{i,j}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Every affine function $g$ satisfies $g=\sum_ig(M_i)\mu_i$. Applied to $\ell_j$ and combined with \eqref{sx:distances}, this gives $\ell_j=\sum_it_iq_{ij}\mu_i$, and taking gradients, $n_j=\sum_it_iq_{ij}\nabla\mu_i$. Thus the matrix with rows $n_j$ equals $KT$ times the matrix with rows $\nabla\mu_i$. Taking Gram matrices, $H=KT\,G\,TK$, where $G$ is the Gram matrix of the $\nabla\mu_i$, and $G=T^{-1}BT^{-1}$ follows from Lemma~\ref{sx:inertia}. The inward unit normal of the facet of $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ opposite $M_i$ is $\nabla\mu_i/|\nabla\mu_i|$; since $T$ is diagonal with positive entries, normalizing $G$ gives \eqref{sx:morleymap}.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{sx:regularity}
Let $\Delta$ be an $n$-simplex and $B=K^{-1}HK^{-1}$. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is regular;
\item $B_{ij}=-\frac1n\sqrt{B_{ii}B_{jj}}$ for all $i\ne j$;
\item there is a vector $d=(d_i)$ with positive entries such that, with $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_i)$,
\begin{equation}\label{sx:regularityeq}
 H=4K\Bigl(D^2-\frac1Ndd^T\Bigr)K.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
If $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is regular with edge length $L$, then \eqref{sx:regularityeq} holds with $d_i=t_i/(\sqrt2L)$. Conversely, let $K$ be a real symmetric $N\times N$ matrix with unit diagonal and $1<q_{ij}<2$ for $i\ne j$, such that $H=\varphi[K]$ satisfies \eqref{sx:kernel} for some $S$. Then $H$ is the normal Gram matrix of an $n$-simplex $\Delta$, unique up to similarity, whose dihedral angles satisfy $q_{ij}=2\cos(\theta_{ij}/3)$. If moreover \eqref{sx:regularityeq} holds for some $d$ with positive entries, then $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is regular.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By Proposition~\ref{sx:gram}, (a) and (b) are equivalent. Put $P=I-\frac1N\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$. If (b) holds, let $e_i=\sqrt{B_{ii}}$ and $E=\operatorname{diag}(e_i)$; then $B=\frac Nn EPE$, and \eqref{sx:regularityeq} follows with $d=\frac12\sqrt{N/n}\,e$, because $DPD=D^2-dd^T/N$. If (c) holds, then $B=4DPD$, so $B_{ii}=4nd_i^2/N$ and $B_{ij}=-4d_id_j/N$, which is (b).

Let $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ be regular with edge $L$. The barycentric coordinates of a regular simplex with edge $L$ and centroid $0$ are $\mu_i(X)=\frac1N+\frac2{L^2}m_i\cdot X$, where $m_i$ are the vertices; indeed $m_i\cdot m_j=\frac{L^2}2(\delta_{ij}-\frac1N)$. So the Gram matrix of the gradients is $\frac2{L^2}P$, and Proposition~\ref{sx:gram} gives $B=\frac2{L^2}TPT=4DPD$ with $d_i=t_i/(\sqrt2L)$.

For the converse, let $n_1,\dots,n_N$ be unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with Gram matrix $H$. They span $\mathbb{R}^n$ and satisfy $\sum_iS_in_i=0$ with $S>0$, so the set $\{X:n_i\cdot X\ge-1\text{ for all }i\}$ is a nondegenerate simplex with inward unit normals $n_i$. Its shape is unique, since translations and a common dilation account for all choices of the $N$ support numbers. Its dihedral angles satisfy $\cos\theta_{ij}=-H_{ij}=-\varphi(q_{ij})=\cos\bigl(3\arccos(q_{ij}/2)\bigr)$, and $3\arccos(q_{ij}/2)\in(0,\pi)$ because $1<q_{ij}<2$. Hence $q_{ij}=2\cos(\theta_{ij}/3)$, and $K$ is the matrix of $\Delta$. By (c)$\Rightarrow$(a), $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is regular.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{sx:reflectiontest}
A hyperplane reflection that maps $\Delta$ to itself interchanges exactly one pair of vertices and fixes the others. The transposition of $A_i$ and $A_j$ is induced by a reflection of $\Delta$ if and only if $|A_iA_k|=|A_jA_k|$ for every $k\notin\{i,j\}$, and this happens if and only if $K$ is invariant under the transposition. More generally, a permutation of the vertices is induced by an isometry of $\Delta$ if and only if it preserves $K$.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
A reflection $\rho$ of $\Delta$ permutes the vertices and is not the identity, so it moves some vertex. If it interchanged two disjoint pairs $A_i,A_j$ and $A_k,A_l$, the vectors $A_i-A_j$ and $A_k-A_l$ would both lie in the $(-1)$-eigenspace of the linear part of $\rho$, which has dimension one; they are linearly independent because the vertices are affinely independent. Hence $\rho$ induces a transposition.

The perpendicular bisector of $A_iA_j$ contains every other vertex exactly under the stated condition on edge lengths, and then the reflection in it induces the transposition. A permutation induced by an isometry preserves dihedral angles and hence $K$. Conversely, a permutation preserving $K$ preserves $H=\varphi[K]$, so it is induced by a similarity of $\Delta$ onto itself (Theorem~\ref{sx:regularity}); a similarity that permutes a finite set spanning $\mathbb{R}^n$ affinely fixes its centroid and preserves the sum of the squared distances to it, so it is an isometry.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{sx:defects}
If $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is regular with edge $L$, then for $i\ne j$
\begin{equation}\label{sx:defecteq}
 \varepsilon_{ij}=q_{ij}+2-\frac{(t_i+t_j)^2}{L^2}\ge0,
\end{equation}
and equality holds exactly when $\Delta$ has the reflection that interchanges $A_i$ and $A_j$. Moreover
\[
 \frac{(t_i+t_j)^2}{L^2(q_{ij}+2)}+\frac{(t_i-t_j)^2}{L^2(2-q_{ij})}\le1.
\]
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $v=M_j-M_i$ and $q=q_{ij}$. By \eqref{sx:distances},
\[
 (n_i-n_j)\cdot v=(q-1)(t_i+t_j),\; (n_i+n_j)\cdot v=(q+1)(t_j-t_i).
\]
The vectors $n_i-n_j$ and $n_i+n_j$ are orthogonal, with squared lengths $2-2\varphi(q)=(q-1)^2(q+2)$ and $2+2\varphi(q)=(2-q)(q+1)^2$. The sum of the squares of the projections of $v$ on these two directions is at most $|v|^2=L^2$, which is the second inequality; the first follows from it. If $\varepsilon_{ij}=0$, then $t_i=t_j$ and $v$ is parallel to $n_i-n_j$. Every other vertex $M_k$ of the regular simplex $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $M_iM_j$, which is now the hyperplane $\ell_i=\ell_j$. By \eqref{sx:distances}, $t_kq_{ki}=t_kq_{kj}$, so $q_{ik}=q_{jk}$ for all $k\notin\{i,j\}$, and Proposition~\ref{sx:reflectiontest} applies. Conversely, if the reflection exists, it fixes $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ and interchanges $M_i$ and $M_j$; then $t_i=t_j$, $v$ is orthogonal to the mirror and so parallel to $n_i-n_j$, and equality holds.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{sx:reflectiondistance}
Suppose that $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is regular with edge length $L$. If $\Delta$ has the reflection that interchanges $A_i$ and $A_j$, then
\[
t_i=t_j=L\cos\frac{\theta_{ij}}6,\;\text{equivalently}\; d_i^2=d_j^2=\frac{q_{ij}+2}8.
\]
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Now $\varepsilon_{ij}=0$ and $t_i=t_j$, so $4t_i^2=L^2(q_{ij}+2)=4L^2\cos^2(\theta_{ij}/6)$.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{sx:v4}
Let $n\ge3$. Suppose that $A_1A_2A_3A_4$ is an isosceles tetrahedron and that every other vertex of $\Delta$ is equidistant from $A_1,\dots,A_4$. If $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is regular, then $\Delta$ has two reflections that interchange disjoint pairs of vertices among $A_1,\dots,A_4$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The three double transpositions of $A_1,\dots,A_4$ preserve all edge lengths and fix the other vertices. Hence
\[
 q_{12}=q_{34}=x,\; q_{13}=q_{24}=y,\; q_{14}=q_{23}=z,\; d_1=d_2=d_3=d_4=\delta>0,
\]
and $q_{1j}=q_{2j}=q_{3j}=q_{4j}$ for $j>4$. The vector $(1,1,-1,-1,0,\dots,0)$ and the two vectors obtained from it by permuting the first four coordinates are common eigenvectors of $K$ and $H$, and they are orthogonal to $d$. Put $\Sigma=1+x+y+z$ and $\Lambda=1+\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)+\varphi(z)$. The eigenvalues of $K$ on these vectors are $2u+2-\Sigma$, and those of $H$ are $2+3u-u^3-\Lambda$, for $u=x,y,z$. Equation \eqref{sx:regularityeq} gives
\[
 u^3-3u-2+\Lambda+4\delta^2(2u+2-\Sigma)^2=0,\; u\in\{x,y,z\}.
\]
If $x,y,z$ were distinct, they would be the three roots of this cubic in $u$, and the coefficient of $u^2$ would give $x+y+z=-16\delta^2<0$, which is impossible. So two of them coincide, say $x=y$. Then $K$ is invariant under the transpositions $(1\,4)$ and $(2\,3)$, and Proposition~\ref{sx:reflectiontest} completes the proof.
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{lin:main}
Let $n\ge2$. Near $H_0$, the set $\Sigma_n$ is a smooth hypersurface in the affine space of symmetric matrices with unit diagonal, with tangent space $W_1\oplus W_2$ at $H_0$. The map \eqref{lin:map} is smooth near $H_0$, maps $\Sigma_n$ into itself, and its derivative at $H_0$ acts on $W_1$ and $W_2$ as multiplication by
\begin{equation}\label{lin:eigen}
\lambda_1=-\frac{(n-2)(q_n-1)}{3n(1+nq_n)},\;\lambda_2=\frac{q_n-1}{3(q_n+1)},
\end{equation}
respectively. Consequently, for $n\ge3$ the regular simplex has a neighbourhood in $\Sigma_n$ that contains no other simplex with regular Morley simplex.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
For $H$ near $H_0$, the smallest eigenvalue $\mu(H)$ of $H$ is simple, with eigenvector close to $\mathbf 1$, and the other eigenvalues are close to $N/n$. So near $H_0$ the set $\Sigma_n$ is the zero set of the smooth function $\mu$, and the kernel vector is automatically positive. The derivative of $\mu$ at $H_0$ is $Z\mapsto\mathbf 1^TZ\mathbf 1/N$, which is not zero, so $\Sigma_n$ is a smooth hypersurface with tangent space $\{Z:\mathbf 1^TZ\mathbf 1=0\}$. This space is $W_1\oplus W_2$: given $Z$ with zero diagonal and $\mathbf 1^TZ\mathbf 1=0$, put $x=Z\mathbf 1/(N-2)$; then $\sum_ix_i=0$, and $X=x\mathbf 1^T+\mathbf 1 x^T-2\operatorname{diag}(x)$ satisfies $X\mathbf 1=(N-2)x=Z\mathbf 1$, so $Z-X\in W_2$. The sum is direct because $X\mathbf 1=0$ forces $x=0$. The map \eqref{lin:map} is smooth near $H_0$, since $f$ is smooth on $(-1,1)$, $K_0=f[H_0]$ is invertible and $B_{ii}>0$; it maps $\Sigma_n$ to $\Sigma_n$ because $\mathcal M(\Delta)$ is a simplex.

Write $q=q_n$, $P=I-\frac1N\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$ and $\Pi=\frac1N\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$. Then $H_0=\frac NnP$ and $K_0=(1-q)P+\lambda\Pi$ with $\lambda=1+nq$, so
\[
K_0^{-1}=\frac{P}{1-q}+\frac{\Pi}{\lambda}=\alpha I+\beta\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T,\;
\alpha=\frac1{1-q},\;\beta=\frac1N\Bigl(\frac1\lambda-\alpha\Bigr),
\]
and $B_0=K_0^{-1}H_0K_0^{-1}=cP$ with $c=N/(n(1-q)^2)$. The diagonal entries of $B_0$ are $1/(1-q)^2$ and the off-diagonal ones are $-1/(n(1-q)^2)$. Let $Z$ be tangent and put $\eta=f'(-1/n)=1/\varphi'(q)=2/(3(1-q^2))$. The derivative of $K$ is $\eta Z$, so
\begin{equation}\label{lin:dB}
\delta B=K_0^{-1}ZK_0^{-1}-\eta\bigl(K_0^{-1}ZB_0+B_0ZK_0^{-1}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and differentiating \eqref{lin:map} at $B_0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{lin:dM}
\delta\mathcal M_{ij}=(1-q)^2\Bigl(\delta B_{ij}+\frac{\delta B_{ii}+\delta B_{jj}}{2n}\Bigr)\;(i\ne j).
\end{equation}
Since $\varphi(q)=-1/n$ and $1-\varphi(q)=\frac12(q-1)^2(q+2)$, we have $(q-1)^2(q+2)=2N/n$, and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{lin:etac}
(1-q)\eta c=\frac{2N}{3n(1+q)(1-q)^2}=\frac{q+2}{3(q+1)}.
\end{equation}

Let $Z\in W_2$. Then $PZ=ZP=Z$ and $\Pi Z=0$, so $K_0^{-1}Z=\alpha Z$ and $B_0Z=cZ$. By \eqref{lin:dB}, $\delta B=(\alpha^2-2\eta c\alpha)Z$, which has zero diagonal, and by \eqref{lin:dM} and \eqref{lin:etac}
\[
\delta\mathcal M=\bigl(1-2(1-q)\eta c\bigr)Z=\Bigl(1-\frac{2(q+2)}{3(q+1)}\Bigr)Z=\lambda_2Z.
\]

Let $Z=X=U-2D_x\in W_1$, where $U=x\mathbf 1^T+\mathbf 1 x^T$, $D_x=\operatorname{diag}(x)$ and $\sum_ix_i=0$. Using $\mathbf 1^Tx=0$, $X\mathbf 1=(N-2)x$ and $K_0^{-1}x=\alpha x$, $K_0^{-1}\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1/\lambda$, one finds
\[
K_0^{-1}UK_0^{-1}=\frac\alpha\lambda U,\; K_0^{-1}D_xK_0^{-1}=\alpha^2D_x+\alpha\beta U,
\]
\[
K_0^{-1}XB_0+B_0XK_0^{-1}=c\Bigl(2\alpha X+(N-2)\Bigl(\beta-\frac\alpha N\Bigr)U\Bigr).
\]
Substituting in \eqref{lin:dB} gives $\delta B=\gamma U-2\alpha(\alpha-2\eta c)D_x$ with
\[
\gamma=\frac{(N-2)(\alpha-\eta c)}{N\lambda}+\frac{2\alpha(\alpha-2\eta c)}{N}.
\]
Hence $\delta B_{ij}=\gamma(x_i+x_j)$ for $i\ne j$ and $\delta B_{ii}=(2\gamma-2\alpha(\alpha-2\eta c))x_i$, and \eqref{lin:dM} gives $\delta\mathcal M=\lambda_1'X$ with
\[
\lambda_1'=\frac{(1-q)^2}{n}\Bigl(\frac{(N-2)(\alpha-\eta c)}{\lambda}+\alpha(\alpha-2\eta c)\Bigr).
\]
By the computation for $W_2$, $(1-q)^2\alpha(\alpha-2\eta c)=\lambda_2$, and by \eqref{lin:etac}, $(1-q)^2(\alpha-\eta c)=(1-q)(2q+1)/(3(q+1))$. Therefore
\[
\lambda_1'=\frac{q-1}{3n(q+1)}\Bigl(1-\frac{(N-2)(2q+1)}{1+(N-1)q}\Bigr)=-\frac{(N-3)(q-1)}{3n(1+nq)}=\lambda_1.
\]

For $n\ge3$ both $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are nonzero, so the derivative of $\mathcal M$ restricted to $\Sigma_n$ is invertible at $H_0$. <!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{ld:galois}
The polynomials $P_8$ and $P_{18}$ are irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ with Galois groups $S_8$ and $S_{18}$. Consequently $p$ and $b$ cannot be expressed by radicals, and no simplex similar to $U_1$ or $U_2$ has vertex coordinates expressible by radicals.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
By Dedekind's theorem, modulo a prime that divides neither the leading coefficient nor the discriminant, the degrees of the irreducible factors give the cycle type of an element of the Galois group $G$. Modulo $17$, $P_8$ is irreducible; modulo $41$ it factors with degrees $7,1$; modulo $7$ with degrees $3,3,2$. So $P_8$ is irreducible and $G$ contains an $8$-cycle. A $7$-cycle fixing a point makes $G$ doubly transitive, hence primitive, and the cube of an element of type $(3,3,2)$ is a transposition. A primitive group containing a transposition is symmetric, so $G=S_8$.

Modulo $193$, $P_{18}$ is irreducible; modulo $229$ it factors with degrees $17,1$; modulo $7$ with degrees $9,5,3,1$. As before $G$ is primitive and contains an $18$-cycle. The ninth power of an element of type $(9,5,3,1)$ is a $5$-cycle. By Jordan's theorem a primitive group of degree $m$ that contains a cycle of prime length at most $m-3$ contains the alternating group; since an $18$-cycle is odd, $G=S_{18}$.

Neither group is solvable. If a simplex similar to $U_2$ had coordinates in radicals, then so would its unit normals and the numbers $\cos\theta_{ij}=-\varphi(q_{ij})$; each $q_{ij}$ is a root of $x^3-3x-2\cos\theta_{ij}$, so $p$ would be expressible by radicals. The same argument applies to $b$ and $U_1$.

\end{equation}
The permutation $\sigma=(B_1\,B'_1)(B_2\,B'_2)(B_3\,B'_3)$ acts on the unknowns, and the system is invariant under it.

The script \texttt{check\_dim6\_dim7.py} starts from approximations to $20$ decimals, improves them by Newton's method, rounds the result to a point $x_0\in\mathbb Q^{28}$ with $60$ decimals, and replaces $x_0$ by the average of $x_0$ and its image under $\sigma$, so that $x_0$ is invariant under $\sigma$. Exact rational arithmetic, together with outward rounded interval arithmetic for an enclosure $DF(\mathcal I)$ of the Jacobian on $\mathcal I$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{hd:bounds}
 \kappa:=\|I-C\,DF(x_0)\|_\infty+\|C\|_\infty\max_i\sum_jw\bigl(DF(\mathcal I)_{ij}\bigr)<4\cdot10^{-13},\;

\end{equation}
where $w$ is the width of an interval. Let $g(x)=x-CF(x)$. For $x,y\in\mathcal I$ we have $g(x)-g(y)=(I-C\bar J)(x-y)$, where $\bar J$ is the mean of $DF$ over the segment from $y$ to $x$; its entries lie in the corresponding intervals of $DF(\mathcal I)$, as do those of $DF(x_0)$. \[

\]
so $\pi$ does not preserve $K(x_*)$. The isometry group is therefore $\{\mathrm{id},\sigma\}$. As $\sigma$ interchanges three pairs of vertices, it is not a reflection.

\label{dh:lemma}

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{jn:reduction}
Such data give a simplex with regular Morley simplex provided $1<p,r<2$ and
\begin{align}
 h_0&=1+2(k-2)\varphi(p)+\binom{k-2}{2}\varphi(r)=0,\label{jn:h0}\\
 h_1&=1+(k-4)\varphi(p)-(k-3)\varphi(r)>0,\notag\\
 h_2&=1-2\varphi(p)+\varphi(r)>0,\notag\\
 h_1\kappa_2^2-h_2\kappa_1^2&=0,\; \kappa_1\kappa_2\ne0,\label{jn:equal}
\end{align}
where
\[
 \kappa_1=1+(k-4)p-(k-3)r,\; \kappa_2=1-2p+r.
\]
If $p\ne r$, the simplex has no hyperplane reflection symmetry.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of intersecting pairs and $T$ the $k\times N$ incidence matrix of points and pairs. Then $T^TT=2I+A$ and $TT^T=(k-2)I+\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$, so $A$ has the eigenvalues $2(k-2)$, $k-4$, $-2$, with multiplicities $1$, $k-1$, $k(k-3)/2$. The matrix of disjoint pairs is $J-I-A$, where $J=\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$. Hence the eigenvalues of $H$ are $h_0,h_1,h_2$, and those of $K$ on the nonconstant eigenspaces are $\kappa_1,\kappa_2$. The conditions give $\ker H=\mathbb{R}\mathbf 1$ and a common positive value $h_1/\kappa_1^2=h_2/\kappa_2^2=4\tau^2$, so \eqref{sx:regularityeq} holds with $d=\tau\mathbf 1$, and Theorem~\ref{sx:regularity} gives the simplex.

For two distinct labels $X,Y$ there is a third label $Z$ that meets one of them but not the other. If $X,Y$ share an element, take one of the unshared elements together with an element outside $X\cup Y$; if they are disjoint, take an element of $X$ and an element outside $X\cup Y$. So when $p\ne r$ no transposition of two labels preserves $K$, and Proposition~\ref{sx:reflectiontest} excludes every reflection.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->

For all sufficiently large $k$ there is a Johnson solution with
\[
 p=p_*+\frac{c}{k}+O(k^{-2}),\; r=\sqrt3+\frac{2}{3k}+O(k^{-2}),

where
\[
 c=\frac{2+(1-2p_*+\sqrt3)^2/[2(p_*-\sqrt3)^2]}{3p_*^2-3}\approx0.836089.

 kh_2\longrightarrow\frac{(1-2p_*+\sqrt3)^2}{2(p_*-\sqrt3)^2}\approx1.379.
\]
