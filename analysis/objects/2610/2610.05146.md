<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:radial}
For every integer $m\ge0$ and integer $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation*}
 \lambda_{m,n}\le\frac{\pi^2n^2}{h^2}
             +\frac{m^2+3/4}{r^2}.
\end{equation*}
For $n=0$ the term $3/\left(4r^2\right)$ can be omitted.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For every nonzero trial function,
\[
 \frac{\int_r^R m^2s^{-1}\left|u\right|^2\,\mathrm d s}
 {\int_r^R s\left|u\right|^2\,\mathrm d s}\le\frac{m^2}{r^2}.
\]
Adding this bound on the same trial subspace and applying the
min--max principle gives
$\lambda_{m,n}\le\lambda_{0,n}+m^2/r^2$.
Here $\lambda_{0,0}=0$. If $\lambda>0$ and
$-u''-s^{-1}u'=\lambda u$ with $u'\left(r\right)=u'\left(R\right)=0$, then $w=u'$
satisfies
\[
 -w''-s^{-1}w'+s^{-2}w=\lambda w,
 \,\,\, w\left(r\right)=w\left(R\right)=0.
\]
Conversely, for a nonzero solution of this Dirichlet problem, set
$u=-\left(w'+s^{-1}w\right)/\lambda$. The differential equation gives $u'=w$,
so $u$ solves the original Neumann problem. The maps are inverse.
Thus, including multiplicities, the positive radial Neumann spectrum
equals the Dirichlet spectrum in angular order one. The unitary
change $v=\sqrt{s}\,w$ turns the latter into
\[
 -v''+\frac{3}{4s^2}v=\lambda v,\,\,\, v\left(r\right)=v\left(R\right)=0.
\]
Comparison of its potential with $3/\left(4r^2\right)$ yields
$\lambda_{0,n}\le\left(\pi n/h\right)^2+3/\left(4r^2\right)$ for $n\ge1$.
The bound for $n=0$ follows separately from $\lambda_{0,0}=0$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:sum}
Let $\left\lfloor a\right\rfloor$ denote the greatest integer not exceeding $a$.
For every $a>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sum}
 S\left(a\right):=\sum_{n=0}^{\left\lfloor a\right\rfloor}
       \sqrt{1-\left(n/a\right)^2}
 \ge\frac\pi4 a+1-\frac\pi4.
\end{equation}
The constant $1-\pi/4$ is optimal in this inequality valid for all $a$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Write $f\left(x\right)=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ on $\left[0,1\right]$ and
$I=\int_0^1f\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x=\pi/4$. For an integer $N\ge2$, let $U_1,\ldots,U_{N-1}$ be independent
uniform random variables on $\left[0,1\right]$, and let
$U_{\left(1\right)}\le\cdots\le U_{\left(N-1\right)}$ denote their
increasing rearrangement (the order statistics). For $1\le j\le N-1$, the density of
$U_{\left(j\right)}$ is
\[
 p_j\left(x\right)=
 \frac{\left(N-1\right)!}{\left(j-1\right)!\left(N-1-j\right)!}
 x^{j-1}\left(1-x\right)^{N-1-j},\qquad 0<x<1.
\]
Indeed, for small $\varepsilon>0$, one sample point falls in
$\left[x,x+\varepsilon\right]$, while exactly $j-1$ of the others
lie below $x$. Choosing these points gives the displayed coefficient
after dividing the probability by $\varepsilon$ and letting
$\varepsilon\to0+$. Events with two points in the short interval
have probability $O\left(\varepsilon^2\right)$ and do not affect the limit.
The elementary integral identity
\[
 \int_0^1x^p\left(1-x\right)^q\,\,\mathrm d x
 =\frac{p!q!}{\left(p+q+1\right)!},
 \qquad p,q\in\left\{0,1,\ldots\right\},
\]
obtained by repeated integration by parts, consequently gives
\[
 \mathbb E U_{\left(j\right)}
 =\int_0^1x p_j\left(x\right)\,\,\mathrm d x=\frac jN.
\]
Here $\mathbb E$ denotes expectation.

For the concave function $f\left(x\right)=\sqrt{1-x^2}$, the usual
concavity inequality gives
\[
 \mathbb E f\left(U_{\left(j\right)}\right)\le f\left(\mathbb E U_{\left(j\right)}\right)=f\left(j/N\right).
\]
In this case the inequality follows directly from the supporting
tangent at $z=j/N$: for every $u\in\left[0,1\right]$,
\[
 f\left(u\right)\le f\left(z\right)+f'\left(z\right)\left(u-z\right).
\]
Taking expectations cancels the last term because
$\mathbb E U_{\left(j\right)}=z$. Sorting leaves the sum of the values
unchanged for every outcome, so
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \sum_{j=1}^{N-1}f\left(j/N\right)
 &\ge\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\mathbb E f\left(U_{\left(j\right)}\right)
   =\mathbb E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}f\left(U_i\right)\right)\\
 &=\left(N-1\right)\int_0^1f\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x=\left(N-1\right)I.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Adding $f\left(0\right)=1$ and $f\left(1\right)=0$ gives
$S\left(N\right)-NI\ge1-I$. The case $N=1$ is equality.

For $a\in\left[N,N+1\right]$, with $N\ge1$, the function $S\left(a\right)-Ia$ is
continuous and concave on that closed interval. Indeed, for
$j\ge1$ and $a>j$,
\[
 \frac{\,\mathrm d^2}{\,\mathrm d a^2}\sqrt{1-\frac{j^2}{a^2}}
 =-\frac{j^2\left(3a^2-2j^2\right)}{a^3\left(a^2-j^2\right)^{3/2}}<0,
\]
and the term appearing at the right endpoint has value zero there.
A concave function is no smaller than the minimum of its endpoint
values. The integer estimate therefore proves \eqref{eq:sum}.
If $0<a\le1$, then $S\left(a\right)=1$ and the result is immediate.
Equality at $a=1$ proves optimality of the constant.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:largesum}
For the sum in Lemma~\ref{lem:sum},
\[
 S\left(a\right)\ge\frac\pi4a+\frac{29}{100},\,\,\, a\ge2.
\]
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For an integer $N\ge2$, put $f_N\left(x\right)=\sqrt{1-\left(x/N\right)^2}$.
The composite trapezoidal rule is the sum of the areas of the
endpoint trapezoids on the $N$ unit intervals:
\[
 T_N:=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f_N\left(j\right)+f_N\left(j+1\right)}2
     =\frac12f_N\left(0\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}f_N\left(j\right)+\frac12f_N\left(N\right)
     =S\left(N\right)-\frac12.
\]
Here $f_N\left(0\right)=1$ and $f_N\left(N\right)=0$. Since $f_N$ is concave, each
endpoint chord lies below its graph. Therefore
\[
 E_N:=\int_0^N f_N\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x-T_N\ge0,\qquad
 S\left(N\right)-\frac{\pi N}{4}=\frac12-E_N.
\]
To estimate the error on a unit interval, define
\[
 K_j\left(x\right)=\frac{\left(x-j\right)\left(j+1-x\right)}2
       =\frac18-\frac12\left(x-j-\frac12\right)^2,
       \qquad j\le x\le j+1.
\]
The completed-square expression shows directly that
$0\le K_j\left(x\right)\le1/8$, with its maximum at $x=j+1/2$.
Thus $K_j$ vanishes at the two endpoints,
$K_j'\left(j\right)=1/2$, $K_j'\left(j+1\right)=-1/2$, and $K_j''=-1$.
For a continuously differentiable function $F$ whose derivative
is absolutely continuous on $\left[j,j+1\right]$, two integrations
by parts give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unittrapezoid}
 \begin{aligned}
 \int_j^{j+1}K_j\left(x\right)F''\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x
 &=\left[K_jF'\right]_j^{j+1}-\left[K_j'F\right]_j^{j+1}
       +\int_j^{j+1}K_j''\left(x\right)F\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x\\
 &=\frac{F\left(j\right)+F\left(j+1\right)}2
       -\int_j^{j+1}F\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For $j=0,\ldots,N-2$, the function $f_N$ is smooth on this interval.
Consequently the concave trapezoidal error on this interval is
\[
 e_j:=\int_j^{j+1}f_N\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x
           -\frac{f_N\left(j\right)+f_N\left(j+1\right)}2
      =-\int_j^{j+1}K_j\left(x\right)f_N''\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x.
\]
Since $0\le K_j\le1/8$ and $f_N''\le0$, summing the first
$N-1$ intervals gives
\[
 0\le\sum_{j=0}^{N-2}e_j
 \le-\frac18\int_0^{N-1}f_N''\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x
 =-\frac{f_N'\left(N-1\right)}8
 =\frac{N-1}{8N\sqrt{2N-1}}.
\]
Indeed $f_N'\left(x\right)=-x/\left(N\sqrt{N^2-x^2}\right)$ and
$f_N''\left(x\right)=-N/\left(N^2-x^2\right)^{3/2}$ for $x<N$.
Since $f_N'\left(x\right)\to-\infty$ as $x\uparrow N$, applying
the derivative-increment bound through the last endpoint would
give no finite estimate. We estimate the last interval directly. With $y=N-x$,
\[
 f_N\left(N-y\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2y}{N}-\frac{y^2}{N^2}}
           \le\sqrt{\frac{2y}{N}},\qquad 0\le y\le1.
\]
As $f_N\left(N-1\right)=\sqrt{2N-1}/N$, this gives
\[
 e_{N-1}
 =\int_0^1f_N\left(N-y\right)\,\mathrm d y-\frac{f_N\left(N-1\right)}2
 \le\frac{2\sqrt2}{3\sqrt N}-\frac{\sqrt{2N-1}}{2N}.
\]
Adding the two bounds proves
\[
 E_N\le\frac{N-1}{8N\sqrt{2N-1}}
          +\frac{2\sqrt2}{3\sqrt N}
          -\frac{\sqrt{2N-1}}{2N}.
\]
For $N\ge2$, use $\left(N-1\right)/N\le1$,
$\sqrt{2N-1}\ge\sqrt N$, and
$\sqrt{2N-1}/N\ge\sqrt{3/2}/\sqrt N$ to obtain
\[
 E_N\le\frac{1/8+2\sqrt2/3-\sqrt{3/2}/2}{\sqrt N}.
\]
The rational comparisons $\sqrt2<99/70$ and
$\sqrt{3/2}>61/50$ bound the numerator by
$641/1400<23/50$. For $N\ge5$, the bound
$\sqrt N\ge\sqrt5>11/5$ therefore gives
\[
 E_N<\frac{23}{110}<\frac{21}{100},\qquad
 S\left(N\right)-\frac{\pi N}{4}>\frac{16}{55}>\frac{29}{100}.
\]

It remains to check $N=2,3,4$. Their sums are
\[
 S\left(2\right)=1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\qquad
 S\left(3\right)=1+\frac{2\sqrt2+\sqrt5}{3},\qquad
 S\left(4\right)=1+\frac{\sqrt{15}+2\sqrt3+\sqrt7}{4}.
\]
Using $\pi<22/7$, $\sqrt3>173/100$, $\sqrt2>7/5$,
$\sqrt5>11/5$, $\sqrt{15}>19/5$ and $\sqrt7>13/5$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 S\left(2\right)-\frac\pi2
 &>1+\frac{173}{200}-\frac{11}{7}=\frac{411}{1400},\\
 S\left(3\right)-\frac{3\pi}{4}
 &>1+\frac{2\left(7/5\right)+11/5}{3}-\frac{33}{14}=\frac{13}{42},\\
 S\left(4\right)-\pi
 &>1+\frac{19/5+2\left(173/100\right)+13/5}{4}-\frac{22}{7}
   =\frac{451}{1400}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Each rational lower bound exceeds $29/100$. Finally, the concavity
of $S\left(a\right)-\pi a/4$ on every $\left[N,N+1\right]$, established in
Lemma~\ref{lem:sum}, extends these integer bounds to every $a\ge2$.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
A radially constant trial function in the integer angular order $m$
has Rayleigh quotient

and hence $\int_0^1g\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x=1/8$. Scaling the two terms in

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:exactphasecount}
Let $n_m\left(t^2\right)$ be the radial Neumann counting function in integer angular
order $m\ge0$, including its zero eigenvalue when $m=0$. For every $t>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exactphasecount}
 n_m\left(t^2\right)=\left\lfloor
 \frac{\varphi_m\left(Rt\right)-\varphi_m\left(t\right)}\pi\right\rfloor+1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Write $d_m\left(t\right)=\varphi_m\left(Rt\right)-\varphi_m\left(t\right)$ and consider
\[
 u_t\left(s\right)=Y_m'\left(t\right)J_m\left(ts\right)-J_m'\left(t\right)Y_m\left(ts\right).
\]
The Bessel Wronskian identity
$J_m\left(x\right)Y_m'\left(x\right)-J_m'\left(x\right)Y_m\left(x\right)=2/\left(\pi x\right)$
\cite[(10.5.2)]{NIST} gives $u_t\left(1\right)=2/\left(\pi t\right)>0$ and
$\partial_su_t\left(1\right)=0$, while
\[
 \partial_su_t\left(R\right)=-t\mathcal M_m\left(t\right)\mathcal M_m\left(Rt\right)\sin d_m\left(t\right).
\]
The right Neumann condition is therefore equivalent to
$\sin d_m\left(t\right)=0$, or $d_m\left(t\right)=j\pi$ for an integer $j$.
Geometrically, the derivative vectors at $t$ and $Rt$ are then
parallel or antiparallel. Their angle must be followed on the
continuous branch in Figure~\ref{fig:derivativephase}; retaining
the number of turns is what turns this boundary condition into
a spectral count. Thus positive radial eigenvalues occur exactly
at the integer levels of $d_m/\pi$. The single minimum property of $\varphi_m$ implies
$d_m\left(t\right)>-\pi/2$. For $m>0$, $d_m\left(t\right)<0$ at small positive $t$;
for $m=0$, $d_0\left(t\right)>0$ and tends to zero as $t\downarrow0$.

To turn this zero condition into an exact count, it remains to
check the direction of each crossing. Every crossing of an
integer level is positive. To see this,
suppose $d_m\left(t\right)=j\pi$. The Wronskian identity gives
\[
 u_t\left(R\right)=\left(-1\right)^j\frac{\mathcal M_m\left(t\right)}{\mathcal M_m\left(Rt\right)}
                       \frac2{\pi Rt}.
\]
Normalize $u_t\left(1\right)=1$ by a positive scalar and write
$z=\partial_\lambda u_t$, where $\lambda=t^2$. The radial
equation is $-\left(su_t'\right)'+m^2s^{-1}u_t=\lambda su_t$.
Multiply its differentiated equation by $u_t$, subtract the
original equation multiplied by $z$, and integrate. Because
$z\left(1\right)=z'\left(1\right)=u_t'\left(R\right)=0$ at an eigenvalue, the boundary terms give
\[
 -Ru_t\left(R\right)\,\partial_\lambda\partial_su_t\left(R\right)
       =\int_1^R s\,u_t\left(s\right)^2\,\mathrm d s>0.
\]
At the root the derivatives of the prefactor multiplying
$\sin d_m$ vanish from the expression, because $\sin d_m=0$.
Since $\partial_\lambda d_m=d_m'\left(t\right)/\left(2t\right)$, differentiating the
right derivative gives a positive prefactor times
$-\left(-1\right)^j d_m'\left(t\right)$. The value $u_t\left(R\right)$ has sign $\left(-1\right)^j$,
so the positive left-hand side of the preceding identity has
the sign of $d_m'\left(t\right)$. Therefore $d_m'\left(t\right)>0$.
Positive radial eigenvalues are simple for this regular
one-dimensional boundary problem \cite{Davies}.
Starting with $-1/2<d_m\left(t\right)/\pi<0$ for $m>0$, or with
$0<d_0\left(t\right)/\pi\ll1$ for $m=0$, each encountered integer level
therefore adds exactly one eigenvalue. Continuity proves
\eqref{eq:exactphasecount}, including equality
at an eigenvalue. The extra initial count for $m=0$ is precisely its
zero eigenvalue. No global monotonicity of $d_m$ is needed.

\label{lem:bvsample}
Let $f\in C^1\left(\left[0,T\right]\right)$ have absolutely continuous derivative, with
$f'\left(0\right)=0$ and $f''\le-\kappa<0$ almost everywhere. For every

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
The continuous periodic function
\[
 \mathcal P\left(y\right)=\frac12\left(\left\{y\right\}-1/2\right)^2-\frac1{16},
 \,\,\, \left|\mathcal P\left(y\right)\right|\le\frac1{16},
\]
is a primitive of $\psi$ almost everywhere. For $0<s<T$, integrate
by parts with $\mathcal P\left(f+b\right)/f'$. Since $f'<0$ and $1/f'$ is
increasing, its endpoint and variation contributions give
\[
 \left|\int_s^T\psi\left(f\left(x\right)+b\right)\,\mathrm d x\right|
 \le\frac1{8\left|f'\left(s\right)\right|}\le\frac1{8\kappa s}.
\]
Indeed, writing $H=1/f'$, the bound is
$\left(\left|H\left(s\right)\right|+\left|H\left(T\right)\right|+\operatorname{Var}_{\left[s,T\right]}H\right)/16
=\left|H\left(s\right)\right|/8$, because $H<0$ is increasing. The initial integral
has absolute value at most $s/2$, because $\left|\psi\right|\le1/2$.
Thus, whenever $0<s<T$,
\[
 \left|\int_0^T\psi\left(f\left(x\right)+b\right)\,\mathrm d x\right|
 \le\frac{s}{2}+\frac{1}{8\kappa s}.
\]
The right-hand side is minimized at $s=1/\left(2\sqrt\kappa\right)$,
where its two terms are both $1/\left(4\sqrt\kappa\right)$. Their sum
is $1/\left(2\sqrt\kappa\right)$. If this choice is at least $T$, the
direct bound $T/2\le1/\left(4\sqrt\kappa\right)$ suffices instead. An integrable
singularity of $f''$ at $T$ is handled by taking a limit from below.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:combinedgrid}
Let $v$ be a left-continuous function of bounded variation on $\left[0,T\right]$,
with its actual value at zero retained. For the multiset
$\mathcal V=\left\{\left|m\right|:m\in\mathbb Z\right\}$,
\[
 \left|\sum_{x\in\mathcal V\cap\left[0,T\right]}v\left(x\right)
                 -2\int_0^T v\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x\right|
 \le\operatorname{Var}_{\left[0,T\right]}v+\left|v\left(T\right)\right|.
\]
Consequently, if $q$ satisfies the regularity and curvature
assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:bvsample} with curvature $\kappa$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:combinedsaw}
 \left|\sum_{x\in\mathcal V\cap\left[0,T\right]}\psi\left(q\left(x\right)\right)\right|
 \le\kappa^{-1/2}+2\left(q\left(0\right)-q\left(T\right)\right)+\frac32.
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The integer-grid discrepancy
\[
 \mathcal D\left(x\right)=\#\left\{m\in\mathbb Z:\left|m\right|\le x\right\}-2x
              =2\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor+1-2x,\qquad x\ge0.
\]
There are $\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor$ positive orders, the same number of
negative orders, and one zero order. Hence
$\mathcal D\left(x\right)=1-2\left\{x\right\}$, which lies in $\left(-1,1\right]$ and has
absolute value at most one. Let $\sigma$ be the signed
Lebesgue--Stieltjes measure encoding the increments of the
left-continuous $v$: $\sigma\left(\left[x,y\right)\right)=v\left(y\right)-v\left(x\right)$ for $0\le x<y\le T$.
This includes a possible right jump at zero. Indeed
$v\left(x\right)=v\left(T\right)-\sigma\left(\left[x,T\right)\right)$. Integrate this identity against the
signed measure consisting of the angular point masses minus
$2\,\mathrm d x$, and exchange the finite measure integrals. The inner
measure of $\left[0,y\right]$ is exactly $\mathcal D\left(y\right)$, giving the exact error
\[
 v\left(T\right)\mathcal D\left(T\right)-\int_{\left[0,T\right)}\mathcal D\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d\sigma\left(x\right).
\]
This proves the first assertion with the stated endpoint convention.
We justify the variation identity with the endpoint convention
used in the preceding Stieltjes calculation. For $T=0$ the
identity is immediate, so suppose $T>0$. The assumptions on $q$
imply $q'\left(x\right)\le-\kappa x<0$ for $x>0$, hence $q$ is strictly
decreasing. It is also continuously differentiable and therefore
absolutely continuous on $\left[0,T\right]$. Put $\Delta=q\left(0\right)-q\left(T\right)$ and
define the finite crossing set
\[
 \mathcal C=\left\{x\in\left[0,T\right):q\left(x\right)\in\mathbb Z\right\}.
\]
Each integer in $\left(q\left(T\right),q\left(0\right)\right]$ has exactly one preimage in
$\mathcal C$, by continuity and strict decrease. Thus
\[
 J=\#\mathcal C
  =\left\lfloor q\left(0\right)\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor q\left(T\right)\right\rfloor.
\]
With $h\left(x\right)=\psi\left(q\left(x\right)\right)=q\left(x\right)-\left\lfloor q\left(x\right)\right\rfloor-1/2$, an
integer crossing has value $h\left(x\right)=-1/2$ and right limit
$h\left(x+\right)=1/2$. Its left limit agrees with its value. Between
crossings, $h'=q'$. More explicitly, for $0\le x<y\le T$,
\[
 h\left(y\right)-h\left(x\right)
  =\int_x^y q'\left(s\right)\,\,\mathrm d s
      +\#\left(\mathcal C\cap\left[x,y\right)\right).
\]
An integer at the initial endpoint is included in this formula,
whereas an integer at $y$ has not yet produced its right jump.
The signed Stieltjes measure associated with these increments
is consequently
\[
 \,\mathrm d\sigma_h=q'\left(x\right)\,\,\mathrm d x+\sum_{c\in\mathcal C}\delta_c
 \quad\hbox{on }\left[0,T\right),
\]
where $\delta_c$ denotes the unit point mass at $c$. Its absolutely
continuous part is nonpositive and its atomic part is positive;
the two parts are mutually singular. Its total variation is
therefore the sum of their masses:
\[
 \operatorname{Var}_{\left[0,T\right]}h
 =-\int_0^T q'\left(x\right)\,\,\mathrm d x+J
 =q\left(0\right)-q\left(T\right)+J=\Delta+J.
\]
A crossing at zero contributes one right jump; a crossing at $T$
produces no jump outside the interval. Finally,
\[
 J=\Delta+\left\{q\left(T\right)\right\}-\left\{q\left(0\right)\right\}<\Delta+1,
 \qquad \left|h\left(T\right)\right|\le\frac12,
\]
so the non-strict estimate $\operatorname{Var}_{\left[0,T\right]}h\le2\Delta+1$
used in the lemma is valid.

The first assertion of the lemma and the continuous integral
estimate now give the complete chain
\[
 \left|\sum_{x\in\mathcal V\cap\left[0,T\right]}h\left(x\right)\right|
 \le2\left|\int_0^T h\left(x\right)\,\mathrm d x\right|
       +\operatorname{Var}_{\left[0,T\right]}h+\left|h\left(T\right)\right|
 \le\kappa^{-1/2}+2\Delta+\frac32.
\]
This is \eqref{eq:combinedsaw}. The density two accounts for the
factor before the integral. The zero order is counted once and
each positive integer order twice, as encoded in $\mathcal D$.

\label{lem:outerfloors}
Use the disk action $G_U$ defined in \eqref{eq:diskaction}. Suppose $U\ge2$, $0\le t<U$, and a unit
grid has first point $a>t$ and $n$ points in $\left[a,U\right]$. Then
\[
 \sum_{\substack{j\ge0\\a+j\le U}}
       \left\lfloor G_U\left(a+j\right)+3/4\right\rfloor
 \ge\sum_{\substack{j\ge0\\a+j\le U}}G_U\left(a+j\right)
       +\frac n4-\frac12G_U\left(t\right)-\frac38U^{1/3}-\frac5{16}.
\]
The assertion also holds for an empty grid.

The quarter-height inverse is taken on $\left[0,U\right]$, where $G_U$ is
continuous and strictly decreasing. It exists because
$G_U\left(0\right)=U/\pi>1/4$ for $U\ge2$, whereas $G_U\left(U\right)=0$. Write
$x_*=G_U^{-1}\left(1/4\right)$ and $w_0=U-x_*$. The level $1/4$ is
dictated by the shift $3/4$: a height $z\ge0$ contributes
zero to $\left\lfloor z+3/4\right\rfloor$ precisely when $z<1/4$.
It is unrelated to the relative-width restriction in the thin
annulus theorem. The lower tail estimate
$w_0>1$ is the established bound of
\cite[Lemma~4.8]{FLPSballs}; we spell out its calculation to verify
the hypothesis needed for the floor-sum theorem.
Set
\[
 \theta_U=\arccos\left(1-\frac1U\right),\qquad
 H\left(U\right)=G_U\left(U-1\right)
     =\frac{\sqrt{2U-1}-\left(U-1\right)\theta_U}{\pi}.
\]
Differentiation gives
\[
 H'\left(U\right)=\frac{\sin\theta_U-\theta_U}{\pi}<0,
 \qquad \sin\theta_U=\frac{\sqrt{2U-1}}U.
\]
The last inequality follows from $\sin\theta<\theta$ for
$\theta>0$. Furthermore,
\[
 H\left(2\right)=\frac{\sqrt3}{\pi}-\frac13<\frac14;
\]
for example, $\pi>3$ and $\sqrt3<7/4$ suffice for this comparison.
Thus $G_U\left(U-1\right)<1/4=G_U\left(x_*\right)$. Strict decrease gives
$x_*<U-1$, or $w_0>1$.

The upper tail bound follows independently from the action
integral and $\arccos\left(1-z\right)\ge\sqrt{2z}$ for $0\le z\le1$:
\[
 \frac14=G_U\left(U-w_0\right)
 \ge\frac1\pi\int_{U-w_0}^{U}
       \sqrt{\frac{2\left(U-s\right)}U}\,\,\mathrm d s
 =\frac{2\sqrt2}{3\pi\sqrt U}w_0^{3/2}.
\]
Solving for $w_0$ gives
\[
 1<w_0\le\left(\frac{9\pi^2}{128}\right)^{1/3}U^{1/3}
       <U^{1/3}.
\]
The last coefficient is less than one, since
$\pi<22/7$ implies $9\pi^2<128$. The threshold $U\ge2$
is sufficient for these estimates; no optimal threshold is claimed.

If the grid is empty, then $n=0$ and both sums vanish. The
remaining terms on the right-hand side of the lemma are
nonpositive, so the assertion holds. If the grid is nonempty
but $a=U$, then $b=U-a=0$, $n=1$, and the only summand in each
sum is zero. The right-hand side is
\[
 -\frac12G_U\left(t\right)-\frac38U^{1/3}-\frac1{16}<0,
\]
which settles this degenerate interval without using a theorem
whose hypothesis requires $b>0$.

We may therefore suppose $a<U$, so that $b=U-a>0$. The profile
$g\left(y\right)=G_U\left(a+y\right)$ on $\left[0,b\right]$ satisfies
\[
 g'\left(y\right)=-\frac1\pi\arccos\left(\frac{a+y}{U}\right),\qquad
 g''\left(y\right)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{U^2-\left(a+y\right)^2}}>0
 \quad\left(0\le y<b\right).
\]
It is nonnegative, strictly decreasing, convex, vanishes at $b$,
and has Lipschitz constant at most $1/2$, since $a>t\ge0$.
If $g\left(0\right)\ge1/4$, its quarter-height abscissa is
\[
 g^{-1}\left(1/4\right)=x_*-a=b-w_0\ge0.
\]
The integer index in \cite[Theorem~6.1]{FLPSballs} is therefore
\[
 M_0=1+\left\lfloor b-w_0\right\rfloor.
\]
Indeed $g\left(j\right)\ge1/4$ is equivalent to $j\le b-w_0$, including
equality at the quarter height. The bounds for the floor function
and $w_0>1$ give
\[
 b-w_0<M_0\le b-w_0+1<b.
\]
Thus $M_0\le b$, as required by the cited theorem, including the
case $g\left(0\right)=1/4$, where $b=w_0>1$ and $M_0=1$. That theorem gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor b\right\rfloor}\left\lfloor g\left(j\right)+\frac34\right\rfloor
 &\ge\int_0^b g\left(y\right)\,\,\mathrm d y-\frac{b-3M_0}{8}\\
 &=\int_0^b g\left(y\right)\,\,\mathrm d y+\frac b4-\frac38\left(b-M_0\right)\\
 &\ge\int_0^b g\left(y\right)\,\,\mathrm d y+\frac b4-\frac{3w_0}{8}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
If instead $g\left(0\right)<1/4$, then $b<w_0$. Convexity puts the graph
below the chord from $\left(0,g\left(0\right)\right)$ to $\left(b,0\right)$, and hence
\[
 \int_0^b g\left(y\right)\,\,\mathrm d y\le\frac{b\,g\left(0\right)}2<\frac b8.
\]
The proposed lower bound is consequently less than
$3\left(b-w_0\right)/8<0$, while every floor summand is nonnegative.
It therefore holds in this case as well.

We next compare the integral with the action sum. The derivative
formulas above give $g\left(b\right)=g'\left(b\right)=0$ and $-g'\left(0\right)\le1/2$.
Put $M=\left\lceil b\right\rceil$ and extend $g$ by zero from $b$ to $M$.
Its derivative remains continuous at $b$; the displayed square-root
singularity of $g''$ is integrable, so $g'$ remains absolutely
continuous and there is no additional jump term at $b$.

We use the unit-interval error identity derived in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:largesum}, now with a convex profile. The
trapezoidal error has the opposite sign: on $\left[j,j+1\right]$,
\[
 E_j:=\frac{g\left(j\right)+g\left(j+1\right)}2-\int_j^{j+1}g\left(y\right)\,\mathrm d y
 =\frac12\int_j^{j+1}\left(y-j\right)\left(j+1-y\right)g''\left(y\right)\,\mathrm d y.
\]
The endpoint chord lies above the convex profile. By
\eqref{eq:unittrapezoid}, its kernel is the same $K_j$, bounded by
$1/8$. Figure~\ref{fig:trapezoid}
shows the chord error and this kernel. Summing the interval errors gives
\[
 0\le\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}E_j
 \le\frac18\int_0^M g''\left(y\right)\,\mathrm d y
 =\frac{g'\left(M\right)-g'\left(0\right)}8=-\frac{g'\left(0\right)}8\le\frac1{16}.
\]
The trapezoidal sum weights the first endpoint by one half,
and every endpoint at or beyond $b$ has value zero. Hence, whether
$b$ is an integer or not,
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{g\left(j\right)+g\left(j+1\right)}2
 =\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor b\right\rfloor}g\left(j\right)-\frac12g\left(0\right),
 \qquad \int_0^M g\left(y\right)\,\mathrm d y=\int_0^b g\left(y\right)\,\mathrm d y.
\]
Combining the last two displays yields
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor b\right\rfloor}g\left(j\right)
 \le\int_0^b g\left(y\right)\,\mathrm d y+\frac12g\left(0\right)+\frac1{16}.
\]
Thus $g\left(0\right)/2$ is the endpoint half-weight correction, and $1/16$
is the kernel bound $1/8$ times the total derivative increase,

Here $G_U$ is the disk action in Section~2.4, and

radius $\rho$. The enlarged form domain is
$H^1\left(B_\rho\right)\oplus H^1\left(A_\rho\right)$, and the inner disk count at
energy $X^2$ is $\mathcal N_B\left(\rho X\right)$ by scaling.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diskcutN}
 \mathcal N_B\left(X\right)\le N_\rho\left(X^2\right)+\mathcal N_B\left(\rho X\right),\,\,\,
 N_\rho\left(X^2\right)\ge \mathcal N_B\left(X\right)-\mathcal N_B\left(\rho X\right).
\end{equation}
Let $n_m^{\mathrm N}\left(Y\right)$ and
$n_m^{\mathrm D}\left(Y\right)$ be the radial Neumann and Dirichlet
counts at energy $Y^2$ in angular order $m$. For $m\ge1$, the
interlacing $j'_{m,k}<j_{m,k}<j'_{m,k+1}$ implies
$n_m^{\mathrm N}\le n_m^{\mathrm D}+1$
\cite[Section~10.21(i)]{NIST}. Indeed, if $n$ derivative zeros
are at most $Y$, then, for $n\ge2$, the $n-1$ ordinary zeros
preceding the second through $n$th derivative zeros are also at most
$Y$. For $n\le1$ the inequality is immediate. The same argument
applies to $m=0$ with the convention $j'_{0,1}=0$, so its extra
mode includes the Neumann zero eigenvalue.

Neumann mode. There are therefore at most $\left\lfloor Y\right\rfloor$
positive orders to retain. Order zero has angular multiplicity one,
and each positive order has multiplicity two. Summing the radial
inequalities gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal N_B\left(Y\right)
 &=n_0^{\mathrm N}\left(Y\right)
   +2\sum_{m=1}^{\left\lfloor Y\right\rfloor}n_m^{\mathrm N}\left(Y\right)\\
 &\le n_0^{\mathrm D}\left(Y\right)
   +2\sum_{m=1}^{\left\lfloor Y\right\rfloor}n_m^{\mathrm D}\left(Y\right)
   +1+2\left\lfloor Y\right\rfloor\\
 &\le\mathcal N_B^{\mathrm D}\left(Y\right)+2\left\lfloor Y\right\rfloor+1.
 \end{aligned}

\label{lem:angularfirstmonotone}
Normalize the outer radius to one. For an integer $m\ge1$, let
$\beta_m\left(\rho\right)$ be the lowest eigenvalue of

 -u''-s^{-1}u'+m^2s^{-2}u=\beta u,
 \,\,\, \rho<s<1,\,\,\, u'\left(\rho\right)=u'\left(1\right)=0.
\]
Then $\beta_m$ is strictly decreasing on $\left(0,1\right)$. In particular,
\[
 \beta_1\left(\rho\right)<\frac{112}{33},\quad
 \beta_2\left(\rho\right)<\frac{75}{8},\quad
 \beta_3\left(\rho\right)<\frac{552}{31},\quad
 \beta_4\left(\rho\right)<\frac{1120}{39}.
\]

<!-- proof-24: body_md -->
Fix $m\ge1$. The variational characterization of the lowest
eigenvalue for this regular separated boundary problem is
\cite{Davies}
\[
 \beta_m\left(\rho\right)
 =\min_{0\ne v\in H^1\left(\rho,1\right)}
 \frac{\displaystyle\int_\rho^1
       \left(s\left|v'\right|^2+\frac{m^2}{s}\left|v\right|^2\right)\,\mathrm d s}
      {\displaystyle\int_\rho^1s\left|v\right|^2\,\mathrm d s}.
\]
A minimizer may be chosen nonnegative, since replacing it by its
absolute value does not change this quotient. The differential
equation then makes it strictly positive on the closed interval:
an interior zero of a nonnegative solution would also have zero
derivative, and a zero at either endpoint is accompanied by the
Neumann condition. In both cases uniqueness for the initial-value
problem would force the solution to vanish identically. Moreover,
every solution satisfying the outer Neumann condition is determined
by its value at $s=1$, so the eigenspace is one-dimensional.
Choose the positive eigenfunction $u\left(s,\rho\right)$ normalized by
$u\left(1,\rho\right)=1$.

We first justify its differentiation with respect to $\rho$.
Let $U\left(s,\lambda\right)$ solve
\[
 -\left(sU_s\right)_s+\frac{m^2}{s}U=\lambda sU,
 \qquad U\left(1,\lambda\right)=1,\qquad
 U_s\left(1,\lambda\right)=0.
\]
Subscripts denote partial derivatives. On any compact interval with
$s>0$, this initial-value solution depends smoothly on $s$ and
$\lambda$. The eigenvalue equation is
$F\left(\rho,\lambda\right):=U_s\left(\rho,\lambda\right)=0$.
At an eigenvalue put $Z=\partial_\lambda U$. Differentiating the
equation for $U$ gives
\[
 -\left(sZ_s\right)_s+\frac{m^2}{s}Z
 =\lambda sZ+sU,\qquad Z\left(1\right)=Z_s\left(1\right)=0.
\]
Multiplying this equation by $U$, subtracting the equation for $U$
multiplied by $Z$, and integrating yields
\[
 \int_\rho^1sU^2\,\mathrm d s
 =\left[s\left(ZU_s-UZ_s\right)\right]_\rho^1
 =\rho U\left(\rho,\lambda\right)Z_s\left(\rho,\lambda\right).
\]
Thus, at $\lambda=\beta_m\left(\rho\right)$,
\[
 \partial_\lambda F\left(\rho,\beta_m\left(\rho\right)\right)
 =\frac{\displaystyle\int_\rho^1sU^2\,\mathrm d s}
        {\rho U\left(\rho,\beta_m\left(\rho\right)\right)}>0.
\]
The implicit-function theorem therefore gives a smooth local
eigenvalue branch and a smooth eigenfunction
$u\left(s,\rho\right)=U\left(s,\beta_m\left(\rho\right)\right)$.
This branch is the lowest eigenvalue: under the linear change
$s=\rho+\left(1-\rho\right)y$, the Rayleigh quotient is defined on
the fixed space $H^1\left(0,1\right)$, with positive coefficients
depending continuously on $\rho$. On compact subsets of
$0<\rho<1$, their relative changes are uniformly small, so the
minimum is continuous in $\rho$. It must consequently agree locally
with the isolated root just constructed. Since $\rho$ was arbitrary,
$\beta_m$ is differentiable throughout $\left(0,1\right)$.

For the derivative calculation, write $\beta=\beta_m\left(\rho\right)$,
$u=u\left(s,\rho\right)$, and
$\dot u=\partial_\rho u\left(s,\rho\right)$, where $s$ is held fixed.
Define the energy and mass
\[
 E\left(\rho\right)=\int_\rho^1
       \left(su_s^2+\frac{m^2}{s}u^2\right)\,\mathrm d s,
 \qquad
 M\left(\rho\right)=\int_\rho^1su^2\,\mathrm d s>0.
\]
The weak eigenvalue equation gives $E=\beta M$. Leibniz's rule,
including the moving lower endpoint and the dependence of $u$ on
$\rho$, gives
\begin{align*}
 E'\left(\rho\right)
 &=-\left(\rho u_s\left(\rho,\rho\right)^2
              +\frac{m^2}{\rho}u\left(\rho,\rho\right)^2\right)
   +2\int_\rho^1\left(su_s\dot u_s+\frac{m^2}{s}u\dot u\right)\,\mathrm d s,
 \\
 M'\left(\rho\right)
 &=-\rho u\left(\rho,\rho\right)^2
   +2\int_\rho^1su\dot u\,\mathrm d s.
\end{align*}
Here $u_s\left(\rho,\rho\right)=0$. The cross term in $E'$ is
computed by integration by parts:
\begin{align*}
 \int_\rho^1\left(su_s\dot u_s+\frac{m^2}{s}u\dot u\right)\,\mathrm d s
 &=\left[su_s\dot u\right]_\rho^1
   +\int_\rho^1\left(-\left(su_s\right)_s+\frac{m^2}{s}u\right)\dot u\,\mathrm d s
 \\
 &=\beta\int_\rho^1su\dot u\,\mathrm d s.
\end{align*}
The boundary term vanishes because the original eigenfunction has
$u_s=0$ at both endpoints. No boundary condition on $\dot u$ is
needed: it is an admissible test function for the Neumann weak
equation. In fact, differentiating the moving boundary condition
$u_s\left(\rho,\rho\right)=0$ gives
$\dot u_s\left(\rho,\rho\right)+u_{ss}\left(\rho,\rho\right)=0$,
so $\dot u$ generally does not satisfy a homogeneous Neumann
condition at the inner endpoint.

Differentiate $E=\beta M$ and substitute the preceding formulas.
The two integrals containing $u\dot u$ cancel exactly, leaving
\[
 \beta_m'\left(\rho\right)M\left(\rho\right)
 =E'\left(\rho\right)-\beta M'\left(\rho\right)
 =\rho\left(\beta-\frac{m^2}{\rho^2}\right)
       u\left(\rho,\rho\right)^2.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \beta_m'\left(\rho\right)
 =\frac{\rho\left(\beta_m\left(\rho\right)-m^2/\rho^2\right)
             u\left(\rho,\rho\right)^2}
        {\displaystyle\int_\rho^1su\left(s,\rho\right)^2\,\mathrm d s}.
\]

To determine its sign, use the constant trial function $v=1$.
Its energy is $m^2\log\left(1/\rho\right)$ and its mass is
$\left(1-\rho^2\right)/2$. Hence
\[
 \beta_m\left(\rho\right)
 \le\frac{2m^2\log\left(1/\rho\right)}{1-\rho^2}
 =\frac{m^2\log\left(\rho^{-2}\right)}{1-\rho^2}
 <\frac{m^2\left(\rho^{-2}-1\right)}{1-\rho^2}
 =\frac{m^2}{\rho^2}.
\]
The strict inequality uses $\log z<z-1$ for $z>1$, with
$z=\rho^{-2}$. Since $\rho>0$, $u\left(\rho,\rho\right)>0$, and
$M\left(\rho\right)>0$, the derivative formula now gives
$\beta_m'\left(\rho\right)<0$ at every $0<\rho<1$.
Thus $\beta_m$ is strictly decreasing on the whole interval.
The assumption $m\ge1$ is essential for strictness; in order zero,
the constant eigenfunction gives $\beta_0\equiv0$.

For the explicit constants use
\[
 f_m\left(s\right)=s^m\left(1-a_ms^2\right),\qquad
 \left(a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4\right)=\left(1/3,1/2,2/3,2/3\right).
\]
The factor $s^m$ ensures regularity at the disk centre. The quadratic
correction reduces the energy of the pure monomial. These rational
coefficients give bounds sufficient for the first-eight-eigenvalue
estimate below; no optimality is claimed. Direct integration on the
unit disk, with the common angular factor suppressed, gives
\[
 K_m=\int_0^1\left(f_m'^2+m^2s^{-2}f_m^2\right)s\,\mathrm d s,
 \qquad M_m=\int_0^1f_m^2s\,\mathrm d s,
\]
Since
$f_m'\left(s\right)=ms^{m-1}-a_m\left(m+2\right)s^{m+1}$, expansion and integration
of the monomials give
\[
\begin{aligned}
 K_m
 &=\int_0^1\left(2m^2s^{2m-1}
     -4a_mm\left(m+1\right)s^{2m+1}
     +a_m^2\left(m^2+\left(m+2\right)^2\right)s^{2m+3}\right)\,\mathrm d s\\
 &=m-2a_mm+\frac{a_m^2\left(m^2+\left(m+2\right)^2\right)}{2m+4}
   =m\left(1-a_m\right)^2+\frac{2a_m^2}{m+2},\\
 M_m
 &=\int_0^1\left(s^{2m+1}-2a_ms^{2m+3}+a_m^2s^{2m+5}\right)\,\mathrm d s\\
 &=\frac1{2m+2}-\frac{a_m}{m+2}+\frac{a_m^2}{2m+6}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Substituting the four rational coefficients gives the exact values
\[
 \begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
 m&a_m&K_m&M_m&K_m/M_m\\\hline
 1&1/3&14/27&11/72&112/33\\
 2&1/2&5/8&1/15&75/8\\
 3&2/3&23/45&31/1080&552/31\\
 4&2/3&16/27&13/630&1120/39
 \end{array}
\]
Fix $\rho>0$. Restricting $f_m$ to $\left[\varepsilon,1\right]$
gives a trial quotient tending to $K_m/M_m$ as
$\varepsilon\downarrow0$. For $0<\varepsilon<\rho/2$, monotonicity
and the variational bound give
$\beta_m\left(\rho/2\right)\le\beta_m\left(\varepsilon\right)
\le E_\varepsilon\left(f_m\right)/M_\varepsilon\left(f_m\right)$,
where $E_\varepsilon$ and $M_\varepsilon$ are the energy and mass
integrals over $\left[\varepsilon,1\right]$. Taking the limit and then
using strict monotonicity yields
\[
 \beta_m\left(\rho\right)<\beta_m\left(\rho/2\right)\le K_m/M_m.
\]
No numerical information about Bessel zeros is used.

Use the disk action $G_X$ from \eqref{eq:diskaction}.
The sectorwise derivative-zero bound is
\cite[Proposition~3.1, equation~(3.3)]{FLPSballs}.
Here $j'_{m,k}$ is the $k$th derivative zero in angular order $m$,

 \#\left\{k\ge1:j'_{m,k}\le X\right\}
 \ge\left\lfloor G_X\left(m\right)+\frac34\right\rfloor.

In that paper the frequency variable is $\lambda=X$, the spectral

$\kappa_{2,0}=1$ and $\kappa_{2,m}=2$ for $m\ge1$.
