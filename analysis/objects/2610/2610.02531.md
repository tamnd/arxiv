\label{thm:area-ldp}\label{thm:main}
As $\gamma\downarrow0$ through arbitrary values, the variables $A_\gamma$
satisfy an LDP on $\mathbb R$ with speed $\gamma^{-2}$ and good rate
function $I_W$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:block}
The densities \eqref{eq:block-pair-density} are probability densities,
and the area of the normalized weight-$W$ disk with left length one
satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-area}
 A_\gamma\overset d=
 \frac{\sin\theta}{2}\sum_{k=1}^N
 \left(\prod_{j<k}\ell_j^2\right)T_k.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Set $r_0=1$ and integrate \eqref{eq:welding-input} over the free final
length $r_N$. The joint boundary-length density is proportional to
$\prod_{k=1}^N K(r_{k-1},r_k)\prod_{k=1}^N\,\mathrm d r_k$.
Put $\ell_k=r_k/r_{k-1}$, including the final ratio $\ell_N$.
The transformation $r_k=\prod_{j\le k}\ell_j$ has Jacobian
\[
 \prod_{k=1}^N r_{k-1}=\prod_{j=1}^N\ell_j^{N-j}.
\]
Since $K(cx,cy)=c^{-2}K(x,y)$, the kernel product contributes
$\prod_{j=1}^N\ell_j^{-2(N-j)}\prod_{k=1}^N K(1,\ell_k)$.
Combining the two factors gives
\[
 \prod_{k=1}^N
 \frac{\ell_k^{\pi/\theta-(N-k)-1}}
      {(1+\ell_k^{\pi/\theta})^2}\,\,\mathrm d\ell_k.
\]
In particular, the boundary bias is negative.

To normalize one factor, substitute $y=l^{\pi/\theta}$ and use the beta
integral. For $|b|<1$,
\[
 \int_0^\infty
 \frac{l^{(\pi/\theta)(1+b)-1}}{(1+l^{\pi/\theta})^2}\,\,\mathrm d l
 =\frac{\theta}{\pi}\Gamma(1+b)\Gamma(1-b)
 =\frac{\theta}{\pi}Z(b).
\]
Here $b=-(N-k)\theta/\pi$ lies in $(-1,0]$, since $N\theta/\pi=W/2<1$.
Thus all factors have finite positive mass, and the boundary ratios
are independent with densities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-ratio-density}
 \frac{\pi}{\theta Z(-(N-k)\theta/\pi)}
 \frac{l^{\pi/\theta-(N-k)-1}}{(1+l^{\pi/\theta})^2}\,\,\mathrm d l.
\end{equation}

Conditional on these ratios, scale the $k$-th disk by $r_{k-1}^{-1}$
in boundary length. Its boundary lengths become $(1,\ell_k)$ and its
area is divided by $r_{k-1}^2$. The one-block area law identifies this
rescaled area as $(\sin\theta/2)T_k$, with conditional duration density
\[
 \frac{\mathsf K_\theta(1/\sin\theta,l/\sin\theta,t)}
 {\int_0^\infty\mathsf K_\theta(1/\sin\theta,l/\sin\theta,u)\,\,\mathrm d u}
 \,\,\mathrm d t.
\]
Multiplying this density by \eqref{eq:block-ratio-density} and using
\eqref{eq:integrated-cone-kernel} gives exactly
\eqref{eq:block-pair-density}. Conditional independence of the component
disks and factorization of the ratio density prove independence of the
pairs. Additivity of area under welding proves \eqref{eq:block-area}.
The final ratio $\ell_N$ is auxiliary: integrating it out gives the
terminal duration law, without any extra tilt.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:partial-area-recursion}
For $1\le m\le N$,
\[
 A^{(m)}=\frac{\sin\theta}{2}T_{N-m+1}
       +\ell_{N-m+1}^2A^{(m-1)},
 \qquad A^{(N)}=A_\gamma.
\]
The new block is independent of $A^{(m-1)}$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
In \eqref{eq:partial-area-definition}, the term $k=N-m+1$ is
$(\sin\theta/2)T_{N-m+1}$. For every $k\ge N-m+2$,
\[
 \prod_{r=N-m+1}^{k-1}\ell_r^2
 =\ell_{N-m+1}^2\prod_{r=N-m+2}^{k-1}\ell_r^2.
\]
The remaining sum is therefore $\ell_{N-m+1}^2A^{(m-1)}$.
This factor is necessary because the last $m-1$ blocks have incoming
length $\ell_{N-m+1}$ in the coordinates used for $A^{(m)}$, whereas
their incoming length is one in the definition of $A^{(m-1)}$.
For $m=1$ the remaining sum is empty and $A^{(0)}=0$.
Independence was noted above, and $m=N$ gives \eqref{eq:block-area}.
Finally, substituting $k=N-m+1$ into the block bias
$-(N-k)\theta/\pi$ gives $-(m-1)\theta/\pi$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:integrated-bessel-term}
Under these conditions,
\begin{align}
 &\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
 l^{\xi-1}u^{d-1}
 e^{-(1+l^2)/(2u)}I_\nu(l/u)\,\,\mathrm d u\,\,\mathrm d l
 \notag\\
 &\qquad=
 \frac{2^{d+1}d!}
 {\displaystyle\prod_{h=0}^d
 \bigl[\nu^2-(\xi+2h)^2\bigr]}.
 \label{eq:integrated-bessel-term}
\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The modified Bessel function has the positive series
\[
 I_\nu(l/u)
 =
 \sum_{m=0}^\infty
 \frac{(l/(2u))^{\nu+2m}}
 {m!\,\Gamma(\nu+m+1)}.
\]
Thus Tonelli's theorem allows us to integrate term by term.
The assumptions imply \(\nu>d\).  Substituting
\(r=(1+l^2)/(2u)\), we obtain
\[
 \int_0^\infty
 u^{d-\nu-2m-1}e^{-(1+l^2)/(2u)}\,\,\mathrm d u
 =
 \Gamma(\nu+2m-d)
 \left(\frac{1+l^2}{2}\right)^{d-\nu-2m}.
\]
Next, the beta integral gives
\begin{align*}
 &\int_0^\infty
 l^{\xi+\nu+2m-1}(1+l^2)^{d-\nu-2m}\,\,\mathrm d l\\
 &\qquad=
 \frac{
 \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+\xi}{2}+m\right)
 \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu-\xi}{2}+m-d\right)}
 {2\Gamma(\nu+2m-d)}.
\end{align*}
The gamma factors in the numerator have positive arguments by the
assumptions on \(\xi\).

Combining these two integrations, the left-hand side of
\eqref{eq:integrated-bessel-term} becomes
\[
 2^{-d-1}
 \sum_{m=0}^\infty
 \frac{
 \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+\xi}{2}+m\right)
 \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu-\xi}{2}-d+m\right)}
 {m!\,\Gamma(\nu+1+m)}.
\]
We evaluate this series using the identity
\[
 \sum_{m=0}^\infty
 \frac{\Gamma(a+m)\Gamma(b+m)}
 {m!\,\Gamma(c+m)}
 =
 \frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-a-b)}
 {\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)},
 \qquad
 a,b>0,\quad c>a+b.
\]
For completeness, this identity follows by writing
\[
 \frac{\Gamma(b+m)}{\Gamma(c+m)}
 =
 \frac1{\Gamma(c-b)}
 \int_0^1 r^{b+m-1}(1-r)^{c-b-1}\,\,\mathrm d r
\]
and summing
\(\sum_{m\geq0}\Gamma(a+m)r^m/m!
=\Gamma(a)(1-r)^{-a}\).
The remaining integral is another beta integral.

Applying the identity with
\(a=(\nu+\xi)/2\), \(b=(\nu-\xi)/2-d\), and \(c=\nu+1\)
gives
\[
 2^{-d-1}d!\,
 \frac{
 \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+\xi}{2}\right)
 \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu-\xi}{2}-d\right)}
 {
 \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu-\xi}{2}+1\right)
 \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+\xi}{2}+d+1\right)}.
\]
Finally,
\[
 \frac{\Gamma(a)}{\Gamma(a+d+1)}
 =
 \frac1{\prod_{h=0}^d(a+h)}.
\]
Applying this to the two gamma ratios and pairing the factors yields
\[
 \prod_{h=0}^d
 \left(\frac{\nu+\xi}{2}+h\right)
 \left(\frac{\nu-\xi}{2}-h\right)
 =
 4^{-(d+1)}
 \prod_{h=0}^d\bigl[\nu^2-(\xi+2h)^2\bigr].
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:integrated-bessel-term}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:mixed-block-moments}
The mixed moment is finite and equals
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed}
 \mathbb E_b[\ell^pT^d]
 =
 \frac{d!}{Z(b)}
 \left(\frac{2\theta^2}{\pi^2\sin^2\theta}\right)^d
 H\left[
 \left(b+\frac{p\theta}{\pi}\right)^2,
 \left(b+\frac{(p+2)\theta}{\pi}\right)^2,
 \ldots,
 \left(b+\frac{(p+2d)\theta}{\pi}\right)^2
 \right].
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We first treat \(d=0\).  Integrating out the running time gives the
boundary-ratio density
\[
 \frac{\pi}{\theta Z(b)}
 \frac{l^{\pi(1+b)/\theta-1}}
 {(1+l^{\pi/\theta})^2}\,\,\mathrm d l.
\]
With the substitution \(y=l^{\pi/\theta}\), we obtain
\begin{align*}
 \mathbb E_b[\ell^p]
 &=
 \frac1{Z(b)}
 \int_0^\infty
 \frac{y^{b+p\theta/\pi}}{(1+y)^2}\,\,\mathrm d y\\
 &=
 \frac{
 \Gamma(1+b+p\theta/\pi)
 \Gamma(1-b-p\theta/\pi)}
 {Z(b)}\\
 &=
 \frac{Z(b+p\theta/\pi)}{Z(b)}.
\end{align*}
The assumptions ensure that both beta-integral parameters are positive.
Since \(Z(x)=H(x^2)\), this is \eqref{eq:mixed} for \(d=0\).

Now suppose \(d\geq1\).  To remove the initial Brownian radius,
substitute \(t=u/\sin^2\theta\).  Directly from the definition of the
kernel,
\[
 \mathsf K_\theta
 \left(\frac1{\sin\theta},
       \frac{l}{\sin\theta},
       \frac{u}{\sin^2\theta}\right)
 =
 \sin^4\theta\,\mathsf K_\theta(1,l,u).
\]
Therefore
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E_b[\ell^pT^d]
 &=
 \frac{\pi^2}{\theta^2Z(b)\sin^{2d}\theta}
 \int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty
 l^{\pi b/\theta+p}u^d
 \mathsf K_\theta(1,l,u)\,\,\mathrm d u\,\,\mathrm d l.
 \label{eq:mixed-rescaling}
\end{align}
Expanding the kernel gives
\begin{align*}
 &l^{\pi b/\theta+p}u^d\mathsf K_\theta(1,l,u)\\
 &\qquad=
 \sum_{j=1}^\infty(-1)^{j+1}j^2
 l^{\pi b/\theta+p-1}u^{d-1}
 e^{-(1+l^2)/(2u)}I_{j\pi/\theta}(l/u).
\end{align*}
Lemma~\ref{lem:integrated-bessel-term}, with
\(\nu=j\pi/\theta\) and \(\xi=\pi b/\theta+p\), evaluates the
integral of each Bessel term as
\[
 \frac{2^{d+1}d!}
 {\displaystyle\prod_{h=0}^d
 \left[
 \left(\frac{j\pi}{\theta}\right)^2
 -
 \left(\frac{\pi b}{\theta}+p+2h\right)^2
 \right]}.
\]
The hypotheses of that lemma hold for every \(j\geq1\).
Moreover, after multiplication by \(j^2\), these positive integrals
are \(O(j^{-2d})\) as \(j\to\infty\).
Since \(d\geq1\), their sum is finite.  Thus the Bessel series may be
integrated term by term despite its alternating signs.

Substituting into \eqref{eq:mixed-rescaling} and extracting
\((\pi/\theta)^2\) from each denominator factor gives
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E_b[\ell^pT^d]
 =
 \frac{2^{d+1}d!}{Z(b)}
 \left(\frac{\theta^2}{\pi^2\sin^2\theta}\right)^d
 \sum_{j=1}^\infty
 \frac{(-1)^{j+1}j^2}
 {\displaystyle\prod_{h=0}^d
 \left[
 j^2-
 \left(b+\frac{(p+2h)\theta}{\pi}\right)^2
 \right]}.
 \label{eq:mixed-series}
\end{align}

It remains to identify this series as a divided difference of \(H\).
The partial-fraction expansion of
\(H(v)=\pi\sqrt v/\sin(\pi\sqrt v)\) is
\[
 H(v)
 =
 1+2v\sum_{j=1}^\infty
 \frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j^2-v},
 \qquad 0\leq v<1.
\]
This series converges locally uniformly.  For the resolvent
\(v\mapsto(j^2-v)^{-1}\), its divided difference is
\[
 \left[\frac1{j^2-v}\right][v_0,\ldots,v_d]
 =
 \prod_{h=0}^d\frac1{j^2-v_h}.
\]
For \(d\geq1\), the constant term has zero divided difference, and
\(v/(j^2-v)=j^2/(j^2-v)-1\).  Consequently,
\[
 H[v_0,\ldots,v_d]
 =
 2\sum_{j=1}^\infty
 \frac{(-1)^{j+1}j^2}
 {\prod_{h=0}^d(j^2-v_h)}.
\]
The identity extends to coincident nodes by continuity.
Taking
\(v_h=(b+(p+2h)\theta/\pi)^2\)
in this formula identifies the series in
\eqref{eq:mixed-series} and proves \eqref{eq:mixed}.

\label{lem:mixed-moments-area-expansion}
For every integer \(0\leq n<2/\gamma^2=\pi/(2\theta)\), the
multinomial expansion of \(\mathbb E[A_\gamma^n]\) involves only
finite mixed moments covered by

<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Choose nonnegative integers \(d_1,\ldots,d_N\) with
\(\sum_{k=1}^Nd_k=n\).  The corresponding term in the expansion of
\[
 A_\gamma^n
 =
 \left[
 \frac{\sin\theta}{2}
 \sum_{k=1}^N
 \left(\prod_{i<k}\ell_i^2\right)T_k
 \right]^n
\]
is
\[
 n!\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{2}\right)^n
 \prod_{k=1}^N
 \frac{
 T_k^{d_k}\ell_k^{\,2\sum_{j>k}d_j}}
 {d_k!}.
\]
Indeed, \(\ell_k^2\) occurs in every area contribution from a block
with index larger than \(k\).

Independence of the block pairs therefore gives
\[
 \mathbb E[A_\gamma^n]
 =
 n!\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{2}\right)^n
 \sum_{\substack{d_1,\ldots,d_N\geq0\\
                 d_1+\cdots+d_N=n}}
 \prod_{k=1}^N
 \frac{
 \mathbb E_{-(N-k)\theta/\pi}
 \left[
 \ell^{\,2\sum_{j>k}d_j}T^{d_k}
 \right]}
 {d_k!}.
\]
For block \(k\), the smallest unsquared node in
\eqref{eq:mixed} is
\[
 -\frac{(N-k)\theta}{\pi}
 +\frac{2\theta}{\pi}\sum_{j>k}d_j
 \geq-\frac{(N-1)\theta}{\pi}>-1.
\]
The largest is
\[
 -\frac{(N-k)\theta}{\pi}
 +\frac{2\theta}{\pi}\sum_{j\geq k}d_j
 \leq\frac{2n\theta}{\pi}<1.
\]
Here we used \(N\theta/\pi=W/2<1\).
Thus every mixed moment is finite and satisfies
\eqref{eq:mixed}.

The factors \(d_k!\) in that formula cancel the multinomial
denominators.  The remaining deterministic factor is
\[
 \left(\frac{\sin\theta}{2}\right)^n
 \prod_{k=1}^N
 \left(\frac{2\theta^2}{\pi^2\sin^2\theta}\right)^{d_k}
 =
 \left(\frac{\theta^2}{\pi^2\sin\theta}\right)^n.
\]
Hence
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E[A_\gamma^n]
 ={}&
 \frac{
 n!\left(\frac{\theta^2}{\pi^2\sin\theta}\right)^n}
 {\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^N
 Z\left(-\frac{(N-k)\theta}{\pi}\right)}
 \notag\\
 &\times
 \sum_{\substack{d_1,\ldots,d_N\geq0\\
                 d_1+\cdots+d_N=n}}
 \prod_{k=1}^N
 H\left[
 \left(
 \frac{\theta}{\pi}
 \left[-(N-k)+2\sum_{j>k}d_j+2h\right]
 \right)^2
 \,:\,h=0,\ldots,d_k
 \right].
 \label{eq:area-composition-moments}
\end{align}
In the last line, the notation inside the brackets lists the
\(d_k+1\) divided-difference nodes in order.
This is the positive sum reorganized into a recurrence below.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We first derive the Brownian-clock representation of a single block.

\medskip

\noindent
\emph{Step 1: From the cone to the strip.}
Consider the conformal map
\[
z\longmapsto w=\log(z\sin\theta)
\]
from the cone \(\{z:0<\arg z<\theta\}\) onto the strip
\(\mathbb R\times(0,\theta)\). An unbiased cone excursion that starts at
radius \(1/\sin\theta\) on the lower side of the cone and ends at radius
\(\ell/\sin\theta\) on the upper side is mapped, in conformal time, to a
strip-crossing excursion.

Write the strip process as \((B_u,Y_u)\), where \(B\) is a standard
Brownian motion and \(Y\) is the vertical strip-crossing excursion. The
two coordinates are independent. Let \(U\) be the crossing time of the
vertical coordinate. Since the terminal point in the strip is
\(B_U+i\theta=\log\ell+i\theta\), we have
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:ell-strip}
\ell=e^{B_U}.
\end{equation}

The inverse conformal map is \(g(w)=e^w/\sin\theta\), and hence
\[
|g'(B_u+iY_u)|^2
=
\frac{e^{2B_u}}{\sin^2\theta}.
\]
The conformal change of clock therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:cone-time}
T
=
\frac1{\sin^2\theta}
\int_0^Ue^{2B_u}\,\mathrm d u.
\end{equation}

\medskip

\noindent
\emph{Step 2: The strip-crossing time.}
We claim that
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:strip-clock-law}
U\overset{d}=\frac{2\theta^2}{\pi^2}V_0.
\end{equation}
To prove this, let \(Y\) be a one-dimensional Brownian motion started at
\(y\in(0,\theta)\), and define
\[
\tau_0=\inf\{u\geq0:Y_u=0\},
\qquad
\tau_\theta=\inf\{u\geq0:Y_u=\theta\}.
\]
The strip-crossing excursion is obtained as the entrance limit
\(y\downarrow0\) of \(Y\) conditioned on \(\tau_\theta<\tau_0\).
Therefore,
\[
\mathbb E[e^{-\lambda U}]
=
\lim_{y\downarrow0}
\mathbb E_y\left[
e^{-\lambda\tau_\theta}
\,\middle|\,
\tau_\theta<\tau_0
\right].
\]

Let
\(q_\lambda(y)=
\mathbb E_y[e^{-\lambda\tau_\theta};\tau_\theta<\tau_0]\).
The one-dimensional Feynman--Kac formula gives
\[
\frac12q_\lambda''=\lambda q_\lambda
\quad\text{on }(0,\theta),
\qquad
q_\lambda(0)=0,
\qquad
q_\lambda(\theta)=1.
\]
Thus
\[
q_\lambda(y)
=
\frac{\sinh(y\sqrt{2\lambda})}
{\sinh(\theta\sqrt{2\lambda})}.
\]
Since \(\mathbb{P}_y(\tau_\theta<\tau_0)=y/\theta\), it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:strip-clock-laplace}
\mathbb E[e^{-\lambda U}]
=
\frac{\theta\sqrt{2\lambda}}
{\sinh(\theta\sqrt{2\lambda})}.
\end{equation}

On the other hand, independence of the \(E_j\)'s and Euler's product
formula give
\[
\mathbb E[e^{-tV_0}]
=
\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{j^2}{j^2+t}
=
\frac{\pi\sqrt t}{\sinh(\pi\sqrt t)}.
\]
Taking \(t=2\lambda\theta^2/\pi^2\), we obtain
\[
\mathbb E\left[
e^{-\lambda(2\theta^2V_0/\pi^2)}
\right]
=
\frac{\theta\sqrt{2\lambda}}
{\sinh(\theta\sqrt{2\lambda})}.
\]
Comparison with \eqref{lower:eq:strip-clock-laplace} proves
\eqref{lower:eq:strip-clock-law}.

\medskip

\noindent
\emph{Step 3: Incorporating the boundary bias.}
The block appearing at the \(n\)-th step of the recursion has index
\(k=N+1-n\). Its boundary weight is
\[
\ell_k^{-(N-k)}=\ell_k^{-(n-1)}.
\]
By \eqref{lower:eq:ell-strip},
\[
\ell_k^{-(n-1)}
=
e^{-(n-1)B_U}.
\]
Factor this weight as
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:girsanov-factor}
e^{-(n-1)B_U}
=
e^{-(n-1)B_U-\frac12(n-1)^2U}
e^{\frac12(n-1)^2U}.
\end{equation}
The first factor on the right is the exponential martingale that changes
the horizontal Brownian motion into a Brownian motion with drift
\(-(n-1)\). By \eqref{lower:eq:strip-clock-law}, the second factor is
\[
e^{\frac12(n-1)^2U}
=
\exp\left\{
\left(\frac{(n-1)\theta}{\pi}\right)^2V_0
\right\}.
\]
It therefore changes \(V_0\) into \(V_n\), as defined in
\eqref{lower:eq:tilted-clock-law}. Its normalizing constant is
\[
H\left(\left(\frac{(n-1)\theta}{\pi}\right)^2\right).
\]

After this change of measure, the horizontal coordinate in conformal
time is \(B_u-(n-1)u\). Using Brownian scaling and the substitution
\[
u=\frac{2\theta^2}{\pi^2}v,
\]
we obtain
\[
B_{2\theta^2v/\pi^2}
-(n-1)\frac{2\theta^2}{\pi^2}v
\overset{d}=
\frac{\sqrt2\,\theta}{\pi}B_v
-\frac{2(n-1)\theta^2}{\pi^2}v.
\]
Thus, if the logarithm of the accumulated boundary scale at the
beginning of the block is \(x\), the logarithmic scale during that block
is precisely the process \(X^{(n)}\) in
\eqref{lower:eq:block-log-process}. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:block-endpoint}
x+\log\ell_k=X_{V_n}^{(n)}.
\end{equation}

Similarly, \eqref{lower:eq:cone-time} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:intrinsic-block-time}
T_k
=
\frac{2\theta^2}{\pi^2\sin^2\theta}
\int_0^{V_n}
\exp\left\{
2\left(
\frac{\sqrt2\,\theta}{\pi}B_v
-\frac{2(n-1)\theta^2}{\pi^2}v
\right)
\right\}
\,\mathrm d v.
\end{equation}
Multiplying by the square \(e^{2x}\) of the accumulated boundary scale
gives
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:scaled-block-area}
e^{2x}T_k
=
\frac{2\theta^2}{\pi^2\sin^2\theta}
\int_0^{V_n}e^{2X_v^{(n)}}\,\mathrm d v.
\end{equation}

\medskip

\noindent
\emph{Step 4: The Laplace coefficient and the recursion.}
Combining \eqref{lower:eq:scaled-block-area} with the coefficient in
\eqref{lower:eq:preliminary-recursion}, we obtain
\[
\frac{s\sin\theta}{2\gamma^2}e^{2x}T_k
=
\frac{s\theta^2}{\pi^2\gamma^2\sin\theta}
\int_0^{V_n}e^{2X_v^{(n)}}\,\mathrm d v.
\]
Since \(\theta=\pi\gamma^2/4\),
\[
\frac{\theta^2}{\pi^2\gamma^2}
=
\frac{\gamma^2}{16}.
\]
Therefore,
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:block-laplace-factor}
\frac{s\sin\theta}{2\gamma^2}e^{2x}T_k
=
\frac{s\gamma^2}{16\sin\theta}
\int_0^{V_n}e^{2X_v^{(n)}}\,\mathrm d v.
\end{equation}

Using \eqref{lower:eq:block-endpoint} and
\eqref{lower:eq:block-laplace-factor}, the conditional recursion
\eqref{lower:eq:preliminary-recursion} becomes
\[
f_n(x)
=
\mathbb E\left[
\exp\left\{
-\frac{s\gamma^2}{16\sin\theta}
\int_0^{V_n}e^{2X_v^{(n)}}\,\mathrm d v
\right\}
f_{n-1}\left(X_{V_n}^{(n)}\right)
\right].
\]
By the definition of \(\mathcal K_n\), this is exactly
\(f_n=\mathcal K_nf_{n-1}\). Iterating the recursion proves
\eqref{lower:eq:transform-recursion}.

For every fixed \(M<\infty\), there exists
\(\eta_\gamma(M)\to0\) such that, for every \(1\leq n\leq N\) and every
\(x\in D\),

We separate large clock values from bounded clock values.
A differential inequality controls the large-clock contribution.
For bounded clocks, an explicit change of measure controls the spatial
variation. Averaging and using the HJ equation gives the one-step bound.

First, we will construct a supersolution for PDE 
\[\begin{cases}
    \partial_r u(r,x) = \mathcal A_{n,\gamma} u(r,x),\qquad r > 0, x\in D\\
    u(r,x) = 0, \qquad r > 0, x\in \partial D\\
    u(0,x) = g_{\gamma,t_n}(x) ,\qquad x\in D
\end{cases}.
\]


Here, $u = T^D_{n,\gamma}(r)g_{\gamma, t_n}(x)$. Direct differentiation gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\mathcal A_{n,\gamma}g_{\gamma,t_n}(x)}
      {g_{\gamma,t_n}(x)}
 &=
 (\mathcal G_x(t_n,x)-t_n)^2
 -\frac{s}{4\pi}e^{2x}
 -t_n^2+r_\gamma(t_n,x),
 \label{lower:eq:generatorconsistency}
\end{align}
where
\[
 r_\gamma(t,x)
 =
 \frac{\gamma^2}{4}\mathcal G_{xx}(t,x)
 -
 \left(
 \frac{s\gamma^2}{16\sin\theta}
 -\frac{s}{4\pi}
 \right)e^{2x}.
\]
Since \(\theta=\pi\gamma^2/4\), smoothness of \(\mathcal G\) gives
\[
 \sup_{\substack{0\leq t\leq N\theta/\pi\\x\in D}}
 |r_\gamma(t,x)|
 =O_M(\gamma^2).
\]

By the strict-domain condition and compactness, there exists
\(\delta_M>0\) such that
\[
 \sup_{\substack{0\leq t\leq N\theta/\pi\\x\in D}}
 \left\{
 (\mathcal G_x(t,x)-t)^2
 -\frac{s}{4\pi}e^{2x}
 \right\}
 \leq1-\delta_M.
\]
Consequently, for all sufficiently small \(\gamma\),
\[
 \mathcal A_{n,\gamma}g_{\gamma,t_n}(x)
 \leq
 \left(1-t_n^2-\frac{\delta_M}{2}\right)
 g_{\gamma,t_n}(x),
 \qquad x\in D.
\]
Set
\[
 v(r,x)
 =
 \exp\left\{
 \left(1-t_n^2-\frac{\delta_M}{2}\right)r
 \right\}g_{\gamma,t_n}(x).
\]
Then $v(r,x)$ is a supersolution, because
\begin{align*}
 \partial_rv-\mathcal A_{n,\gamma}v
 &=
 e^{(1-t_n^2-\delta_M/2)r}
 \left[
 \left(1-t_n^2-\frac{\delta_M}{2}\right)
 g_{\gamma,t_n}
 -
 \mathcal A_{n,\gamma}g_{\gamma,t_n}
 \right]
 \geq0.
\end{align*}
Moreover, \(u(0,\cdot)=v(0,\cdot)\) in \(D\), while
\(u=0\leq v\) on \(\partial D\).  The parabolic maximum principle
therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:semigrouptailbound}
 T_{n,\gamma}^D(r)g_{\gamma,t_n}(x)
 \leq
 \exp\left\{
 \left(1-t_n^2-\frac{\delta_M}{2}\right)r
 \right\}g_{\gamma,t_n}(x).
\end{equation}
The above computation automatically yields a bound for $\mathcal K^D_n g_{\gamma, t_n}/g_{\gamma, t_{n+1}}$ when $V_n$ is large: 
\begin{align}
 &\frac{1}{g_{\gamma,t_n}(x)}

 T_{n,\gamma}^D(V_n)g_{\gamma,t_n}(x);

 \right] \notag\\
 &\qquad\leq

 \mathbb E\left[
 e^{(1-t_n^2-\delta_M/4)V_n}
 \right] \notag\\
 &\qquad=
 e^{-\delta_MK/4}
 \frac{H(1-\delta_M/4)}{H(t_n^2)}
 \leq
 C_Me^{-\delta_MK/4}.
 \label{lower:eq:clocktailcomparison}
\end{align}

Under the original probability measure, the process in the \(n\)-th

\[

 =
 \frac{\gamma^2}{2\sqrt{2}}\,\mathrm d B_v
 -
 \frac{\gamma^2t_n}{2}\,\mathrm d v,

\]

 Z_v
 &=
 \exp\left\{

 \frac{\gamma^2}{2\sqrt{2}}\,
 \partial_x\log g_{\gamma,t_n}(X_a)\,\,\mathrm d B_a
 \right. \notag\\

 \right\}.

\end{align}
On the compact interval \(D\), the derivative
\(\partial_x\log g_{\gamma,t_n}\) is bounded.  Thus the stopped
stochastic exponential
\(Z_{v\wedge\tau_D^{(n,x)}}\) satisfies Novikov's condition and is a

We define \(\widehat{\mathbb P}_x\) by

 \left.
 \frac{\,\mathrm d\widehat{\mathbb P}_x}{\,\mathrm d\mathbb P_x}

 Z_{r\wedge\tau_D^{(n,x)}},

\(\widehat{\mathbb P}_x\).

 \frac{H(v_*(t_n))}{H(t_n^2)}.
\]
Thus \(\mathbb E L_n=1\).

Since each $L_n$ depends only on the independent pair $(B^{(n)},V_n)$,
the product has expectation one and block independence is preserved.

To identify this law explicitly, factor the density as
\begin{align*}
 L_n
 ={}&
 \exp\left\{
 \sqrt2P(t_n)B_{V_n}^{(n)}-P(t_n)^2V_n
 \right\}\\
 &\times
 \exp\left\{
 \bigl(v_*(t_n)-t_n^2\bigr)V_n
 \right\}
 \frac{H(t_n^2)}{H(v_*(t_n))}.
\end{align*}
For each fixed clock value, the first factor changes the Brownian drift
to \(\sqrt2P(t_n)\).  The second factor changes the clock distribution.
Under \(\widehat{\mathbb P}_\gamma\), we may therefore realize the blocks
using independent standard Brownian motions \(\widehat B^{(n)}\),
independent of the new clocks, such that
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:controlled-clock}
 \widehat{\mathbb E}_\gamma e^{\lambda V_n}
 =
 \frac{H(v_*(t_n)+\lambda)}{H(v_*(t_n))},
 \qquad \lambda<1-v_*(t_n),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:controlled-segment}
 X_v^{(n)}
 =
 Y_n+\frac{\gamma^2}{2\sqrt2}\widehat B_v^{(n)}
 +\frac{\gamma^2}{2}(P(t_n)-t_n)v.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{align}
 \widehat{\mathbb E}_\gamma\Delta_n
 &=
 \frac{\gamma^2}{2}
 (P(t_n)-t_n)h(v_*(t_n)),
 \label{lower:eq:increment-moments}\\
 \operatorname{Var}_{\widehat{\mathbb P}_\gamma}(\Delta_n)
 &=
 \frac{\gamma^4}{8}h(v_*(t_n))
 +
 \frac{\gamma^4}{4}(P(t_n)-t_n)^2h'(v_*(t_n))
 \leq C\gamma^4.
 \notag
\end{align}
Here we used
\(\widehat{\mathbb E}_\gamma V_n=h(v_*(t_n))\) and
\(\operatorname{Var}_{\widehat{\mathbb P}_\gamma}(V_n)
=h'(v_*(t_n))\).

By \eqref{lower:eq:target-characteristic},
\[
 \widehat{\mathbb E}_\gamma\Delta_n
 =
 x_*(t_n)-x_*(t_{n+1})+O(\gamma^4).
\]
Thus the expected displacement under the new law agrees, to leading
order, with the displacement along the target characteristic.

\label{lower:lem:controlled-convergence}
Under $\widehat{\mathbb P}_\gamma$, in probability,
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:controlled-path-convergence}
 \max_{1\le n\le N}\sup_{0\le v\le V_n}

\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-19 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->

\eqref{lower:eq:controlled-clock} imply that, for some
\(\lambda_0>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{lower:eq:uniform-controlled-clock}
 \sup_{\gamma,n}
 \widehat{\mathbb E}_\gamma e^{\lambda_0V_n}<\infty.
\end{equation}
In particular, all clock moments are uniformly bounded.

We first control the block endpoints.
Since \(Y_N=0\), we have
\(Y_n=\sum_{k=n+1}^N\Delta_k\).
Independence and Doob's maximal inequality, applied in the physical
order \(N,N-1,\ldots,1\), give
\[
 \widehat{\mathbb E}_\gamma
 \max_{0\leq n\leq N}
 \left|
 \sum_{k=n+1}^N
 \bigl(\Delta_k-\widehat{\mathbb E}_\gamma\Delta_k\bigr)
 \right|^2
 \leq
 4\sum_{k=1}^N
 \operatorname{Var}_{\widehat{\mathbb P}_\gamma}(\Delta_k)
 \leq CN\gamma^4=O(\gamma^2).
\]
On the other hand, the estimate for the mean displacement telescopes:
\[
 \max_{0\leq n\leq N}
 \left|
 \sum_{k=n+1}^N\widehat{\mathbb E}_\gamma\Delta_k
 -x_*(t_{n+1})
 \right|
 \leq CN\gamma^4=O(\gamma^2).
\]
We used \(x_*(t_{N+1})=0\).
It follows that
\[
 \max_{0\leq n\leq N}
 |Y_n-x_*(t_{n+1})|\longrightarrow0
 \quad\text{in probability}.
\]

To include segment interiors, fix a sufficiently large constant
\(C_0>0\).  The exponential clock bound implies
\[
 \widehat{\mathbb P}_\gamma
 \left(
 \max_{1\leq n\leq N}V_n>C_0\log(1/\gamma)
 \right)
 \leq CN\gamma^{\lambda_0C_0}\longrightarrow0.
\]
On the complementary event, the absolute drift displacement in
\eqref{lower:eq:controlled-segment} is at most
\(C\gamma^2\log(1/\gamma)\), which tends to zero.
For any fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), the reflection principle also gives
\[
 \widehat{\mathbb P}_\gamma
 \left(
 \max_{1\leq n\leq N}
 \sup_{v\leq C_0\log(1/\gamma)}
 \frac{\gamma^2}{2\sqrt2}
 |\widehat B_v^{(n)}|>\varepsilon
 \right)
 \leq
 4N\exp\left\{
 -\frac{4\varepsilon^2}
 {C_0\gamma^4\log(1/\gamma)}
 \right\}
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
Hence the maximum displacement within any one segment tends to zero.
Since
\(\sup_n|x_*(t_{n+1})-x_*(t_n)|=O(\gamma^2)\),
this proves \eqref{lower:eq:controlled-path-convergence}.

\label{lem:boundary-identities}
The boundary moments satisfy

 B_{\gamma,w}(p)=B_{\gamma,w}(2w/\gamma^2-1-p).

\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary-welding-moments}
 B_{\gamma,w+r}(p)
 =\frac{B_{\gamma,w}(p-2r/\gamma^2)}

\end{equation}

For the second identity, weld a weight-$w$ disk on the left of a

The normalization is finite and positive because the welded disk

\label{lem:uniform-boundary-moments}
For every fixed $p>0$, there is a finite constant $C_{K,p}$ such that
\[
 \mathbb E_{\gamma,w}[R^p]+\mathbb E_{\gamma,w}[R^{-p}]\le C_{K,p}.
\]

boundary ratios and the beta integral give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:integer-boundary-moments}
 B_{\gamma,n\gamma^2/2}(p)
 =\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}
 \frac{Z(-i\theta/\pi+p\theta/\pi)}{Z(-i\theta/\pi)}.
\end{equation}
For fixed $p$, all arguments remain in a compact subset of $(-1,1)$.
The logarithm of each factor is $O(\gamma^2)$, uniformly for weights
in a slightly larger compact interval. There are $O(\gamma^{-2})$
factors, so the product and its reciprocal are uniformly bounded.

For a noninteger weight $w$, choose $r\in(0,\gamma^2/2)$ such that
$w+r=\lceil2w/\gamma^2\rceil\gamma^2/2$, and set
$b=2r/\gamma^2\in(0,1)$. Choose a fixed integer $k\ge\max\{1,p\}$.
By \eqref{eq:boundary-reflection}, $B_{\gamma,r}(b-1)=1$.
Monotonicity of $L^p$ norms applied to $R^{-1}$ gives
$B_{\gamma,r}(-k)\ge1$. Thus
\eqref{eq:boundary-welding-moments} and the integer-block estimate imply
\[
 B_{\gamma,w}(-k-b)
 \le C B_{\gamma,w}(-b)<\infty.
\]
Here finiteness also follows from the same identity: its left-hand
side at weight $w+r$ is finite and both factors on its right are
strictly positive.

Convexity of $\log B_{\gamma,w}$ and $B_{\gamma,w}(0)=1$ give
\[
 B_{\gamma,w}(-b)
 \le B_{\gamma,w}(-k-b)^{b/(k+b)}.
\]
Combining the last two inequalities yields
$B_{\gamma,w}(-k-b)\le C^{(k+b)/k}$, and interpolation with the
zeroth moment bounds $B_{\gamma,w}(-p)$ uniformly.
Finally, for small $\gamma$,
$0<p<2w/\gamma^2-1$ uniformly on $K$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:boundary-reflection} and H\"older's inequality give
$B_{\gamma,w}(p)\le1$. This proves the claim.

weight-$(W_+-W)$ disk to the right of a weight-$W$ disk proves the
second inequality. No estimate on the residual areas is needed.

<!-- proposition-7: body_md -->
\label{prop:all-gamma}
The limits in Theorem~\ref{thm:intro-transforms} hold as
$\gamma\downarrow0$ through arbitrary values, with the same
$\Lambda_W$ and $\Psi_W$.
<!-- end proposition-7 -->
<!-- proof-36: body_md -->
Fix $h>1$. For $0<q<2$ choose $h$ sufficiently close to one that
$hq<2$. The area sandwich and
Lemma~\ref{lem:remove-boundary-tilt} give
\[
 C^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb E_{\gamma,W_-}[A^{q/(h\gamma^2)}]\bigr)^h
 \le\mathbb E_{\gamma,W}[A^{q/\gamma^2}]
 \le C\bigl(\mathbb E_{\gamma,W_+}[A^{hq/\gamma^2}]\bigr)^{1/h}.
\]
For the upper inequality, first compare the unweighted weight-$W$
law to its boundary tilt, and then use the second welding coupling.
The constants are independent of $\gamma$. Since $W_\pm\to W$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:moving-endpoints} yields
\[
 h\Lambda_W(q/h)
 \le\liminf_{\gamma\downarrow0}\Lambda_{\gamma,W}(q)
 \le\limsup_{\gamma\downarrow0}\Lambda_{\gamma,W}(q)
 \le h^{-1}\Lambda_W(hq).
\]
Continuity and $h\downarrow1$ prove moment-transform convergence.

For the Laplace transform, the function $a\mapsto e^{-sa/\gamma^2}$
is decreasing. The same two comparisons therefore give
\[
 C^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb E_{\gamma,W_+}[e^{-sA/(h\gamma^2)}]\bigr)^h
 \le\mathbb E_{\gamma,W}[e^{-sA/\gamma^2}]
 \le C\bigl(\mathbb E_{\gamma,W_-}[e^{-hsA/\gamma^2}]\bigr)^{1/h}.
\]
For $s>0$, it follows that
\[
 h\Psi_W(s/h)
 \le\liminf_{\gamma\downarrow0}\Psi_{\gamma,W}(s)
 \le\limsup_{\gamma\downarrow0}\Psi_{\gamma,W}(s)
 \le h^{-1}\Psi_W(hs).
\]
Letting $h\downarrow1$ proves the claim; $s=0$ is immediate.
The values of $\gamma$ for which $W=W_-$ are already covered by
Lemma~\ref{lem:moving-endpoints}.

<!-- lemma-29: body_md -->
\label{lem:exponential-tilting}
Under $\mathbb{P}_{\gamma,\lambda}$, $Y_\gamma\to y$ in probability, and
\[
 \lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\liminf_{\gamma\downarrow0}
 \gamma^2\log\mathbb{P}(|Y_\gamma-y|<\delta)
 \ge L(\lambda)-\lambda y.
\]
<!-- end lemma-29 -->
<!-- proof-37: body_md -->
For small $h>0$ with $\lambda\pm h\in J$, exponential Markov bounds give
\[
 \limsup_{\gamma\downarrow0}\gamma^2
 \log\mathbb{P}_{\gamma,\lambda}(\pm(Y_\gamma-y)\ge\delta)
 \le L(\lambda\pm h)-L(\lambda)\mp hy-h\delta.
\]
Differentiability makes both bounds negative for sufficiently small $h$.
On $|Y_\gamma-y|<\delta$, the inverse density is at least
$\exp\{[L_\gamma(\lambda)-\lambda y-|\lambda|\delta]/\gamma^2\}$.
Integration over this event and then $\delta\downarrow0$ prove the claim.

<!-- proof-38: body_md -->
Proposition~\ref{prop:all-gamma} provides the unrestricted transform limits.

Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:exponential-tilting} to $Y_\gamma=\log A_\gamma$
with transform $\Lambda_{\gamma,W}$, and to $Y_\gamma=-A_\gamma$
with transform $\Psi_{\gamma,W}$.
The slope ranges proved in Lemmas~\ref{upper:lem:slopes}
and~\ref{lower:lem:slopes} ensure that every $a>a_*(W)$ is reached by
a power tilt and every $0<a<a_*(W)$ by a negative exponential tilt.
Convexity identifies the corresponding costs as the two suprema
defining $I_W$. The lemma therefore gives the required
fixed-neighborhood lower bound at every such $a$.

Markov's inequality gives the matching one-sided upper bounds
\begin{align*}
 \limsup_{\gamma\downarrow0}\gamma^2\log\mathbb{P}(A_\gamma\ge a)
 &\le-I_W(a),\qquad a>a_*(W),\\
 \limsup_{\gamma\downarrow0}\gamma^2\log\mathbb{P}(A_\gamma\le a)
 &\le-I_W(a),\qquad 0<a<a_*(W).
\end{align*}
The endpoint slopes at zero show that these costs are positive.
Hence every fixed neighborhood of $a_*(W)$ has probability tending to
one, which supplies the lower bound there as well.

The unique maximizing parameters tend to zero as $a\to a_*(W)$.
The transform formulas imply that $I_W$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$
and vanishes at $a_*(W)$.
It is strictly increasing above $a_*(W)$ and strictly decreasing below
it: insert the positive maximizer for one value of $a$ into the
supremum for a more extreme value. This uses no derivative of $I_W$.

For completeness, the endpoint bounds needed for goodness are short.
Any fixed $q\in(0,2)$ gives
$I_W(a)\ge q\log a-\Lambda_W(q)\to\infty$ as $a\to\infty$.
The scalar Laplace formula gives
\[
 -\Psi_W'(s)
 \ge\frac{\sin(\pi W/2)}{\sqrt{\pi s}}-\frac Ws,
\]
because $s=(4/\pi)(C^2-1)H(v)^2$ and $C/\sqrt{C^2-1}\ge1$.
Integration shows $\Psi_W(s)\to-\infty$.
Now $I_W(a)\ge-sa-\Psi_W(s)$, first with $a\downarrow0$ and then
$s\to\infty$, implies $I_W(a)\to\infty$ at zero.
Thus the stated extension of $I_W$ to $\mathbb R$ is a good rate function.

For each positive $a$, the Markov bounds above give a neighborhood
upper bound by using the threshold $a-\delta$ above $a_*(W)$ and
$a+\delta$ below it. At $a_*(W)$ use the bound by one; at zero use
$I_W(\delta)\to\infty$; negative points have neighborhoods of
probability zero. A finite cover yields the upper bound on compact
sets. Finally,
\[
 \limsup_{\gamma\downarrow0}\gamma^2\log\mathbb{P}(A_\gamma>R)
 \le\Lambda_W(q)-q\log R\longrightarrow-\infty
\]
for any fixed $q\in(0,2)$, proving exponential tightness.
This extends the compact upper bound to every closed set.
For an open set, choose a positive point and a neighborhood contained
in the set and apply the lower bounds already proved.
This establishes the LDP and convergence in probability.
