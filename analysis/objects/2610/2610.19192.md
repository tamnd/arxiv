Let \(u\) and \(C\) belong to a Grassmann algebra \(\mathcal{G}\)
generated by a finite number of elements
\(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_s\), \(s>1\), with \(u\) belonging to the even
part of \(\mathcal{G}\) and \(C\) to the odd part of \(\mathcal{G}\).
Assume that \(u\) and \(C\) belong to
\(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\), in the sense that every coefficient with
respect to the basis generated by
\(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_s\) belongs to
\(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\). Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:X-Phi-commutator}
[\widetilde{X},\Phi]=0.
\end{equation}

We first notice that, besides \(
[D_x,\widetilde{X}]=0,
\) one also has
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Dx-Phi-commutator}
[D_x,\Phi]=0.
\end{equation}
In fact, the same argument which yields
\eqref{eq:Dx-X-commutator} also applies to
\eqref{eq:Dx-Phi-commutator}.

In order to prove the lemma, it is enough to verify
\begin{align}
[\widetilde{X},\Phi]u&=0,
\label{eq:X-Phi-u}\\
[\widetilde{X},\Phi]C&=0.
\label{eq:X-Phi-C}
\end{align}

Let us check \eqref{eq:X-Phi-u}. We have
\[
\widetilde{X}u=K[u],
\qquad
\Phi u=P[u]C.
\]
Then, using \eqref{eq:Dx-X-commutator}, we get
\begin{align}

&=
D_x^3\widetilde{X}C
+

+

\nonumber\\

\bigl(D_x^4u+D_x(3uu_x)\bigr)C

\nonumber\\

\label{eq:X-Phi-u-first}
\end{align}

Under the assumptions above, the resolvent sets of $\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}$ and $\mathscr{L}_{0}$ satisfy
\[
\rho(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon})
=
\rho(\mathscr{L}_0).
\]
Consequently, their spectra satisfy
\[
\sigma(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon})
=
\sigma(\mathscr{L}_0).
\]
<!-- end teorema-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
In fact, the spectra of $\mathscr{L}_\varepsilon$ and $\mathscr{L}_0$ are defined by
\[
\sigma(\mathscr{L}_\varepsilon)
=
\Lambda_0\setminus\rho(\mathscr{L}_\varepsilon),
\]
and
\[
\sigma(\mathscr{L}_0)
=
\Lambda_0\setminus\rho(\mathscr{L}_0),
\]
respectively. Therefore,
\[
\sigma(\mathscr{L}_\varepsilon)
=
\sigma(\mathscr{L}_0).
\]
That is, the spectra of $\mathscr{L}_\varepsilon$ and
$\mathscr{L}_0$, both with domain
$H^2(\mathbb{R})\otimes\Lambda$, coincide.
Notice that the elements of the spectrum belong to $\Lambda_0$.

The resolvent set of $\mathscr{L}_\varepsilon$ is the set of
$\lambda\in\Lambda_0$ such that
\[
\mathscr{L}_\varepsilon-\lambda I:
H^2(\mathbb{R})\otimes\Lambda
\longrightarrow
L^2(\mathbb{R})\otimes\Lambda
\]
is bijective and its inverse is bounded.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We have
\[
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda I
=
G^{-1}
(\mathscr{L}_0-\lambda I)G,
\]
hence
\[
(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda I)^{-1}
=
G^{-1}
(\mathscr{L}_0-\lambda I)^{-1}G.
\]

Consequently, the inverse of one operator exists if and only if the inverse
of the other operator exists. Moreover, one operator is bounded if and only
if the other one is also bounded. This is so, under the assumption that the
Grassmann algebra valued fields $u$ and $C$ belong to the Schwartz space.
In fact, G is bounded and the product of $G$ with elements of the Hilbert space $L^2$ or $H^2$
also belongs to $L^2$ or $H^2$ respectively. We then have
\[
\rho(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon})
=
\rho(\mathscr{L}_0),
\]
which implies
\[
\sigma(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon})
=
\sigma(\mathscr{L}_0).
\]

The following holds:

\sigma_B(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}) = \sigma_B(\mathscr{L}_0) = \sigma(\mathscr{L}_{0B})
\]
                                                                             

where
\(
\mathscr{L}_{0B}
=
D_x^2+\frac{1}{2}u_BI
\)
is the body operator on \(L^2(\mathbb{R})\), with domain
\(H^2(\mathbb{R})\).
<!-- end lema-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $\lambda\in\Lambda_0$ and decompose $\lambda$ and $u$ into their body
and soul parts as
\[
\lambda=\lambda_B+\lambda_S,\qquad
u=u_B+u_S.
\]
If $\lambda_B\in\rho(\mathcal L_{0B})$, then
\[
\mathscr{L}_0 - \lambda I = \Big(\mathscr{L}_{0B} - \lambda_B I \Big)\Bigg[ I - \Big(\mathscr{L}_{0B} - \lambda_B I \Big)^{-1} \left(\lambda_S - \dfrac{1}{2}u_S \right)  \Bigg]
\]
where $\ (\mathscr{L}_{0B} - \lambda_B I)^{-1}\left(\lambda_S - \dfrac{1}{2}u_S \right)$ is nilpotent. Thus $I - (\mathscr{L}_{0B} - \lambda_B I)^{-1} \left(\lambda_S - \dfrac{1}{2}u_S \right)$
is invertible. Consequently, if $\lambda_B\in\rho(\mathcal{L}_{0B})$, then, for every
nilpotent $\lambda_S$, the element $\lambda=\lambda_B+\lambda_S$ belongs to
$\rho(\mathcal{L}_0)$. Conversely, if $\lambda\in\rho(\mathcal{L}_0)$, then
its body $\lambda_B$ belongs to $\rho(\mathcal{L}_{0B})$. Therefore,
\[
\rho_B(\mathcal{L}_0)=\rho(\mathcal{L}_{0B}),
\]
and hence
\[
\sigma_B(\mathcal{L}_0)=\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{0B}).
\]

Let $\hat{\lambda}$ be an isolated eigenvalue of $\mathscr{L}_{0B}$ and $P$ the spectral projection from $H^2 (\mathbb{R}) \otimes \Lambda$ to the space of eigenfunctions with eigenvalue $\hat{\lambda}$.

\label{prop:factorization-identities}
For arbitrary sufficiently smooth \(p\) and \(\eta\), the fields
defined by
\eqref{eq:factorization-u-fields} and
\eqref{eq:factorization-v-fields} satisfy
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:factorization-identities}
\begin{align}
\mathcal{R}[u]
&=
-2\left(D_x+2p\right)E_p,
\label{eq:factorization-R-u}
\\
\mathcal{R}[\widetilde{u}]
&=
2\left(D_x-2p\right)E_p,
\label{eq:factorization-R-utilde}
\\
\mathcal{L}_u[v]
&=
-2\left[
\left(D_x+2p\right)E_{\eta}
+2\eta E_p
\right],
\label{eq:factorization-L-u}
\\
\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{u}}[\widetilde{v}]
&=
2\left[
\left(D_x-2p\right)E_{\eta}
-2\eta E_p
\right].
\label{eq:factorization-L-utilde}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
<!-- end proposicion-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We first prove the two bosonic identities. From
\[
u=2\alpha-2p^2-2p_x,
\]
we obtain
\[
u_t=
-4pp_t-2p_{xt}, \qquad
u_x
=
-4pp_x-2p_{xx}, \qquad
u_{xxx}
=
-12p_xp_{xx}-4pp_{xxx}-2p_{xxxx}.
\]

Substitution into
\(
\mathcal{R}[u]
\)
and collection of terms gives
\[
\mathcal{R}[u]
=
-2\partial_x E_p-4pE_p
=
-2(D_x+2p)E_p,
\]
which proves~\eqref{eq:factorization-R-u}.

Similarly, substituting
\[
\widetilde{u}
=
2\alpha-2p^2+2p_x
\]
into the KdV residue gives
\[
\mathcal{R}[\widetilde{u}]
=
2\left(D_x-2p\right)E_p,
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:factorization-R-utilde}. Equivalently, this
second identity follows from the first one under the involution
\(
p\longmapsto -p.
\) Indeed, under \(p\mapsto -p\) one has
\(u\mapsto\widetilde u\) and \(E_p\mapsto -E_p\).

We now derive the ghost identities by nilpotent linearization. Let
\(
p_{\varepsilon}
=
p+\varepsilon\eta,
\quad
\varepsilon^2=0,
\)
where \(\varepsilon\) and \(\eta\) are odd, so that
\(p_{\varepsilon}\) is even. The corresponding fields satisfy
\begin{align*}
u_{\varepsilon}
&=
2\alpha-2p_{\varepsilon}^2
-2(p_{\varepsilon})_x
=
u+\varepsilon v,
\\
\widetilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
&=
2\alpha-2p_{\varepsilon}^2
+2(p_{\varepsilon})_x
=
\widetilde{u}
+\varepsilon\widetilde{v}.
\end{align*}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Ep-nilpotent-expansion}
E_{p_{\varepsilon}}
=
E_p+\varepsilon E_{\eta}.
\end{equation}

Since \(p_\varepsilon\) is even, the algebraic identity~(52a)
applies to \(p_\varepsilon\). In fact,
\[
\mathcal{R}[u_{\varepsilon}]
=
-2
\left(
D_x+2p_{\varepsilon}
\right)
E_{p_{\varepsilon}}.
\]
Using \(
\mathcal{R}[u+\varepsilon v]
=
\mathcal{R}[u]
+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_u[v]
\) and \eqref{eq:Ep-nilpotent-expansion}, the coefficient of
\(\varepsilon\) yields
\[
\mathcal{L}_u[v]
=
-2\left[
\left(D_x+2p\right)E_{\eta}
+2\eta E_p
\right].
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:factorization-L-u}.

Likewise, applying \eqref{eq:factorization-R-utilde} to
\(p_{\varepsilon}\) gives
\[
\mathcal{R}[\widetilde{u}_{\varepsilon}]
=
2
\left(
D_x-2p_{\varepsilon}
\right)
E_{p_{\varepsilon}}.
\]
Taking the coefficient of \(\varepsilon\), we obtain
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{u}}[\widetilde{v}]
=
2\left[
\left(D_x-2p\right)E_{\eta}
-2\eta E_p
\right],
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:factorization-L-utilde}.
