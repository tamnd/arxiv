<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:relax}                                                             
In general, one way to approximate a combinatorial problem is to solve its convex relaxation: in our situation, this would mean replacing $\ensuremath{\mathcal{V}_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$ with its convex hull, the hypersimplex~$\ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$, 
\begin{align}\label{eq:relax}
    \min_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace \in \ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace} \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace).
\end{align}
However, we easily see that this relaxation is uninformative: solving this relaxation yields both a zero optimal value and a constant optimal solution, regardless of the instance. This is formalized,  for any divergence, in \cref{prop:convexrelax} in \cref{apx:additionalproofs}. Still, in \cref{sec:FWSwap}, we will use continuous optimization methods, but in an original way, to get relevant information on the combinatorial problem.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:genbound}
Consider the Sinkhorn divergence $D_{\varepsilon}$ with ground cost $c({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})=\left\| {\bm{y}}-{\bm{z}} \right\|_2^2$.
Suppose that the loss function $f_{\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace}$ is $L$-Lipschitz for any $\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace$.  
Then the coreset loss is bounded uniformly in $\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace$ by
\begin{align*}
\hat{R}_n(\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace) - \hat{R}_{S^*}(\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace) & \leq L \sqrt{ \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\hat{\mu}_{S^*},\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) + \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\mu}_{S^*}] + \mathrm{Var}[\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace] }.
\end{align*}
Moreover, if the underlying data distribution $\mu$ satisfies
$\left\| {\bm{y}} \right\|_2 \leq \frac12$ for $\mu$-almost every sample ${\bm{y}}$,
then, for any estimator $\hat\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace$, the generalization with respect to the true risk is bounded by
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}\left[ R(\hat\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace) -  \hat{R}_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace}(\hat\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace) \right] 
& \leq L \sqrt{ \frac{\kappa(\varepsilon)}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} + \frac{2 \ensuremath{n}\xspace -1}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} \mathrm{Var}[\mu] } 
\end{align*}
where $\kappa(\varepsilon)>0$ is a constant depending only on the regularization parameter.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The Sinkhorn divergence is smooth only for $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace$ in the relative interior of the hypersimplex; as a consequence, at the boundary,
the potentials cannot be obtained as fixed points of the Sinkhorn iterations.
In \citet{yin2025wasserstein}, this issue is handled by a smooth optimization scheme that short-circuits the Sinkhorn algorithm. 
In our work, we prefer to stabilize the evaluations with a mixture term $\lambda$, which we rely on both numerically and for the analysis of \cref{sec:algoanalysis}.
The empirical distribution on the selection is then defined as
$\alpha_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}^{\lambda} = (1-\lambda) \ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace} + \lambda \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$.
This stabilization avoids issues in the computation of the potentials
at the boundary of the hypersimplex; 
For simplicity of exposition, we present $\textbf{FW-Swap}\xspace$ in \cref{alg:fw-swap} without\;$\lambda$.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:probcoverOT}
The optimization problems underlying  the ProbCover and k-medoid methods
can be cast as minimizing a Wasserstein distance from the dataset to a measure supported on $\ensuremath{B}\xspace$ points, with k-medoids equipped with the Euclidean distance and ProbCover equipped with the ground cost:
\begin{align*}
c^{\mathrm{PC}}({\bm{y}}_1, {\bm{y}}_2) = \begin{cases}
0 & \text{if }\, \|{\bm{y}}_1 - {\bm{y}}_2\|_2 \leq \delta \\
1 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{align*}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We show the criterion optimized by both methods can, for any \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace, be expressed as the value of an OT problem in which the selected marginal is free over the points in the support of \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace.

The k-medoids criterion for a selection $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace \in \ensuremath{\mathcal{V}_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$
is $\sum_{j \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} \min_{i \in \mathrm{supp}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace)} C_{ij}$
which assigns each dataset point to its nearest medoid at cost $C_{ij}$.
This is exactly the minimum Wasserstein distance under a free second marginal
\begin{align*}
\min_{\substack{{\bm{m}} \in \ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace}}\xspace \\ {\bm{m}}_i \leq \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_i}} W_C(\sum_{i\in[\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} {\bm{m}}_i \delta_{{\bm{y}}_i}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace).
\end{align*}

Since the optimal transport plan assigns the full mass of each point to its nearest medoid,
minimizing over $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace\in\ensuremath{\mathcal{V}_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$ recovers k-medoids exactly.


A similar formulation can be proposed for ProbCover, for which the underlying optimization problem can be expressed as
\begin{align*}
\min_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace \in \ensuremath{\mathcal{V}_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace}
\frac{1}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} \sum_{i \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} \mathbf{1}\Big( \min_{j:\,\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_j=1} \|{\bm{y}}_i - {\bm{y}}_j\|_2 > \delta \Big).
\end{align*}
Consider the optimal transport problem
\begin{align*}
W_{c^{\mathrm{PC}}}(\mu, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace)
= \min_{\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace \in \Pi(\mu, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace)}
\sum_{(i,j) \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace] \times [\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} \ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace_{ij} \, c^{\mathrm{PC}}({\bm{y}}_i, {\bm{y}}_j),
\end{align*}
where $\Pi(\mu, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace)$ is the set of couplings with marginals $\mu$ and $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$.
Because the cost $c^{\mathrm{PC}}$ is binary, any mass transported between points $i$ and $j$ with $\|{\bm{y}}_i - {\bm{y}}_j\|_2 \leq \delta$ incurs zero cost, and any other assignment incurs a unit cost.\\
For each ${\bm{y}}_i$, if there exists a selected point ${\bm{y}}_j$ such that $\|{\bm{y}}_i - {\bm{y}}_j\|_2 \leq \delta$, then assigning its full mass $1 / \ensuremath{n}\xspace$ to such a point yields zero cost.
Furthermore, a selected center can be attributed a mass $k /\ensuremath{n}\xspace$, with $k$ the number of points it captures.
All points outside the circle around any of the centers needs to be transported to one of the centers arbitrarily, inducing a constant cost of one.
Hence, the optimal transport cost satisfies for any $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace \in \ensuremath{\mathcal{V}_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$
\begin{align*}
\min_{\substack{{\bm{m}} \in \ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace}}\xspace \\ {\bm{m}}_i \leq \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_i}} W_{c^{\mathrm{PC}}}(\sum_{i \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} \delta_{{\bm{y}}_i} {\bm{u}}_i, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace )
= \frac{1}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} \sum_{i=1}^\ensuremath{n}\xspace \mathbf{1}\Big( \min_{j:\,\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_j=1} \|{\bm{y}}_i - {\bm{y}}_j\|_2 > \delta \Big),
\end{align*}
recovering the ProbCover loss.
The problems underlying both methods can be expressed as the minimization of a Wasserstein distance from a distribution supported on the coreset to \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:convexrelax}
                                                                                                                                                       
                    
                                                                                                            
                  
                                                                                                        
For any divergence $D$, the convex relaxation of the coreset minimization problem~\eqref{eq:genericlearning} is the following trivial optimization problem over the hypersimplex $\ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$
\begin{align*}
    \min_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace \in \ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace} D(\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = 0,
\end{align*}
which attains its minimum at $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace = \frac{\ensuremath{B}\xspace}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace}\1$ and is independent of the data.

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
By symmetry, the point $\frac{\ensuremath{B}\xspace}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace}\1$ is the center of $\ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$.
The corresponding probability distribution, once rescaled, is exactly $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$.
Since $D$ is a divergence, the unique optimum over $\ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$, which is a convex combination of all vertices of $\ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$ with equal weights, trivially achieves an objective value of zero.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:strongduality}
Let $\alpha,\beta$ be probability measures with bounded support on $\mathbb{R}^d$ and let $\varepsilon>0$.
For bounded measurable $f,g:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ set the dual objective
\begin{align}\label{eq:generaldualobj}
\Phi_{f,g}(\alpha,\beta) \;=\; \left\langlef, \alpha \right\rangle + \left\langleg, \beta \right\rangle
  \;-\; \varepsilon \left\langle\alpha \otimes \beta, e^{(f\oplus g-c)/\varepsilon}-1 \right\rangle,
\end{align}
where $(f \oplus g)({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}) = f({\bm{y}})+g({\bm{z}})$ (we define $\otimes$ similarly).
Then the dual entropic OT problem is
\begin{align}\label{eq:strongdual}
\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\beta) \;=\; \sup_{(f,g)\in L^\infty(\alpha)\times L^\infty(\beta)} \Phi_{f,g}(\alpha,\beta),
\end{align}
and the supremum is attained at a pair $(f,g)$, unique up to $(f,g)\mapsto(f+a,g-a)$, $a\in\mathbb{R}$.
Moreover, primal and dual solutions are linked as follows: for a pair $(f,g)$, let $\pi$ be the
measure with density $e^{(f\oplus g-c)/\varepsilon}$ with respect to $\alpha\otimes\beta$. Then
$(f,g)$ is optimal for~\eqref{eq:strongdual} if and only if $\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)$, in which
case $\pi$ is the optimal plan.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:samplecomplexsink}
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be two distributions satisfying \cref{assum:unitsupport}.
Let $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu_n,\nu_n)$ be the entropic optimal transport cost between two
empirical distributions with $n$ mutually independent samples from $\mu$ and $\nu$ respectively.
Then, there exists $\kappa_0(\varepsilon)>0$ depending (exponentially) only on the regularization parameter $\varepsilon$
and such that
\begin{align}\label{eq:samplecomplexsink}
|\mathbb{E}[\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\hat{\mu}_n,\hat{\nu}_n)] - \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\nu)| \leq \frac{\kappa_0(\varepsilon)}{n}.
\end{align}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lemma:samplecomplexdiv}
Let $\mu$ satisfy \cref{assum:unitsupport} and let $\hat\mu_n$ be the empirical measure of
$n$ i.i.d.~samples from $\mu$. Set $M=\sup_{{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)}c({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})$,
so that $M\leq 1$ under \cref{assum:unitsupport}. Then
\begin{align}\label{eq:lemmabound}
  \mathbb{E}\big[\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\hat\mu_n)\big]
  \;\leq\;\frac1n\Big(\kappa_0(\varepsilon)+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\big(e^{M/\varepsilon}-1\big)\Big)
  \;\leq\;\frac{\kappa(\varepsilon)}{n},
\end{align}
with $\kappa(\varepsilon)\;:=\;\kappa_0(\varepsilon)+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\big(e^{1/\varepsilon}-1\big)$,
and $\kappa_0(\varepsilon)$ the constant of \cref{thm:samplecomplexsink}.
In particular, $\kappa$ depends only (exponentially) on $1/\varepsilon$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By the definition of the Sinkhorn divergence,
\begin{align}\label{eq:decompdiv}
  \mathbb{E}\big[\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\hat\mu_n)\big]
  =\mathbb{E}\big[\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\hat\mu_n)\big]
  -\tfrac12 \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu)
  -\tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}\big[\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\hat\mu_n,\hat\mu_n)\big].
\end{align}
We bound the first term from above and the third from below.
Throughout, $0\leq \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\beta)\leq M$ for $\alpha,\beta$ supported in
$\mathrm{supp}(\mu)$: the lower bound because $c\geq0$ and $\ensuremath{\mathrm{KL}}\geq0$, the upper one by
evaluating the primal objective with the product plan $\pi = \alpha\otimes\beta$.
Every $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace$ that depends on the sample has an empirical measure in at least one argument,
$\Phi_{f, g}$ in~\eqref{eq:strongdual} is then a continuous function of the positions of the atoms of this empirical measure.

We first bound the cross term.
Let $\beta$ be any probability measure supported on $\mathrm{supp}(\mu)$ and let $(f,g)$ be an
optimal pair of potentials for $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\beta)$ from \cref{thm:strongduality}.
Integrating the last term of~\eqref{eq:generaldualobj} in the second argument ${\bm{z}}$ first,
\begin{align*}
\Phi_{f,g}(\alpha,\beta) & = \left\langleh, \alpha \right\rangle + \left\langleg, \beta \right\rangle, \\
h({\bm{y}}) & := f({\bm{y}}) - \varepsilon \int \Big( e^{(f({\bm{y}})+g({\bm{z}})-c({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}))/\varepsilon} - 1 \Big) \mathrm{d}\beta({\bm{z}}),
\end{align*}
so that $\alpha \mapsto \Phi_{f,g}(\alpha,\beta)$ is affine.
Let $\hat\mu_n'$ be the empirical measure of a sample $X_1',\dots,X_n'\sim\mu$ and
let $\mathbb{E}'$ denote the expectation over it. Since
$\mathbb{E}'\left\langleh, \hat\mu_n' \right\rangle = \left\langleh, \mu \right\rangle$,
we have
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\beta) = \Phi_{f,g}(\mu,\beta)
  = \mathbb{E}'\big[\Phi_{f,g}(\hat\mu_n',\beta)\big]
  \;\leq\; \mathbb{E}'\big[\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\hat\mu_n',\beta)\big],
\end{align*}
the first equality by optimality of $(f,g)$, the second by affineness, and the inequality because
$f$ and $g$ are bounded, hence feasible solutions to~\eqref{eq:strongdual} for the pair
$(\hat\mu_n',\beta)$.
Running the same argument on a mixture $\alpha=(1-t)\alpha_0+t\alpha_1$ shows that
$\alpha\mapsto\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\beta)$ is convex, and the inequality above corresponds to Jensen's.
Applying it with $\beta=\hat\mu_n$, which is independent of $\hat\mu_n'$, and averaging,
\begin{align}\label{eq:crossbound}
  \mathbb{E}\big[\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\hat\mu_n)\big]
  \;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\big[\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\hat\mu_n',\hat\mu_n)\big]
  \;\leq\; \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu)+\frac{\kappa_0(\varepsilon)}{n},
\end{align}
the last inequality being \cref{thm:samplecomplexsink} applied with $\nu=\mu$: the two samples
are i.i.d.~from $\mu$, of equal size $n$, and mutually independent as required.

We can now bound the self-transport term.
Let $(f,g)$ be an optimal pair for $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu)$, again given by
\cref{thm:strongduality}; it is deterministic. Write $\Psi := e^{(f\oplus g-c)/\varepsilon}-1$.
By \cref{thm:strongduality} the measure with density $\Psi+1$ with respect to $\mu\otimes\mu$ has
first marginal $\mu$, that is, its density integrates to one in the second variable:
\begin{align}\label{eq:schrodinger}
\int e^{(f({\bm{y}})+g({\bm{z}})-c({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}))/\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\mu({\bm{z}}) = 1
\qquad\text{for $\mu$-a.e.\ }{\bm{y}} .
\end{align}
Integrating~\eqref{eq:schrodinger} against $\mu$ gives $\left\langle\mu\otimes\mu, \Psi \right\rangle=0$, hence
\begin{align}\label{eq:Smumu}
\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu) = \Phi_{f,g}(\mu,\mu) = \left\langlef+g, \mu \right\rangle.
\end{align}
Since $f$ and $g$ are bounded, they are admissible in~\eqref{eq:strongdual} for the pair
$(\hat\mu_n,\hat\mu_n)$, so
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\hat\mu_n,\hat\mu_n) \;\geq\; \Phi_{f,g}(\hat\mu_n,\hat\mu_n)
= \left\langlef+g, \hat\mu_n \right\rangle - \varepsilon \left\langle\hat\mu_n\otimes\hat\mu_n, \Psi \right\rangle.
\end{align*}
Since $X_i\sim\mu$ we have $\mathbb{E}\left\langlef+g, \hat\mu_n \right\rangle=\left\langlef+g, \mu \right\rangle$.
Separating diagonal from off-diagonal pairs,
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}\left\langle\hat\mu_n\otimes\hat\mu_n, \Psi \right\rangle & = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}\big[\Psi(X_i,X_j)\big]
   +\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\Psi(X_i,X_i)\big] \\
  & =\Big(1-\tfrac1n\Big)\underbrace{\left\langle\mu\otimes\mu, \Psi \right\rangle}_{=0}
   +\frac1n\Big(\mathbb{E}_\mu\big[e^{(f+g)/\varepsilon}\big]-1\Big),
\end{align*}
where we used that $X_i$ and $X_j$ are independent for $i\neq j$, and
$\Psi({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}})=e^{(f({\bm{y}})+g({\bm{y}}))/\varepsilon}-1$ since $c({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}})=0$. Combining with~\eqref{eq:Smumu},
\begin{align}\label{eq:selfbound}
  \mathbb{E}\big[\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\hat\mu_n,\hat\mu_n)\big]
  \;\geq\; \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu)-\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\Big(\mathbb{E}_\mu\big[e^{(f+g)/\varepsilon}\big]-1\Big).
\end{align}

                                                                                                                                
We can now bound the second quantity of the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:selfbound}.
Set $I_g=\int e^{g/\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\mu\in(0,\infty)$.
By~\eqref{eq:schrodinger} and $c\leq M$ on $\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\mu)$,
\begin{align*}
& 1 = \int e^{(f({\bm{y}})+g({\bm{z}})-c({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}))/\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\mu({\bm{z}})
  \;\geq\; e^{-M/\varepsilon}\,e^{f({\bm{y}})/\varepsilon} I_g , \\
  & e^{f({\bm{y}})/\varepsilon} \leq\;\frac{e^{M/\varepsilon}}{I_g} \qquad\text{for $\mu$-a.e.\ }{\bm{y}} 
\end{align*}
Integrating the remaining factor,
\begin{align}\label{eq:diagbound}
\mathbb{E}_\mu\big[e^{(f+g)/\varepsilon}\big]
= \int e^{f({\bm{y}})/\varepsilon}\,e^{g({\bm{y}})/\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\mu({\bm{y}})
\;\leq\; \frac{e^{M/\varepsilon}}{I_g}\int e^{g/\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\mu
\;=\; e^{M/\varepsilon},
\end{align}
and therefore $\mathbb{E}_\mu[e^{(f+g)/\varepsilon}]-1 \leq e^{M/\varepsilon}-1$.

Substituting~\eqref{eq:crossbound},~\eqref{eq:selfbound} and~\eqref{eq:diagbound} into~\eqref{eq:decompdiv}, the three terms $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu)$ cancel and
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}\big[\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\hat\mu_n)\big]
  \;\leq\;\frac{\kappa_0(\varepsilon)}{n}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2n}\big(e^{M/\varepsilon}-1\big),
\end{align*}
which is~\eqref{eq:lemmabound}; the final bound follows from $M\leq1$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lemma:selfotbounds}
The entropic self-transport is bounded by two times the variance: $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu) \leq 2\mathrm{Var}[\mu]$. Moreover if $\mu$ satisfying \cref{assum:unitsupport}, we have 
\begin{align*}
 \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu) \leq 2\mathrm{Var}[\mu]\leq 1.
\end{align*}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The entropic self-transport cost is bounded by the cost of any valid coupling;
we consider in particular the product $\mu \otimes \mu$
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\mu) \leq \left\langleC, \mu \otimes \mu \right\rangle + \ensuremath{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu \otimes \mu, \mu \otimes \mu).
\end{align*}
The $\ensuremath{\mathrm{KL}}$ divergence is zero when evaluated on the same two measures and the product coupling gives
\begin{align*}
\left\langleC, \mu \otimes \mu \right\rangle = \int \int \left\| {\bm{z}}-{\bm{y}} \right\|_2^2 d\mu({\bm{z}}) d\mu({\bm{y}}) = 2\mathrm{Var}[\mu].
\end{align*}
By assumption, we have $\left\| {\bm{z}}-{\bm{y}} \right\|_2^2 \leq 1$ pointwise almost everywhere, yielding the upper bound of one.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
For any two measures $\alpha,\beta$ respecting \cref{assum:unitsupport}, let $\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace$ be the optimal entropic coupling minimizing $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\beta)$,
we can derive
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\int f_{\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace} d\alpha - \int f_{\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace} d\beta &= \int (f_{\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace}({\bm{z}}) - f_{\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace}({\bm{y}})) d\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace({\bm{z}},{\bm{y}}) \label{eq:genericcouplingineq1} \\
& \leq L  \int \left\| {\bm{z}}-{\bm{y}} \right\|_2 d\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace({\bm{z}},{\bm{y}}) \label{eq:genericcouplingineq2} \\
& \leq L \sqrt{\int \left\| {\bm{z}}-{\bm{y}} \right\|_2^2 d\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace({\bm{z}},{\bm{y}}) } \label{eq:genericcouplingineq3} \\
& \leq L \sqrt{\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\beta)},\label{eq:genericcouplingineq4} \\
& = L \sqrt{\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\beta) + \frac12 \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\alpha) + \frac12 \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\beta,\beta)} \label{eq:genericcouplingineq5}\\
& \leq L \sqrt{\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\beta) + \mathrm{Var}[\alpha] + \mathrm{Var}[\beta] } \label{eq:genericcouplingineq6}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the first equality~\eqref{eq:genericcouplingineq1} uses that $\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace$ is a coupling,
~\eqref{eq:genericcouplingineq2} uses the Lipschitz bound,~\eqref{eq:genericcouplingineq3} applies Cauchy-Schwarz and
~\eqref{eq:genericcouplingineq4} uses the nonnegativity of $\ensuremath{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot, \alpha \otimes \beta)$,
~\eqref{eq:genericcouplingineq5} uses the definition of the divergence and~\eqref{eq:genericcouplingineq6} uses \cref{lemma:selfotbounds}.
Applying this inequality with $\hat{\mu}_{S^*}$ and $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$, we get the first bound on the empirical risk and the coreset risk. 
Applying the inequality a second time with $\mu$ and $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$ gives
\begin{align*}
R(\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace) - \hat{R}_n(\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace) & \leq L \sqrt{ \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\mu,\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) + \mathrm{Var}[\mu] + \mathrm{Var}[\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace] } \\
\mathbb{E}[R(\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace) - \hat{R}_n(\ensuremath{\bm{\theta}}\xspace)] & \leq L \sqrt{ \frac{\kappa(\varepsilon)}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} + \frac{2\ensuremath{n}\xspace-1}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} \mathrm{Var}[\mu] },
\end{align*}
where the second line takes the expectation over possible realizations of $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$, applies \cref{lemma:samplecomplexdiv} and Jensen's inequality to $\sqrt{\cdot}$,
along with the expectation of the empirical variance.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:wasscostunivariate}
The Wasserstein cost of the three possible selections are:
\begin{align*}
W(\alpha_C, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = \frac{2m \Delta^2}{2m+1}, \quad
W(\alpha_L, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = \frac{\Delta^2}{2}, \quad\text{and}\quad
W(\alpha_E, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = \frac{\Delta^2}{2m+1}
\end{align*}
with $\alpha_E$ being the configuration with minimum cost.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
To transport $\alpha_C$ to $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$, the same mass must be transported to both the left and right points.
The reverse is performed to transport $\alpha_E$ to $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$, with $\frac{1}{2} - \frac{m}{2m+1}$ being the excess mass at each extreme point that has to be transported to the origin. To transport $\alpha_L$, the left point has an excess of mass that needs to be transported of $\frac12 - \frac{m}{2m+1} = \frac{1}{2(2m+1)}$.
Because the ground cost is the Euclidean squared distance, the optimal plan transports this excess to the origin, and displaces to the right point enough mass from the origin to match its weight in $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$
which is $\frac{m}{2m+1}$.
The total transport cost is $\Delta^2 \left( \frac{1}{2(2m+1)} + \frac{m}{2m+1} \right) = \frac{\Delta^2}{2}$.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
The limit of the Sinkhorn objective values of the three configurations at $\varepsilon \rightarrow \infty$:
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}(\alpha_C, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = \frac{2m \Delta^2}{2m+1}, \quad
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}(\alpha_L, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = \frac{(6m+1)\Delta^2}{2 (2m+1)}, \quad
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}(\alpha_E, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = \frac{(4m+1)\Delta^2}{2m+1}.
\end{align*}
There exists a finite $\varepsilon$ for which $\alpha_C$ results in the smallest loss.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The Sinkhorn cost of a transport plan is the sum of the inner product with the distance matrix plus the regularization term, $\varepsilon$ multiplied by the KL divergence.
Driving $\varepsilon \rightarrow \infty$ forces the KL divergence to zero, meaning the transport plan becomes the outer product of the two input weight vectors,
and the cost becomes $\sum_{i\in [n_{\alpha}], j \in \{1,2,3\} } (x_i - y_j)^2 \alpha_i \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace_j$, with $x_i$ the $i$-th point of the $\alpha$ distribution and $y_j$ the $j$-th point of the dataset.
We can compute the corresponding expression for each of the configurations.
Since the Sinkhorn objective is continuous with respect to $\varepsilon$ and $\mathcal{S}_{+\infty}(\alpha_C, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace)$ is strictly smaller than the loss of the two other configurations,
there exists a finite $\bar\varepsilon$ above which the central configuration is always preferred.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lemma:sinklossexpralphaE}
The entropy losses of the extreme configuration can be expressed as
\begin{align}\label{eq:sinklossexpralphaE}
\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E,\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = W(\alpha_E,\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) + \frac{2m}{2m+1} \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E,\alpha_E).
\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
The coupling $\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace$ is a $2\times 3$ matrix with rows corresponding to the mass supported on $\{-\Delta,\Delta\}$
and columns corresponding to the mass supported on $\{-\Delta,0,\Delta\}$, with marginals $(\frac12,\frac12)$ and $(\frac{m}{2m+1}, \frac{1}{2m+1}, \frac{m}{2m+1})$.
Both marginals and the ground costs are symmetric around the origin, the optimal plan is also symmetric and can be expressed as:
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace^* = \begin{bmatrix*}
   a & b & d \\
   d & b & a
\end{bmatrix*}.
\end{align*}
The marginal constraint of the central column results in $2b = \frac{1}{2m+1} \Rightarrow b = \frac{1}{4m+2}$.
This value corresponds to the product of the probabilities of the marginals for the whole column, the corresponding term in the KL divergence is thus zero.
The contribution to the transport cost is the constant $2b\Delta^2 = \Delta^2 / (2m+1)$.

Using $p = \frac{m}{2m+1}$ for convenience and setting $d = p - a$, joint optimization over the whole transport plan reduces to determining the optimal $a$.
The weights of the marginal $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace$ on the remaining $2\times 2$ block are $(p, p)$, which is proportional to $\alpha_E$.
Using $\ensuremath{\mathrm{KL}}(p \mu, \nu) = \lambda \ensuremath{\mathrm{KL}}(p \mu, \nu)$ for $p > 0$, we can reformulate the objective as:
\begin{align*}
F(a) & = (1-2p) \Delta^2 + 2p\left[4\Delta^2\cdot\tfrac{d}{2p} + \varepsilon\,\ensuremath{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace,\alpha_E\otimes\alpha_E)\right] = (1-2p)\Delta^2 + 2p\cdot G\!\left(\tfrac{d}{2p}\right) \\
\text{where } G(s) &= 4\Delta^2 s + \varepsilon\,\ensuremath{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace(s),\alpha_E \otimes \alpha_E).
\end{align*}
$G(s)$ is exactly the Sinkhorn objective for $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E,\alpha_E)$ parameterized by its off-diagonal entry $s$.
Combining with the expression of the Wasserstein cost in \cref{prop:wasscostunivariate}, we can identify the expression~\eqref{eq:sinklossexpralphaE} and conclude.
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
The Sinkhorn divergence $D_\varepsilon$ leads to the extreme configuration being preferred for any value of $\varepsilon$.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
We first derive the expression of the symmetric optimal transport cost for any $\varepsilon$.
The measure $\alpha_C$ has a single atom, the transport cost and the KL divergence are both zero, $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_C, \alpha_C) = 0 \, \forall \varepsilon$.
The measures $\alpha_L$ and $\alpha_E$ are both supported on two atoms with uniform weights $(1/2, 1/2)$, the analysis is similar for both.

Defining the two-point measure as $1/2(\delta_a + \delta_b)$, setting $c = (a-b)^2$ and noting that the transport plan $\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace$ is symmetric,
it is fully parameterized by a single scalar $t$ such that $\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace_{aa} = \ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace_{bb} = \frac12 - t$, $\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace_{ab} = \ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace_{ba} = t$.
The expression $\left\langleC, \ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace \right\rangle$ reduces to $2tc$.
The product measure has weight $1/4$ on all entries, the KL divergence becomes
\begin{align*}
2(\frac12 - t)\log\left(\frac{\frac12 - t}{\frac14} \right) + 2t\log\left(\frac{t}{\frac14}\right)
\end{align*}
and we can derive the complete expression as a function of $t$, and the optimal $t^*$ from first-order optimality:
\begin{align*}
& \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha,\alpha) = \phi(t) = 2tc + \varepsilon \left((1-2t)\log(2-4t) + t2 \log(4t) \right)\\
& t^* = \frac{1}{2(1 + e^{c/\varepsilon})} \\
& \phi(t^*) = \frac{c}{1 + e^{c/\varepsilon}} + \frac{c e^{c/\varepsilon}}{1 + e^{c/\varepsilon}} + \varepsilon \log \frac{2}{1+ e^{c/\varepsilon}} \\
& \phi(t^*) = c + \varepsilon \log\left( \frac{2}{1 + e^{c/\varepsilon}} \right) = \varepsilon \log\left( \frac{2}{1 + e^{-c/\varepsilon}} \right).
\end{align*}
Specializing for $\alpha=\alpha_L$ with $c=\Delta^2$ and $\alpha_E$ with $c=4\Delta^2$, we obtain:
\begin{align}
& \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_L,\alpha_L) = \varepsilon \log \frac{2}{1 + e^{-\Delta^2 / \varepsilon}} \label{eq:sinkSelfLossLL} \\
& \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E,\alpha_E) = \varepsilon \log \frac{2}{1 + e^{-4\Delta^2 / \varepsilon}} \label{eq:sinkSelfLossEE}.
\end{align}

We can now compare the divergences pairwise between the different configurations, starting with the extreme and central ones.
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) - \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_C, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) &= \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) - \frac{1}{2} \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E, \alpha_E) - W(\alpha_C, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) \label{eq:divdiffEC1} \\
&= W(\alpha_E,\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) + (2p-\frac12) \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E,\alpha_E) - W(\alpha_C, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) \label{eq:divdiffEC2} \\
&= \frac{\Delta^2}{2m+1} + (2p-\frac12) \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E,\alpha_E) - \frac{2m\Delta^2}{2m+1} \label{eq:divdiffEC3} \\
&= \frac{2m-1}{2(2m+1)} \left(\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E,\alpha_E) - 2\Delta^2 \right) \label{eq:divdiffEC4} \\
&= \frac{2m-1}{2(2m+1)} \left( \varepsilon \log \frac{2}{1 + e^{-4\Delta^2 / \varepsilon}} - 2\Delta^2 \right) < 0 \label{eq:divdiffEC5}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where~\eqref{eq:divdiffEC2} uses \cref{lemma:sinklossexpralphaE},
~\eqref{eq:divdiffEC3} uses the Wasserstein cost expressions from \cref{prop:wasscostunivariate},
~\eqref{eq:divdiffEC4} rearranges the terms and~\eqref{eq:divdiffEC5} uses the expression~\eqref{eq:sinkSelfLossEE} for the symmetric Sinkhorn.
Negativity of the expression comes from $\varepsilon \log \frac{2}{1 + e^{-4\Delta^2 / \varepsilon}} - 2\Delta^2$ being valid for any finite $\varepsilon$.

We now establish the inequality for the extreme and left configurations.
The pair of dual potentials $(f, g)$ with $f= (\Delta^2,0)$,
$g = (-\Delta^2,0,\Delta^2)$ is optimal for the Wasserstein dual problem
$W(\alpha_L, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace)$, since it results in an objective $\frac{\Delta^2}{2}$
matching the primal and the potentials are feasible.                      

Weak duality for the Sinkhorn problem then yields
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_L,\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) \geq & \langle f,\alpha_L\rangle + \langle g, \ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace \rangle - \varepsilon\sum_{i,j}(\alpha_L)_i\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace_j\!\left(e^{(f_i + g_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon}-1\right) \\
   \geq & \frac{\Delta^2}{2} + \varepsilon p (1 - e^{-\frac{2\Delta^2}{\varepsilon}}).
\end{align*}

Using the expression~\eqref{eq:sinkSelfLossLL} for $\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_L,\alpha_L)$
and the fact that $\log\left( \frac{2}{1 + e^{- \Delta^2 / \varepsilon}} \right) \leq 1- e^{- \Delta^2 / \varepsilon} $,
we have
\begin{align*}
\frac12 \ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_L,\alpha_L) \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{2} (1 - e^{-\Delta^2 / \varepsilon}).
\end{align*}
Factoring $1-e^{-2\Delta^2/\varepsilon} = (1-e^{-\Delta^2/\varepsilon})(1+e^{-\Delta^2/\varepsilon})$,
we have
\begin{align*}
   \varepsilon p(1-e^{-2\Delta^2/\varepsilon}) - \tfrac{1}{2}\ensuremath{S_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_L,\alpha_L) \geq \varepsilon(1-e^{-\Delta^2/\varepsilon})\!\left[p(1+e^{-\Delta^2/\varepsilon})-\tfrac{1}{2}\right] \geq \varepsilon(1-e^{-\Delta^2/\varepsilon})\cdot\tfrac{2m-1}{2(2m+1)},
\end{align*}
where the last step uses $p(1+e^{-x}) - \tfrac{1}{2} \geq p - \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{2m-1}{2(2m+1)} > 0$.
Combining the different inequalities, we conclude
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_L, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) - \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_E, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) \geq \frac{2m-1}{2(2m+1)} \left(\Delta^2 + \varepsilon (1 - e^{-\Delta^2 / \varepsilon}) - \varepsilon \log \frac{2}{1 + e^{-4\Delta^2 / \varepsilon}} \right).
\end{align*}
Setting $h(t) = t + 1 - e^{-t} - \log\left(\frac{2}{1+e^{-4t}}\right)$, the expression is proportional to $\varepsilon h(\Delta^2 / \varepsilon)$.
We can establish $h(0) = 0$, $h'(0) = 0$, $h'(t) > 0 \, \forall x > 0$, so the expression is positive for $\Delta > 0$. For any value of $\varepsilon$, the divergence of the extreme configuration is lower than that of the left or central configurations, concluding the proof.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lemma:swapdirlip}
Let $C \in \mathbb{R}^{\ensuremath{n}\xspace \times \ensuremath{n}\xspace}$ be the symmetric cost matrix computed from the pairwise distances of points in the dataset
with $\left\| C \right\|_{\infty} = M$.
Let $\lambda \in (0,1)$ be a mixture parameter and define the mixed distribution as
\begin{align*}
& \alpha^{\lambda}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace} = (1 - \lambda) \ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace} + \lambda \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace
\quad\text{with weight vector }\quad {\bm{a}}^{\lambda} = \frac{1-\lambda}{\ensuremath{B}\xspace} \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace + \frac{\lambda}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} \1.
\end{align*}

Fix any $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace \in \ensuremath{\mathcal{V}_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace' \in \ensuremath{\mathrm{swap}}\xspace(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace)$ and set ${\bm{d}} = \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace' - \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace$.
Define the univariate function
\begin{align*}
\phi_{{\bm{d}},\lambda}(t) = \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace( \alpha^{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace + t {\bm{d}}), \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace ).
\end{align*}
There is a constant $\ensuremath{L^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}}\xspace$ depending on $\varepsilon$ and the mixture parameter $\lambda$ such that $\phi_{{\bm{d}},\lambda}''(t) \leq \ensuremath{L^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}}\xspace$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
We first show that the second derivative is a Hessian bilinear form and then that
the Hessian is bounded by the Lipschitz constant of the Sinkhorn potentials.

Let ${\bm{a}}^{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace + t {\bm{d}})$ be the vector of weights along the path from $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace$ to $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace'$, which is an affine function of $t$ with
$\partial {\bm{a}}^{\lambda} / \partial t = (1-\lambda) {\bm{d}} / \ensuremath{B}\xspace$,
and define $h: \ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace}}\xspace \to \mathbb{R}_+$, $h({\bm{a}}) = \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\sum_{i\in[\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} {\bm{a}}_i \delta_{{\bm{y}}_i}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace)$.

By the chain rule,
\begin{align*}
\phi_{{\bm{d}},\lambda}''(t) = \frac{(1 - \lambda)^2}{\ensuremath{B}\xspace^2} {\bm{d}}^\top \nabla^2 h({\bm{a}}^{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace + t {\bm{d}})) {\bm{d}}.
\end{align*}

We recall from Equation~\eqref{eq:sinkdivgradient} that the gradient of $h$ w.r.t.~the weights is given by $\nabla h({\bm{a}}) = \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace^*_{{\bm{a}}} - \ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace^*_{{\bm{a}}}$.

Define the Sinkhorn marginal Lipschitz constant
\begin{align*}
L_\varepsilon^{\mathrm{f,p}}(\lambda_0) := \sup_{\substack{{\bm{a}}, {\bm{a}}' \in \ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace}}\xspace\\ \min_k {\bm{a}}_k, {\bm{a}}'_k \geq \lambda_0}}\,\frac{\|\ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace^\star_{{\bm{a}}} - \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace^\star_{{\bm{a}}'} \|_\infty + \|\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace^\star_{{\bm{a}}} - \ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace^\star_{{\bm{a}}'} \|_\infty}{\|{\bm{a}} - {\bm{a}}'\|_1},
\end{align*}
which is well-defined and finite, from the implicit function theorem on the system of equations defining the fixed point of the Sinkhorn algorithm see, e.g., in \citet{nutz2023stability,deligiannidis2024quantitative,carlier2024displacement} for results on the stability of Sinkhorn potentials w.r.t.~their marginals up to normalization.
This quantity depends on $\exp(M/\varepsilon)$ which is not problematic in our setup considering large regularization and a small diameter.

The swap direction has only two nonzero entries, thus
\begin{align*}
{\bm{d}}^\top \nabla^2 h({\bm{a}}) d & = H_{ii} + H_{jj} - 2H_{ij} \\
|{\bm{d}}^\top \nabla^2 h({\bm{a}}) {\bm{d}}| & \leq 4 \max_{(i,j) \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace] \times [\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} |H_{ij}|
\end{align*}
with $H$ the Hessian of $h$ at ${\bm{a}}$.
Each entry is bounded in absolute value by the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla h$,
and using $\lambda_0 = \lambda / \ensuremath{n}\xspace$, the minimum weight from mixture, we have
\begin{align*}
{\bm{d}}^\top \nabla^2 h({\bm{a}}) d &\leq 4 L_\varepsilon^{\mathrm{f,p}}(\lambda / \ensuremath{n}\xspace) \\
\phi_{{\bm{d}},\lambda}''(t) & \leq \ensuremath{L^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}}\xspace = \frac{4 (1-\lambda)^2 L_\varepsilon^{\mathrm{f,p}}(\lambda / \ensuremath{n}\xspace)}{\ensuremath{B}\xspace^2}.
\end{align*}

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lemma:potentialboundatvtx}
Let $C \in \mathbb{R}^{\ensuremath{n}\xspace\times \ensuremath{n}\xspace}$ be the symmetric cost matrix with $\left\| C \right\|_{\infty} \leq M$,
then the following bound on the optimal potentials holds at any $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace$ in the relative interior of $\ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace$, under the normalization $\sum_{i} \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_i = 0$:
\begin{align}\label{eq:potentialinfbound}
\left\| \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace - \ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace \right\|_{\infty} \leq 2M.
\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Using the expression of the optimal plan $\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace$ of the EOT problem from the potentials:
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace_{ij} = e^{(\ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_i + \ensuremath{\mathbf{g}}\xspace_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon} \frac{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_i}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace \ensuremath{B}\xspace} \; \forall i \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace], j \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace],
\end{align*}
the fact that $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_i > 0 \, \forall i \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace]$,
and the marginal constraint $\sum_{j\in[\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} \ensuremath{\bm{\pi}}\xspace_{ij} = \frac{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_i}{\ensuremath{B}\xspace}$
results in
\begin{align*}
& \sum_{j\in[\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} e^{(\ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_i + \ensuremath{\mathbf{g}}\xspace_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon} \frac{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_i}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace \ensuremath{B}\xspace} = \frac{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace_i}{\ensuremath{B}\xspace} \quad \forall i \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace] \\
& e^{\ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_i / \varepsilon} \sum_{j\in[\ensuremath{n}\xspace]} e^{(\ensuremath{\mathbf{g}}\xspace_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon} = \ensuremath{n}\xspace \quad \forall i \in [\ensuremath{n}\xspace] \\
& \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_i - \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_{i'} = -\varepsilon \log \frac{\sum_j e^{(\ensuremath{\mathbf{g}}\xspace_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon}}{\sum_j e^{(\ensuremath{\mathbf{g}}\xspace_j - C_{i'j})/\varepsilon}} \\
& \max_{i,i'} |\ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_{i'} - \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_{i} | \leq M
\end{align*}
where the last inequality uses cancellation of the $e^{\ensuremath{\mathbf{g}}\xspace_j / \varepsilon}$ terms and $C_{ij} \leq M$.
Under the normalization $\sum_i \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_i = 0$ and
applying the same derivation to the self-transport problem for $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace$, we obtain the desired inequality~\eqref{eq:potentialinfbound}

The swap descent algorithm terminates when the new iterate was already visited in a previous iteration, creating a cycle in the vertex graph of the hypersimplex.
On this cycle, let $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}$ be the minimizer of the function and $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c+1)}$ be its immediate successor in the cycle,
and set ${\bm{d}}_c = \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c+1)} - \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}$.
We will denote $M = \left\| C \right\|_{\infty}$ throughout the proof.

Define $f_{\lambda}: \ensuremath{\Delta_{\ensuremath{n}\xspace,\ensuremath{B}\xspace}}\xspace \to \mathbb{R}_+$, $f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace) = \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace( \alpha_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace), \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace )$,
which is convex, and note that $f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace + t {\bm{d}}) = \phi_{{\bm{d}},\lambda}(t)$ if ${\bm{d}}$ is a swap direction, with $\phi_{{\bm{d}},\lambda}$ defined as in \cref{lemma:swapdirlip}.
The Lipschitz smoothness along ${\bm{d}}_c$ yields
\begin{align*}
f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c+1)}) & \leq f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}) + \left\langle\nabla f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}), {\bm{d}}_c \right\rangle + \frac{\ensuremath{L^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}}\xspace}{2}.
\end{align*}
Using the cycle-optimality of $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}$, and re-arranging,
\begin{align*}
& 0 \leq f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c+1)}) - f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}) \leq \left\langle\nabla f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}), {\bm{d}}_c \right\rangle + \frac{\ensuremath{L^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}}\xspace}{2} \\
& \left\langle\nabla f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}), {\bm{d}}_c \right\rangle \geq -\frac{\ensuremath{L^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}}\xspace}{2}.
\end{align*}
The last inequality also holds for any swap direction by optimality of ${\bm{d}}_c$.
By convexity, we have
\begin{align*}
   f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}) - f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^*) \leq -\left\langle\nabla f_\lambda(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}), \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^* - \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)} \right\rangle
\end{align*}
Decompose $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^*$ into $k^* \leq \ensuremath{B}\xspace$ swaps from $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}$:
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^* - \ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)} = \sum_{l=1}^{k^*} {\bm{d}}_l
\end{align*}
with each ${\bm{d}}_l$ corresponding to a single swap from the support of $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}$ to the support of $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^*$.
We have
\begin{align}\label{eq:suboptimalityswap}
f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}) - f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^*) \leq \sum_{l=1}^{k^*} -\left\langle\nabla f_{\lambda}(\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^{(c)}), {\bm{d}}_l \right\rangle \leq k^* \frac{\ensuremath{L^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}}\xspace}{2}.
\end{align}
Conservatively, we can bound $k^* \leq \ensuremath{B}\xspace$ for an instance-independent guarantee.

Finally, we bound the bias introduced by the mixture parameter $\lambda$.
We start by noting that $\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}^\lambda, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace)$ is a convex decreasing function of lambda on $[0,1]$.
Using this fact with the expression of the gradient of the Sinkhorn divergence
with respect to the weights, and the continuity of
$\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\cdot,\ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace)$,
\begin{align*}
\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha^{\lambda}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) - \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha^{0}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) = & \lim_{\tau \to 0} \lambda \int_{\tau}^1 \left\langle\ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_t - \ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace_t , \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace - \alpha^{0}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace} \right\rangle dt \\
|\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha^{\lambda}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) - \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha^{0}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) | \leq & \lim_{\tau \to 0} \lambda \left\| \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace - \alpha^{\tau}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace} \right\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \left\| \ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_\tau - \ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace_\tau  \right\|_{\infty} \\
|\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha^{\lambda}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) - \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha^{0}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) | \leq & 2\lambda M \left\| \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace - \alpha^{0}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace} \right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\end{align*}
with $\ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace_t$, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace_t$, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{f}}\xspace$, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}\xspace$ the potentials of the Sinkhorn divergence evaluated at $\alpha^{t}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}$,
$\alpha^{0}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}$ respectively.
The first step uses the fact that the divergence is convex and monotonically decreasing in $t$, such that its derivative w.r.t.~and attains its greatest absolute value at $t=\tau$, together with Hölder's inequality.
Since $\alpha^{0}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}$ is a measure supported on $\ensuremath{B}\xspace$ points,
$\left\| \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace - \alpha^0_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace} \right\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = 2 \left(1 - \frac{\ensuremath{B}\xspace}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} \right)$.
We can therefore bound the mixing bias by
\begin{align}\label{eq:biascontrollambda}
|\ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha^{\lambda}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) - \ensuremath{D_{\varepsilon}}\xspace(\alpha^{0}_{\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace}, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{\hat{\mu}}\xspace^{\ensuremath{\mathcal{D}}\xspace}}\xspace) | \leq & 4\lambda M \left(1 - \frac{\ensuremath{B}\xspace}{\ensuremath{n}\xspace} \right).
                                                              
\end{align}
We can combine inequality~\eqref{eq:suboptimalityswap} with the control of the mixture bias
~\eqref{eq:biascontrollambda} applied at $\bar\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace$, together with the fact
that the bias is non-positive at $\ensuremath{\mathbf{x}}\xspace^*$ and can be dropped from the inequality,
providing the desired result~\eqref{eq:swapsuboptimality}.
