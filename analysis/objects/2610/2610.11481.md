<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For Bernoulli jobs on $m$ identical machines and every $\eta\in(0,1]$, a policy with expected sum of completion times at most $(1+\eta)\mathrm{OPT}$ can be computed in time $2^{O(\eta^{-2}\log^2(N/\eta))}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\mathcal L)$.
Each decision of the policy is a table lookup.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:fewsizes}
If the jobs have $n$ distinct sizes, a $(1+\eta)$-approximation can be computed in time $(Nm)^{O(n/\eta)}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\mathcal L)$ for fixed $n$ and~$\eta$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:mapprox}
A simple adaptive priority rule runs in polynomial time and has expected cost at most $\min\{m,1+\sum_jq_j\}\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:hardintro}
For Bernoulli jobs on two machines, computing $\mathrm{OPT}$, deciding whether $\mathrm{OPT}\le x$, and computing the expected cost of the shortest-expected-processing-time rule (SEPT) are \#P-hard.
On the other hand, the expected cost of any given non-idling policy can be approximated within a factor $1\pm\rho$ with probability $1-\delta$ from $O(N\rho^{-2}\log(2/\delta))$ simulations.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:barriersintro}
There are instances on two machines in which every policy that fixes the job order in advance costs $\Omega(\log N)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:nonidle}
From every state, some optimal policy is deterministic and non-idling.
In particular, $\mathrm{OPT}$ is a minimum over finitely many decision trees.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $V(\ell,U)$ be the infimum of the cost from a state with release vector $\ell$ and remaining jobs~$U$, with $V(\ell,\emptyset)=0$.
If $\ell\le\ell'$ coordinatewise, then $V(\ell,U)\le V(\ell',U)$: a policy for $\ell'$ can be run from $\ell$ with the same start times, using the machine of rank~$i$ in $\ell$ for the machine of rank~$i$ in~$\ell'$.
We show by induction on $|U|$ that $V(\ell,U)$ is attained by a deterministic non-idling policy.
Consider any policy and fix the outcome of its coins.
Its first decision starts a job $x$ at some time $s\ge\ell_1$ on a machine that is free at time~$s$.
Afterwards its release vector is obtained from the sorted vector of the values $\max\{\ell_i,s\}$, whose minimum entry is~$s$, by replacing that minimum entry by $s+X_x$.
Starting $x$ at time $\ell_1$ instead, on a machine with release time $\ell_1$, gives $x$ a completion time that is not larger, and the release vector is then obtained from $\ell$ by replacing its minimum entry $\ell_1$ by $\ell_1+X_x$.
We have $\ell_i\le\max\{\ell_i,s\}$ for every $i$ and $\ell_1+X_x\le s+X_x$.
Replacing the minimum entries of two coordinatewise dominated sorted vectors by values $x'\le y'$ and sorting again preserves the domination.
So the release vector after the shifted start is dominated by the one after the original start.
By monotonicity and induction, continuing with a deterministic non-idling optimal policy is no worse than the original continuation.
Averaging over the coins and choosing the best first job proves the claim for $U$.
There are finitely many deterministic non-idling decision trees, which gives the second statement.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:basic}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $P\le\mathrm{OPT}$.
\item $\mathrm{OPT}\ge\mathrm{OPT}(U)+P(J\setminus U)$ for every set $U$ of jobs, where $J$ is the set of all jobs.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Part~(a) holds because $C_j\ge X_j$ for every job.
For part~(b), let $\Pi$ be an optimal policy for~$J$.
Define a policy $\Pi_U$ for the jobs in~$U$ as follows.
It simulates~$\Pi$; whenever $\Pi$ starts a job $j\notin U$ on some machine, $\Pi_U$ draws an independent virtual outcome distributed as $X_j$ and keeps that machine idle for the virtual processing time.
This is a valid policy for~$U$, and the joint distribution of the start times and outcomes of the jobs in~$U$ is the same under~$\Pi_U$ as under~$\Pi$.
Hence $\mathrm{OPT}(U)\le\sum_{j\in U}\mathbb{E}[C^\Pi_j]$.
The jobs outside~$U$ contribute $\sum_{j\notin U}\mathbb{E}[C^\Pi_j]\ge P(J\setminus U)$, and adding the two bounds gives~(b).
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:freetime}
Let all machines be free at time~$0$, and consider a realization in which every job starts at the earliest time at which some machine is free.
This holds on every realization of a non-idling policy.
Then for every $0\le k<N$,
\[
  F_k\;\le\;W_{m-1}(S_k)\;\le\;m\,F_k.
\]
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
On such a realization every machine is busy without gaps from time~$0$ until it becomes free.
So after $k$ starts, the time at which machine~$i$ becomes free equals its load $L_i$, the total realized processing time of the jobs started on it, and $F_k=f:=\min_iL_i$.

The $m-1$ jobs with the largest realized processing times in $S_k$ lie on at most $m-1$ machines.
Some machine holds none of them, so its load is at most $W_{m-1}(S_k)$, and $f$ is at most that load.

For the second inequality, consider a machine with $L_i>f$ and let $J_i$ be the last job started on it.
The job $J_i$ is positive.
It started at the earliest free time of that moment, which is at most~$f$, since the earliest free time never decreases.
Hence $L_i-f\le X_{J_i}$.
At most $m-1$ machines have $L_i>f$, and their last jobs are distinct.
So the total realized processing time of $S_k$ is $\sum_iL_i\le mf+\sum_{i:L_i>f}X_{J_i}$, which is at most $mf$ plus the sum of the $m-1$ largest realized processing times in $S_k$.
This gives $W_{m-1}(S_k)\le mf$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:qorder}
From every state, some optimal policy is deterministic, non-idling, and respects the class orders.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We use induction on the number of remaining jobs; the case of at most one job is trivial.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:nonidle}, take a deterministic non-idling optimal policy $T$, let $x$ be its first job, and let $t_0$ be its start time, the minimum entry of the release vector.
Let $a$ be the first remaining job of the class of $x$, so that $q_a\le q_x$, and let $p$ be their common size.
It suffices to find an optimal non-idling policy that starts $a$ first.
Indeed, after the outcome of $a$ is revealed we can then replace the continuation by a deterministic non-idling optimal policy for the resulting state that respects the class orders, which exists by induction.
If $a=x$ there is nothing to do, and if $q_a=q_x$, exchanging the names of $a$ and $x$ gives such a policy.

So let $q_a<q_x$.
The policy $T$ starts $a$ on every path.
Let $T_0$ and $T_1$ be its continuations after $x$ is zero and positive, and let $\sigma_0$ and $\sigma_1$ be the times at which they start~$a$.
These random times do not depend on the outcome of $a$, and $\sigma_0,\sigma_1\ge t_0$ since the minimum release time never decreases.
Let $\vartheta=(q_x-q_a)/((1-q_a)q_x)\in(0,1]$.
The policy $T'$ starts $a$ at time~$t_0$ on the machine used by~$T$ for~$x$.
If $a$ is positive, it continues as $T_1$, with $x$ started in place of~$a$.
If $a$ is zero, then with probability $1-\vartheta$ it continues as $T_0$, with $x$ started in place of~$a$.
With probability $\vartheta$ it instead starts $x$ at time $t_0$ and continues as $T_0$ or $T_1$, according to the outcome of $x$, treating the node of $a$ as a zero job.

The policy $T'$ is non-idling, and it continues as a copy of $T_1$ with probability $q_a+(1-q_a)\vartheta q_x=q_x$ and as a copy of $T_0$ with probability $1-q_x$.
Each copy is entered with the same release vector as in~$T$, because $a$ and $x$ have the same size.
In a copy of $T_1$, the job run at the node of $a$ is positive with probability $q_aq_x/q_x=q_a$.
In a copy of $T_0$, it is positive with probability $(1-q_a)(1-\vartheta)q_x/(1-q_x)=q_a$.
In both cases this outcome is independent of all other jobs.
So every job other than $a$ and $x$ has the same expected completion time in $T$ and~$T'$.
For $a$ and $x$, a direct computation from the branch probabilities above gives
\[
  \mathbb{E}_T[C_a+C_x]-\mathbb{E}_{T'}[C_a+C_x]\;=\;\frac{(1-q_x)(q_x-q_a)}{q_x}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\sigma_0]-t_0\bigr)+(q_x-q_a)\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\sigma_1]-t_0\bigr)\;\ge\;0,
\]
where the terms with the factor $p$ cancel.
So $T'$ is an optimal non-idling policy that starts $a$ first.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:reduction}
If $\operatorname{cost}(A)\le\alpha\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(M)$ for some $\alpha\ge1$, then $\operatorname{cost}(\Pi_A)\le(\alpha+2\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$.
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $E$ be the event that some large job is positive.

\emph{The event $E$.}
On~$E$, all decisions before the first positive large job occur at time~$0$, and the rest is list scheduling.
So on every realization in~$E$, every job starts at the earliest time at which some machine is free, and Lemma~\ref{lem:freetime} bounds every start time by $W_{m-1}(S_k)\le W_1(S_k)\le W_1(J)$, using $m\ge2$ and the monotonicity of~$W_r$.
If $\ell$ is a positive large job, then $W_1(J)$ is at most the total realized processing time minus~$X_\ell$.
Summing over all large jobs therefore gives, on every realization,
\[
  \mathbf 1_E\,W_1(J)\;\le\;\sum_{\ell\in L}\mathbf 1[X_\ell>0]\sum_{i\ne\ell}X_i .
\]
By independence, $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf 1[X_\ell>0]X_i]=q_\ell\mu_i$ for $i\ne\ell$.
Moreover $\sum_{\ell\in L}q_\ell\le\sum_{\ell\in L}\mu_\ell/T\le P/T$.
Hence $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf 1_EW_1(J)]\le P^2/T$, and
\[
  \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbf 1_E\sum_jS_j\Bigr]\;\le\;N\cdot\frac{P^2}{T}\;=\;\varepsilon P .
\]

\emph{The complement of~$E$.}
On the complement, every large job starts at time~$0$.
The small jobs have total realized processing time at most $|Q|\sigma\le D$, so every machine has completed its small jobs by time~$D$, and all machines are free when $A$ starts.
Every medium job starts at time $D$ plus its start time under~$A$.
The event~$E$ depends only on the large jobs, so conditioning on its complement does not change the distribution of the other outcomes.
Therefore
\[
  \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbf 1_{\overline E}\sum_{j\notin L}S_j\Bigr]
  \;\le\;(|Q|+|M|)\,D+\sum_{j\in M}\mathbb{E}\bigl[S^A_j\bigr]
  \;\le\;\varepsilon P+\operatorname{cost}(A)-P(M).
\]

\emph{Combining.}
Adding the two bounds,
\[
  \operatorname{cost}(\Pi_A)=P+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_jS_j\Bigr]
  \;\le\;P(L)+P(Q)+\operatorname{cost}(A)+2\varepsilon P
  \;\le\;P(L\cup Q)+\alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}(M)+2\varepsilon P .
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}(b) with $U=M$ we have $\mathrm{OPT}(M)+P(L\cup Q)\le\mathrm{OPT}$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}(a) we have $P\le\mathrm{OPT}$.
Since $\alpha\ge1$, the right-hand side is at most $\alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}+2\varepsilon\,\mathrm{OPT}$.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:reuse}
For every size $p$ and every $s\ge0$:
(a) $s+p\le R_p(s)<s+\gamma p$;
(b) $R_p$ is non-decreasing;
(c) $R_p(s)$ is an integer multiple of $g_p$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We have $g_p\lceil s/g_p\rceil\in[s,s+g_p)$ and $Kg_p=p$, so $R_p(s)\in[s+p,s+p+g_p)=[s+p,s+\gamma p)$.
Parts (b) and (c) are immediate.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:stretch}
There is a grid-non-idling reuse-rule policy $\Pi$ that respects the class orders and satisfies $S^\Pi_j\le\gamma\,S^{\Pi^*}_j$ for every job $j$ and every realization, where $\Pi^*$ is a deterministic optimal policy as in Lemma~\ref{lem:qorder} and both policies see the same outcomes.
In particular, $\operatorname{cost}(\Pi)\le P+\gamma\,(\mathrm{OPT}-P)$.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Figure~\ref{fig:stretch} illustrates the proof in one realization.

\emph{Stretching.}
Let $\Pi_\gamma$ start every job on the same machine as~$\Pi^*$ at $\gamma$ times its start time in~$\Pi^*$.
At time $\gamma t$, the policy $\Pi_\gamma$ has observed exactly the outcomes that $\Pi^*$ has observed at time~$t$, so it can make the same decisions.
Suppose a job $i$ of size $p$ is positive, starts at time $s$ on some machine in~$\Pi^*$, and the next job on that machine starts at time $s'\ge s+p$.
In $\Pi_\gamma$ the machine is released at $R_p(\gamma s)<\gamma s+\gamma p\le\gamma s'$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:reuse}(a).
After a zero job, the next start on the machine is at $\gamma s'\ge\gamma s$.
So $\Pi_\gamma$ is a reuse-rule policy, possibly with idle time, with $S^{\Pi_\gamma}_j=\gamma S^{\Pi^*}_j$.

\emph{Compressing.}
The policy $\Pi^*$ makes its decisions in non-decreasing order of start time, so $\Pi_\gamma$ starts the jobs in the same order.
The policy~$\Pi$ starts the jobs in this order, each at its own current minimum release time and on a machine attaining it.
This is possible online: the $k$-th job of the order is determined by the outcomes of the first $k-1$ jobs, which $\Pi$ has already observed.
The order within each size class is that of~$\Pi^*$, so $\Pi$ respects the class orders.

For vectors $b\in\mathbb R^m$ and $\tau\ge0$, let $b\vee\tau$ be the vector $(\max\{b_1,\tau\},\dots,\max\{b_m,\tau\})$ sorted increasingly.
Let $a$ and $b$ be the sorted release vectors of $\Pi$ and $\Pi_\gamma$ just before the $k$-th start, and let $\tau_k$ be the start time of the $k$-th job in~$\Pi_\gamma$.
We show by induction that $a\le b\vee\tau_k$ coordinatewise.
Initially $a=b=0$.
The policy $\Pi_\gamma$ starts the $k$-th job at~$\tau_k$ on a machine released by~$\tau_k$, so the corresponding entry of $b\vee\tau_k$ equals $\tau_k$, the minimum of $b\vee\tau_k$.
Hence $\Pi$ starts the job at time $s_k=a_1\le\tau_k$.
If the job is zero, neither release vector changes.
If it is positive with size $p$, the new release vector of $\Pi$ is obtained from $a$ by replacing its minimum entry by $R_p(s_k)$ and sorting.
Likewise, since $R_p(\tau_k)\ge\tau_k$, the vector $b'\vee\tau_k$ for the new release vector $b'$ of $\Pi_\gamma$ is obtained from $b\vee\tau_k$ by replacing the entry $\tau_k$ of the used machine by $R_p(\tau_k)$ and sorting.
Since $R_p(s_k)\le R_p(\tau_k)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:reuse}(b), the new vectors satisfy the same coordinatewise domination: replacing the minimum entries of two dominated sorted vectors by values $x\le y$ and sorting again preserves domination.
Finally $\tau_{k+1}\ge\tau_k$, so $b'\vee\tau_{k+1}\ge b'\vee\tau_k$ coordinatewise.
This completes the induction, and $S^\Pi_j=s_k\le\tau_k=\gamma S^{\Pi^*}_j$ for the $k$-th job~$j$.

Summing over the jobs, $\operatorname{cost}(\Pi)=P+\sum_j\mathbb{E}[S^\Pi_j]\le P+\gamma\sum_j\mathbb{E}[S^{\Pi^*}_j]=P+\gamma(\mathrm{OPT}-P)$.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:bellman}
$V\bigl((0,\dots,0),(N_1,\dots,N_n)\bigr)$ is the minimum cost of a grid-non-idling reuse-rule policy that respects the class orders.
A policy attaining it chooses, in each state, a class $c$ attaining the minimum and starts~$j_c(\nu)$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
In such a policy, the next job starts at time $\ell_1$, so the expected completion time of the job started in state $(\ell,\nu)$ is $\ell_1+q_jp_c$.
After the start, the state is $(\ell,\nu-e_c)$ if the job is zero and $(\ell^{(c)},\nu-e_c)$ if it is positive.
The outcomes of the remaining jobs are independent of the past, the costs add up, and the machines are identical.
So the minimum expected cost of the remaining jobs depends only on the state and satisfies the recursion.
The recursion is acyclic because $\sum_c\nu_c$ decreases.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:times}
All release times that occur in the dynamic program lie in a set of size at most $\binom{(K+1)N+n-1}{n}$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Write $g_c=g_{p_c}$ and $R_c=R_{p_c}$.
A release time is either $0$ or equal to $R_c(t)$ for a decision time $t$ at which a positive job of class~$c$ starts.
The time $t$ is itself $0$ or an earlier release time.
Following this chain backwards shows that every release time has the form $v=R_{c_k}\circ\dots\circ R_{c_1}(0)$, where $k\le N$ because the maps correspond to distinct positive jobs.
We encode such a composition by a vector in $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^n$.

Split the sequence $c_1,\dots,c_k$ into blocks.
The first block ends at the last occurrence of the class with the largest grid width in the sequence, and the remaining suffix is split in the same way.
The largest grid widths of successive blocks strictly decrease.
Consider a block of length~$r$ whose largest grid width is $g$, which maps the value $u$ to the value~$v$.
Each map $R_{c'}$ in the block has $g_{c'}\le g$ and increases its argument by an amount in $[Kg_{c'},(K+1)g_{c'})$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:reuse}(a).
Hence $Kg\le v-u<(K+1)rg$.
The last map of the block has grid width~$g$, so $v$ is a multiple of~$g$, and we can write $v=g(\lceil u/g\rceil+a)$ for an integer~$a$.
Since $g\lceil u/g\rceil\in[u,u+g)$, the two bounds on $v-u$ give $K\le a\le(K+1)r-1$.

The encoding records the value~$a$ of each block at the coordinate of the block's largest class, and $0$ at every other coordinate.
Processing the nonzero coordinates in decreasing order of grid width, starting from $u=0$, reconstructs~$v$.
With $\kappa\ge1$ blocks, the coordinates sum to at most $(K+1)k-\kappa\le(K+1)N-1$.
The number of vectors in $\mathbb Z^n_{\ge0}$ with coordinate sum at most $(K+1)N-1$ is $\binom{(K+1)N+n-1}{n}$.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:profiles}
For every decision time $t$, the number of sorted release vectors $\ell$ with $\ell_1=t$ that occur in the dynamic program is at most $\binom{m+d-1}{d-1}$, where $d=n(K+1)$.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Consider a machine whose last positive job belongs to class~$c$ and started at time $s\le t$.
Its release time satisfies $t\le\ell_i=R_c(s)\le R_c(t)=g_c(\lceil t/g_c\rceil+K)$, and $\ell_i$ is a multiple of~$g_c$.
Hence $\ell_i\in\{g_c(\lceil t/g_c\rceil+a):0\le a\le K\}$.
A machine without a positive job has release time~$0$, which happens only if $t=0$ and is then contained in this set with $a=0$.
So every entry of~$\ell$ takes one of at most $d$ values, and a sorted vector of $m$ such entries is a multiset of size~$m$ drawn from $d$ values.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:dp}
For every instance with $n$ size classes and every integer $K\ge1$, a policy $\Pi$ with
\[
  \operatorname{cost}(\Pi)\le P+\Bigl(1+\frac1K\Bigr)(\mathrm{OPT}-P)
\]
can be computed in time
\[
  \binom{(K+1)N+n-1}{n}\binom{m+n(K+1)-1}{n(K+1)-1}\prod_{c=1}^n(N_c+1)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(N,m,\mathcal L),
\]
where $\mathcal L$ is the input length.
Each decision of $\Pi$ is then a table lookup.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:stretch}, some policy in the class optimized by the dynamic program has cost at most $P+\gamma(\mathrm{OPT}-P)$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:bellman}, the dynamic program finds a policy in this class of minimum cost.
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:times} and~\ref{lem:profiles}, the number of states is at most the product of the three factors.
Each transition takes $O(n+m)$ arithmetic operations.
All release times are multiples of $1/(K\cdot\mathrm{lcm})$, where $\mathrm{lcm}$ is the least common multiple of the size denominators.
All values of $V$ are rationals whose denominators divide the product of $K\cdot\mathrm{lcm}$ and the denominators of all~$q_j$.
So the numbers have polynomial bit length.

\label{cor:fewsizes}

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:greedyopt}
For every multiset $A$ and every set $R$ of remaining jobs, the greedy rule minimizes $\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^s(s-i)D'_i]$ over all adaptive orders.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
We use induction on $s=|R|$; the case $s\le1$ is trivial.
The objective equals $(s-1)D'_1$ plus the future part of the objective from the state after the first decision, which has $s-1$ remaining jobs.
So by induction, every order may continue greedily after its first decision without becoming worse.
Let $a$ be the job chosen by the greedy rule, let $\theta=\min A$, and write $\mu_b=q_bp_b$.
Take an optimal order that is greedy after its first decision, and suppose it starts with a job $j\ne a$.
We construct an order that starts with~$a$ and is no worse.

\emph{Case 1: $p_a\le\theta$.}
Then $d_a(\theta')=\mu_a$ for every $\theta'\ge\theta$, while $d_b(\theta')$ is non-decreasing in $\theta'$ for every other job~$b$.
The threshold never decreases, so after $j$ the job $a$ is still the greedy choice.
The optimal order thus starts with $j,a$.
Swap them to $a,j$.
In both orders, $a$ contributes the increment $X_a$, because $p_a\le\theta$ is at most the threshold at any later time, and $j$ contributes $\min\{X_j,\theta\}$, because $a$ does not change~$A$.
Both orders end in the same state, and the swap changes the expected objective by $\mu_a-d_j(\theta)\le0$.

\emph{Case 2: $p_a>\theta$ and $p_j\le\theta$.}
Now $j$ does not change~$A$, so after $j$ the job~$a$ is again the greedy choice.
Swapping $j,a$ to $a,j$ leads to the same state and changes the expected objective by $q_a\theta-\mu_j=d_a(\theta)-d_j(\theta)\le0$.

\emph{Case 3: $p_a>\theta$ and $p_j>\theta$.}
First, $q_a=\min_bq_b$ and $q_a\theta\le\mu_b$ for every remaining job~$b$.
If $\theta>0$, a job $b$ with $q_b<q_a$ would have $d_b(\theta)\le q_b\theta<q_a\theta=d_a(\theta)$, contradicting the choice of~$a$; if $\theta=0$, the second key selects a job of minimum~$q$.
Moreover $q_a\theta=d_a(\theta)\le d_b(\theta)\le\mu_b$.

Let $\theta_2$ be the second smallest element of~$A$, with $\theta_2=\infty$ if $r=1$.
After a positive outcome of~$a$, the new threshold is $\theta_a=\min\{p_a,\theta_2\}$; after a positive outcome of $j$ it is $\theta_j=\min\{p_j,\theta_2\}$.
Let $u=\min\{p_a,p_j,\theta_2\}$ and
\[
  \mathcal B=\{b\in R\setminus\{a,j\}:\mu_b<q_au\}.
\]
Every $b\in\mathcal B$ has $q_b\ge q_a$ and therefore $p_b=\mu_b/q_b<u$.
So after $a$ or $j$ is positive, the jobs of~$\mathcal B$ do not change~$A$, and each of them contributes the increment~$X_b$.

We first determine the optimal order, which is greedy after~$j$.
If $j$ is positive, the threshold is $\theta_j\ge u$.
Every $b\in\mathcal B$ then has key $d_b(\theta_j)=\mu_b<q_au$, and $a$ has key $q_a\min\{p_a,\theta_j\}=q_au$.
Every other job $c$ has key $\min\{\mu_c,q_c\theta_j\}\ge q_au$.
If a job $c\notin\mathcal B$ has key exactly $q_au$ and $q_c=q_a$, then $\mu_c\ge q_au\ge q_a\theta$, so $c$ and $a$ had equal first and second keys at the start, and the rank favours~$a$.
So the order processes $\mathcal B$ in the order of the keys $(\mu_b,q_b,\rho(b))$, during which $A$ does not change, and then~$a$.
If $j$ is zero, the order starts~$a$ next.
If $a$ is then positive, the same argument with $\theta_a\ge u$ shows that it processes~$\mathcal B$ next, in the same order.

The new order starts with~$a$.
If $a$ is positive, it processes $\mathcal B$ in the same order and then starts~$j$.
If $a$ is zero, it starts~$j$, and if $j$ is positive it then processes~$\mathcal B$ in the same order.
In each case it then continues as the optimal order does from the same state.
Writing $X_{\mathcal B}$ for the sequence of outcomes of the jobs in $\mathcal B$ in this order, the increments are as follows.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
outcomes of $a$ and $j$ & optimal order & new order\\
\midrule
both zero & $0,0$ & $0,0$\\
$a$ positive, $j$ zero & $0,\theta,X_{\mathcal B}$ & $\theta,X_{\mathcal B},0$\\
$a$ zero, $j$ positive & $\theta,X_{\mathcal B},0$ & $0,\theta,X_{\mathcal B}$\\
both positive & $\theta,X_{\mathcal B},u$ & $\theta,X_{\mathcal B},u$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In every row the two segments have the same length and end in the same state, because the final multiset $A$ does not depend on the order of insertion.
The weights of consecutive positions differ by exactly one.
So the new order is worse by $\theta+\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}X_b$ in the second row and better by the same amount in the third row.
The outcomes of the jobs in $\mathcal B$ are independent of those of $a$ and~$j$.
So the expected change is
\[
  \bigl(q_a(1-q_j)-(1-q_a)q_j\bigr)\Bigl(\theta+\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\mu_b\Bigr)=(q_a-q_j)\Bigl(\theta+\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\mu_b\Bigr)\le0. \qedhere
\]

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:wvw}
Let $X_1\le_{\mathrm{st}}\dots\le_{\mathrm{st}}X_N$ be Bernoulli jobs, and let the machines become available at given times $t_1,\dots,t_m\ge0$.
The list policy that starts the jobs in the order $1,\dots,N$, each at the earliest time at which some machine is available, minimizes $\sum_j\mathbb{E}[C_j]$ among all policies.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
For a list $\lambda$ of jobs and a vector $\tau$ of availability times, let $c(\tau;\lambda)$ be the expected cost of the list policy~$\lambda$ from~$\tau$.
For $k>1$, let $L^*_k$ be the list of the jobs in $\{2,\dots,N\}\setminus\{k\}$ in increasing order.

\emph{An exchange inequality.}
For every $\tau$ and every $k>1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wvwexchange}
  c\bigl(\tau;(k,1)+L^*_k\bigr)\;\ge\;c\bigl(\tau;(1,k)+L^*_k\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $+$ denotes concatenation.
Weber, Varaiya, and Walrand prove~\eqref{eq:wvwexchange} for stochastically ordered processing times with densities and every vector of availability times, in the proof of their theorem~\cite[pp.~845--846]{WVW86}.
In their notation the inequality reads $R(\tau;L_{1,k})\ge R(\tau;L_{k,1})$ for the reward $r(t)=-t$, and its proof uses only their Lemmas~1--3.
For Bernoulli jobs, let $\delta>0$, let $U_1,\dots,U_N$ be independent and uniform on $[0,1]$, and let $Y_j=X_j+\delta U_j$.
The variables $Y_j$ have densities, and $Y_1\le_{\mathrm{st}}\dots\le_{\mathrm{st}}Y_N$, because adding independent copies of the same variable preserves the stochastic order.
So~\eqref{eq:wvwexchange} holds for the processing times~$Y_j$.
For a fixed list, couple the two runs through $Y_j=X_j+\delta U_j$.
At each start, the coordinatewise distance between the two sorted vectors of availability times grows by at most~$\delta$, since sorting is $1$-Lipschitz in the maximum norm.
So every start time changes by at most $N\delta$, and every list cost changes by at most $N(N+1)\delta$.
Letting $\delta\to0$ proves~\eqref{eq:wvwexchange} for the jobs~$X_j$.

\emph{Optimality.}
We show by induction on $N$ that, for every $\tau$, the list $(1,\dots,N)$ is optimal from~$\tau$; the case $N=1$ is trivial.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:nonidle}, some optimal policy from $\tau$ is deterministic and non-idling; let $k$ be its first job, started at the minimum entry of~$\tau$.
After the outcome of $k$ is revealed, the continuation is a policy for the other jobs from the resulting availability times.
All of them are at least the start time of $k$, so the continuation problem is an instance of the lemma with fewer jobs, and by induction the list of the other jobs in increasing order is optimal there.
So the list $(k)$ followed by the other jobs in increasing order is optimal from~$\tau$.
If $k=1$, this is the list $(1,\dots,N)$.
Otherwise it is $(k,1)+L^*_k$, which by~\eqref{eq:wvwexchange} is no better than $(1,k)+L^*_k$.
After job~$1$ starts, the list $(k)+L^*_k$ is a policy for the jobs $2,\dots,N$, so by induction the list $(2,\dots,N)$ is no worse.
Hence $(1,\dots,N)$ is optimal.

<!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{lem:blockerfirst}
In the instances $I_{L_\pm}$, consider a state at time~$0$ in which $b$ has not started and a machine is free at time~$0$.
Some optimal continuation from this state is non-idling and starts $b$ next, at time~$0$.
<!-- end lemma-12 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
We use induction on the number of remaining jobs; the case of one job is trivial.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:nonidle}, some optimal continuation is deterministic and non-idling, so it starts its first job at time~$0$.
Every job other than~$b$ is positive with probability at least $1/2$, and every positive processing time is at least~$1$.

Suppose exactly one machine is free at time~$0$, and a non-idling optimal continuation starts a job $i\ne b$ there.
The other machine runs a job that started at time~$0$ and has at least one time unit left.
So if $i$ is positive, both machines are busy until time~$1$, and $\mathbb{E}[S_b]\ge1/2$.
Let $c_0$ be the minimum expected sum of start times for the remaining jobs other than~$b$ from the current state, allowing idle time.
Simulating $b$ with an independent virtual outcome, as in Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}(b), shows that the continuation's start times of the jobs other than~$b$ sum to at least $c_0$ in expectation.
So its expected sum of start times is at least $c_0+1/2$.
Now start $b$ instead.
If $b$ is zero, continue optimally, with expected sum of start times~$c_0$.
If $b$ is positive, which happens with probability~$q$, run the remaining jobs one after another.
At most $k+3\le2\bar k$ jobs remain, each of size at most $B+1\le2B$.
So every start time is at most $4\bar kB$, and the sum of start times is at most $8\bar k^2B$.
This alternative has expected sum of start times at most $c_0+q\cdot8\bar k^2B=c_0+1/8<c_0+1/2$, a contradiction.
So every non-idling optimal continuation starts $b$ at time~$0$.

Suppose both machines are free at time~$0$, and a non-idling optimal continuation starts $i\ne b$ at time~$0$.
After $i$ starts, a machine is still free at time~$0$, so by induction we may replace the continuation by one that starts $b$ next, at time~$0$.
Exchanging the order of $i$ and $b$ leaves both start times equal to~$0$.
It also leads to the same remaining jobs and the same multiset of machine release times in every realization, so the cost is unchanged.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:blockerfirst}, some optimal policy starts $b$ at time~$0$.
Afterwards the machines become available at times $(0,0)$ if $b$ is zero and $(0,L)$ if $b$ is positive.
The remaining jobs are stochastically ordered.
A job of size $a_i$ with probability $1/2$ is dominated by one of size $a_{i'}\ge a_i$ with the same probability.
The job $H$ dominates all of them because $h=2S>a_i$.
The job $d$ dominates $H$ because $B+1>h$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:wvw}, SEPT is optimal from either state, so SEPT is optimal for $I_{L_-}$ and~$I_{L_+}$.
So computing $\mathrm{OPT}$ and computing the cost of SEPT coincide on these instances.

We compare the two instances under SEPT, with the same outcomes for all jobs.
If $b$ is zero, the two schedules are identical.
Suppose $b$ is positive, and let $W=\sum_ia_iZ_i$ be the total realized processing time of the jobs $a_1,\dots,a_k$, where $Z_i$ are their outcome indicators.
Since $W\le S<B<L_-$, all these jobs run on the free machine before time~$L$, and $H$ starts there at time~$W$.
If $H$ is zero, $d$ starts at time $W$.
If $H$ is positive and $W\le B_0$, then $H$ completes at $W+h\le B<L_-$, and $d$ starts at time $W+h$.
If $H$ is positive and $W>B_0$, then $W\ge B_0+1$, so $H$ runs until $W+h\ge B+1>L_+$, and $d$ starts at time~$L$ on the other machine.
Only in the last case do the start times differ, by $L_+-L_-=1/4$.
The completion time of $b$ itself also differs by $1/4$ when $b$ is positive.
Hence
\[
  \mathrm{OPT}(I_{L_+})-\mathrm{OPT}(I_{L_-})=\frac q4\Bigl(1+\frac12\cdot\frac{K_{\mathrm{NO}}}{2^k}\Bigr)=\frac q4\Bigl(1+\frac{K_{\mathrm{NO}}}{2^{\bar k}}\Bigr),
\]
and two exact evaluations determine $K_{\mathrm{NO}}$.
All numbers have bit length polynomial in the input.
Multiplying all sizes by~$4$ makes them integers and does not change the argument.

For the decision version, note that all processing times are multiples of $1/4$ and the probability of every joint outcome is a multiple of $1/(Q2^{\bar k})$.
So $\mathrm{OPT}$ is a multiple of $1/(4Q2^{\bar k})$.
It is also at most $8\bar k^2B+(k+3)(B+1)$.

<!-- theorem-10: body_md -->
\label{thm:fpras}
Let $\Pi$ be a non-idling policy whose decisions can be simulated in polynomial time.
For $\rho,\delta\in(0,1)$, an estimate $\hat C$ with $(1-\rho)\operatorname{cost}(\Pi)\le\hat C\le(1+\rho)\operatorname{cost}(\Pi)$ with probability at least $1-\delta$ can be computed from $\lceil3N\rho^{-2}\ln(2/\delta)\rceil$ simulations of $\Pi$.
<!-- end theorem-10 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
For a realization $\omega$, let $V(\omega)=\sum_jX_j(\omega)$ and $C(\omega)=\sum_jC^\Pi_j(\omega)$.
A non-idling policy completes every job by time $V(\omega)$, so $0\le C(\omega)\le N\,V(\omega)$, and $C(\omega)=0$ if $V(\omega)=0$.
Sample realizations from the following distribution~$\mathbb Q$.
Choose a job $i$ with probability $\mu_i/P$, set $X_i=p_i$, and draw all other outcomes from their own distributions.
The policy sees only the outcomes it observes when starting jobs.
Under~$\mathbb Q$, the density of a realization with respect to the true distribution is
\[
  \sum_i\frac{\mu_i}{P}\cdot\frac{\mathbf 1[X_i=p_i]}{q_i}=\frac{V(\omega)}{P}.
\]
Hence the estimator $Z=P\,C/V$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb Q}[Z]=\operatorname{cost}(\Pi)$.
It takes values in $[0,NP]$, and its mean is at least $P$.
The multiplicative Chernoff bound for the average of $\lceil3N\rho^{-2}\ln(2/\delta)\rceil$ independent copies of $Z/(NP)\in[0,1]$, whose mean is at least $1/N$, gives the claim.

<!-- theorem-11: body_md -->
\label{thm:list}
For every integer $b\ge2$, let $M=2^b$ and consider two machines with $M$ jobs of each type $i=0,\dots,b-1$, where a job of type $i$ has
\[
  p_i=\frac{M^2}{4^i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_i=\frac{2^i}{M^2}.
\]
Let $N=bM$.
Every list policy costs at least $Mb^2/128$, whereas $\mathrm{OPT}\le6Mb$.
Hence every list policy has ratio at least $b/768\ge\log_2N/1536$.
<!-- end theorem-11 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Fix a deterministic list; for a random list, the bound follows by averaging over the coins.
For $0\le k<N$, let $F_k$ be the start time of the $(k+1)$-st job of the list, let $k_i$ be the number of type-$i$ jobs among the first $k$, and let $B_i\sim\mathrm{Bin}(k_i,q_i)$ be the number of positive ones among them.
At time $F_k$ all these $k$ jobs have started and a machine is free, so at most one of them is still running.
So at least $(B_i-1)_+$ positive jobs of type $i$ have completed for each $i$, and since two machines process at most $2F_k$ units of work by time $F_k$,
\[
  2F_k\;\ge\;\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}p_i(B_i-1)_+ .
\]
For $k_i\ge2$, keeping only the event that exactly two jobs are positive gives
\[
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[(B_i-1)_+\bigr]\;\ge\;\binom{k_i}{2}q_i^2(1-q_i)^{k_i-2}\;\ge\;\frac14k_i(k_i-1)q_i^2,
\]
because $(1-q_i)^{k_i-2}\ge1-Mq_i\ge1/2$.
The bound also holds for $k_i\le1$, as both sides are then zero.
Using $p_iq_i^2=M^{-2}$ and $\sum_i(k_i-k/b)^2\ge0$,
\[
  \mathbb{E}[F_k]\;\ge\;\frac1{8M^2}\sum_ik_i(k_i-1)\;\ge\;\frac1{8M^2}\Bigl(\frac{k^2}{b}-k\Bigr).
\]
For $N/2\le k<N$ we have $k\ge bM/2\ge2b$, so $k^2/b-k\ge k^2/(2b)\ge bM^2/8$ and $\mathbb{E}[F_k]\ge b/64$.
There are $N/2$ such values of~$k$, so the cost is at least $P+(N/2)(b/64)\ge Mb^2/128$.
<!-- end proof-18 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Consider the following policy.
On machine~$1$, start jobs in increasing order of type until the first positive outcome; all earlier jobs are zero and complete at time~$0$.
If all jobs are zero, every job completes at time~$0$.
Otherwise, let $i$ be the type of the first positive job.
Leave that job on machine~$1$, and process all remaining jobs one after another on machine~$2$, starting at time~$0$, in decreasing order of type.

Let $E_i$ be the event that the first positive job has type~$i$.
It requires a positive job among the $M$ jobs of type~$i$, so $\Pr[E_i]\le Mq_i=2^i/M$.
Condition on the history up to the first positive job.
The types below~$i$ are exhausted, and the remaining jobs keep their original distributions.
On a single machine, the processing time of a job is added to the start time of each later job.
In decreasing order of type, a job of type $j\ge i$ has at most $M(j-i+1)$ later jobs, and there are at most $M$ jobs of type~$j$, each of mean~$2^{-j}$.
So the conditional expected sum of start times is at most
\[
  M^2\sum_{j\ge i}(j-i+1)2^{-j}\;\le\;M^22^{-i}\sum_{t\ge0}(t+1)2^{-t}\;=\;4M^22^{-i}.
\]
Averaging over the disjoint events $E_i$, the expected sum of start times is at most $\sum_i(2^i/M)\cdot4M^22^{-i}=4Mb$.
So the cost is at most $P+4Mb\le6Mb$.
