<!-- head-1: body_md -->
We would like to clarify that throughout this paper, ${\hat{\Omega}^s}$ denotes the reference domain of the Naghdi shell, whereas ${\boldsymbol\omega}$ denotes its rotation vector.
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Because the coefficients of the shell model depend only on first derivatives
of $\varphi$, they are defined almost everywhere and remain bounded under the
assumptions of Section~\ref{shelldomain}. No separate boundary condition is
imposed at an internal band-transition curve. The global
$H^1({\hat{\Omega}^s};\mathbb R^3)^2$ regularity of
$(\boldsymbol\eta,{\boldsymbol\omega})$ enforces continuity across each such
curve in the trace sense and therefore represents a connected, welded shell.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
Recall that the surface force $\boldsymbol f_s^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is related to the surface force $\boldsymbol f^\varepsilon$ appearing in  the shell equation \eqref{Naghdi_shell} as follows:
\[
\boldsymbol f_s^{\mathrm{ref}}
=
\boldsymbol f^\varepsilon\sqrt a,
\]
where $a$ is defined in \eqref{def:a}, and 
$
dS_0
=
\sqrt a\,dx\,dy_{\mathrm{arc}}
$.

                                                                             
                                                                              

            
  
     
  
                               
   
                                         
   
                               
  
                                  
   
                                                        
Therefore, the fluid induced force appearing in the shell equation is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol f^\varepsilon(x,y_{\mathrm{arc}},t)
={}&-
\boldsymbol\sigma_f
\Bigl(
\boldsymbol u
\left(
\Phi_s(x,y_{\mathrm{arc}},t),t
\right),
p
\left(
\Phi_s(x,y_{\mathrm{arc}},t),t
\right)
\Bigr)
\boldsymbol n_f
\Bigl(
\Phi_s(x,y_{\mathrm{arc}},t),t
\Bigr)
\frac{J_s(x,y_{\mathrm{arc}},t)}{\sqrt a}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\hat{\Omega}^s}
\boldsymbol f^\varepsilon
\cdot
\boldsymbol v
\sqrt a
\,dx\,dy_{\mathrm{arc}}
=
\int_{\hat{\Omega}^s}
\boldsymbol f_s^{\mathrm{ref}}
\cdot
\boldsymbol v
\,dx\,dy_{\mathrm{arc}}
=
\int_{\Gamma(t)}
\boldsymbol f_s^\Gamma
\cdot
\boldsymbol v^\Gamma
\,dS_t.
\end{aligned}
\]
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
In a general Naghdi shell model, the external interface virtual work may
contain contributions from both a distributed surface force and a
distributed surface couple:
\[
\delta W_{\Gamma}
=
\int_{\Gamma(t)}
\boldsymbol f_s^\Gamma
\cdot
\boldsymbol v^\Gamma
\,dS_t
+
\int_{\Gamma(t)}
\boldsymbol m_s^\Gamma
\cdot
\boldsymbol w^\Gamma
\,dS_t,
\]
where $\boldsymbol m_s^\Gamma$ is the couple per unit current surface
area, and
$
\boldsymbol w^\Gamma\circ\Phi_s=\boldsymbol w$.
In the present midsurface-coupling approximation, the fluid traction is
applied directly to the shell midsurface and is represented only by the
surface-force resultant
$\boldsymbol f_s^\Gamma
=
-\boldsymbol\sigma_f(\boldsymbol u,p)\boldsymbol n_f$.
We neglect an independent moment resultant, including any moment produced
by the offset between the physical fluid-contacting surface and the shell
midsurface, and therefore set
\[
\boldsymbol m_s^\Gamma=\boldsymbol0.
\]
Consequently, the interface virtual work reduces to
\[
\delta W_{\Gamma}
=
\int_{\Gamma(t)}
\boldsymbol f_s^\Gamma
\cdot
\boldsymbol v^\Gamma
\,dS_t
=
\int_{\hat{\Omega}^s}
\boldsymbol f_s^{\mathrm{ref}}
\cdot
\boldsymbol v
\,dx\,dy_{\mathrm{arc}},
\]
and no direct interface-work term involving the rotation test function
$\boldsymbol w$ appears. Nevertheless, this does not mean that ${\boldsymbol\omega}$ remains unchanged. The applied force can still generate rotation indirectly because $\boldsymbol\eta$ and ${\boldsymbol\omega}$ are coupled through the shell bilinear forms. It only means that the fluid does not apply a separate external couple directly to ${\boldsymbol\omega}$.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
At a band-transition curve, $R'$ may jump. Consequently, the tangent
vectors and unit normal may have different one-sided values, so a single
classical normal may not exist exactly on the transition curve.
Nevertheless, these quantities are defined almost everywhere on each
smooth part of the surface.

A transition curve is one-dimensional, whereas the shell surface is
two-dimensional, so the curve has zero surface measure. Therefore, it
does not contribute to an ordinary surface integral such as
\[
\int_{\Gamma(t)}
\boldsymbol f_s^\Gamma
\cdot
\boldsymbol v^\Gamma
\,dS_t.
\]
No additional condition or integral is required there for a regular
distributed traction.
If the physical model includes a force or couple concentrated along a
band-transition curve $\mathcal C_j$, its virtual work must instead be
included explicitly through line terms of the form
\[
\sum_j
\int_{\mathcal C_j}
\boldsymbol f_{\mathrm{line},j}
\cdot
\boldsymbol v
\,ds
+
\sum_j
\int_{\mathcal C_j}
\boldsymbol m_{\mathrm{line},j}
\cdot
\boldsymbol w
\,ds.
\]
Such concentrated line forces $\boldsymbol f_{\mathrm{line},j}$ and line couples $\boldsymbol m_{\mathrm{line},j}$ are not included in our
present model.
<!-- end remark-4 -->
<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
To make the interface-traction energy well defined, we assume that the
pulled-back fluid traction satisfies
\[
\boldsymbol\tau_f^n
\in
L^2({\hat{\Omega}^s};\mathbb R^3),
\qquad
n=0,\ldots,N.
\]
This assumption may be interpreted either as an additional regularity
assumption on the time discrete solution or, at the fully discrete level,
as the use of an $L^2$-projected traction in a common finite element
interface space. Because of the assumption of the geometry given in \eqref{def:R}, the translational surface mass
density is uniformly nondegenerate:
\[
0<m_*
\le
m_t(x,y_{\mathrm{arc}})
\le
m^*
<\infty
\qquad
\text{for a.e. }
(x,y_{\mathrm{arc}})\in{\hat{\Omega}^s}.
\]
Consequently, $m_t^{-1}\in L^\infty({\hat{\Omega}^s})$, and the inverse mass norm
\[
\|\boldsymbol g\|_{M_t^{-1}}^2
=
\int_{\hat{\Omega}^s}
\frac{|\boldsymbol g|^2}{m_t}
\,dx\,dy_{\mathrm{arc}}
\]
is well defined.
<!-- end remark-5 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:fixed_beta_stability}
Assume that the fluid domain and reference interface are fixed, that the
inlet condition is homogeneous so that $\boldsymbol u^{n+1}$ is an
admissible fluid test function. Assume also that the pulled-back fluid traction satisfies
$\boldsymbol\tau_f^n
\in
L^2({\hat{\Omega}^s};\mathbb R^3),~
n=0,\ldots,N$, and that the Robin interface relation
\eqref{fixed_beta_robin_relation} holds in
$L^2({\hat{\Omega}^s};\mathbb R^3)$. Consequently, the inverse-mass traction norm
\[
\|\boldsymbol\tau_f^n\|_{M_t^{-1}}^2
=
\int_{\hat{\Omega}^s}
\frac{|\boldsymbol\tau_f^n|^2}{m_t}
\,dx\,dy_{\mathrm{arc}}
\]
is well defined. Let $0\leq\beta\leq1$ and assume that $P_v$ is constant.
Then the splitting scheme satisfies
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal E^{n+1}
&+
\mathcal D_{\mathrm{phys}}^{n+1}
+
\mathcal D_{\mathrm{num}}^{n+1}
+
\mathcal D_\beta^{n+1}
+
\mathcal C_f^{n+1}
=
\mathcal E^n
+
\Delta t
\left(
\boldsymbol F_f^{n+1},
\boldsymbol u^{n+1}
\right)_{\Omega_f}
-
\Delta t\,P_vQ^{n+1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{fixed_beta_energy_identity}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal C_f^{n+1}$ denotes the contribution of the standard
semi-implicit convective term defined in equation~\eqref{convective_energy_contribution}. If the convective term is
omitted or replaced by the skew-symmetric form, the same identity holds
with $\mathcal C_f^{n+1}=0$, and if

The same inequality holds for the standard semi-implicit convective form

We want to point out that for the prescribed nonzero inlet velocity used in the full problem,
$\boldsymbol u^{n+1}$ is not an admissible homogeneous test function.
One must first introduce an $H^1$ lifting of
$\boldsymbol u_{\mathrm{in}}^{n+1}$ and test the fluid equation with the
homogeneous remainder. The resulting estimate contains additional work

the prescribed inlet does not invalidate the energy method, but its work
cannot be omitted from the right-hand side of the estimate.

\label{remark7_1}
For small-amplitude, long-wavelength disturbances in a compliant vessel, the
wave speed is approximately
\[
c^2
\approx
\frac{A_0}
{\rho_f
\left.
\dfrac{dA}{dp}
\right|_{A=A_0}},
\]
where $A_0$ is the undeformed cross-sectional area and
$\left.dA/dp\right|_{A=A_0}$ is the tangent cross-sectional compliance.

To isolate the effect of dimensionality, suppose that the 2D and 3D models
satisfy the same linear pressure radial displacement relation, we have
\[
p=K\eta_r,
\qquad
\frac{d\eta_r}{dp}=\frac{1}{K},
\]
where $K$ is the radial stiffness per unit displacement. For the 2D case, the cross-sectional area per unit out of plane depth is
\[
A_{\mathrm{2D}}=R+\eta_r.
\]
Therefore,
\[
\frac{dA_{\mathrm{2D}}}{dp}
=
\frac{dA_{\mathrm{2D}}}{d\eta_r}
\frac{d\eta_r}{dp}
=
\frac{1}{K},
\]
and thus
\[
c_{\mathrm{2D}}^2
\approx
\frac{KR}{\rho_f}.
\]
While for the 3D case, the cross-sectional area of the cylindrical tube is given by
\[
A_{\mathrm{3D}}
=
\pi(R+\eta_r)^2.
\]
Linearization about the undeformed configuration $\eta_r=0$ gives
\[
A_{\mathrm{3D}}
=
\pi R^2
+
2\pi R\eta_r
+
\mathcal O(\eta_r^2).
\]
Hence,
\[
\frac{dA_{\mathrm{3D}}}{dp}
=
2\pi R\frac{d\eta_r}{dp}
=
\frac{2\pi R}{K}.
\]
It follows that
\[
c_{\mathrm{3D}}^2
\approx
\frac{\pi R^2}
{\rho_f(2\pi R/K)}
=
\frac{KR}{2\rho_f}
=
\frac{1}{2}c_{\mathrm{2D}}^2,
\]
and therefore the wave speeds satisfy that
\[
\boxed{
c_{\mathrm{3D}}
\approx
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,
c_{\mathrm{2D}}.
}
\]
This means that under the assumed common radial stiffness $K$, the relative cross-sectional area change in the 3D model is twice that in the 2D model.
The 3D model consequently has a
lower pressure-wave speed, which is about $\sqrt{2}$ times slower.

<!-- remark-8: body_md -->
For long-time simulations, a conforming mesh between the fluid and shell meshes at the fluid–structure interface is necessary in the present study. When the fluid and shell meshes are generated independently, repeated projection and interpolation of displacement, velocity, and traction between non-conforming interface nodes may fail to preserve circumferential symmetry and the discrete balance of forces and moments. Although the resulting transfer error may be small at each time step, it can accumulate over time and excite a spurious internal rotational mode (Our simulations with non-conforming meshes has observed this effects). This artificial rotation can contaminate the computed displacement, stress, and flow fields and, through the ALE update, progressively distort the fluid mesh. The resulting deterioration in mesh quality may eventually cause solver stagnation or premature termination of the simulation. Constructing the shell mesh directly from the corresponding fluid-interface facets provides consistent geometric and topological correspondence, suppresses artificial interface torque, and substantially improves the accuracy and robustness of long-time FSI simulations.
