<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Suppose that \eqref{hyp:A} holds and $\Lambda_J(\rho)>0$ for every nonempty $J\subseteq I$. For any $c_{i,0}\in C^\infty(M)$ with $c_{i,0}>0$ and $\int_M c_{i,0}\,\mathrm dV_g=\rho_i$, system \eqref{eq:flow} with $c_i(0)=c_{i,0}$ has a unique global smooth positive solution preserving each mass. Moreover, there exists a smooth positive stationary state $c^\infty$ such that, for every integer $m\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:whole}
\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\|c(t)-c^\infty\right\|_{C^m(M)^n}=0.
\end{equation}
For every $\tau>0$, integer $m\ge0$ and $0<\alpha<1$, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:high}
\begin{split}
&\sup_{t\ge\tau}\sum_i\left(\left\|c_i(t)\right\|_{C^{m,\alpha}}+\left\|U_i[c(t)]\right\|_{C^{m+2,\alpha}}\right)<\infty,\\
&\inf_{t\ge\tau,\,x\in M,\,i\in I}c_i(t,x)>0.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The zero-mean functions $u_i^\infty=U_i[c^\infty]$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elliptic}
\Delta_g u_i^\infty+\sum_j a_{ij}\rho_j
\left(\frac{h_j e^{u_j^\infty}}{\int_M h_j e^{u_j^\infty}\,\mathrm dV_g}-1\right)=0\text{ in }M\text{ for }i\in I.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
One should note the following details.
\begin{itemize}
    \item [$(a)$] The limit $u_i^\infty$ may depend on the initial data. Uniqueness in the theorem concerns the evolution with prescribed initial data.
    \item [$(b)$] Since $u_i^\infty$ is smooth on the compact surface, each component has finite Dirichlet energy and a finite positive exponential integral. More precisely, we get
 $$\int_M|\nabla_g u_i^\infty|_g^2\,\mathrm dV_g<\infty\text{ and }0<(\min_M h_i)e^{\min_M u_i^\infty}
 \le\int_M h_i e^{u_i^\infty}\,\mathrm dV_g \le(\max_M h_i)e^{\max_M u_i^\infty}<\infty.$$
Thus the densities $\rho_i h_i e^{u_i^\infty}/\int_M h_i e^{u_i^\infty}\,\mathrm dV_g$ are well defined and have masses $\rho_i$.
\end{itemize}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:energy-finite}
For every $i\in I$, suppose that $c_i\ge0$ has mass $\rho_i$ and finite entropy $\int_M c_i\log c_i\,\mathrm dV_g$. Then the integrals defining the interaction energy $-W_A(c)$ in \eqref{eq:energy} are absolutely convergent, and $\mathcal F_\rho(c)$ is a finite real number.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Put $p_i=c_i/\rho_i$. Since $\operatorname{vol}_g(M)=1$, both $dV_g$ and $p_i\,dV_g$ are probability measures. For a bounded measurable function $\psi$, the entropy inequality
\cite[Eq.~(2.8)]{BOPR} gives
\[
\int p_i\psi\,\mathrm dV_g\le\int p_i\log p_i\,\mathrm dV_g+\log\int e^\psi\,\mathrm dV_g.
\]
Indeed, integrate $s\log s-s+1\ge0$ against the probability density $q=e^\psi/\int e^\psi\,\mathrm dV_g$ with $s=p_i/q$. Fix $0<\gamma<2$ and apply the inequality to $\psi_N(y)=\min\{N,\gamma\log^+(1/d_g(x,y))\}$. The small-ball volume bound on the surface gives
\[
\sup_x\int_M\max\{1,d_g(x,y)^{-\gamma}\}\,\mathrm dV_g(y)<\infty.
\]
For example, summing over annuli of radii $2^{-k}r_0$ bounds the singular part by a constant times $\sum_{k\ge0}(2^{-k}r_0)^{2-\gamma}$. Monotone convergence therefore gives
\[
\sup_x\int p_j(y)\log^+(1/d_g(x,y))\,\mathrm dV_g(y)<\infty.
\]
The negative part of $\log(1/d_g(x,y))$ is bounded by $\log^+\operatorname{diam}(M)$, and $R$ is bounded. Applying the same kernel estimate also to the constant density gives
\[
\sup_x\int_M |G(x,y)|c_j(y)\,\mathrm dV_g(y)<\infty,
\qquad \sup_x\int_M |G(x,y)|\,\mathrm dV_g(y)<\infty.
\]
Using $|c_i-\rho_i|\le c_i+\rho_i$ and Tonelli's theorem, we obtain
\[
\iint_{M\times M}|G(x,y)|\,|c_i(x)-\rho_i|\,|c_j(y)-\rho_j|
\,\mathrm dV_g(x)\,\mathrm dV_g(y)<\infty.
\]
Finally, the Boltzmann entropy $H(c)$ is finite by assumption, with its negative part bounded by $s\log s\ge-e^{-1}$. The external-potential energy is finite because $\log(\rho_i h_i)$ is bounded.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:variation}
For a smooth positive density $c$ of prescribed masses and $\zeta\in C^\infty(M)^n$ with $\int_M\zeta_i\,\mathrm dV_g=0$ for every $i$, the directional derivative, with $A$, $\rho$ and $h$ fixed, satisfies
\[
D\mathcal F_\rho(c)[\zeta]
:=\left.\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varepsilon}
\mathcal F_\rho(c+\varepsilon\zeta)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}
=\sum_i\int_M\mu_i(c)\zeta_i\,\mathrm dV_g,
\]
where
\begin{align}\label{def:mu}
\mu_i(c)=\log\frac{c_i}{\rho_i h_i}+1-U_i[c].
\end{align}
Along a smooth positive solution of \eqref{eq:flow}, the free-energy dissipation identity reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dissipation}
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\mathcal F_\rho(c(t))=-\mathcal D(c(t)),\qquad
\mathcal D(c)=\sum_i\int c_i|\nabla_g\mu_i(c)|^2\,\mathrm dV_g.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The zero-integral condition preserves each mass under $c\mapsto c+\varepsilon\zeta$. Since $c$ is smooth and positive on the compact surface, this perturbation remains positive for sufficiently small $|\varepsilon|$. Differentiating the Boltzmann entropy and the external-potential energy gives $\sum_i\int(\log(c_i/(\rho_i h_i))+1)\zeta_i\,\mathrm dV_g$. By symmetry of $A$ and $G$, the derivative of the interaction energy satisfies
\[
\begin{aligned}
D(-W_A)(c)[\zeta]
&=-\frac12\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}
\left(
\int\zeta_i\mathcal G(c_j-\rho_j)\,\mathrm dV_g
+\int(c_i-\rho_i)\mathcal G\zeta_j\,\mathrm dV_g
\right)\\
&=-\sum_i\int U_i[c]\zeta_i\,\mathrm dV_g.
\end{aligned}
\]
Combining these derivatives in \eqref{eq:energy} gives the variation formula. Integrating \eqref{eq:flow} on the closed surface gives
\[
\int_M\partial_t c_i\,\mathrm dV_g
=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_M c_i\,\mathrm dV_g=0.
\]
We can therefore apply the first-variation formula with $\zeta_i=\partial_t c_i$.  Since $\mu_i$ differs from $\log(c_i/h_i)-U_i[c]$ by the constant $1-\log\rho_i$, integration by parts gives
\[
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\mathcal F_\rho(c(t))
=\sum_i\int\mu_i\operatorname{div}_g(c_i\nabla_g\mu_i)\,\mathrm dV_g
=-\sum_i\int c_i|\nabla_g\mu_i|^2\,\mathrm dV_g.\qedhere
\]

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:metric}
We explain the relation between \eqref{eq:metric} and
\eqref{eq:flow}.
At a smooth positive density $c$, every smooth variation $\zeta$ with $\int_M\zeta_i\,\mathrm dV_g=0$ can be written as
\[
\zeta_i=-\operatorname{div}_g(c_i\nabla_g\psi_i).
\]
Indeed, the operator $-\operatorname{div}_g(c_i\nabla_g\cdot)$ is uniformly elliptic, its kernel consists of the constants, and the zero-integral condition ensures solvability. Each $\psi_i$ is unique up to an additive constant.

Writing $p_i=c_i/\rho_i$, the quadratic form corresponding to \eqref{eq:metric} is \cite[Section~2.6]{AS}\cite[Section~1]{Otto}
\[
\sum_i\rho_i\int p_i|\nabla_g\psi_i|^2\,\mathrm dV_g
=\sum_i\int c_i|\nabla_g\psi_i|^2\,\mathrm dV_g.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:variation} and integration by parts,
\[
D\mathcal F_\rho(c)[\zeta]
=\sum_i\int\mu_i(c)\zeta_i\,\mathrm dV_g
=\sum_i\int c_i\nabla_g\mu_i(c)\cdot\nabla_g\psi_i\,\mathrm dV_g.
\]
Thus, with respect to this quadratic form, the negative gradient is $\bigl(\operatorname{div}_g(c_i\nabla_g\mu_i)\bigr)_i$, giving \eqref{eq:flow}.

Without the factors $\rho_i$ in \eqref{eq:metric}, the corresponding calculation uses
$\mathcal E(p)=\mathcal F_\rho(\rho_1p_1,\ldots,\rho_np_n)$. For a smooth variation $\eta$ with $\int_M\eta_i\,\mathrm dV_g=0$,
\[
D\mathcal E(p)[\eta]
=\sum_i\rho_i\int_M\mu_i(\rho p)\eta_i\,\mathrm dV_g.
\]
It would therefore give $\partial_t p_i=\operatorname{div}_g(p_i\nabla_g(\rho_i\mu_i))$, introducing an additional factor $\rho_i$ in the $i$th density equation.

\label{lem:stationary}
For a smooth positive density $c$ with $\int_M c_i\,\mathrm dV_g=\rho_i$, the following conditions are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $c$ is a constrained critical point of $\mathcal F_\rho$.
\item $c$ is stationary for \eqref{eq:flow}.
\item For each $i\in I$, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationary}
c_i=\rho_i\frac{h_i e^{U_i[c]}}{\int_M h_i e^{U_i[c]}\,\mathrm dV_g}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Under these conditions, $u_i=U_i[c]$ solves \eqref{eq:elliptic} and has zero mean.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Vanishing of the variation against all zero-mean test functions means that each $\mu_i$ is constant. Solving this relation for $c_i$ and imposing its mass gives \eqref{eq:stationary}. Conversely, that formula makes each $\mu_i$ constant, so both the constrained variation and the right-hand side of the flow vanish. If the flow is stationary, testing it with $\mu_i$ gives $\mathcal D(c)=0$. Positivity and connectedness imply that each $\mu_i$ is constant. Finally, substitution into \eqref{eq:poisson} gives \eqref{eq:elliptic}.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:heat}
Let $S(t)=e^{t\Delta_g}$ and let $K_t(x,y)$ be its heat kernel on $M$. For $0<t\le1$ and $1<q<\infty$, the following estimates hold for scalar functions $f$ and tangent vector fields $X$ with finite right-hand sides,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heat}
\begin{split}
\left\|S(t)f\right\|_\infty&\le C_qt^{-1/q}\left\|f\right\|_q,\\
\left\|S(t)\operatorname{div}_g X\right\|_\infty&\le C_qt^{-1/2-1/q}\left\|X\right\|_q,\\
\left\|S(t)\operatorname{div}_g X\right\|_\infty&\le Ct^{-1/2}\left\|X\right\|_\infty.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
For nonnegative integers $j,k,\ell$, there are constants depending only on $(M,g)$ and these orders such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
|\partial_t^j\nabla_x^k\nabla_y^\ell K_t(x,y)|
\le C_{j,k,\ell}t^{-1-j-(k+\ell)/2}e^{-d_g(x,y)^2/(Ct)}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The kernel estimate \eqref{eq:kernel} follows from Ludewig \cite[Theorems 1.1 and 3.5]{Ludewig}. 

By compactness, we find a $C_V$ such that
\[
\operatorname{vol}_g(B_s(x))\le C_Vs^2
\qquad(x\in M,\ s>0).
\]
For $a>0$, set $A_k=\{y\in M:k\sqrt t\le d_g(x,y)<(k+1)\sqrt t\}$.
Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_M e^{-a d_g(x,y)^2/t}\,\mathrm dV_g(y)
&=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\int_{A_k}e^{-a d_g(x,y)^2/t}\,\mathrm dV_g(y)\\
&\le\sum_{k=0}^\infty e^{-ak^2}
  \operatorname{vol}_g(B_{(k+1)\sqrt t}(x))\\
&\le C_Vt\sum_{k=0}^\infty(k+1)^2e^{-ak^2}
=:C_at,
\end{aligned}
\]
where $C_a<\infty$ is independent of $x$ and $t$. For $1<q<\infty$, let $q'=q/(q-1)$. The heat-kernel estimates \eqref{eq:kernel} and the preceding integral bound give, uniformly in $x\in M$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|K_t(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{q'}}
&\le Ct^{-1}
\left(\int_M e^{-q'd_g(x,y)^2/(Ct)}\,\mathrm dV_g(y)\right)^{1/q'}
\le C_qt^{-1+1/q'}=C_qt^{-1/q},\\
\|\nabla_yK_t(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{q'}}
&\le Ct^{-3/2}
\left(\int_M e^{-q'd_g(x,y)^2/(Ct)}\,\mathrm dV_g(y)\right)^{1/q'}
\le C_qt^{-3/2+1/q'}=C_qt^{-1/2-1/q}.
\end{aligned}
\] 
Integrating by parts, we get $S(t)\operatorname{div}_g X(x)=-\int\langle\nabla_yK_t(x,y),X(y)\rangle_g\,\mathrm dV_g(y)$. H\"older's inequality proves \eqref{eq:heat}. The last estimate uses $q'=1$.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:smoothing}
Let $c$ be a continuous mild solution of $\partial_t c_i=\Delta_g c_i-\operatorname{div}_g(c_i b_i[c])$ on $[a,a+L]$, where $0<L\le1$, $b_i[c]=\nabla_g\log h_i+\nabla_g U_i[c]$ and the component masses are fixed. If $\sup_{[a,a+L]}\left\|c\right\|_\infty\le B$, then for $0<\delta<L$, every integer $m\ge0$ and $0<\alpha<1$,
\[
\sup_{a+\delta\le t\le a+L}\sum_i
\left(\left\|c_i(t)\right\|_{C^{m,\alpha}}+\left\|U_i[c(t)]\right\|_{C^{m+2,\alpha}}\right)
\le C(B,L,\delta,m,\alpha).
\]
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1. H\"older continuity.}
Set $F_i=c_i b_i[c]$ and $q_i=\Delta_g\log h_i-\sum_j a_{ij}(c_j-\rho_j)$. The elliptic estimates bound $b$, $q$ and $F$ in terms of $B$ and the fixed data. We study the solution in the sense of (\ref{eq:mild}). Put $T(r)X=S(r)\operatorname{div}_g X$ and write
\[
w(t)=\int_a^tT(t-s)F(s)\,\mathrm ds,
\qquad M_F=\sup_{a\le s\le a+L}\|F(s)\|_\infty.
\]
Integrating the heat-kernel derivative estimates \eqref{eq:kernel} in the $y$ variable gives, for $0<r\le1$,
\begin{align}\label{ineq:Txinfty}
\|T(r)X\|_\infty\le Cr^{-1/2}\|X\|_\infty.
\end{align}
Differentiating the kernel representation in $x$ and using the Gaussian integral bound, we obtain
\begin{align}\label{ineq:NablaTxinfty}
\|\nabla_gT(r)X\|_\infty
&\le \|X\|_\infty
\sup_{x\in M}\int_M|\nabla_x\nabla_yK_r(x,y)|_g\,\,\mathrm dV_g(y)\nonumber\\
&\le Cr^{-2}\|X\|_\infty
\sup_{x\in M}\int_M e^{-d_g(x,y)^2/(Cr)}\,\,\mathrm dV_g(y)\nonumber\\
&\le Cr^{-1}\|X\|_\infty.
\end{align}
A similar calculation using the estimate for $\partial_r\nabla_yK_r$ gives
\begin{align}\label{ineq:PartialTTxinfty}
\|(\partial_rT(r))X\|_\infty
\le Cr^{-3/2}\|X\|_\infty.
\end{align}
First we have the H\"older bound in spatial variables. For $d=d_g(x,y)<1$, (\ref{ineq:Txinfty}) and (\ref{ineq:NablaTxinfty}) yield
\[
|w(t,x)-w(t,y)|
\le CM_F\int_0^L\min\{r^{-1/2},dr^{-1}\}\,\mathrm dr
\le C_LM_Fd(1+|\log d|).
\]
Now we study the H\"older bound in the time variable. For $a\le t<t+h\le a+L$, (\ref{ineq:PartialTTxinfty}) yields
\[
\|w(t+h)-w(t)\|_\infty
\le CM_F\left(\int_0^h r^{-1/2}\,\mathrm dr
+h\int_h^L r^{-3/2}\,\mathrm dr\right)
\le CM_F\sqrt h.
\]
Since $d(1+|\log d|)\le C_\gamma d^\gamma$ for $d<1$ and the initial term $S(t-a)c(a)$ is smooth away from $t=a$, we obtain $c\in C^{\gamma,\gamma/2}$ on interior time intervals for every $0<\gamma<1$. The elliptic estimates \eqref{eq:ell-est} give $b$ the same time regularity and a uniform spatial $C^{1,\gamma}$ bound there. Hence $F=cb$ is spatially $C^\gamma$ uniformly in time.
\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 2. Classical solutions.}
Fix $b\in(a,a+L)$ and choose $a<a_0<b$. We establish the required regularity on $[b,a+L]$ by applying the mild formulation \eqref{eq:mild} with initial time $a_0$. Fix $0<\gamma<1$.
By Step~1,
\[
M_\gamma:=\sup_{a_0\le s\le a+L}\|F(s)\|_{C^\gamma}<\infty.
\]
Write
\[
c(t)=S(t-a_0)c(a_0)-w(t),
\qquad
w(t)=\int_{a_0}^tT(t-s)F(s)\,\mathrm ds.
\]
The initial semigroup term is smooth, with controlled derivatives, on every time interval whose left endpoint is greater than $a_0$. It therefore suffices first to estimate $w$.

We work component-wise and initially hold the time variable fixed. To use the spatial H\"older continuity of the vector field $F$, choose a finite coordinate cover and a subordinate smooth partition of unity $\{\chi_\ell\}$. In each chart write $F=\sum_aF_\ell^a\partial_{x_\ell^a}$, and choose a smooth cutoff $\psi_\ell$ supported in that chart and equal to one near $\operatorname{supp}\chi_\ell$. Set
\[
f_{\ell,a}=\chi_\ell F_\ell^a,
\qquad
Y_{\ell,a}=\psi_\ell\partial_{x_\ell^a},
\]
extending both by zero outside the chart. Relabeling the pairs
$(\ell,a)$ by $\nu$ gives
\[
F=\sum_\nu f_\nu Y_\nu,
\qquad
\sum_\nu\|f_\nu\|_{C^\gamma}\le C\|F\|_{C^\gamma},
\]
where the vector fields $Y_\nu$ are fixed and globally smooth.

Consider one term $fY$. Fix $x\in M$ and consider variable $z$. The kernel representation and integration by parts give
\[
\begin{aligned}
T(r)(fY)(z)
&=T(r)((f-f(x))Y)(z)+f(x)S(r)(\operatorname{div}_g Y)(z)\\
&=-\int_M
\langle\nabla_yK_r(z,y),Y(y)\rangle_g
\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr)\,\,\mathrm dV_g(y)
+f(x)S(r)(\operatorname{div}_g Y)(z).
\end{aligned}
\]
We differentiate in $z$ while keeping $x$ fixed and only afterwards set $z=x$. Thus no derivatives of $f$ are required.

By \eqref{eq:kernel},
\[
|\nabla_z^k\partial_r^j\nabla_yK_r(z,y)|_{z=x}
\le Cr^{-1-j-(k+1)/2}
e^{-d_g(x,y)^2/(Cr)}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
|f(y)-f(x)|\le[f]_{C^\gamma}d_g(x,y)^\gamma.
\]
The elementary inequality
\[
d^\gamma e^{-d^2/(Cr)}
\le C_\gamma r^{\gamma/2}e^{-d^2/(2Cr)}
\qquad(d\ge0)
\]
and the Gaussian integral estimate proved above imply
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_Md_g(x,y)^\gamma
e^{-d_g(x,y)^2/(Cr)}\,\,\mathrm dV_g(y)
\le C_\gamma r^{\gamma/2}
\int_Me^{-d_g(x,y)^2/(2Cr)}\,\,\mathrm dV_g(y)\le Cr^{1+\gamma/2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently,
\begin{align}
\Bigg|\nabla_z^k\partial_r^j
\int_M &
\langle\nabla_yK_r(z,y),Y(y)\rangle_g
\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr)\,\,\mathrm dV_g(y)
\Bigg|_{z=x}
\Bigg|_g\nonumber\\
&\quad\le [f]_{C^\gamma}\|Y\|_\infty
\int_M
|\nabla_x^k\partial_r^j\nabla_yK_r(x,y)|_g
d_g(x,y)^\gamma\,\,\mathrm dV_g(y)\nonumber\\
&\quad\le C[f]_{C^\gamma}r^{-1-j-(k+1)/2}
\int_Md_g(x,y)^\gamma
e^{-d_g(x,y)^2/(Cr)}\,\,\mathrm dV_g(y)\nonumber\\
&\quad\le C[f]_{C^\gamma}
r^{-1-j-(k+1)/2}r^{1+\gamma/2}.\nonumber
\end{align}
For the remaining term, $\operatorname{div}_g Y$ is fixed and smooth, and
\[
\partial_r^jS(r)(\operatorname{div}_g Y)
=S(r)\Delta_g^j(\operatorname{div}_g Y).
\]
Using $H^{k+2}(M)\hookrightarrow C^k(M)$ and the contractivity of $S(r)$ in the spectral Sobolev norm, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\nabla_z^k\partial_r^j
\bigl[f(x)S(r)(\operatorname{div}_g Y)(z)\bigr]|_{z=x}|_g&\le \|f\|_\infty
\|\nabla_g^kS(r)\Delta_g^j(\operatorname{div}_g Y)\|_\infty\\
&\quad\le C\|f\|_\infty
\|S(r)\Delta_g^j(\operatorname{div}_g Y)\|_{H^{k+2}}\\
&\quad\le C\|f\|_\infty
\|\Delta_g^j(\operatorname{div}_g Y)\|_{H^{k+2}}
\le C_{k,j,Y}\|f\|_\infty,
\end{aligned}
\]
uniformly for $0<r\le1$, since $Y$ is fixed and smooth. Summing over the finite decomposition, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:holder-kernel}
\begin{aligned}
\|\nabla^k\partial_r^jT(r)F\|_\infty
&\le C\left(
r^{-1-j-(k+1)/2}r^{1+\gamma/2}+1
\right)\|F\|_{C^\gamma}\\
&\le Cr^{-(1+k+2j-\gamma)/2}\|F\|_{C^\gamma}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
for $k=0,1,2$, $j=0,1$ and $0<r\le1$. The last inequality uses $1+k+2j-\gamma>0$.

Fix $0<\beta<\gamma$. The interpolation estimate
\[
\|u\|_{C^{1,\beta}}
\le C\left(
\|u\|_{C^1}
+\|\nabla_gu\|_\infty^{1-\beta}
 \|\nabla_g^2u\|_\infty^\beta
\right)
\]
follows by comparing the gradient at two points by parallel transport and using the smaller of its supremum bound and its Lipschitz bound. See also \cite[Section~6.8]{GT}. Applying it to $u=T(r)F$ and using \eqref{eq:holder-kernel}, we get
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|T(r)F\|_{C^{1,\beta}}
&\le C\left[
r^{-1+\gamma/2}
+\bigl(r^{-1+\gamma/2}\bigr)^{1-\beta}
 \bigl(r^{-3/2+\gamma/2}\bigr)^\beta
\right]\|F\|_{C^\gamma}\\
&\le Cr^{-1+(\gamma-\beta)/2}\|F\|_{C^\gamma},
\end{aligned}
\]
since
\[
\left(-1+\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)(1-\beta)
+\left(-\frac32+\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\beta
=-1+\frac{\gamma-\beta}{2}.
\]
The exponent is greater than $-1$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|w(t)\|_{C^{1,\beta}}
\le CM_\gamma\int_0^{t-a_0}
r^{-1+(\gamma-\beta)/2}\,\mathrm dr=\frac{2CM_\gamma}{\gamma-\beta}
(t-a_0)^{(\gamma-\beta)/2}
\le C_{\gamma,\beta}M_\gamma.
\end{aligned}
\]
These estimates can first be applied to the integrals truncated at $s=t-\varepsilon$. The same integrable bound shows that the truncated integrals converge in $C^{1,\beta}$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, and justifies differentiation under the integral sign once in space.

We next establish the time H\"older estimates. For $a_0\le t<t+h\le a+L$ and $k=0,1$, changing variables gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^kw(t+h)-\nabla^kw(t)
=\int_0^h\nabla^kT(r)F(t+h-r)\,\mathrm dr+\int_0^{t-a_0}
\nabla^k[T(r+h)-T(r)]F(t-r)\,\mathrm dr.
\end{aligned}
\]
Set
\[
p_k=\frac{1+k-\gamma}{2}\in(0,1).
\]
Formula \eqref{eq:holder-kernel} gives
\[
\|\nabla^kT(r)F(s)\|_\infty
\le CM_\gamma r^{-p_k},
\qquad
\|\nabla^k\partial_rT(r)F(s)\|_\infty
\le CM_\gamma r^{-p_k-1}.
\]
The new interval contributes at most $CM_\gamma\int_0^h r^{-p_k}\,\mathrm dr$. On the old interval with $r<h$, estimate the two operators separately, using $(r+h)^{-p_k}\le r^{-p_k}$. On the part $r\ge h$, use
\[
[T(r+h)-T(r)]X
=\int_r^{r+h}(\partial_\sigma T(\sigma))X\,\mathrm d\sigma
\]
to obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\nabla^k[T(r+h)-T(r)]F(t-r)\|_\infty
\le CM_\gamma\int_r^{r+h}\sigma^{-p_k-1}\,\mathrm d\sigma\le CM_\gamma h r^{-p_k-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
No time derivative of $F$ is used. Since $L\le1$, these bounds yield
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\nabla^kw(t+h)-\nabla^kw(t)\|_\infty
&\le CM_\gamma\left(
3\int_0^h r^{-p_k}\,\mathrm dr
+h\int_h^1r^{-p_k-1}\,\mathrm dr
\right)\\
&\le CM_\gamma
\left(\frac{3}{1-p_k}+\frac{1}{p_k}\right)h^{1-p_k}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking $k=0$ and $k=1$, respectively, gives
\[
\begin{split}
\|w(t+h)-w(t)\|_\infty
&\le C_\gamma M_\gamma h^{(1+\gamma)/2},\\
\|\nabla_gw(t+h)-\nabla_gw(t)\|_\infty
&\le C_\gamma M_\gamma h^{\gamma/2}.
\end{split}
\]
Together with the spatial $C^{1,\beta}$ bound and the smoothness of $S(t-a_0)c(a_0)$ away from $a_0$, these estimates imply $c\in C^{1+\beta,(1+\beta)/2}$ on smaller time intervals. Indeed, $\gamma>\beta$ supplies the required time exponents $(1+\beta)/2$ for $c$ and $\beta/2$ for $\nabla_gc$.

We now obtain the second spatial derivatives and the first time derivative. Since
\[
\operatorname{div}_g b_i
=\Delta_g\log h_i+\Delta_gU_i[c]
=\Delta_g\log h_i-\sum_j a_{ij}(c_j-\rho_j),
\]
the nondivergence form is 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear-form}
\partial_tc_i=\Delta_gc_i-b_i\cdot\nabla_gc_i-q_ic_i,
\qquad
q_i=\Delta_g\log h_i-\sum_j a_{ij}(c_j-\rho_j).
\end{equation}
At this stage the equation holds in distributions, as follows from \eqref{eq:mild}. The preceding spatial regularity justifies the identity $\operatorname{div}_g(c_ib_i)=b_i\cdot\nabla_gc_i+q_ic_i$.

Choose $a_1$ with $a_0<a_1<b$ and work on $J=[a_1,a+L]$. Elliptic regularity gives uniform spatial H\"older bounds for $b_i$ and $q_i$. It also gives, for $s,t\in J$,
\[
\|b_i(t)-b_i(s)\|_\infty
+\|q_i(t)-q_i(s)\|_\infty
\le C\|c(t)-c(s)\|_\infty.
\]
For the first term, this follows from the $W^{2,q}$ estimate with any fixed $q>2$ and the two-dimensional Sobolev embedding. Consequently,
\[
b_i,\ q_i,\ c_i,\ \nabla_gc_i\in C^{\beta,\beta/2}(M\times J).
\]
Define
\[
H_i:=-b_i\cdot\nabla_gc_i-q_ic_i.
\]
The product estimate in the parabolic H\"older space yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|H_i\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}}
\le C\bigl(
&\|b_i\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}}
 \|\nabla_gc_i\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}}\\
&+\|q_i\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}}
 \|c_i\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}}
\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\]
so $(\partial_t-\Delta_g)c_i=H_i$ has a controlled $C^{\beta,\beta/2}$ right-hand side.

We record the interior Schauder estimate in coordinates. Write
\[
\Delta_gu=a^{ab}\partial_{ab}u+d_g^a\partial_au,
\qquad
a^{ab}=g^{ab},\quad
d_g^b=|g|^{-1/2}\partial_a(|g|^{1/2}g^{ab}),
\]
using the Einstein summation convention. For a classical solution of
\[
u_t-a^{ab}u_{ab}-d^au_a+qu=f
\]
and nested coordinate cylinders $Q'\Subset Q$, the interior Schauder estimate \cite[Theorem~4.9]{Lieberman} gives
\[
\|u\|_{C^{2+\beta,1+\beta/2}(Q')}
\le C\left(
\|u\|_{C^0(Q)}+\|f\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}(Q)}
\right).
\]
The constant depends on the ellipticity, the coefficient $C^{\beta,\beta/2}$ bounds and the separation of the cylinders.

To apply this estimate without assuming classical regularity in advance, we approximate the right-hand side of the fixed heat equation. Extend $H_i$ constantly in time beyond the endpoints of $J$ and mollify in a finite atlas. This gives smooth functions $H_{i,\varepsilon}$ satisfying
\[
H_{i,\varepsilon}\longrightarrow H_i
\quad\text{uniformly on }M\times J,
\qquad
\sup_\varepsilon
\|H_{i,\varepsilon}\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}(M\times J)}
\le C\|H_i\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}(M\times J)}.
\]
Define
\[
u_{i,\varepsilon}(t)
=S(t-a_1)c_i(a_1)
+\int_{a_1}^tS(t-s)H_{i,\varepsilon}(s)\,\mathrm ds.
\]
These functions are smooth for $t>a_1$ and solve
\[
(\partial_t-\Delta_g)u_{i,\varepsilon}
=H_{i,\varepsilon}.
\]

Testing the distributional equation for $c_i$ against a Laplace eigenfunction $e_\ell$ gives
\[
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle c_i(t),e_\ell\rangle
+\lambda_\ell\langle c_i(t),e_\ell\rangle
=\langle H_i(t),e_\ell\rangle.
\]
Solving these scalar equations and using continuity at $a_1$ identifies
\[
c_i(t)
=S(t-a_1)c_i(a_1)
+\int_{a_1}^tS(t-s)H_i(s)\,\mathrm ds.
\]
Since $S(t)$ is contractive on $C(M)$,
\[
\sup_{t\in J}\|u_{i,\varepsilon}(t)-c_i(t)\|_\infty
\le L\|H_{i,\varepsilon}-H_i\|_{C^0(M\times J)}
\longrightarrow0.
\]
The interior Schauder estimate for the fixed heat operator, applied to $u_{i,\varepsilon}$, gives on smaller cylinders
\[
\sup_\varepsilon
\|u_{i,\varepsilon}\|_{C^{2+\beta,1+\beta/2}(Q')}
\le C.
\]
Compactness at any smaller H\"older exponent identifies the limit with $c_i$. Lower semicontinuity of the $\beta$-H\"older seminorms preserves the displayed bound. Thus \(c_i\in C^{2+\beta,1+\beta/2}\), and \eqref{eq:linear-form} holds classically.

For \eqref{eq:linear-form}, the coordinate coefficients are
\[
d^a=d_g^a-b_i^a,\qquad q=q_i.
\]
Applying the same estimate with $u=c_i$ and $f=0$ on a finite atlas gives the interior $C^{2+\beta,1+\beta/2}$ bound. We use backward parabolic cylinders ending at times at most $a+L$. The estimates at the upper endpoint follow by limits from earlier times. All bounds on $[b,a+L]$ depend only on the previously controlled norms, the fixed data and the chosen time separations.


\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 3. Higher derivatives.}
We argue by induction. On a larger time interval $J$, assume
\[
\|c\|_{C^{k+1+\gamma,(k+1+\gamma)/2}(M\times J)}
\le C_k,
\qquad k\ge1,\quad 0<\beta<\gamma<1.
\]
Set
\[
L_i=\partial_t-a^{ab}\partial_{ab}-d^a\partial_a+q_i.
\]
For $|\eta|=k$, formal differentiation of $L_ic_i=0$ gives
\[
L_i(D^\eta c_i)=R_{i,\eta},
\]
where
\begin{align}
R_{i,\eta}=
\sum_{0<\nu\le\eta}\binom{\eta}{\nu}
(D^\nu a^{ab})D^{\eta-\nu}\partial_{ab}c_i+\sum_{0<\nu\le\eta}\binom{\eta}{\nu}
(D^\nu d^a)D^{\eta-\nu}\partial_ac_i
-\sum_{0<\nu\le\eta}\binom{\eta}{\nu}
(D^\nu q_i)D^{\eta-\nu}c_i.\nonumber
\end{align}
We set $R_{i,0}=0$. The induction hypothesis and elliptic regularity give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sup_{t\in J}\|\partial_tc(t)\|_{C^{k-1,\gamma}}
&\le C_k,\\
\|c(t)-c(s)\|_{C^{k-1,\beta}}
&\le C_k|t-s|,\\
\|U(t)-U(s)\|_{C^{k+1,\beta}}
&\le C\|c(t)-c(s)\|_{C^{k-1,\beta}}
\le C_k|t-s|.
\end{aligned}
\]
Moreover,
\[
\|D^kc(t)-D^kc(s)\|_\infty
\le C_k|t-s|^{(1+\gamma)/2}.
\]
Together, they give
\[
\|R_{i,\eta}\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}}\le C_k.
\]

To study the differentiation, write $\eta=\xi+e_\ell$ and set
\[
v=D^\xi c_i,\qquad
v_h=\delta_hv
:=\frac{v(t,x+he_\ell)-v(t,x)}{h}.
\]
Here $|\xi|=k-1$, so $v\in C^{2+\gamma,1+\gamma/2}$ by the induction hypothesis. We obtain
\[
(v_h)_t-a_h^{ab}(v_h)_{ab}-d_h^a(v_h)_a+q_{i,h}v_h
=G_h,
\]
where the coefficients with subscript $h$ are evaluated at $(t,x+he_\ell)$, and
\[
G_h=\delta_hR_{i,\xi}
+(\delta_ha^{ab})v_{ab}
+(\delta_hd^a)v_a-(\delta_hq_i)v.
\]
On smaller coordinate cylinders, the preceding bounds give
\[
\sup_{0<|h|<h_0}
\left(
\|v_h\|_{C^0(Q)}
+\|G_h\|_{C^{\beta,\beta/2}(Q)}
\right)\le C_k.
\]
Indeed, $\partial_\ell R_{i,\xi}$ involves at most $k+1$ spatial derivatives of $c$. The interior Schauder estimate therefore yields
\[
\sup_{0<|h|<h_0}
\|v_h\|_{C^{2+\beta,1+\beta/2}(Q')}
\le C_k,
\qquad Q'\Subset Q.
\]
Passing to the limit $h\to0$, using compactness and the lower semicontinuity of the H\"older seminorms, we get
\[
D^\eta c_i\in C^{2+\beta,1+\beta/2}(Q'),
\qquad
L_i(D^\eta c_i)=R_{i,\eta}.
\]

The mixed time derivatives are controlled by
\begin{align}
\partial_t^{\ell+1}c_i
=\Delta_g\partial_t^\ell c_i-\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{r}
\left[
(\partial_t^rb_i)\cdot\nabla_g\partial_t^{\ell-r}c_i
+(\partial_t^rq_i)\partial_t^{\ell-r}c_i
\right],\nonumber
\end{align}
together with, for $\ell\ge1$,
\[
-\Delta_g\partial_t^\ell U_i
=\sum_j a_{ij}\partial_t^\ell c_j,
\qquad
\int_M\partial_t^\ell U_i\,\mathrm dV_g=0.
\]
Thus elliptic regularity and the preceding spatial estimates give, on a smaller time interval $J'$,
\[
\|c\|_{C^{k+2+\beta,(k+2+\beta)/2}(M\times J')}
\le C_{k+1},
\]
which advances the induction by one spatial order. For any prescribed $m$ and $\alpha\in(0,1)$, the above arguments give
\[
\sup_{a+\delta\le t\le a+L}\sum_i
\left(
\|c_i(t)\|_{C^{m,\alpha}}
+\|U_i[c(t)]\|_{C^{m+2,\alpha}}
\right)
\le C(B,L,\delta,m,\alpha).
\]
The constant also depends on the fixed equation and geometric data.

For smooth positive initial densities of the prescribed masses, there exists a unique maximal smooth positive solution on $[0,T_{\max})$. If $T_{\max}<\infty$, then

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1. Construction of a mild solution in short time.} We apply Banach fixed point theorem to achieve this goal. Set
\[
b_i[c]=\nabla_g\log h_i+\nabla_gU_i[c],\qquad F_i(c)=c_i b_i[c].
\] 
By the Sobolev embedding of dimension two and the standard elliptic $W^{2,q}$ estimates on the closed surface $M$ \cite[Chapter~9]{GT}, we get for fixed $q>2$

\begin{align}
\|b[c]-b[d]\|_\infty
\le C\|U[c]-U[d]\|_{W^{2,q}}\le C\|c-d\|_q\le C\|c-d\|_\infty.\nonumber
\end{align}

Here $c$ and $d$ have the same prescribed masses. The product identity $F_i(c)-F_i(d)=(c_i-d_i)b_i[c]+d_i(b_i[c]-b_i[d])$ therefore yields, on any fixed bounded set of densities,

\[
\left\|b[c]-b[d]\right\|_\infty\le C\left\|c-d\right\|_\infty,\qquad
\left\|F(c)-F(d)\right\|_\infty\le L_B\left\|c-d\right\|_\infty.
\]
Take $B=\left\|c_0\right\|_\infty+1$. In $C([0,T];C(M)^n)$ consider the closed set
\[
\mathcal B={\Bigg\{}c:c(0)=c_0,\ \int c_i(t)\,\mathrm dV_g=\rho_i,\
\sup_{0\le t\le T}\left\|c(t)\right\|_\infty\le B{\Bigg\}}.
\]
It is nonempty because it contains $S(t)c_0$. It is complete in the supremum distance. Define
\[
\Phi(c)_i(t)=S(t)c_{i,0}-\int_0^t S(t-s)\operatorname{div}_g F_i(c(s))\,\mathrm ds.
\]

Recall the time-space norm introduced at the beginning of Section~\ref{sec:local} (p.~\pageref{not:time}). On $[0,T]$, it is
\[
\|v\|_{C_tC_x}=\sup_{0\le t\le T}\max_{i\in I}\|v_i(t)\|_\infty.
\]
Let $M_B$ bound $\left\|F(c)\right\|_\infty$ on the ball. By \eqref{eq:heat},
\[\left\|\Phi(c)-S(\cdot)c_0\right\|_{C_tC_x}\le2CM_B\sqrt T\text{ and }\left\|\Phi(c)-\Phi(d)\right\|_{C_tC_x}\le2CL_B\sqrt T\left\|c-d\right\|_{C_tC_x}.\]
Choose $T\le1$ so that $2CM_B\sqrt T\le1$ and $2CL_B\sqrt T\le\tfrac12$. By self-adjointness and $S(r)1=1$,
\[
\int_M S(r)\operatorname{div}_g X\,\,\mathrm dV_g
=-\int_M\langle X,\nabla_gS(r)1\rangle_g\,\,\mathrm dV_g=0.
\]
Consequently, $\int_M\Phi(c)_i(t)\,\,\mathrm dV_g=\rho_i$ and $\|\Phi(c)(t)\|_\infty \le\|c_0\|_\infty+2CM_B\sqrt T\le B$. This implies that $\Phi:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}$. To verify the contraction, we write
\[
W_c(t):=\int_0^tS(t-s)\operatorname{div}_g F(c(s))\,\mathrm ds.
\]
The estimate
\[
\|W_c(t)\|_\infty
\le CM_B\int_0^t(t-s)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm ds
=2CM_B\sqrt t
\]
and strong continuity of $S(t)$ on $C(M)$ give
\[
\|\Phi(c)(t)-c_0\|_\infty
\le\|S(t)c_0-c_0\|_\infty+2CM_B\sqrt t
\longrightarrow0\mbox{ as }t\to0.
\]
For $\varepsilon>0$, set
\[
W_{c,\varepsilon}(t)
:=\int_0^{\max\{t-\varepsilon,0\}}
S(t-s)\operatorname{div}_g F(c(s))\,\mathrm ds.
\]
Since the semigroup time stays away from zero, $W_{c,\varepsilon}\in C([0,T];C(M)^n)$ by heat-kernel smoothing and strong semigroup continuity. Moreover,
\begin{align}
\sup_{0\le t\le T}
\|W_c(t)-W_{c,\varepsilon}(t)\|_\infty
\le CM_B\sup_{0\le t\le T}
\int_{\max\{t-\varepsilon,0\}}^t(t-s)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm ds\le2CM_B\sqrt\varepsilon.\nonumber
\end{align}
Thus $W_c$, and hence $\Phi(c)$, belongs to $C([0,T];C(M)^n)$. This proves $\Phi(\mathcal B)\subseteq\mathcal B$. Furthermore,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\Phi(c)-\Phi(d)\|_{C_tC_x}
&\le CL_B\sup_{0\le t\le T}
\int_0^t(t-s)^{-1/2}\|c(s)-d(s)\|_\infty\,\mathrm ds\\
&\le2CL_B\sqrt T\,\|c-d\|_{C_tC_x}
\le\tfrac12\|c-d\|_{C_tC_x}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore, $\Phi$ is a contraction. Banach's fixed point theorem therefore gives a unique fixed point in $\mathcal B$. The unique existence result  follows.

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 2. Smoothness and positivity.}
Smoothness at positive times follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:smoothing} on each compact subinterval.

For regularity up to $t=0$, fix an integer $s>2$. We construct the same solution in $C([0,T_s];H^s(M)^n)$ for a sufficiently small $T_s>0$. Elliptic regularity \cite[Section~6.3.1, Theorem~2]{Evans} gives
\[
\|\nabla_gU[c]\|_{H^{s+1}}\le C_s\|c\|_{H^s},
\qquad
\|\nabla_g(U[c]-U[d])\|_{H^{s+1}}
\le C_s\|c-d\|_{H^s}.
\]
Since $H^s(M)$ is an algebra, we get the identity
\[
F_i(c)-F_i(d)
=(c_i-d_i)b_i[c]+d_i\bigl(b_i[c]-b_i[d]\bigr)
\]
and the inequalities
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|F(c)\|_{H^s}
&\le C_s\|c\|_{H^s}(1+\|c\|_{H^s}),\\
\|F(c)-F(d)\|_{H^s}
&\le C_s\bigl(1+\|c\|_{H^s}+\|d\|_{H^s}\bigr)
\|c-d\|_{H^s}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here vector-field Sobolev norms are defined in a fixed finite atlas.

We next establish the semigroup estimate needed for the contraction. Write $f_\ell=\langle f,e_\ell\rangle$, where $-\Delta_ge_\ell=\lambda_\ell e_\ell$ is the orthonormal eigenbasis used to define the Sobolev norms. For $0<t\le1$,
\begin{align}
\|S(t)f\|_{H^s}^2=\sum_\ell(1+\lambda_\ell)^s
e^{-2t\lambda_\ell}|f_\ell|^2\le
\sup_{\lambda\ge0}\bigl((1+\lambda)e^{-2t\lambda}\bigr)
\|f\|_{H^{s-1}}^2
\le Ct^{-1}\|f\|_{H^{s-1}}^2,\nonumber
\end{align}
because
\(
(1+\lambda)e^{-2t\lambda}
\le1+(2et)^{-1}\le Ct^{-1}\).
Moreover, the coordinate formula $\operatorname{div}_g X=|g|^{-1/2}\partial_a(|g|^{1/2}X^a)$ gives
\[
\|\operatorname{div}_g X\|_{H^{s-1}}\le C_s\|X\|_{H^s(TM)}.
\]
Taking square roots and combining these estimates yields
\[
\|S(t)\operatorname{div}_g X\|_{H^s}
\le C_st^{-1/2}\|X\|_{H^s(TM)}.
\]
The estimate extends from smooth vector fields to $H^s(TM)$ by density.

Set
\[
B_s=\|c_0\|_{H^s}+1,\qquad
\mathcal X_s=C([0,T_s];H^s(M)^n),
\qquad
\|c\|_{\mathcal X_s}
=\sup_{0\le t\le T_s}\|c(t)\|_{H^s},
\]
and consider the closed subset
\[
\mathcal B_s=
\left\{
c\in\mathcal X_s:
c(0)=c_0,\quad
\int_Mc_i(t)\,\,\mathrm dV_g=\rho_i,\quad
\|c\|_{\mathcal X_s}\le B_s
\right\}.
\]
It contains $S(\cdot)c_0$. Let $M_s$ and $L_s$ be the bound and Lipschitz constant for $F$ on the $H^s$ ball of radius $B_s$. For the same map $\Phi$ as in Step~1, the preceding estimates give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\Phi(c)-S(\cdot)c_0\|_{\mathcal X_s}
&\le C_sM_s\sup_{0\le t\le T_s}
\int_0^t(t-r)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm dr
\le2C_sM_s\sqrt{T_s},\\
\|\Phi(c)-\Phi(d)\|_{\mathcal X_s}
&\le2C_sL_s\sqrt{T_s}\,
\|c-d\|_{\mathcal X_s}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Arguing as in Step~1 in $\mathcal X_s$, we obtain a fixed point of $\Phi$ in $\mathcal B_s$ for sufficiently small $T_s>0$. By uniqueness, it agrees with the previously constructed solution, so $c\in C([0,T_s];H^s(M)^n)$.

Put $N(c)=\Delta_gc-\operatorname{div}_g F(c)$. Since $\partial_tc=N(c)$ in distributions and $N(c)\in C([0,T_s];H^{s-2})$, we have
\[
c(t)=c_0+\int_0^tN(c(r))\,\mathrm dr
\quad\text{in }H^{s-2},
\]
and hence $c\in C^1([0,T_s];H^{s-2})$. The product and elliptic estimates make $N:H^r\to H^{r-2}$ smooth on the space of functions with the prescribed masses. Iterating at lower Sobolev orders gives $c\in C^j([0,T_s];H^{s-2j})$ for $s-2j>2$. For any fixed $m,j$, choosing $s-2j>\max\{m+1,2\}$ and applying Sobolev embedding yields $\partial_t^jc\in C([0,T_s];C^m)$.
Together with Lemma~\ref{lem:smoothing} on positive-time intervals, this proves smoothness up to $t=0$.

We prove positivity by the weak parabolic maximum principle \cite[Section~7.1.4, Theorem~9]{Evans}, giving the comparison argument directly. Fix $T$ within the existence interval and choose $Q\ge\max\{0,\sup_{i,[0,T]\times M}q_i\}$. Set $v_i(t)=(\min_M c_{i,0})e^{-Qt}$ and $P_i=\partial_t-\Delta_g+b_i\cdot\nabla_g+q_i$. Then $P_ic_i=0$ and $P_iv_i=(q_i-Q)v_i\le0$, so $w_i=c_i-v_i$ satisfies $P_iw_i\ge0$ and $w_i(0)\ge0$. Choose $K=1+\sup_{i,[0,T]\times M}|q_i|$ and put $z_i=e^{-Kt}w_i$. It follows that
\[
\partial_tz_i-\Delta_gz_i+b_i\cdot\nabla_gz_i+(q_i+K)z_i\ge0,
\qquad q_i+K\ge1.
\]
If $z_i$ had a negative minimum on $[0,T]\times M$, it would be attained at $(t_*,x_*)$ with $t_*>0$. At that point, $\partial_tz_i\le0$, $\nabla_gz_i=0$ and $\Delta_gz_i\ge0$, so the left-hand side would be strictly negative, a contradiction. There is no spatial boundary because $M$ is closed. Hence $z_i\ge0$ and $c_i\ge v_i>0$ throughout $[0,T]\times M$.


\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 3. Continuation.}
Finally, the construction restarts with a time length depending only on a supremum bound and the fixed data. If a sequence of times tending to a finite $T_{\max}$ had bounded suprema, restarting at those times would extend the unique solution beyond $T_{\max}$. This proves the stated continuation criterion.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:GN}

Fix $p>1$. Let $B_{2r}=B_{2r}(x_0)$ be a sufficiently small geodesic ball and let $f,\eta\in C_c^\infty(B_{2r})$ satisfy $f\ge0$ and $0\le\eta\le1$. Define $v=\eta f^{p/2}$. Then, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GN}
\int v^{2(p+1)/p}\,\mathrm dV_g\le C_p\left(\int_{B_{2r}}f\,\mathrm dV_g\right)
\int|\nabla_g v|^2\,\mathrm dV_g,
\end{equation}
with $C_p$ independent of the small radius and the center.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
For $w\in C_c^1(\mathbb R^2)$, the fundamental theorem of calculus in each coordinate gives
\[
|w(x_1,x_2)|\le\int_{\mathbb R}|\partial_1w(s,x_2)|\,\mathrm ds,
\qquad
|w(x_1,x_2)|\le\int_{\mathbb R}|\partial_2w(x_1,s)|\,\mathrm ds.
\]
Multiplication and integration yield $\left\|w\right\|_2^2\le\left\|\partial_1w\right\|_1\left\|\partial_2w\right\|_1\le\left\|\nabla w\right\|_1^2$.

For $w\in W^{1,1}_0(\mathbb R^2)$, choose $w_k\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^2)$ such that $w_k\to w$ in $W^{1,1}$. After passing to a subsequence, $w_k\to w$ almost everywhere. Fatou's lemma and $\nabla w_k\to\nabla w$ in $L^1$ give
\[
\left\|w\right\|_2^2\le\liminf_{k\to\infty}\left\|w_k\right\|_2^2
\le\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\nabla w_k\right\|_1^2
=\left\|\nabla w\right\|_1^2.
\]
We explain why its constant remains uniform in geodesic coordinates. Choose $0<r_*<\operatorname{inj}(M,g)$ sufficiently small. Since $M$ is compact and the metric is smooth, normal coordinates based on an orthonormal frame at any $x_0\in M$ satisfy
\[
\frac12|\xi|^2\le g_{ab}(y)\xi^a\xi^b\le2|\xi|^2,
\qquad \frac12\le\sqrt{\det g(y)}\le2
\quad(|y|<r_*).
\]
The constants are independent of $x_0$. Indeed, $g_{ab}(0)=\delta_{ab}$ and the coordinate coefficients depend smoothly on the base point, the orthonormal frame and $y$; compactness of the orthonormal frame bundle gives a common radius for these bounds. The inverse matrix has the same eigenvalue bounds. For $2r\le r_*$ and $\widehat w=w\circ\exp_{x_0}$, extended by zero to $\mathbb R^2$, we have
\[
\int_{B_{2r}}|w|^2\,\mathrm dV_g\le2\int_{\mathbb R^2}|\widehat w|^2\,dy,
\qquad
\int_{\mathbb R^2}|D\widehat w|\,dy
\le2\sqrt2\int_{B_{2r}}|\nabla_gw|\,\mathrm dV_g.
\]
Consequently the Euclidean inequality gives
\[
\int_{B_{2r}}|w|^2\,\mathrm dV_g
\le16\left(\int_{B_{2r}}|\nabla_gw|\,\mathrm dV_g\right)^2,
\]
uniformly in $r$ and $x_0$. The same volume-density bounds give $\operatorname{vol}_g(B_s(x_0))\le2\pi s^2$ for $s\le r_*$. Apply the preceding Sobolev inequality to $w=v^{1+1/p}$ and use Cauchy--Schwarz to obtain

\[
\int v^{2(p+1)/p}\,\mathrm dV_g
\le C_p\left(\int v^{1/p}|\nabla_gv|\,\mathrm dV_g\right)^2
\le C_p\int v^{2/p}\,\mathrm dV_g\int|\nabla_gv|^2\,\mathrm dV_g.
\]

The required Sobolev regularity is valid also for noninteger $p>1$. For the nonnegative smooth compactly supported $f$, Taylor's theorem in coordinates gives $|Df|^2\le C_f f$. Thus $|D(f^{p/2})|\le C_{p,f}f^{(p-1)/2}$ on $\{f>0\}$. Regularizing by $(f+\varepsilon)^{p/2}$ and passing to the limit shows that $v\in H^1_0(B_{2r})$. To justify the preceding chain rule, first use $v_N=\min\{v,N\}$. \par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1. The differential inequality.}
Write $b_i=\nabla_g\log h_i+\nabla_gU_i[c]$, so that $\partial_tc_i=\Delta_gc_i-\operatorname{div}_g(c_ib_i)$ and $\operatorname{div}_g b_i=\Delta_g\log h_i-\sum_j a_{ij}(c_j-\rho_j)$. Set
\[
Y_i=\int c_i^p\,\mathrm dV_g,\quad D_i=\int|\nabla_gc_i^{p/2}|^2\,\mathrm dV_g,
\quad Y=\sum_iY_i,\quad D_p=\sum_iD_i.
\]
Strict positivity on compact time intervals justifies testing by $c_i^{p-1}$ even for noninteger $p$. Integration by parts gives
\[
\int_M c_i^{p-1}\Delta_gc_i\,\mathrm dV_g
=-(p-1)\int_Mc_i^{p-2}|\nabla_gc_i|^2\,\mathrm dV_g
=-\frac{4(p-1)}{p^2}D_i.
\]
For the drift term, we have

\[
-\int c_i^{p-1}\operatorname{div}_g(c_ib_i)\,\mathrm dV_g
=\frac{p-1}{p}\int\nabla_g(c_i^p)\cdot b_i\,\mathrm dV_g
=-\frac{p-1}{p}\int c_i^p\operatorname{div}_g b_i\,\mathrm dV_g.
\]
Since $a_{ij}\rho_j\ge0$, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lp-test}
Y'+\frac{4(p-1)}pD_p
\le C_{p,h}Y+(p-1)\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\int c_i^pc_j\,\mathrm dV_g.
\end{equation}

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 2. Absorption on small balls.}

Choose $\delta>0$, to be fixed below, and decrease the radius $r_\delta$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:smallmass} so that $r=r_\delta$ satisfies
\[
\frac{5r}{2}\le r_*,\qquad
c_Vs^2\le\operatorname{vol}_g(B_s(x))\le C_Vs^2
\quad(x\in M,\ 0<s\le r_*),
\]
where $r_*$ is the common normal-coordinate radius from the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:GN}. The metric bounds there hold for $|y|<r_*$. We use a standard covering argument for balls of equal radius. See, for example, \cite[Chapter~I, Section~7.1, p.~32]{SteinHA}. Choose a maximal finite set $\{x_\ell\}_{\ell=1}^N$ satisfying
\[
d_g(x_\ell,x_k)\ge r\quad(\ell\ne k).
\]
Such a set is obtained by successively adding points at distance at least $r$ from all previous centers. Compactness makes this process finite, and maximality gives
\[
M=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^N B_r(x_\ell),\qquad
B_{r/2}(x_\ell)\cap B_{r/2}(x_k)=\varnothing\quad(\ell\ne k).
\]
For fixed $x\in M$, put $N(x)=\#\{\ell\mid x\in B_{2r}(x_\ell)\}$. For every index counted in $N(x)$, $B_{r/2}(x_\ell)\subset B_{5r/2}(x)$. Hence
\[
N(x)c_V(r/2)^2\le\operatorname{vol}_g(B_{5r/2}(x))
\le C_V(5r/2)^2,
\qquad
N(x)\le N_0:=\left\lceil\frac{25C_V}{c_V}\right\rceil.
\]
Here $\lceil x\rceil$ denotes the smallest integer greater than or equal to $x$. Thus the enlarged balls have uniformly bounded overlap. Choose disjoint measurable sets $E_\ell\subset B_r(x_\ell)$ covering $M$ and cutoffs $\eta_\ell$ equal to one on $B_r(x_\ell)$, supported in $B_{2r}(x_\ell)$, with $|\nabla\eta_\ell|\le C/r$.

For each $\ell$, choose $\chi_\ell\in C_c^\infty(B_{2r}(x_\ell))$ with $0\le\chi_\ell\le1$ and $\chi_\ell=1$ on a neighborhood of $\operatorname{supp}\eta_\ell$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:GN} to $f=\chi_\ell c_i$ and $\eta=\eta_\ell$. Then $\eta_\ell f^{p/2}=\eta_\ell c_i^{p/2}$ and
$\int_{B_{2r}(x_\ell)}f\,\mathrm dV_g\le\int_{B_{2r}(x_\ell)}c_i\,\mathrm dV_g<\delta$. Young's inequality and \eqref{eq:GN} therefore give

\[
c_i^pc_j\le\frac p{p+1}c_i^{p+1}+\frac1{p+1}c_j^{p+1},\qquad
\int_{E_\ell}c_i^{p+1}\,\mathrm dV_g\le C_p\delta\int|\nabla_g(\eta_\ell c_i^{p/2})|^2\,\mathrm dV_g.
\]
Expanding the cutoff derivative and using bounded overlap,
\[
\sum_\ell\int|\nabla_g(\eta_\ell c_i^{p/2})|^2\,\mathrm dV_g
\le C D_i+Cr^{-2}Y_i.
\]
Let $R_A=\max_i\sum_j a_{ij}$. Symmetry gives the same bound for column sums. Consequently,
\[
\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\int c_i^pc_j\,\mathrm dV_g
\le R_A\sum_i\int c_i^{p+1}\,\mathrm dV_g
\le C_{p,A}\delta D_p+C_{p,A}\delta r^{-2}Y.
\]
The coefficient of $\delta D_p$ is independent of $r$, $\delta$ and the number of balls. Choose $\delta$ so that $(p-1)C_{p,A}\delta\le2(p-1)/p$ and then fix $r=r_\delta$. We obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lp-absorb}
Y'+\kappa_pD_p\le C_pY,\qquad \kappa_p=2(p-1)/p>0.
\end{equation}

If $A=0$, then \(\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\int_Mc_i^pc_j\,\mathrm dV_g=0\), and \eqref{eq:Lp-absorb} follows directly from \eqref{eq:Lp-test}.


\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 3. A bound uniform in time.}

Since $D_p\ge0$, \eqref{eq:Lp-absorb} and Gronwall's inequality give
\[
Y(t)\le Y(0)e^{C_pt}\qquad(0\le t<T_{\max}).
\]
In particular, for every finite $T>0$, $\sup_{0\le t<\min\{T,T_{\max}\}}Y(t)\le Y(0)e^{C_pT}$. This excludes finite-time divergence of $Y$ on the interval of existence. Global existence of the solution will follow from the supremum estimate and continuation argument in Proposition~\ref{prop:regularity}. To obtain a bound independent of time, apply the cutoff estimate once more with a fixed finite cover and the global mass bound. It yields $\int c_i^{p+1}\,\mathrm dV_g\le C_{p,\rho_i}(D_i+Y_i)$. H\"older's inequality gives
\[
Y_i=\int c_i^{1/p}(c_i^{p+1})^{(p-1)/p}\,\mathrm dV_g
\le\rho_i^{1/p}\left(\int c_i^{p+1}\,\mathrm dV_g\right)^{(p-1)/p}.
\]
With $r_p=p/(p-1)>1$, this implies $Y_i^{r_p}\le C(D_i+Y_i)$. The inequality $Cy\le y^{r_p}/2+C'$ for $y\ge0$, obtained by splitting at $(2C)^{1/(r_p-1)}$, absorbs the last term. Thus
\[
Y^{r_p}\le n^{r_p-1}\sum_iY_i^{r_p}\le C(1+D_p).
\]
Substituting in \eqref{eq:Lp-absorb} gives $Y'+a_pY^{r_p}\le b_p(1+Y)$ for fixed positive $a_p,b_p$. Choose
\[
R\ge\max\{1,(4b_p/a_p)^{1/(r_p-1)}\}.
\]
The derivative is negative whenever $Y\ge R$. Scalar comparison proves $Y(t)\le\max\{Y(0),R\}$ throughout the interval of existence.

\label{lem:LS}

Let $c^*$ be a smooth positive stationary density with $\int_Mc_i^*\,\mathrm dV_g=\rho_i$ and let $0<\alpha<1$. There exist $\theta\in(0,1/2]$, $C>0$ and $\sigma>0$ such that every $c\in C^\infty(M)^n$ belonging to

satisfies

|\mathcal F_\rho(c)-\mathcal F_\rho(c^*)|^{1-\theta}\le C\left\|P\mu(c)\right\|_2,
\qquad (Pf)_i=f_i-\int_M f_i\,\mathrm dV_g,\quad i=1,\ldots,n
\end{equation}
and $\mu$ is defined as in (\ref{def:mu}).

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1. The linearized operator.}
Let 
\[
\mathcal H
:=
\left\{
\xi=(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)\in L^2(M)^n
\;\middle|\;
\int_M \xi_i\,dV_g=0
\quad\text{for every }i=1,\ldots,n
\right\}
\]
and let $X=C^{0,\alpha}(M)^n\cap\mathcal H$, equipped with the H\"older norm. For $\xi\in X$ sufficiently small, set
\[
\mathcal M(\xi):=P\mu(c^*+\xi)
=P\left(
\log\frac{c_i^*+\xi_i}{\rho_i h_i}+1
-\sum_j a_{ij}\mathcal G(c_j^*+\xi_j-\rho_j)
\right)_{i\in I}.
\]
This defines a map from a neighborhood of $0$ in $X$ into $X$. Since $c^*$ is stationary, $\mathcal M(0)=0$. Then, $D\mathcal F_\rho(c^*+\xi)[\zeta]=\langle\mathcal M(\xi),\zeta\rangle$ for $\zeta\in X$, where the inner product is that of $L^2$. The linearization is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hessian}
L\xi=P\left[\left(\frac{\xi_i}{c_i^*}\right)_i-
\left(\sum_j a_{ij}\mathcal G\xi_j\right)_i\right]=L_0\xi-K\xi.
\end{equation}
Here
\[
 (L_0\xi)_i=P_i(\xi_i/c_i^*),\qquad
 (K\xi)_i=\sum_j a_{ij}\mathcal G\xi_j.
\]
$L_0$ is a linear operator from $\mathcal H$ into itself. The Green potentials have zero mean, so $K$ also maps $\mathcal H$ into itself. On both $\mathcal H$ and $X$, the operator $L_0$ has the bounded inverse
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse}
(L_0^{-1}f)_i=c_i^*\left(f_i-\frac1{\rho_i}\int c_i^*f_i\,\mathrm dV_g\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed, for $f\in\mathcal H$, the vector $\xi$ defined by the right-hand
side of \eqref{eq:inverse} satisfies
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_M\xi_i\,\mathrm dV_g
&=\int_Mc_i^*f_i\,\mathrm dV_g
-\frac{\int_Mc_i^*f_i\,\mathrm dV_g}{\rho_i}\int_Mc_i^*\,\mathrm dV_g=0,\\
P_i(\xi_i/c_i^*)
&=P_i\left(f_i-\frac1{\rho_i}\int_Mc_i^*f_i\,\mathrm dV_g\right)
=f_i.
\end{aligned}
\]
Moreover, $L_0\xi=0$ implies $\xi_i=\lambda_i c_i^*$, and $0=\int_M\xi_i\,\mathrm dV_g=\lambda_i\rho_i$ forces $\lambda_i=0$. Thus \eqref{eq:inverse} is indeed the inverse of $L_0$. The Green operator maps $L^2_0$ boundedly into $H^2$, which embeds compactly into $L^2$. Hence $K$ is compact on $\mathcal H$ and $L=L_0(1-L_0^{-1}K)$ is Fredholm of index zero. It is self-adjoint, since $A$ and $G$ are symmetric and $P$ can be omitted when paired against a zero-mean vector. With
\[
\mathcal K=\ker L,\qquad \mathcal R=\mathcal K^\perp,
\]
self-adjointness gives
$(\operatorname{ran}L)^\perp=\ker L$, and closedness of the range therefore yields $\operatorname{ran}L=\mathcal R$. Thus $L:\mathcal R\to\mathcal R$ is a bounded bijection, and the bounded inverse theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L-inverse-bound}
\left\|v\right\|_2\le C_L\left\|Lv\right\|_2\qquad(v\in\mathcal R).
\end{equation}

By \eqref{eq:Hessian}, every $\xi\in\mathcal K$ satisfies
\[
\frac{\xi_i}{c_i^*}
=\lambda_i+\sum_j a_{ij}\mathcal G\xi_j,
\qquad \lambda_i\in\mathbb R,\quad i\in I.
\]
Elliptic regularity, starting from $L^2$, shows that every kernel element is smooth. Let $\{e_\ell\}_{\ell=1}^d$ be a smooth $L^2$-orthonormal basis of $\mathcal K$, where $d=\dim\mathcal K$. Define the orthogonal projection by
\[
\Pi_\mathcal K:\mathcal H\longrightarrow\mathcal K,\qquad
\Pi_\mathcal K v:=\sum_{\ell=1}^d\langle v,e_\ell\rangle_{L^2}e_\ell.
\]
Since each $e_\ell$ is smooth, its restriction to $X$ is bounded. The projection $\Pi_\mathcal R=1-\Pi_\mathcal K$ is also bounded on $X$. For $f\in\mathcal R\cap X$, solve $Lw=f$ in $\mathcal R$ and write $w=L_0^{-1}(f+Kw)$. Since $H^2(M)\hookrightarrow C^{0,\alpha}(M)$ in dimension two for $\alpha<1$,

Hence $L:\mathcal R\cap X\to\mathcal R\cap X$ is also an isomorphism.

\end{aligned}
\]
Recall that $f(0)=\mathcal F_\rho(c^*)$ and $1/2\le a<1$. Combining this estimate with \eqref{eq:finite} and \eqref{eq:reducedgradient}, and shrinking the neighborhood so that $\left\|\mathcal M(\xi)\right\|_2\le1$, yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal F_\rho(c^*+\xi)-\mathcal F_\rho(c^*)|^a
&\le|\mathcal F_\rho(c^*+\xi)-f(z)|^a+|f(z)-f(0)|^a\\
&\le C\left\|\mathcal M(\xi)\right\|_2^{2a}
+C\left\|\Pi_\mathcal K\mathcal M(\xi_0)\right\|_2\\
&\le C\left(\left\|\mathcal M(\xi)\right\|_2^{2a}
+\left\|\mathcal M(\xi)\right\|_2\right)
\le C\left\|\mathcal M(\xi)\right\|_2,
\end{aligned}
\]
where the last inequality uses $2a\ge1$. Set $\theta=1-a$. All analyticity used above concerns the density variable in $X$.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:whole}
The state $c^*$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:subsequence} is the limit of the whole trajectory in $C^m(M)^n$ for every integer $m\geq0$.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1. Length in the gradient neighborhood.}
For all sufficiently large times, fix $0<m_0\le c_i(t,x)\le M_0$. Poincar\'e's inequality implies
\begin{align}
\left\|P\mu(c(t))\right\|_2^2
\le C\sum_i\left\|\nabla_g\mu_i(c(t))\right\|_2^2\le \frac{C}{m_0}\sum_i\int_M
c_i(t,x)|\nabla_g\mu_i(c(t))|^2\,\mathrm dV_g=\frac{C}{m_0}\mathcal D(c(t)).\nonumber
\end{align}
Let $E(t)=\mathcal F_\rho(c(t))-\mathcal F_\rho(c^*)$. Proposition~\ref{prop:subsequence} identifies the energy limit, so $E(t)\ge0$ and $E(t)\to0$. In the neighborhood of Lemma~\ref{lem:LS}, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LSdiss}
E(t)^{1-\theta}\le C\mathcal D(c(t))^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
If $E(t_0)=0$ at a finite time, the energy is constant thereafter. Continuity and the dissipation identity give $\mathcal D=0$, so the solution is stationary. Its subsequential limit identifies it with $c^*$. We may therefore assume $E(t)>0$ at every finite time. In the same neighborhood, \eqref{eq:LSdiss} implies $\mathcal D>0$ and
\[
-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}E^\theta
=\theta\frac{\mathcal D}{E^{1-\theta}}\ge\frac\theta C\mathcal D^{1/2}.
\]
Use $H^{-1}(M)=(H^1(M))^*$. Testing the equation against $\varphi\in H^1$, we get
\[
|\langle\partial_tc_i,\varphi\rangle|
\le\left(\int c_i|\nabla_g\mu_i|^2\,\mathrm dV_g\right)^{1/2}
M_0^{1/2}\left\|\varphi\right\|_{H^1}.
\]
Consequently, as long as the trajectory remains in the neighborhood on $[T,S]$, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:length}
\int_T^S\left\|\partial_tc\right\|_{H^{-1}}\,\mathrm dt
\le C\bigl(E(T)^\theta-E(S)^\theta\bigr)\le CE(T)^\theta.
\end{equation}

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 2. The first-exit argument.}
We prove that the trajectory remains in $\mathcal U$ after some time. Choose $s>3+\alpha$ and an integer $N>s$. With $\beta=(N-s)/(N+1)$, by Sobolev embedding and interpolation, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trap}
\left\|v\right\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}\le C\left\|v\right\|_{H^s}
\le C\left\|v\right\|_{H^{-1}}^\beta\left\|v\right\|_{H^N}^{1-\beta}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, in an orthonormal eigenbasis of $-\Delta_g$, the identity $s=-\beta+(1-\beta)N$ gives
\[
\sum_\ell(1+\lambda_\ell)^s|v_\ell|^2
=\sum_\ell\bigl((1+\lambda_\ell)^{-1}|v_\ell|^2\bigr)^\beta
\bigl((1+\lambda_\ell)^N|v_\ell|^2\bigr)^{1-\beta}.
\]
H\"older's inequality with exponents $1/\beta$ and $1/(1-\beta)$ proves the interpolation estimate (\ref{eq:trap}).

Let $B_N$ bound $\left\|c(t)\right\|_{H^N}$ at large times. Choose $\sigma>0$ so that the closed $C^{2,\alpha}$ ball of radius $\sigma$ about $c^*$ in the fixed-mass class consists of positive densities and lies in $\mathcal U$. Along the subsequence, take $T$ sufficiently large that
\begin{align}\label{ineq:CT<sigma/4}
\left\|c(T)-c^*\right\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}<\sigma/4,
\qquad C(2B_N)^{1-\beta}E(T)^{\beta\theta}<\sigma/4.
\end{align}
The first condition follows from convergence in $C^3$, hence in $C^{2,\alpha}$. We define the first exit time
\[
S=\inf\{t\ge T:\left\|c(t)-c^*\right\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}\ge\sigma\},
\qquad\inf\varnothing=\infty.
\]
By \eqref{ineq:CT<sigma/4} and continuity, $S>T$. Suppose that $S<\infty$. Then, $\left\|c(S)-c^*\right\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}=\sigma$. For $T\le t<S$, the trajectory remains in $\mathcal U$, so \eqref{eq:length} gives
\[
\left\|c(t)-c(T)\right\|_{H^{-1}}
\le\int_T^t\left\|\partial_sc(s)\right\|_{H^{-1}}\,\mathrm ds
\le CE(T)^\theta.
\]
Since $\left\|c(t)-c(T)\right\|_{H^N}\le2B_N$, \eqref{eq:trap} yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left\|c(t)-c(T)\right\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}
&\le C\left\|c(t)-c(T)\right\|_{H^{-1}}^\beta
       \left\|c(t)-c(T)\right\|_{H^N}^{1-\beta}\\
&\le C(2B_N)^{1-\beta}E(T)^{\beta\theta}
<\sigma/4.
\end{aligned}
\]
Passing to $t=S$ by continuity and using \eqref{ineq:CT<sigma/4}, we obtain
\[
\left\|c(S)-c^*\right\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}
\le\left\|c(S)-c(T)\right\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}
+\left\|c(T)-c^*\right\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}
<\sigma/2,
\]
a contradiction. Hence $S=\infty$, and the trajectory remains in $\mathcal U$ for all $t\ge T$.

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 3. Convergence of the whole trajectory.}
We may now apply \eqref{eq:length} to every interval $[T',S]$ with $S\ge T'\ge T$. Since $E(T')\to0$,
\[
\sup_{S\ge T'}\left\|c(S)-c(T')\right\|_{H^{-1}}\le CE(T')^\theta\longrightarrow0.
\]
Completeness of $H^{-1}$ gives convergence. The known subsequence identifies its limit as $c^*$. For each integer $m\ge0$, choose $s>m+1$ and an integer $N>s$, and set $\beta=(N-s)/(N+1)\in(0,1)$. The two-dimensional Sobolev embedding and interpolation give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left\|c(t)-c^*\right\|_{C^m}
&\le C\left\|c(t)-c^*\right\|_{H^s}\\
&\le C\left\|c(t)-c^*\right\|_{H^{-1}}^\beta
       \left\|c(t)-c^*\right\|_{H^N}^{1-\beta}\\
&\le C\left\|c(t)-c^*\right\|_{H^{-1}}^\beta
\longrightarrow0,
\end{aligned}
\]
where the last inequality uses the uniform $H^N$ bound and the smoothness of $c^*$. Letting $S\to\infty$ in \eqref{eq:length} also proves finite $H^{-1}$ length.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:rates}
Under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, let $c(t)$ be the solution and let $c^*=c^\infty$ be its stationary limit. There exists an exponent $\theta\in(0,1/2]$ for which the following estimates hold. Set
\[
 E(t)=\mathcal F_\rho(c(t))-\mathcal F_\rho(c^*),
 \qquad u_i^*=U_i[c^*].
\]
There exist $T\geq0$ and $C,\kappa>0$ such that, for all $t\geq T$, we get
\[
 \|c(t)-c^*\|_{H^{-1}(M)^n}+d_\rho(c(t),c^*)
 \leq C E(t)^\theta.
\]
If $0<\theta<1/2$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rates-algebraic}\begin{aligned}
 &E(t)\leq C(1+t-T)^{-1/(1-2\theta)},\\
 &\|c(t)-c^*\|_{H^{-1}(M)^n}+d_\rho(c(t),c^*)
 \leq C(1+t-T)^{-\theta/(1-2\theta)}.
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
If $\theta=1/2$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rates-exponential}\begin{aligned}
 &E(t)\leq C e^{-\kappa(t-T)},\\
 &\|c(t)-c^*\|_{H^{-1}(M)^n}+d_\rho(c(t),c^*)
 \leq C e^{-\kappa(t-T)/2}.
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
Moreover, for every integer $m\geq0$, every $\alpha\in(0,1)$ and every $b\in(0,1)$, there exists a constant $C_{m,\alpha,b}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rates-high}\begin{split}
 \|c(t)-c^*\|_{C^{m,\alpha}(M)^n}
 &+\sum_i\|U_i[c(t)]-u_i^*\|_{C^{m+2,\alpha}(M)}
 \\ &\leq C_{m,\alpha,b}
 \begin{cases}
 (1+t-T)^{-b\theta/(1-2\theta)},&0<\theta<1/2,\\
 e^{-b\kappa(t-T)/2},&\theta=1/2.
 \end{cases}
\end{split}\end{equation}
All constants depend on the fixed equation data, the trajectory and the limiting stationary density. The higher-norm constants also
depend on $m,\alpha,b$.

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1. Energy and $H^{-1}$ estimates.}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:whole}, the trajectory remains in the neighborhood of Lemma~\ref{lem:LS} for all sufficiently large $t\ge T$. The estimates established in Step~1 of its proof, integrated from $t$ to infinity, give
\[
E(t)^{1-\theta}\le C_0\mathcal D(c(t))^{1/2},
\qquad
\int_t^\infty\mathcal D(c(r))^{1/2}\,\mathrm dr
\le \frac{C_0}{\theta}E(t)^\theta,
\qquad
\|c(t)-c^*\|_{H^{-1}}\le CE(t)^\theta.
\]
Here the second estimate follows from the differential inequality for $E^\theta$, and the third follows from \eqref{eq:length} and convergence to $c^*$.

Set $\kappa=C_0^{-2}$. The energy identity implies
\[
E'(t)=-\mathcal D(c(t))\le-\kappa E(t)^{2(1-\theta)}.
\]
As in Proposition~\ref{prop:whole}, if $E$ reaches zero, the solution equals $c^*$ thereafter. Otherwise, integration gives
\[
E(t)\le
\begin{cases}
\bigl(E(T)^{-q}+q\kappa(t-T)\bigr)^{-1/q},
  &q=1-2\theta>0,\\
E(T)e^{-\kappa(t-T)},&\theta=1/2.
\end{cases}
\]
Together with the preceding $H^{-1}$ estimate, this proves the energy and $H^{-1}$ rates in \eqref{eq:rates-algebraic}--\eqref{eq:rates-exponential}.

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 2. Transport distance.}
Put $p_i=c_i/\rho_i$ and $v_i=-\nabla_g\mu_i$, so that $\partial_t p_i+\operatorname{div}_g(p_iv_i)=0$. On $[t,s]$, let $X_i(r;t,x)$ be the smooth flow of $v_i$. The characteristic representation for the continuity equation (cf.~\cite[Proposition~2.12]{AS}, whose proof applies in local coordinates on $M$) gives
\[
X_i(s;t,\cdot)_\#(p_i(t)\,dV_g)=p_i(s)\,dV_g.
\]
The induced couplings, the bound of endpoint distance by path length, and Minkowski's inequality therefore yield
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_\rho(c(t),c(s))
\le\int_t^s
\left(\sum_i\int_M c_i(r)|v_i(r)|_g^2\,dV_g\right)^{1/2}
\,\mathrm dr=\int_t^s\mathcal D(c(r))^{1/2}\,\mathrm dr.
\end{aligned}
\]
Coupling the common parts of the component measures along the diagonal and their remaining masses arbitrarily gives
\[
d_\rho(c(s),c^*)^2
\le\frac{\operatorname{diam}(M)^2}{2}
\sum_i\|c_i(s)-c_i^*\|_{L^1}\longrightarrow0.
\]
Letting $s\to\infty$ and using Step~1 proves $d_\rho(c(t),c^*)\le CE(t)^\theta$, hence the asserted transport rates.

\par\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Step 3. Higher spatial norms.}
Fix $m\ge0$, $\alpha\in(0,1)$ and $b\in(0,1)$. Choose $s>m+\alpha+1$ and an integer $N>s$ so large that $\beta=(N-s)/(N+1)>b$. Using the same interpolation as in Proposition~\ref{prop:whole}, together with the uniform $H^N$ bounds, gives
\begin{align}
\|c(t)-c^*\|_{C^{m,\alpha}}
\le C\|c(t)-c^*\|_{H^{-1}}^\beta
       \|c(t)-c^*\|_{H^N}^{1-\beta}\le C_{m,\alpha,b}E(t)^{\beta\theta}.
\end{align}
Moreover, \eqref{eq:poisson} and the elliptic Schauder estimate give
\[
\sum_i\|U_i[c(t)]-u_i^*\|_{C^{m+2,\alpha}}
\le C\|c(t)-c^*\|_{C^{m,\alpha}}.
\]
Substituting the energy rates and using $\beta>b$ proves \eqref{eq:rates-high}.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:negative}
If $\Lambda_J(\rho)<0$ for some nonempty $J\subseteq I$, then $\mathcal F_\rho$ is unbounded below on the nonnegative fixed-mass class of finite entropy.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Choose normal coordinates at $p\in M$ and a nonnegative $\phi\in C_c^\infty(B_1(0))$ with $\int_{\mathbb R^2}\phi=1$. Let $J_g$ denote the volume density in these coordinates and set
\[
Z_\varepsilon=\int\phi(z)J_g(\varepsilon z)\,\mathrm dz=1+O(\varepsilon^2),
\qquad
c_i^\varepsilon(\exp_p(\varepsilon z))=
\frac{\rho_i}{Z_\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{-2}\phi(z),\quad i\in J.
\]
Extend these functions by zero outside of $B_1(0)$. Their supports lie strictly inside the coordinate ball, so the extensions are smooth and the change of variables shows that their masses are exactly $\rho_i$. Fix smooth nonnegative densities of the prescribed masses for $i\notin J$. Since $J_g(y)=1+O(|y|^2)$ and $\phi\log\phi$ is integrable,
\[
\int c_i^\varepsilon\log c_i^\varepsilon\,\mathrm dV_g
=2\rho_i\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1)\mbox{ for } i\in J\mbox{ as }\varepsilon\to0.
\]
The coordinate distance is uniformly comparable to $\varepsilon|z-w|$. Thus
\[
\log d_g(\exp_p(\varepsilon z),\exp_p(\varepsilon w))
=\log\varepsilon+\log|z-w|+E_\varepsilon(z,w),\qquad |E_\varepsilon|\le C
\]
off the diagonal. The probability measures $\mathrm d\nu_\varepsilon=Z_\varepsilon^{-1}\phi(z)J_g(\varepsilon z)\,\mathrm dz$ have uniformly bounded densities supported in $B_1$. Local integrability of $|\log|z-w||$ in two dimensions therefore bounds its double integral uniformly. To estimate the interaction energy, \eqref{eq:singularity} gives
\[
\int(c_i^\varepsilon-\rho_i)\mathcal G(c_j^\varepsilon-\rho_j)\,\mathrm dV_g
=\frac{\rho_i\rho_j}{2\pi}\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1)\text{ for }i,j\in J\text{ as }\varepsilon\to0.
\]
If $j\notin J$, its fixed density produces a bounded smooth potential $V_j=\mathcal G(c_j-\rho_j)$ of zero mean and $|\int(c_i^\varepsilon-\rho_i)V_j\,\mathrm dV_g|\le\rho_i\left\|V_j\right\|_\infty$. Symmetry handles the reversed mixed terms. The terms with both indices outside $J$ are fixed, and the external-potential energy remains bounded. Consequently,
\[
\mathcal F_\rho(c^\varepsilon)=\left(2\sum_{i\in J}\rho_i-
\frac1{4\pi}\sum_{i,j\in J}a_{ij}\rho_i\rho_j\right)\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1)
=\frac{\Lambda_J(\rho)}{4\pi}\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1)\longrightarrow-\infty
\]
as $\varepsilon\to0$.

<!-- example-1: body_md -->
Let
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/10\\1/10&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A^{-1}=\frac{100}{99}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1/10\\-1/10&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The matrix is symmetric and entrywise nonnegative, but $a^{11}=a^{22}=100/99>0$ and $a^{12}=a^{21}=-10/99<0$, so $(H_2)$ fails. Taking $\rho_1=\rho_2=2\pi$, we obtain
\[
\frac{\Lambda_{\{1\}}(\rho)}{64\pi^2}
=\frac{\Lambda_{\{2\}}(\rho)}{64\pi^2}=\frac3{16}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\Lambda_{\{1,2\}}(\rho)}{64\pi^2}=\frac{29}{80}>0.
\]
Hence Theorem~\ref{thm:main} applies although $(H_2)$ does not hold. For the same matrix, the different mass vector $\rho=8\pi(11/10,2/5)$ gives
\[
\frac{\Lambda_I(\rho)}{64\pi^2}=\frac{21}{500}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\Lambda_{\{1\}}(\rho)}{64\pi^2}=-\frac{11}{100}<0.
\]
This also shows why the full-set inequality alone is insufficient for the general matrix class considered here.
