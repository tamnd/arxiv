\label{thm:existence}
There exists a finite nonlocal game \(G\) with two players and
payoffs in \(\{0,1\}\) such that
\[
 \omega_q(G)=\omega_{qa}(G)<1=\omega_{qc}(G).
\]
In particular, \(C_{qa}\ne C_{qc}\) for its question and answer sets.

\label{thm:main}\label{thm:construction}
The construction in Sections~\ref{sec:cover}, \ref{sec:obstruction},
and~\ref{sec:translation} specifies
\[
 A\in\operatorname{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb F_2),\qquad b=e_{1{,}417{,}141},\qquad
 m=1{,}417{,}152,\quad n=1{,}889{,}684.
\]
Every row of \(A\) has exactly three nonzero entries, and

\[
 \omega_q(G)=\omega_{qa}(G)<1=\omega_{qc}(G).
\]
Let \(\Sigma=\Gamma(A,b)=\langle S_\Sigma\mid R_\Sigma\rangle\),
where \(S_\Sigma=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n,J_\Sigma\}\) and
\(R_\Sigma\) is the standard relator list obtained from the
solution group equations by writing each \(u=v\) as \(uv^{-1}\).
Then \(J_\Sigma\ne1\). Nevertheless, for every \(\eta>0\)
, there exists \(\delta>0\) such that, for every \(d\in\mathbb N\)
and every homomorphism \(f:F(S_\Sigma)\to\operatorname{U}(d)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-quantifiers}
 \max_{r\in R_\Sigma}\left\lVert f(r)-I_d\right\rVert_{2,d}\le\delta

\end{equation}

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:approx}
Let \(P=\langle S\mid R\rangle\) be a finite presentation and
\(\delta\ge0\). A \(\delta\)-representation is a homomorphism
\(f:F(S)\to\operatorname{U}(d)\), for some \(d\in\mathbb N\), whose maximum relator error satisfies
\[
 \operatorname{err}_R(f)
 :=\max_{r\in R}\left\lVert f(r)-I_d\right\rVert_{2,d}\le\delta.
\]
                      
           
    
                                                             
    
with \(\operatorname{err}_R(f)=0\) when \(R\) is empty.
A word \(v\in F(S)\) is uniformly asymptotically trivial if for
every \(\eta>0\) there is \(\delta>0\) such that every
\(\delta\)-representation, in every dimension, satisfies
\(\left\lVert f(v)-I_d\right\rVert_{2,d}<\eta\).

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:ultraproduct}
Let \(P=\langle S\mid R\rangle\) be a finite presentation and
\(v\in F(S)\). The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item every homomorphism \(P\to\operatorname{U}(\mathcal M)\), for every tracial matrix
ultraproduct \(\mathcal M\), sends the image of \(v\) to \(1\);
\item \(v\) is uniformly asymptotically trivial.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Suppose (ii) fails. There are \(\eta_0>0\), dimensions \(d_k\),
and homomorphisms \(f_k:F(S)\to\operatorname{U}(d_k)\) such that
\[
 \operatorname{err}_R(f_k)\le1/k,\qquad
 \left\lVert f_k(v)-I_{d_k}\right\rVert_{2,d_k}\ge\eta_0.
\]
The unitary classes \([(f_k(s))]\), for \(s\in S\), satisfy every
relator and therefore define \(\pi:P\to\operatorname{U}(\mathcal M)\), with \(\mathcal M\)
as in \eqref{eq:ultraproduct}. Since
\[
 \left\lVert \pi(v)-1\right\rVert_2
   =\lim_{k\to\omega}\left\lVert f_k(v)-I_{d_k}\right\rVert_{2,d_k}\ge\eta_0,
\]
condition (i) fails. This proves (i)\(\Rightarrow\)(ii).

Conversely, assume (ii) and let \(\pi:P\to\operatorname{U}(\mathcal M)\).
Every unitary \(u=[(a_k)]\) in \(\mathcal M\) has unitary matrix
representatives. Indeed, completing the polar part of each \(a_k\)
to a unitary \(u_k\) and using \(|t-1|\le|t^2-1|\) for
\(t\ge0\), we obtain
\[
 \left\lVert a_k-u_k\right\rVert_{2,d_k}
 \le\left\lVert a_k^*a_k-I_{d_k}\right\rVert_{2,d_k}
 \underset{k\to\omega}{\longrightarrow}0.
\]
Thus \([(u_k)]=u\). Choose such representatives for each
\(\pi(s)\) and extend them to homomorphisms
\(f_k:F(S)\to\operatorname{U}(d_k)\). Each relator error tends to zero along
\(\omega\), and finiteness of \(R\) implies the same for
\(\operatorname{err}_R(f_k)\). By (ii),
\(\left\lVert f_k(v)-I_{d_k}\right\rVert_{2,d_k}\) also tends to zero along
\(\omega\). Taking ultralimits gives \(\left\lVert \pi(v)-1\right\rVert_2=0\),
and hence \(\pi(v)=1\).

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:embedding-direction}
Let \(\iota:P\to Q\) be a homomorphism and \(v\in P\).
If every homomorphism from \(P\) into the unitary group of a
tracial matrix ultraproduct sends \(v\) to \(1\), then every such
homomorphism \(\rho\) from \(Q\) satisfies
\(\rho(\iota(v))=(\rho\circ\iota)(v)=1\).
If \(\iota\) is injective and \(v\ne1\), then \(\iota(v)\ne1\).
For finitely presented groups, Lemma~\ref{lem:ultraproduct}
converts the vanishing conclusion into uniform asymptotic
triviality of a word representing \(\iota(v)\).
No extension of approximate representations is required.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:infranormal}
For \(H\le Q\), its compression semigroup is
\[
 P_H=\{t\in Q:tHt^{-1}\le H\}.
\]
The subgroup \(H\) is infranormal in \(Q\) if
\(\langle P_H\rangle=Q\), where generation is as a group and
therefore allows inverses. An element \(t\in P_H\) is a strict
compressor if \(tHt^{-1}<H\).

Let \(H\le Q\) be Kazhdan groups, with \(H\) infranormal in \(Q\).
For every homomorphism \(\pi:Q\to\operatorname{U}(\mathcal M)\) into a tracial matrix
ultraproduct,

 \pi(q)\bigl(\pi(H)'\cap\mathcal M\bigr)\pi(q)^{-1}
   =\pi(H)'\cap\mathcal M\qquad(q\in Q).
\]

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:finite-cover}
For every $d\ge1$, the group $\Delta_d$ is finitely presented and
Kazhdan. If an associative unital $\mathbb F_5$-algebra $R$ is generated as a unital
algebra by $a_1,\ldots,a_d$, there is a surjective homomorphism
\[
 \Delta_d\longrightarrow\operatorname{EL}_3(R),\qquad
 Y_{ij}(0)\longmapsto e_{ij}(1),\qquad
 Y_{ij}(m)\longmapsto e_{ij}(a_m)\quad(1\le m\le d).
\]

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Apply \cite[Theorem~6.9]{EJZ} with $n=3$, base field $\mathbb F_5$, and
the free associative algebra $\mathbb F_5\langle z_1,\ldots,z_d\rangle$.
The field has cardinality $5$, as required when $n<7$. Its basis
over the prime field is $\{1\}$, so the defining relations
in that theorem specialize to (E0)--(E4).
Family (E5) is tautological: for distinct $i,k\in I$ there is only
one possible intermediate index. Family (E6) applies only to the
base ring $\mathbb Z$. Thus the cover in that theorem is exactly
$\Delta_d$, proving property~(T).

For general $R$, the substitution $z_m\mapsto a_m$ is a surjective
algebra homomorphism. Applied to matrix entries, it induces a
surjection between elementary groups, since every coefficient in
$R$ has a preimage. Composing with the cover of the free algebra
gives the asserted map.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:double-obstruction}
Let \(Q\) be finitely presented, let \(H\le Q\) be finitely generated,
and suppose that both groups have property~\((T)\).
Assume \(H\) is infranormal in \(Q\) and choose
\[
 t\in Q,\quad tHt^{-1}<H,\qquad h\in H\setminus tHt^{-1}.
\]
In \(D=Q*_H Q\), write \(t_1,t_2\) for the copies of \(t\),
and identify \(h\) with its common image in the two factors. Set
\[
 w=[h,t_2t_1^{-1}].
\]
Then \(D\) is finitely presented, \(w\) has infinite order, and
every homomorphism \(D\to\operatorname{U}(\mathcal M)\) into the unitary group of a
tracial matrix ultraproduct sends \(w\) to the identity.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Choose a finite presentation \(Q=\langle S_Q\mid R_Q\rangle\)
and words \(v_1,\ldots,v_s\) in \(S_Q\) representing generators of \(H\).
Take two copies of this presentation and add the relations
\[
 v_j(S_Q^{(1)})=v_j(S_Q^{(2)})\qquad(1\le j\le s),
\]
where \(S_Q^{(i)}\) denotes the generator set in copy \(i\).
Agreement on these generators implies agreement on all of \(H\).
The universal property therefore identifies the resulting finitely
presented group with \(D\). Only finite generation of \(H\) is needed.

Expanding the commutator gives
\[
 w=h t_2t_1^{-1}h^{-1}t_1t_2^{-1},\qquad
 t_2^{-1}wt_2=(t_2^{-1}ht_2)(t_1^{-1}h^{-1}t_1).
\]
Since \(h\notin tHt^{-1}\), we have \(t^{-1}ht\notin H\).
Its inverse \(t^{-1}h^{-1}t\) is also outside \(H\).
Thus the two factors in the conjugate above lie outside \(H\)
and belong to different copies of \(Q\). For every integer
\(k\ge1\), the \(k\)-th power of this conjugate is a reduced product
of \(2k\) alternating factors, so it is nonidentity by the normal
form theorem. Hence \(w\)
has infinite order.

Now let \(\pi:D\to\operatorname{U}(\mathcal M)\) be any homomorphism and put
\(C=\pi(H)'\cap\mathcal M\). Set \(c=t_1^{-1}t_2\).
For \(a\in H\), compression gives \(tat^{-1}\in H\), so its
copies \(t_1at_1^{-1}\) and \(t_2at_2^{-1}\) coincide in \(D\).
Consequently
\[
 cac^{-1}
 =t_1^{-1}(t_2at_2^{-1})t_1
 =t_1^{-1}(t_1at_1^{-1})t_1=a.
\]
Thus \(c\) commutes with \(H\), and \(\pi(c)\in C\).
Since \(Q,H\) are Kazhdan and \(H\) is infranormal,
Theorem~\ref{thm:normalization} applied to the first factor gives
\(\pi(t_1)C\pi(t_1)^{-1}=C\). Hence
\[
 \pi(t_2t_1^{-1})
 =\pi(t_1ct_1^{-1})=\pi(t_1)\pi(c)\pi(t_1)^{-1}\in C.
\]
This operator commutes with \(\pi(h)\), giving \(\pi(w)=1\).

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:central-obstruction}
Let \(D,w\) be as in Proposition~\ref{prop:double-obstruction},
and let \(S_D\) be a finite generating list of \(D\).
Adjoin two generators \(J,z\) and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda-presentation}
 \Lambda=
 \left\langle D,J,z\ \middle|\
 \begin{array}{l}
 J^2=1,\quad [J,g]=1\ (g\in S_D),\\
 {}[J,z]=1,\quad zwz^{-1}=wJ
 \end{array}\right\rangle .
\end{equation}
Here \(D\) abbreviates a chosen finite presentation.
Then \(\Lambda\) is finitely presented, \(J\) is a nontrivial
central involution, and every homomorphism
\(\Lambda\to\operatorname{U}(\mathcal M)\) into the unitary group of a tracial matrix
ultraproduct sends \(J\) to the identity.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let \(C_2=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\) and \(B=D\times C_2\).
Identify \(D\) with \(D\times\{0\}\) and put \(J=(1,1)\).
Then \(J\) has order two and commutes with \(D\).
Since \(w\) has infinite order, the map
\[
 \mathbb Z\times C_2\longrightarrow E:=\langle w,J\rangle\le B,
 \qquad (k,\epsilon)\longmapsto w^kJ^\epsilon
\]
is an isomorphism. Indeed, it is surjective, and
\(w^kJ^\epsilon=1\) forces \(w^k=1\) and \(\epsilon=0\)
in the two coordinates, hence \(k=0\).

The assignments \(\alpha(w)=wJ\) and \(\alpha(J)=J\)
correspond to
\[
 (k,\epsilon)\longmapsto(k,\epsilon+k),
\]
with the second coordinate computed modulo \(2\).
This map preserves addition and its square is the identity,
so \(\alpha\) is an automorphism of \(E\).
Form the HNN extension of \(B\) with this automorphism and
stable letter \(z\). Its conjugation relations reduce to
\(zwz^{-1}=wJ\) and \(zJz^{-1}=J\): for every \(k\in\mathbb Z\)
and \(\epsilon\in C_2\), these imply
\[
 z(w^kJ^\epsilon)z^{-1}
 =(wJ)^kJ^\epsilon
 =w^kJ^{k+\epsilon}
 =\alpha(w^kJ^\epsilon).
\]
This calculation is valid for negative \(k\) because \(w\)
and \(J\) commute. Since \(S_D\) generates \(D\), its commutation
relations with \(J\), together with \(J^2=1\), present
\(B=D\times C_2\) over \(D\). Thus the HNN presentation is
exactly \eqref{eq:lambda-presentation}.

The normal form theorem embeds \(B\) in \(\Lambda\), so \(J\)
remains nontrivial and has order two. The defining relations make
it commute with \(D\) and \(z\), so it is central.
The presentation is finite because \(D\) is finitely presented
and only finitely many generators and relations were added.

Finally, if \(\pi:\Lambda\to\operatorname{U}(\mathcal M)\) is a homomorphism,
its restriction to \(D\) sends \(w\) to the identity by
Proposition~\ref{prop:double-obstruction}. Applying \(\pi\)
to \(zwz^{-1}=wJ\) gives
\[
 1=\pi(z)\pi(w)\pi(z)^{-1}=\pi(w)\pi(J)=\pi(J).\qedhere
\]

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
After each scanned prefix, the stack is reduced and represents that
prefix: appending preserves reduction unless the last pair cancels,
in which case popping performs that cancellation. Thus the first step
preserves the element of $F(S_\Lambda)$. Removing the end letters of
$aua^{-1}$ replaces it by a conjugate, as does rotating $uv$ to
$vu=u^{-1}(uv)u$. Conjugation and inversion preserve the normal
closure of a relator. Deleting trivial words or repetitions and
reordering also preserve the collective normal closure. Hence the
two presentations have the same kernel in $F(S_\Lambda)$, so their
identification fixes every generator.

$\left\lVert U-I_d\right\rVert_{2,d}$ is unchanged by unitary conjugation or inversion.

deleting repetitions or zero errors preserves their maximum.
This proves the defect identity.

noninverse end letters. Rotation and inversion preserve these cyclic
adjacency conditions, so every retained word is cyclically reduced.

finite enumeration; Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives its complete length

 \label{eq:last-wheel-word}

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Each block in~\eqref{eq:involution-substitution} alternates between
two distinct letters. Blocks from different original generators use
disjoint pairs, so their boundary letters differ. For the same generator,
a positive block begins with $u_k$ and ends with $v_k$, whereas a
negative block begins with $v_k$ and ends with $u_k$. Equal boundary
letters therefore occur exactly for opposite signs, which would give
an adjacent inverse pair in $r_i$. Free reduction excludes this within
the word, and cyclic reduction excludes it at the last and first
blocks. Thus $\phi(r_i)$ is cyclically reduced as displayed and has
ordinary length $4|r_i|$.

Each original signed occurrence contributes twice to each of its two
ordinary letters, so every multiplicity is even. The appended relator
is also cyclically reduced, has ordinary length four, and contains
each of $y_{145},y_{146}$ twice. Every relator therefore has length
at least four, and the adjacency condition is vacuous. This proves
collegiality and~\eqref{eq:last-wheel-word}; summing lengths gives

four recovers its original signed tuple. Distinct $r_i$ therefore give
distinct reduced words. The appended relator has central exponent one,
so differs from all of them. Hence there are exactly $N_0+1$ explicit
relators. This is the even exponent argument used in the proof of

\label{prop:commuting}

<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
On $\ell^2(\Sigma)$, with orthonormal basis
$\{\delta_g:g\in\Sigma\}$, define the left and right regular
representations by
\[
 \lambda(g)\delta_k=\delta_{gk},\qquad
 \rho(g)\delta_k=\delta_{kg^{-1}}.
\]
These are unitary representations and commute with one another,
as their actions on the basis show. Let
\[
 \mathcal H_-=\{\xi\in\ell^2(\Sigma):\lambda(J_\Sigma)\xi=-\xi\},
 \qquad
 \Omega=\frac{\delta_1-\delta_{J_\Sigma}}{\sqrt2}.
\]
Since $J_\Sigma\ne1$ and $J_\Sigma^2=1$, the vector $\Omega$ is
a unit vector in $\mathcal H_-$. Centrality of $J_\Sigma$ and commutation
of the two regular representations make $\mathcal H_-$ invariant under
all $\lambda(g)$, $\rho(g)$, and their inverses.

Set $X_e=\lambda(x_e)|_{\mathcal H_-}$ and
$Y_e=\rho(x_e)|_{\mathcal H_-}$. They are selfadjoint unitaries because
$x_e^2=1$. For each row $r$, its three $X_e$ commute and satisfy
\[
 \prod_{j=1}^3X_{e_{r,j}}=(-1)^{b_r}I_{\mathcal H_-}.
\]
Define Alice's and Bob's measurement effects by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint-projectors}
 P^r_a=\prod_{j=1}^3
       \frac{I_{\mathcal H_-}+(-1)^{a_j}X_{e_{r,j}}}{2},
 \qquad
 Q^e_\beta=\frac{I_{\mathcal H_-}+(-1)^\beta Y_e}{2},
\end{equation}
where $a\in\mathbb F_2^3$ and $\beta\in\mathbb F_2$.
For a selfadjoint unitary $Z$, $(I+Z)/2$ and $(I-Z)/2$ are
orthogonal projections summing to the identity. The commuting
factors in Alice's product therefore give mutually orthogonal
projections whose sum is the identity; the same holds for Bob.
All Alice effects commute with all Bob effects because left and
right regular operators commute.

By~\eqref{eq:joint-projectors},
$X_{e_{r,j}}P^r_a=(-1)^{a_j}P^r_a$. Multiplying these identities
and using the row product gives
\[
 (-1)^{a_1+a_2+a_3}P^r_a=(-1)^{b_r}P^r_a.
\]
Thus $P^r_a=0$ whenever $a$ has the wrong parity.
Moreover, centrality of $J_\Sigma$ and $x_e^{-1}=x_e$ imply
\[
 X_e\Omega
 =\frac{\delta_{x_e}-\delta_{x_eJ_\Sigma}}{\sqrt2}
 =Y_e\Omega.
\]
At incidence $e=e_{r,j}$, Alice's marginal for bit $a_j$ is
$(I+(-1)^{a_j}X_e)/2$, obtained by summing over the other bits.
The probability of disagreement with Bob is consequently
\begin{align*}
 &\left\langle\Omega,
 \left(\frac{I+X_e}{2}\frac{I-Y_e}{2}
       +\frac{I-X_e}{2}\frac{I+Y_e}{2}\right)\Omega\right\rangle\\
 &\qquad=\frac12\bigl(1-\langle\Omega,X_eY_e\Omega\rangle\bigr)
 =\frac14\left\lVert (X_e-Y_e)\Omega\right\rVert^2=0.
\end{align*}
Here the norm is the Hilbert space norm; the second equality uses
commutation and $X_e^2=Y_e^2=I$. Both winning conditions therefore
hold at every incidence with probability one.
