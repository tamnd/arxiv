<!-- fact-1: body_md -->
\label{fact:baire}
Let $Y$ be a Polish space and $f\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}$ a function. The following are
equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)}]
\item the function $f$ is Baire-1;
\item for every open $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}$, the set $f^{-1}[U]$ is $F_\sigma$;
\item for every closed $F\subseteq Y$, the restriction $f|_F$ has a point of
continuity.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, if $f$ is not Baire-1, then there are countable sets
$D_0,D_1\subseteq Y$ with $\overline{D_0}=\overline{D_1}$ and reals $a<b$ such
that $f\le a$ on $D_0$ and $f\ge b$ on $D_1$.
<!-- end fact-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:relcompact}
Let $Y$ be a Polish space and $\mathcal R\subseteqB_1(Y)$ a pointwise bounded
family. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item the family $\mathcal R$ is relatively compact in $B_1(Y)$;
\item every countable subset of $\mathcal R$ has an accumulation point in
$B_1(Y)$;
\item we have $\overline{\mathcal R}\subseteqB_1(Y)$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
This is the argument of Bourgain, Fremlin and
Talagrand~\cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}. The implication (iii)$\Rightarrow$(i)
is Tychonoff's theorem. If some $f\in\overline\mathcal R$ is not Baire-1, take $D_0$,
$D_1$, $a$ and $b$ as in Fact~\ref{fact:baire}; a sequence in $\mathcal R$ converging
to $f$ on $D_0\cup D_1$ has no accumulation point in $B_1(Y)$, because such
a point would have no point of continuity on $\overline{D_0}$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:IP}
Let $Y$ be a set. A family $\mathcal R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^Y$ has the \emph{independence
property} if there are a sequence $(r_n)$ in $\mathcal R$ and reals $a<b$ such that
for all finite disjoint sets $E,F\subseteq\mathbb{N}$, there is $y\in Y$ with
$r_n(y)\le a$ for $n\in E$ and $r_n(y)\ge b$ for $n\in F$. Otherwise, the
family $\mathcal R$ is \emph{NIP}.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- fact-2: body_md -->
\label{fact:rosenthal}
Let $Y$ be a Polish space and $(r_n)$ a pointwise bounded sequence in $C_p(Y)$.
Then the sequence $(r_n)$ has either a pointwise convergent subsequence or a
subsequence whose closure in $\mathbb{R}^Y$ is homeomorphic to $\beta\mathbb{N}$.
<!-- end fact-2 -->
<!-- fact-3: body_md -->
\label{fact:BFT}
Let $Y$ be a Polish space and $\mathcal R\subseteqC_p(Y)$ a pointwise bounded family.
Then $\overline{\mathcal R}\subseteqB_1(Y)$ if and only if for every sequence $(r_n)$
in $\mathcal R$ and all reals $a<b$, there is $I\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ such that no $y\in Y$
satisfies $r_n(y)\le a$ for all $n\in I$ and $r_n(y)\ge b$ for all
$n\notin I$.
<!-- end fact-3 -->
<!-- keytheorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:dichotomy}
Let $Y$ be a compact metrizable space and $\mathcal R\subseteqC_p(Y)$ a pointwise
bounded family. Then exactly one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item we have $\overline{\mathcal R}\subseteqB_1(Y)$;
\item the family $\mathcal R$ has the independence property.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end keytheorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For a sequence $(r_n)$ in $\mathcal R$, reals $a<b$ and $I\subseteq\mathbb{N}$, the sets
$\{y:r_n(y)\le a\}$ for $n\in I$ and $\{y:r_n(y)\ge b\}$ for $n\notin I$ are
closed, because each function $r_n$ is continuous. By compactness of $Y$, these
closed sets have empty intersection if and only if finitely many of them do.
Hence the condition in Fact~\ref{fact:BFT} fails for some $(r_n)$, $a$ and $b$
exactly when every finite pattern is realized, that is, exactly when the
family $\mathcal R$ has the independence property.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:NIPclosure}
Let $Y$ be a topological space, $\mathcal R\subseteqC_p(Y)$ a family and $L\subseteq Y$
a dense set. Then the family $\mathcal R$ is NIP if and only if the family
$\{r|_L:r\in\mathcal R\}$ of restrictions is NIP.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
If $(r_n)$ and $a<b$ witness the independence property of $\mathcal R$, then, for
$a<a'<b'<b$, the open sets on which a finite pattern holds strictly with
$a'$ and $b'$ meet $L$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:symmetry}
For a formula of a first-order theory, NIP is symmetric in the two variables:
$\varphi(x,y)$ is NIP if and only if $\varphi^{\mathrm{opp}}(y,x)=\varphi(x,y)$ is
NIP (see~\cite[Chapter~II, Section~4]{Shelah:1990}
and~\cite[Lemma~2.5]{simon2015nip}); if $\varphi$ has VC dimension $d$, then
$\varphi^{\mathrm{opp}}$ has VC dimension less than
$2^{d+1}$~\cite{Assouad:1983}. The finite combinatorial argument applies, with
the reals $a<b$ fixed, to real-valued functions. For the uniform notion of
Remark~\ref{rem:uniform}, the roles of Alice and Bob are therefore
interchangeable. Definition~\ref{def:IP}, which asks for an infinite sequence
inside a single family, is not symmetric: the relation $a\in b$ between
numbers $a$ and finite sets $b\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ has the independence property in the
variable $a$ but not in the variable $b$.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:equivalent}
Let $X$ be a Polish space.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)}]
\item Two pointwise bounded families $\{f_i:i\in I\}$ and $\{g_i:i\in I\}$ of
real functions, indexed by the same countable set $I$, are \emph{equivalent}
if the map $f_i\mapsto g_i$ extends to a homeomorphism between their pointwise
closures.
\item A family $\{f_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ that is relatively compact in $B_1(X)$
is \emph{minimal} if it is equivalent to $\{f_{s_t}:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ for every
dyadic subtree $(s_t)$ of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end definition-2 -->

\label{fact:hepta}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item Up to equivalence, there are exactly seven minimal families, namely
$D_1,\dots,D_7$, and they are pairwise non-equivalent.
\item Let $X$ be a Polish space and $\{f_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ a family that is
relatively compact in $B_1(X)$. Then there is a regular dyadic subtree
$(s_t)$ of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ such that $\{f_{s_t}:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is equivalent to one
of $D_1,\dots,D_7$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- fact-5: body_md -->
\label{fact:tricho}
Let $K$ be a separable Rosenthal compactum and $\{f_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ a countable
dense subset of $K$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If $K$ consists of bounded functions, is metrizable and is not separable
in the supremum norm, then $D_2$ canonically embeds into $K$.
\item If $K$ is hereditarily separable but not metrizable, then $D_3$ or
$D_4$ canonically embeds into $K$.
\item If $K$ is first countable but not hereditarily separable, then $D_6$ or
$D_7$ canonically embeds into $K$.
\item If $K$ is not first countable, then $D_5$ canonically embeds into $K$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:NIPlevels}
Let $Y$ be a compact metrizable space and $\mathcal R\subseteqC_p(Y)$ a pointwise
bounded NIP family whose closure $\overline\mathcal R$ is separable. The family $\mathcal R$
is \emph{NIP$_1$}, \emph{NIP$_2$} or \emph{NIP$_3$} if $\overline\mathcal R$ is,
respectively, first countable, hereditarily separable or metrizable.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:countable-probes}
Let $Y$ be a set and $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^Y$ a set that is compact in the topology of
pointwise convergence. Then $K$ is metrizable if and only if some countable
set $D\subseteq Y$ separates $K$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Suppose that a countable set $D$ separates $K$. Restriction to $D$ is a
continuous injection of $K$ into $\mathbb{R}^D$, which is metrizable because $D$ is
countable. A continuous injection of a compact space into a Hausdorff space is
a homeomorphism onto its image, so $K$ is metrizable.

Conversely, suppose that $K$ is metrizable. Being compact, it is second
countable, and so is $K\times K$. Hence the open set
$W=\{(f,g)\in K\times K:f\ne g\}$ is Lindel\"of. For $y\in Y$ the set
$U_y=\{(f,g)\in K\times K:f(y)\ne g(y)\}$ is open, and these sets cover $W$.
If $\{U_y:y\in D\}$ is a countable subcover, then $D$ separates $K$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:levels-separation}
Let $Y$ be a compact metrizable space and $\mathcal R\subseteqC_p(Y)$ a pointwise
bounded NIP family whose closure $K=\overline\mathcal R$ is separable.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item The family is NIP$_3$ if and only if one countable set $D\subseteq Y$
separates $K$.
\item The family is NIP$_1$ if and only if every $f\in K$ is \emph{countably
determined}: there is a countable set $D_f\subseteq Y$ such that $f$ is the
only member of $K$ that agrees with $f$ on $D_f$.
\item The family is NIP$_2$ if and only if there is no set $E\subseteq K$ of
cardinality $2^{\aleph_0}$ whose members are \emph{finitely separated}: for each
$f\in E$ there are a finite set $F_f\subseteq Y$ and $\varepsilon_f>0$ such
that every $g\in E\setminus\{f\}$ satisfies $|g(y)-f(y)|\ge\varepsilon_f$ for
some $y\in F_f$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
(a) is Proposition~\ref{prop:countable-probes}. (b) If $f$ is countably
determined, then $\{f\}$ is the intersection of the countably many open sets
$\{g\in K:|g(y)-f(y)|<\frac1m\}$, for $y\in D_f$ and $m\ge1$, and in a
compact Hausdorff space a point that is a countable intersection of open sets
has a countable base of neighbourhoods. Conversely, if $(U_k)$ is a countable
base at $f$ and each $U_k$ contains a basic neighbourhood defined by a finite
set $F_k\subseteq Y$, then $D_f=\bigcup_kF_k$ works: a member of $K$ that
agrees with $f$ on $D_f$ lies in every $U_k$. (c) A set is finitely separated
exactly when it is a discrete subspace, and a separable Rosenthal compactum is
hereditarily separable if and only if it has no discrete subspace of
cardinality $2^{\aleph_0}$, by a theorem of
Todor\v{c}evi\'c~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire} (see
also~\cite[Theorem~0.4]{glasner2022tame}).

\label{lem:probe-space}
Let $C$ be an observable on $H_1\otimes H_2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item The probe space $Y_C$ is compact and metrizable.
\item Every response is continuous on $Y_C$ and bounded by $\|C\|$, so the
response family $\mathcal R_C$ is a pointwise bounded subset of $C_p(Y_C)$.
\item The restrictions to $\mathfrak A_C$ of the actual states of Bob's system form a
dense subset of $Y_C$, and for $\sigma\in\mathfrak D(H_2)$ we have
$r_\rho(\sigma|_{\mathfrak A_C})=\varphi_C(\rho,\sigma)$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
(a) Since $H_1$ is separable, the space $\mathcal T(H_1)$ is separable, and since the
slice map is norm continuous, its image is separable, and hence so is the
C$^*$-algebra $\mathfrak A_C$ that the image generates. Hence the compact space $Y_C$ is metrizable.

(b) The response $r_\rho$ is evaluation at the element $T_C(\rho)$ of $\mathfrak A_C$,

\cite[Lemma~2.2]{IovinoStability}). Restriction to $\mathfrak A_C$ is weak$^*$ continuous
and onto, so it maps a dense set onto a dense set. The formula is the
definition of the effective observable.

\label{lem:closure}
Let $C$ be an observable on $H_1\otimes H_2$. For a state $\omega$ of $B(H_1)$,
let $r_\omega\colon Y_C\to\mathbb{R}$ be given by

\[

<!-- keytheorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:levels}
Let $C$ be an observable on $H_1\otimes H_2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item The observable $C$ is stable if and only if every idealized response is
continuous, that is, $\overline{\mathcal R_C}\subseteqC_p(Y_C)$.
\item The observable $C$ is NIP for Alice's responses if and only if every
idealized response is Baire-1, that is, $\overline{\mathcal R_C}\subseteqB_1(Y_C)$.
In that case, the set $\overline{\mathcal R_C}$ of idealized responses is a
Rosenthal compactum.
\item If the observable $C$ is not NIP for Alice's responses, then the response
family $\mathcal R_C$ has the independence property.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end keytheorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:probe-space}, Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy} applies to the
family $\mathcal R_C\subseteqC_p(Y_C)$, and parts (b) and (c) follow from it together
with Lemma~\ref{lem:relcompact}.

For part (a), we use the characterization of stability by weak compactness
(see \cite[Theorem~5.1]{IovinoStability}). Recall that a bounded operator
between Banach spaces is \emph{weakly compact} if it maps bounded sets to
relatively weakly compact sets, and that, by Gantmacher's theorem, this holds
exactly when its bitranspose takes values in the original target space.
Suppose first that $C$ is stable. Then the slice map is weakly compact as a
map into $B(H_2)$, and hence also as a map into the closed subspace $\mathfrak A_C$,
whose weak topology is the restriction of that of $B(H_2)$. It follows that
the bitranspose $T_C^{**}$ maps $B(H_1)^*$ into $\mathfrak A_C$, and
every idealized response $r_\omega$ is evaluation at an element of $\mathfrak A_C$, hence
continuous on $Y_C$.

Conversely, suppose that every idealized response is continuous, and let
$\omega$ be a state of $B(H_1)$. The element $T_C^{**}(\omega)$ of $\mathfrak A_C^{**}$ is
self-adjoint, so the idealized response $r_\omega$ is a continuous affine real
function on $Y_C$. By Kadison's function representation, there is a self-adjoint
$a\in\mathfrak A_C$ with $r_\omega(\tau)=\tau(a)$ for all $\tau\in Y_C$. The elements
$T_C^{**}(\omega)$ and $a$ of $\mathfrak A_C^{**}$ agree on all states, and states span
$\mathfrak A_C^*$, so $T_C^{**}(\omega)=a\in B(H_2)$. Since this holds for every state
$\omega$, the observable $C$ is stable (see \cite[Theorem~5.1]{IovinoStability}).

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:uniform}
In~\cite{IovinoStability}, stability (no infinite ladder) is distinguished from
uniform stability (bounded ladders at every margin, equivalently stability on
ultrapowers). The same distinction appears here. Definition~\ref{def:IP} forbids
an infinite independent sequence, whereas \emph{uniform NIP} bounds, at each
margin, the size of finite independent sets, that is, the fat-shattering
dimension. Uniform NIP implies NIP. Comparisons $[A>B]$ of two quantities are
uniformly NIP, with bounds independent of the Hilbert spaces
(Corollary~\ref{cor:comparisons-uniform}), and they are unstable when both
quantities have discrete spectra unbounded above, by the ladder argument of
Section~\ref{sec:orders}. By Theorem~\ref{thm:levels}, their idealized
responses form a Rosenthal compactum of Baire-1 functions, not all
continuous. As in Part~I, the uniform notion is the one that survives
ultrapowers: a family is uniformly NIP if and only if its extension to every
ultrapower is NIP. Here the extension of a family to an ultrapower is the
family of ultralimits of sequences of responses, evaluated at ultralimits of
sequences of probes, and a finite set of responses is \emph{$\delta$-shattered}
if it is independent, in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:IP}, with a gap
$b-a\ge\delta$. By \L o\'s's theorem, a $\delta$-shattered set of size $n$ in an
ultrapower gives, for every $\delta'<\delta$, a $\delta'$-shattered set of size
$n$ in the original family. Hence, if the original family has finite
fat-shattering dimension at every margin, so does its extension, which then
has no infinite independent sequence; conversely, if
the fat-shattering dimension at some margin is infinite, then in an ultrapower
by a countably incomplete ultrafilter the finite shattered sets combine, by
$\aleph_1$-saturation, into an infinite independent sequence.

<!-- keytheorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:convex}
Let $Y$ be a set and $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^Y$ a separable compact convex set, with the
topology of pointwise convergence, that is a Rosenthal compactum. If $K$ is
hereditarily separable, then $K$ is metrizable.
<!-- end keytheorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Suppose that $K$ is hereditarily separable but not metrizable. By
Fact~\ref{fact:tricho}(b), there is a homeomorphism $h$ from $\hat S_+(2^\mathbb{N})$ or
$\hat S_-(2^\mathbb{N})$ onto a subspace of $K$. We treat the case
$\hat S=\hat S_+(2^\mathbb{N})$. The other case is symmetric: the map $c$ that
exchanges the digits $0$ and $1$ reverses $\prec$, and the map
$f\mapsto1-f\circ c$ carries $D_4$ to a family of the form of $D_3$ for the
tree $c(R)$. Write $Q=\{s^\frown0^\infty:s\in R\}$ for the countable set of
points indexing $D_3$, and $\Sigma$ for the closure of $Q$ in $2^\mathbb{N}$. The
idea is the one described before the theorem: the midpoints of the images
under $h$ of the split pairs of $\hat S$ form a subspace of $K$ that is not
separable.

We first record the structure of $\hat S$. If $(\sigma_i)$ is a strictly
increasing sequence in $2^\mathbb{N}$ converging to $\sigma$, then
$x^\pm_{\sigma_i}\to x^+_\sigma$ pointwise; if it is strictly decreasing, then
$x^\pm_{\sigma_i}\to x^-_\sigma$. Indeed, for $\tau\in2^\mathbb{N}$, the value of
$x^\pm_{\sigma_i}$ at $\tau$ is eventually $1$ if $\tau\succ\sigma$, eventually
$0$ if $\tau\prec\sigma$, and at $\tau=\sigma$ it is eventually $1$ in the
increasing case and $0$ in the decreasing case. Since $\hat S$ is
Fr\'echet--Urysohn (see~\cite[Theorem~3F]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire})
and every sequence of distinct points of $2^\mathbb{N}$ has a strictly monotone
subsequence, every point of $\hat S\setminus D_3$ has the form $x^+_\rho$ or
$x^-_\rho$ with $\rho\in\Sigma$; and if $x^{e_i}_{\sigma_i}\in\hat S$ for a
strictly monotone sequence $(\sigma_i)$, then the limit computed above lies in
$\hat S$, because $\hat S$ is closed. Note also that $x^+_\sigma=x^-_\rho$
exactly when $\sigma$ is the immediate successor of $\rho$ in $\prec$, and that
two of these functions with the same sign are equal only when their indices
are.

We use the following fact repeatedly: if $A\subseteq2^\mathbb{N}$ is uncountable, then
all but countably many points of $A$ are limits of strictly increasing and of
strictly decreasing sequences from $A$. Indeed, if $\sigma\in A$ is not a limit
of a strictly increasing sequence from $A$, there is $\tau_\sigma\prec\sigma$
with no point of $A$ strictly between them, and the intervals
$(\tau_\sigma,\sigma]$ for distinct such $\sigma$ are disjoint; each of them
either contains a basic clopen set or makes $\tau_\sigma$ the immediate
predecessor of $\sigma$, and both happen for only countably many $\sigma$. The
decreasing case is the same. Let $P$ be the set of
$\sigma\in\Sigma$ that are limits of strictly increasing and of strictly
decreasing sequences from $Q$. The set $\Sigma$ is uncountable, because
distinct branches of the dyadic tree $R$ give distinct limits, and all but
countably many points of $\Sigma$ belong to $P$, by the same argument applied
to $Q$ (an open interval that misses $Q$ also misses $\Sigma$); hence the set
$P$ is uncountable. For $\sigma\in P$, both $x^+_\sigma$ and $x^-_\sigma$
belong to $\hat S$, they differ at $\sigma$, and neither equals $x^\pm_\rho$
for $\rho\ne\sigma$, because $\sigma$ has neither an immediate predecessor nor
an immediate successor in $\prec$.

The split pairs indexed by $P$ now supply the midpoints. For $\sigma\in P$,
put $F^\pm_\sigma=h(x^\pm_\sigma)$ and $M_\sigma=\frac12(F^+_\sigma+F^-_\sigma)$,
which lies in $K$ by convexity. Averaging is continuous for the pointwise
topology; hence, if $(\sigma_i)$ is a strictly increasing (respectively,
decreasing) sequence in $P$ converging to $\sigma$, then
$M_{\sigma_i}\to h(x^+_\sigma)$ (respectively, $h(x^-_\sigma)$). The proof
that the midpoints form a non-separable subspace takes three steps.

\emph{Step 1: each value is taken by countably many midpoints.} Let $m\in K$
and suppose that $A=\{\sigma\in P:M_\sigma=m\}$ is uncountable. Choose
$\sigma\in A$ that is a limit of strictly increasing and of strictly
decreasing sequences from $A$. Then $m=h(x^+_\sigma)$ and $m=h(x^-_\sigma)$,
so $x^+_\sigma=x^-_\sigma$, a contradiction.

\emph{Step 2: few midpoints lie on the copy of $\hat S$.} Let
$E=\{\sigma\in P:M_\sigma\in h(\hat S)\}$. By Step 1 and the countability of
$D_3$, all but countably many $\sigma\in E$ satisfy
$M_\sigma=h(x^{e(\sigma)}_{\rho(\sigma)})$ for some sign $e(\sigma)$ and some
$\rho(\sigma)\in\Sigma$, and $\rho(\sigma)\ne\sigma$, since a midpoint of two
distinct points differs from both. Suppose that uncountably many such
$\sigma$ exist. Fix a metric $d$ on $2^\mathbb{N}$; then for some $n$ the set $E_n$ of
these $\sigma$ with $d(\sigma,\rho(\sigma))\ge1/n$ is uncountable. Choose
$\sigma\in E_n$ and a strictly monotone sequence $(\sigma_i)$ in $E_n$
converging to $\sigma$, say increasing. Passing to a subsequence, we may
assume that the signs $e(\sigma_i)$ are constant and that the points
$\rho(\sigma_i)$ are either constant or strictly monotone, converging to some
$\rho^*$. Then the midpoints $M_{\sigma_i}$ converge both to
$h(x^+_\sigma)$ and to $h(x^{\pm}_{\rho^*})$. Since $h$ is injective, we have
$x^+_\sigma=x^\pm_{\rho^*}$, and since $\sigma\in P$ has no immediate
predecessor, we have $\rho^*=\sigma$. Hence
$d(\sigma_i,\rho(\sigma_i))\to0$, contradicting $\sigma_i\in E_n$.
Consequently, the set $E$ is countable.

\emph{Step 3: the midpoints form a non-separable subspace.} Let
$P'=P\setminus E$, which is uncountable, and $L=\{M_\sigma:\sigma\in P'\}$. Let
$D\subseteq L$ be countable. By Step 1, there is $\sigma\in P'$ with
$M_\sigma\notin D$. If $M_\sigma$ were in the closure of $D$, then, since
Rosenthal compacta are Fr\'echet--Urysohn
(see~\cite[Theorem~3F]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}), there would be a sequence $(\sigma_i)$ in $P'$ with $M_{\sigma_i}\in D$ and
$M_{\sigma_i}\to M_\sigma$. As $M_\sigma\notin D$, no point occurs infinitely
often in $(\sigma_i)$, so a subsequence is strictly monotone and converges to
some $\rho\in\Sigma$, and then $M_{\sigma_i}$ converges to $h(x^+_\rho)$ or
$h(x^-_\rho)$. Thus $M_\sigma\in h(\hat S)$, contradicting $\sigma\notin E$.
Hence the subspace $L$ is not separable, and the space $K$ is not
hereditarily separable, a contradiction.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:convex-literature}
The proof uses convexity only to know that midpoints of points of $K$ lie in
$K$. The same midpoints settle a question about the split interval
$S=[0,1]\times\{0,1\}$, ordered lexicographically, with the order topology. In
a remark following Proposition~10 of~\cite{Godefroy:1980}, the space $P(S)$ of
probability measures on $S$ is stated to be hereditarily separable. This is
not the case. For $0<a<1$, let $a^-=(a,0)$ and $a^+=(a,1)$, and let $m_a$ be the
midpoint $\frac12(\delta_{a^-}+\delta_{a^+})$. The set $\{s\in S:s\le a^-\}$ is
clopen, so its indicator $f_a$ is continuous, and $m_a(f_a)=\frac12$, while
$m_b(f_a)\in\{0,1\}$ for $b\ne a$. Hence $\{m_a:0<a<1\}$ is an uncountable
discrete subspace of $P(S)$, exactly as in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:convex}. Todor\v{c}evi\'c has pointed out to us that
Theorem~\ref{thm:convex} is known, and that it holds in the larger class of
locally connected Rosenthal compacta, which contains the convex ones: it
follows from Theorems~5 and~7 of~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire}
and an argument of Gruenhage, Lemma~2.4 of~\cite{Gruenhage:1988}. The proof
above is direct and uses only the midpoints, the same midpoints that refute
the remark of~\cite{Godefroy:1980}. The theorem answers, for convex
compacta, the question of which classes of the Todor\v{c}evi\'c trichotomy occur, in the spirit
of the hierarchy of tame systems of Glasner and
Megrelishvili~\cite{glasner2022tame}.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:product}
Let $C_R$ be a relation observable. For states $\rho$ of Alice's system and
$\sigma$ of Bob's,
\[
\varphi_{C_R}(\rho,\sigma)=(\mu_\rho\otimes\mu_\sigma)(R)=\int g_\rho\,d\mu_\sigma,
\qquad g_\rho(y)=\mu_\rho(R^y),
\]
and the effective observable $T_{C_R}(\rho)$ is multiplication by $g_\rho\circ\beta$.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
For unit vectors $\psi\in H_1$ and $\chi\in H_2$, with distributions
$\mu_\psi$ and $\mu_\chi$ in the corresponding pure states, Tonelli's theorem
and a change of variables give
\[
\langle\psi\otimes\chi,C_R(\psi\otimes\chi)\rangle
=\iint\mathbf 1_R\bigl(\alpha(s),\beta(t)\bigr)\,|\psi(s)|^2|\chi(t)|^2\,dm_1(s)\,dm_2(t)
=(\mu_\psi\otimes\mu_\chi)(R).
\]
A density operator is a countable convex combination of pure states,
$\rho=\sum_jp_j|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j|$, converging in trace norm, and then
$\mu_\rho=\sum_jp_j\mu_{\psi_j}$. Both sides of the first identity are affine in
each variable and preserve such combinations, so the first identity holds for
all states; the second is Tonelli's theorem, and $g_\rho$ is measurable by the
same theorem. Finally, for every unit vector $\chi\in H_2$,
\[
\langle\chi,T_{C_R}(\rho)\chi\rangle=\varphi_{C_R}(\rho,|\chi\rangle\langle\chi|)
=\int g_\rho\bigl(\beta(t)\bigr)|\chi(t)|^2\,dm_2(t),
\]
and a bounded operator on a complex Hilbert space is determined by these values.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:monotone-shatter}
Let $\delta>0$ and $m=\lceil9/\delta\rceil$. Let $h_1,\dots,h_n\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$
be functions of the form $h_i=u_i-v_i$, where $u_i,v_i\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]$ are
nondecreasing, and let $a<b$ be reals with $b-a\ge\delta$. Suppose that for every
$S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ there is a Borel probability measure $\nu_S$ on $\mathbb{R}$
with $\int h_i\,d\nu_S\ge b$ for $i\in S$ and $\int h_i\,d\nu_S\le a$ for
$i\notin S$. Then $2^n\le(2nm+1)^m$. In particular, $n\le N(\delta)$, where
$N(\delta)$ is the largest integer $n$ satisfying this inequality; it is finite
and depends only on $\delta$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
We first approximate a measure by $m$ equally weighted points. Let $\nu$ be a
Borel probability measure on $\mathbb{R}$, with distribution function
$F(t)=\nu((-\infty,t])$. For $k=1,\dots,m$, let $y_k=\min\{t:F(t)\ge k/m\}$; the
minimum exists because $F$ is nondecreasing and right continuous, with limits
$0$ and $1$ at $-\infty$ and $+\infty$. Let
$\nu'=\frac1m\sum_{k=1}^m\delta_{y_k}$. Since $y_k\le t$ exactly when
$F(t)\ge k/m$, we have $\nu'((-\infty,t])=\lfloor mF(t)\rfloor/m$, so
$|\nu((-\infty,t])-\nu'((-\infty,t])|\le1/m$ for every $t$, and letting $t$
increase to a given point, the same bound holds for the half-lines
$(-\infty,t)$. Hence $|\nu(J)-\nu'(J)|\le1/m$ for every set $J$ of the form
$\emptyset$, $\mathbb{R}$, $[t,\infty)$ or $(t,\infty)$. For a nondecreasing
$u\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]$, every set $\{u>s\}$ has this form, and
$\int u\,d\nu=\int_0^1\nu(\{u>s\})\,ds$; therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quantile}
\Bigl|\int u\,d\nu-\int u\,d\nu'\Bigr|\le\frac1m.
\end{equation}

Next we discretize the functions. Let $\tilde u_i=\lfloor mu_i\rfloor/m$ and
$\tilde v_i=\lfloor mv_i\rfloor/m$. These functions are nondecreasing, take
values in $\{0,\frac1m,\dots,1\}$, and differ from $u_i$ and $v_i$ by at most
$\frac1m$. Each of the $2n$ functions $\tilde u_i,\tilde v_i$ changes value at
most $m$ times along $\mathbb{R}$, so the map
$Q(y)=(\tilde u_1(y),\dots,\tilde u_n(y),\tilde v_1(y),\dots,\tilde v_n(y))$ takes
at most $2nm+1$ values.

For each $S$, let $y^S_1,\dots,y^S_m$ be the points constructed from $\nu_S$ as
above, and let $P(S)=(Q(y^S_1),\dots,Q(y^S_m))$. By~\eqref{eq:quantile},
applied to $u_i$ and to $v_i$, and by the bound on the discretization,
\[
\Bigl|\int h_i\,d\nu_S-\frac1m\sum_{k=1}^m\bigl(\tilde u_i(y^S_k)-\tilde v_i(y^S_k)\bigr)\Bigr|
\le\frac4m\le\frac{4\delta}9 ,
\]
and the average on the left is determined by $P(S)$. If $S\ne S'$, choose $i$
in one of the two sets but not the other, say $i\in S\setminus S'$. Then
$\int h_i\,d\nu_S-\int h_i\,d\nu_{S'}\ge\delta>\frac{8\delta}9$, so the two
averages differ, and $P(S)\ne P(S')$. Thus the map $S\mapsto P(S)$ is injective,
and $2^n\le(2nm+1)^m$.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rem:commutative}
In the orders of this section, as in the label-controlled observables of
Section~\ref{sec:hepta}, the probe algebra is commutative, and the responses are
integrals against classical probability distributions. This does not reduce the
paper to classical probability. First, the definitions of
Section~\ref{sec:responses} and Theorems~\ref{thm:levels},
\ref{thm:observable-classes} and~\ref{thm:convex} are proved for every
observable on $H_1\otimes H_2$, with no commutativity assumption, and they apply
equally to observables such as the swap test, whose probe algebra contains all
compact operators. The examples show that every class of the classification is
inhabited, and that commuting observables already suffice is information in
itself, just as in model theory the dividing lines are first exhibited by the
simplest structures, such as the random graph and the dense linear order.
Second, the framework imposes restrictions that hold for all observables: the
idealized responses always form a compact convex set, because they are the
affine image of the state space of $B(H_1)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:closure}), and by
Corollary~\ref{cor:NIP2} convexity alone removes a class of the
Todor\v{c}evi\'c trichotomy. Third, noncommutativity enters where the physics
requires it: in Section~\ref{sec:ssb} the responses are magnetizations of the
Ising model in a transverse field, a Hamiltonian whose terms do not commute, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:qssb} shows that the classification detects its phase
transition. Finally, whenever an observable is a relation between commuting
quantities measured by Alice and by Bob, Lemma~\ref{lem:product} reduces its
classification to classical probability, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:uniform-orders} shows how that reduction yields bounds
that do not depend on the Hilbert spaces. Whether noncommutative probe algebras
produce phenomena that commuting observables cannot is
Question~\ref{q:noncommutative}.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:monotone-nets}
Let $\nu$ be a Borel probability measure on $K$ and $(g_i)$ a net in $M_K$
converging pointwise to $g$. Then $\int g_i\,d\nu\to\int g\,d\nu$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Let $\varepsilon>0$ be given. Since $\nu$ has only finitely many atoms of mass
at least $\varepsilon$, there are points $0=p_0<p_1<\cdots<p_m=1$ of $K$ such
that each open cell $c_k=K\cap(p_{k-1},p_k)$ has $\nu(c_k)<\varepsilon$. Put
$\eta_i=\max_k|g_i(p_k)-g(p_k)|$, which tends to $0$. On $c_k$ both $g_i$ and
$g$ take values between their values at $p_k$ and at $p_{k-1}$, because they
are nonincreasing. Hence
\[
\Bigl|\int_{c_k}g_i\,d\nu-\int_{c_k}g\,d\nu\Bigr|
\le\nu(c_k)\bigl(g(p_{k-1})-g(p_k)+2\eta_i\bigr),
\]
and summing over $k$, together with the contribution of the points $p_k$, gives
$|\int g_i\,d\nu-\int g\,d\nu|\le\varepsilon+3\eta_i$, since the differences
$g(p_{k-1})-g(p_k)$ add up to at most $1$.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:order-responses}
In both cases, the probe algebra $\mathfrak A_C$ is $\{f(B):f\in C(K)\}$, so the probe
space $Y_C$ is the space $P(K)$ of Borel probability measures on $K$ with the
weak$^*$ topology. The map $\Phi\colon r\mapsto\bigl(r(\delta_y)\bigr)_{y\in K}$
is a homeomorphism from $\overline{\mathcal R_C}$ onto $M_K$, and every idealized
response is given by $r(\nu)=\int\Phi(r)\,d\nu$.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
The functions $g_\rho$ for states $\rho$ whose distribution has full support
in $K$ separate the points of $K$, and the map $f\mapsto f(B)$ is an isometric
$*$-isomorphism from $C(K)$ onto its image, because the spectrum of $B$ is
$K$. By the Stone--Weierstrass theorem, the unital C$^*$-algebra generated by
the operators $g_\rho(B)$ is $\{f(B):f\in C(K)\}$, and the slice map takes
values in it. By the Riesz representation theorem, its state space is $P(K)$,
and the response of $\rho$ is $r_\rho(\nu)=\int g_\rho\,d\nu$.

The map $\Phi$ is continuous, because its coordinates are evaluations. Let
$r\in\overline{\mathcal R_C}$ be the pointwise limit of a net $(r_{\rho_i})$. Then
$g_{\rho_i}=\Phi(r_{\rho_i})$ converges pointwise to $g=\Phi(r)\in M_K$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:monotone-nets} gives
$r(\nu)=\lim_i\int g_{\rho_i}\,d\nu=\int g\,d\nu$.
Thus $r$ is determined by $\Phi(r)$, the map $\Phi$ is injective, and, as a
continuous injection from a compact space, it is a homeomorphism onto its
image. The image is compact and contains the functions $g_\rho$.

It remains to be shown that the functions $g_\rho$ are dense in $M_K$. Let
$g\in M_K$ and a finite set $F\subseteq K$ containing $0$ and $1$ be given. In
the continuous case, the piecewise linear interpolation $\tilde g$ of $g$ on
$F$ is continuous and nonincreasing, with values $1$ at $0$ and $0$ at $1$;
hence the function $\tilde g$ is $g_\rho$ for a state whose distribution has
the absolutely continuous distribution function $1-\tilde g$, and it agrees
with $g$ on $F$. In the discrete
case, write $F=\{f_0<f_1<\cdots<f_m\}$, with $f_0=0$ and $f_m=1$, and choose
a point $f^*\in K$ with $f_{m-1}<f^*<1$. The probability measure with mass
$1-g(0)$ at $0$, mass $g(f_{j-1})-g(f_j)$ at $f_j$ for $0<j<m$, and mass
$g(f_{m-1})$ at $f^*$ is the distribution of a state $\rho$ with finitely
many photons, and $g_\rho$ agrees with $g$ on $F$. Hence every neighbourhood of $g$ contains some $g_\rho$.

\label{lem:label}
Let $C$ be as in Definition~\ref{def:label}, and suppose that the functions
$f_i$ separate the points of $L$. Then the probe algebra is
$\mathfrak A_C=\{M_f:f\in C(L)\}$, the probe space $Y_C$ is the space $P(L)$ of Borel

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:transfer}
Let $L$ be a compact metrizable space and $(f_t)_{t\in T}$ a countable family
of continuous functions $L\to[0,1]$ that is relatively compact in $B_1(L)$.
For $t\in T$ and $\nu\in P(L)$, put $F_t(\nu)=\int f_t\,d\nu$. Then the
families $\{F_t\}$, of functions on $P(L)$, and $\{f_t\}$, of functions on $L$,
are equivalent, and $\{F_t\}$ is NIP.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
Every sequence $(f_{t_n})$ has a subsequence that converges pointwise on $L$.
Indeed, by Fact~\ref{fact:rosenthal}, otherwise a subsequence would have
closure homeomorphic to $\beta\mathbb{N}$, which is not Fr\'echet--Urysohn, while the
closure of $\{f_t\}$ is a Rosenthal compactum and therefore Fr\'echet--Urysohn
(see~\cite[Theorem~3F]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}). By the dominated convergence
theorem, the corresponding subsequence of $(F_{t_n})$ converges pointwise on
$P(L)$.

Consequently the family $\{F_t\}$ is NIP. If a sequence $(F_{t_n})$ and reals
$a<b$ witnessed the independence property, then, since $P(L)$ is compact and
the functions $F_t$ are continuous, for each $E\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ there would be
$\nu$ with $F_{t_n}(\nu)\le a$ for $n\in E$ and $F_{t_n}(\nu)\ge b$ for
$n\notin E$ (see the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy}); the same holds for
every subsequence, and taking $E$ to contain every other index of a
subsequence shows that no subsequence converges at the corresponding $\nu$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy}, the closure of $\{F_t\}$ is contained in
$B_1(P(L))$; it is a Rosenthal compactum, hence Fr\'echet--Urysohn.

Let $\Phi$ send a function $G$ on $P(L)$ to the function
$x\mapsto G(\delta_x)$ on $L$. The map $\Phi$ is continuous for the pointwise
topologies, and $\Phi(F_t)=f_t$. The map $\Phi$ is injective on the closure of $\{F_t\}$:
if $G$ lies in that closure, then $G$ is the pointwise limit of a sequence
$(F_{t_n})$, so $(f_{t_n})$ converges pointwise to $\Phi(G)$, which is
therefore a bounded Borel function, and by dominated convergence
$G(\nu)=\lim_n\int f_{t_n}\,d\nu=\int\Phi(G)\,d\nu$ for every $\nu$. Thus $G$ is
determined by $\Phi(G)$. As a continuous injection on a compact space, the
map $\Phi$ is a homeomorphism from the closure of $\{F_t\}$ onto its image,
which is compact, contains $\{f_t\}$ and is contained in the closure of
$\{f_t\}$; hence the image is that closure.

<!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{lem:restrict}
Let $L$ be a set, $L_0\subseteq L$, and $(g_t)_{t\in T}$ a countable pointwise
bounded family of real functions on $L$ whose pointwise closure $K$, a compact
space, is Fr\'echet--Urysohn. Suppose that
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item the restrictions $g_t|_{L_0}$ are pairwise distinct, and none of them lies
in the closure of the others;
\item any two limits of sequences $(g_{t_k})$ with pairwise distinct indices
that agree on $L_0$ are equal.
\end{enumerate}
Then the families $(g_t)$ and $(g_t|_{L_0})$ are equivalent.
<!-- end lemma-12 -->
<!-- proof-29: body_md -->
Restriction $\pi\colon K\to\mathbb{R}^{L_0}$ is continuous, and its image is the closure of
$(g_t|_{L_0})$. Let $f\in K$ be different from every $g_t$. Since $K$ is
Fr\'echet--Urysohn, $f$ is the limit of a sequence $(g_{t_k})$; since $K$ is
Hausdorff and $f$ is not a $g_t$, no index occurs infinitely often, so after
passing to a subsequence the indices are pairwise distinct. By~(i), $\pi$ is injective on the family, and $\pi(g_t)$ is not
$\pi(f)$ for such a limit $f$, because $\pi(f)$ lies in the closure of
$\{g_s|_{L_0}:s\ne t\}$. By~(ii), $\pi$ is injective on the limits. Hence $\pi$ is a
continuous injection of the compact space $K$, and so a homeomorphism onto its
image.

<!-- proposition-8: body_md -->
\label{prop:label-NIP}
Let $C$ be a label-controlled observable whose functions $f_i$ separate the
points of $L$ and have the property that every sequence of them has a
subsequence converging pointwise on $L$. Then $C$ is NIP for Alice's
responses.
<!-- end proposition-8 -->
<!-- proof-31: body_md -->
For a density operator $\rho$, put $p_i=\langle e_i,\rho e_i\rangle$, a probability
distribution on $I$, and $g_\rho=\sum_ip_if_i$, a norm-convergent sum in
$C(L)$; then $r_\rho(\nu)=\int g_\rho\,d\nu$. By dominated convergence, a
sequence of functions $f_i$ that converges pointwise on $L$ is weakly Cauchy in
$C(L)$; hence the set $\{f_i\}$ is weakly precompact. By Stegall's theorem,
so is its absolutely convex hull, and so is the norm closure of that hull:
given $y_n$ in the closure, choose $x_n$ in the hull with $\|x_n-y_n\|<1/n$ and a
weakly Cauchy subsequence of $(x_n)$; the corresponding subsequence of $(y_n)$
is weakly Cauchy. The partial sums of $g_\rho$ lie in the hull; hence every
function $g_\rho$ lies in this closure. Consequently, every sequence
$(r_{\rho_n})$ has a subsequence that converges pointwise on $P(L)=Y_C$, and,
as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer}, an independent sequence has no
such subsequence.

\label{lem:ssb-closure}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item every $g_{a,c}$ with $-1<a<1$ belongs to $\overline\mathcal R$;

\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-13 -->

is closed and $g_{a,c}\to g_{a,\pm m^*}$ pointwise as $c\to\pm m^*$, it suffices

\item \emph{Lee--Yang theorem}~\cite{LeeYang:1952}
(see~\cite[Chapter~3]{FriedliVelenik:2017}). Let $G$ be a finite graph with

functions on a connected open set $U$ that is uniformly bounded on each compact
subset of $U$ and converges at every point of a set with an accumulation point
in $U$ converges uniformly on compact subsets of $U$; by Weierstrass's
theorem, so do the derivatives.
\end{enumerate}

\label{prop:qLY}

Write $X=\beta J\sum_{x\sim y}\sigma^z_x\sigma^z_y+hS_N$, which is diagonal in the
basis $\{|s\rangle:s\in\{-1,1\}^{\Lambda_N}\}$ of eigenvectors of the operators $\sigma^z_x$, and

exponential series converges locally uniformly in $h$.

\emph{Step 1: the Lie--Trotter formula.} For matrices $A$ and $B$ we have
$e^{A/M}e^{B/M}=I+(A+B)/M+O(M^{-2})$, uniformly for $A$ and $B$ in a bounded set;
taking the principal logarithm, $e^{A/M}e^{B/M}=\exp\bigl((A+B)/M+R_M\bigr)$ with
$\|R_M\|=O(M^{-2})$, and hence $(e^{A/M}e^{B/M})^M=\exp(A+B+MR_M)\to e^{A+B}$,
uniformly for $A$ and $B$ in a bounded set. Applied to $X$ and $Y$, this gives
$Z_{N,M}(h)=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{X/M}e^{Y/M})^M\to Z_N(h)$ as $M\to\infty$, uniformly for $h$ in compact
subsets of $\mathbb C$.

\emph{Step 2: a classical ferromagnet.} Suppose first that $\Gamma>0$, and put
$\varepsilon=\beta\Gamma/M$. Since $e^{\varepsilon\sigma^x}=\cosh\varepsilon\,I+\sinh\varepsilon\,\sigma^x$, the
matrix element $\langle s|e^{\varepsilon\sigma^x}|s'\rangle$ of a single spin is $\cosh\varepsilon$ if $s=s'$
and $\sinh\varepsilon$ if $s\ne s'$, that is,
$\langle s|e^{\varepsilon\sigma^x}|s'\rangle=c_\varepsilon e^{K_\varepsilon ss'}$ with
$c_\varepsilon=(\sinh\varepsilon\cosh\varepsilon)^{1/2}>0$ and $K_\varepsilon=\frac12\log\coth\varepsilon>0$. Inserting the
basis between the factors of $(e^{X/M}e^{Y/M})^M$ gives, for $M\ge3$,
\[
Z_{N,M}(h)=c_\varepsilon^{M|\Lambda_N|}\sum_{s^1,\dots,s^M}\exp\sum_{k=1}^M\Bigl(\frac{\beta J}M\sum_{x\sim y}s^k_xs^k_y
+K_\varepsilon\sum_xs^k_xs^{k+1}_x+\frac hM\sum_xs^k_x\Bigr),
\]
where $s^1,\dots,s^M$ range over $\{-1,1\}^{\Lambda_N}$ and $s^{M+1}=s^1$. This is
$c_\varepsilon^{M|\Lambda_N|}$ times the partition function of a classical Ising model
on the finite graph $\Lambda_N\times\{1,\dots,M\}$, with nonnegative couplings and
the same field $h/M$ at every site. By Fact~\ref{fact:toolbox}(a), it does not
vanish when $\operatorname{Re}h>0$. Moreover, by the triangle
inequality applied to the sum, $|Z_{N,M}(h)|\le Z_{N,M}(\operatorname{Re}h)$. If $\Gamma=0$,
then $Z_N$ itself is a classical Ising partition function, and the same two
statements hold directly.

\emph{Step 3: Hurwitz's theorem.} The functions $Z_{N,M}$ have no zeros in the
connected open set $\{\operatorname{Re}h>0\}$ and converge to $Z_N$ uniformly on
its compact subsets, and $Z_N$ is not identically zero there, since $Z_N(1)>0$.
By Fact~\ref{fact:toolbox}(b), $Z_N$ has no zeros in $\{\operatorname{Re}h>0\}$, and by
the evenness in~(Q1) none in $\{\operatorname{Re}h<0\}$. The inequality
$|Z_N(h)|\le Z_N(\operatorname{Re}h)$ follows from Step~2 in the limit.

<!-- proposition-10: body_md -->
\label{prop:qanalytic}
The pressure $p$ is real analytic on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$, and $m_N(h)\to p'(h)$ for
every $h\ne0$.
<!-- end proposition-10 -->
<!-- proof-36: body_md -->
By evenness it suffices to treat $h>0$. The half-plane $\{\operatorname{Re}h>0\}$ is
simply connected and $Z_N$ does not vanish there, so $Z_N$ has a holomorphic
logarithm on it, and we let $f_N$ be $|\Lambda_N|^{-1}$ times the logarithm that
is real on $(0,\infty)$; thus $f_N=p_N$ on $(0,\infty)$ and $\operatorname{Re}f_N(h)=|\Lambda_N|^{-1}\log|Z_N(h)|\le p_N(\operatorname{Re}h)$,
by Proposition~\ref{prop:qLY}.

We bound $p_N$ on the real line uniformly in $N$. The torus has $d|\Lambda_N|$
bonds, so $\|\beta H_N\|\le\beta(dJ+\Gamma)|\Lambda_N|$; since the trace of the exponential of a
self-adjoint matrix of size $2^{|\Lambda_N|}$ and norm at most $c$ lies between
$2^{|\Lambda_N|}e^{-c}$ and $2^{|\Lambda_N|}e^c$, we get $|p_N(0)-\log2|\le\beta(dJ+\Gamma)=:b$, and since
$|p_N'|\le1$, also $|p_N(x)-\log2|\le b+|x|$ for real $x$.

Fix $x_0>0$, and put $R=x_0/2$ and $r=x_0/4$. On the disc $|h-x_0|\le R$ we have
$\operatorname{Re}f_N\le\log2+b+\frac32x_0$, and $f_N(x_0)=p_N(x_0)\ge\log2-b-x_0$. By the
Borel--Carath\'eodory inequality (Fact~\ref{fact:toolbox}(c)),
\[
|f_N(h)-f_N(x_0)|\le\frac{2r}{R-r}\bigl(\sup_{|z-x_0|\le R}\operatorname{Re}f_N(z)-\operatorname{Re}f_N(x_0)\bigr)
\le2\bigl(2b+\tfrac52x_0\bigr)
\]
for $|h-x_0|\le r$. Since also $|f_N(x_0)|\le\log2+b+x_0$, the functions $f_N$ are
uniformly bounded on each
disc $\{|h-x_0|<x_0/4\}$, and therefore locally uniformly bounded on the
connected open set $U$ that is the union of these discs, which contains $(0,\infty)$. They converge on
$(0,\infty)$, by~(Q2). By Vitali's theorem (Fact~\ref{fact:toolbox}(d)), they converge
uniformly on compact subsets of $U$ to a holomorphic function $F$, which equals
$p$ on $(0,\infty)$; hence $p$ is real analytic there, and $m_N=f_N'\to F'=p'$ on
$(0,\infty)$.

\label{thm:qssb}
Let $d\ge1$, $J>0$, $\Gamma\ge0$ and $\beta>0$ be given.

<!-- lemma-14: body_md -->
\label{lem:single}
Let $Q$ be a compact metrizable space, $q_0$ a point of $Q$ that is not
isolated, and $q\mapsto g_q$ a map from $Q$ into $\mathbb{R}^{[0,1]}$ that is
continuous for the topology of pointwise convergence. Suppose that $g_q$ is
continuous for $q\ne q_0$, and that $G=g_{q_0}$ is continuous on $(0,1]$. Then
the family $\mathcal R=\{g_q:q\ne q_0\}$ is NIP$_3$, and it is stable if and only if
$G$ is continuous at $0$. If $G(0^+)=\lim_{s\to0^+}G(s)$ exists, then for
$q_k\to q_0$ with $q_k\ne q_0$ and $s_l\to0$ with $s_l>0$,
\[
\lim_l\lim_kg_{q_k}(s_l)=G(0^+),\qquad\lim_k\lim_lg_{q_k}(s_l)=G(0).
\]
<!-- end lemma-14 -->
<!-- proof-38: body_md -->
The image of $Q$ is compact, contains $\mathcal R$ and is contained in
$\overline\mathcal R$, because $q_0$ is not isolated; hence it is $\overline\mathcal R$. Its
members are continuous on $(0,1]$, so two distinct members differ at a
rational point of $[0,1]$, and $\overline\mathcal R$ is metrizable by
Proposition~\ref{prop:countable-probes}. The function $G$ is of the first Baire
class, as the pointwise limit of the continuous functions that equal $G$ on
$[1/n,1]$ and are linear on $[0,1/n]$ with value $G(0)$ at $0$; hence the family
is NIP, by Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy}, and it is NIP$_3$. By Grothendieck's
double limit criterion it is stable exactly when every member of
$\overline\mathcal R$ is continuous, that is, exactly when $G$ is continuous at $0$.
The iterated limits follow from the continuity of $q\mapsto g_q$ and of each
$g_{q_k}$.

<!-- lemma-15: body_md -->
\label{lem:poisson}
Assume \textup{(C1)} and \textup{(C2)}. Then $\Sigma_V(m)\to\Sigma(m)=\int\frac{2m}{m^2+\lambda^2}\,d\rho(\lambda)$
for every $m>0$, the function $\Sigma$ is continuous on $(0,1]$, and
$\Sigma(m)\to\pi f(0)$ as $m\to0^+$.
<!-- end lemma-15 -->
<!-- proof-39: body_md -->
For fixed $m>0$, the function $\lambda\mapsto2m/(m^2+\lambda^2)$ is continuous and
bounded on $[0,\Lambda]$, so the first statement is weak convergence, and the
second follows from dominated convergence. For the third, choose $\delta$ so
small that $f$ is bounded on $[0,\delta]$. On $(\delta,\Lambda]$ the integrand is at
most $2m/\delta^2$, so that part tends to $0$. On $[0,\delta]$, the substitution
$\lambda=mu$ gives
\[
\int_0^\delta\frac{2m}{m^2+\lambda^2}f(\lambda)\,d\lambda
=\int_0^{\delta/m}\frac{2}{1+u^2}f(mu)\,du,
\]
which tends to $f(0)\int_0^\infty\frac{2\,du}{1+u^2}=\pi f(0)$ by dominated
convergence. The measure $\rho$ has no atom at $0$, because it has a density
near $0$.

\label{thm:chiral}
Assume \textup{(C1)} and \textup{(C2)}.

<!-- keytheorem-13: body_md -->
\label{thm:sc}
Assume \textup{(S1)} and \textup{(S2)}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item We have $D_s=D_0-\tau_0\bigl([0,\Lambda]\bigr)$ and

\item The family of responses $\{g_q:0<q\le1\}$ is stable if and only if
$D=D_s$. In particular, the responses of a clean superconductor and of an
insulator are stable, and those of a clean normal metal are unstable.
\item In all cases the family is NIP$_3$.
\end{enumerate}

Write $\kappa_\omega(\nu)=\nu^2/(\nu^2+\omega^2)$. For $\omega>0$ the function
$\kappa_\omega$ is continuous on $[0,\Lambda]$ with $\kappa_\omega(0)=0$, while
$\kappa_0=1$ on $(0,\Lambda]$. Each $g_q$ is continuous on $[0,1]$, by dominated

and the computation just made, and $G$ is continuous on $(0,1]$ with
$G(0)-G(0^+)=\tau_0(\{0\})=D-D_s$. Lemma~\ref{lem:single} gives (b) and~(c).
