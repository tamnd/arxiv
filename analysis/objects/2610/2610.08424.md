\label{conj:53}
Let $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ be a multiplicative function such that for each $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$ there are at least two primes $p_1,p_2$ such that $f(p_j) \neq p_j^k$, $j = 1,2$. Then $\mathcal{L}_f$ and $\mathcal{S}_f$ are both infinite sets.

<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:multAtMost1}
Let $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ be a multiplicative function such that for each $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$ there are at least two primes $p_1,p_2$ such that $f(p_j) \neq p_j^k$ for $j = 1,2$. Then for each $\omega \in (0,1]$, both $\mathcal{L}_f(\omega)$ and $\mathcal{S}_f(\omega)$ have positive upper logarithmic density. In particular, $\mathcal{L}_f$ and $\mathcal{S}_f$ are both infinite.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:counter}
For each $\omega >1$ there exist multiplicative functions $f_1,f_2: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ such that for both $j = 1,2$ and for each $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$ there are infinitely many primes $p$ such that $f_j(p) \neq p^k$, yet $|\mathcal{S}_{f_1}(\omega)| < \infty$ and $|\mathcal{L}_{f_2}(\omega)| < \infty$. Indeed, there exists $M = M(\omega)$ such that for each prime $p_0$ such functions $f_j$ can be constructed with the following properties: 
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item there are constants $C_1,C_2 > 0$ such that
$$
C_1 n^M \leq f_j(n) \leq C_2 n^M \text{ for all } p_0 \nmid n,
$$
\item we have
$$
\sup_{k \geq 1} f_1(p_0^k)/p_0^{kM} = \infty, \quad \inf_{k \geq 1} f_2(p_0^k)/p_0^{kM} = 0.                                              
$$
\end{enumerate}

<!-- def1-1: body_md -->
Given a positive-valued multiplicative function $f$, we say that a prime $p$ is \emph{powerfully bad for $f$} if 
$$
\sup_{k \geq 1} |\log(f(p^k))| = \infty. 
$$
We define $\mathcal{B}_f$ to denote the set of all powerfully bad primes for $f$.
<!-- end def1-1 -->
<!-- rem-2: body_md -->
We note that when $f$ is completely multiplicative, i.e., $f(mn) = f(m)f(n)$ for all $m,n \in \mathbb{N}$,
                                                             
we have
$$
\mathcal{B}_f = \{p : f(p) \neq 1\}.
$$

<!-- thm-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:PowBad}
Let $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow (0,\infty)$ be a multiplicative function. Let $\omega > 1$, and suppose that $\delta(\mathcal{S}_f(\omega))\delta(\mathcal{L}_f(\omega)) = 0$. Then there exists $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $|\mathcal{B}_{f_{\lambda}}| \leq 1$. Moreover, there exists an integer $P$ with $\Omega(P) \leq 1$ such that
$$
\sup_{(n,P) = 1} |\log(f(n)/n^\lambda)| < \infty.
$$

In light of the example from the proof of Proposition \ref{prop:counter}, this classification is sharp.

<!-- thm-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:SarkCM}
If $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ is a completely multiplicative function such that for each $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$ there are at least two primes $p_1,p_2$ such that $f(p_j) \neq p_j^k$ for $j = 1,2$, then for each $\omega > 0$, both $\mathcal{L}_f(\omega)$ and $\mathcal{S}_f(\omega)$ have positive upper logarithmic density.
<!-- end thm-3 -->
<!-- rem-4: body_md -->
It seems plausible that one could extend this classification to the broader class of real-valued multiplicative functions $f: \mathbb{N}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. For example, restricting to those functions that do not vanish, one can consider the subset of $n \in \mathcal{L}_f(\omega)$ for which $(\sigma_f(n-1),\sigma_f(n),\sigma_f(n+1)) = (-1,+1,-1)$, where $\sigma_f(n) := \text{sign}(f(n))$, and it is not hard to show using the existing literature that if $\mathcal{L}_f(\omega)$ has logarithmic density $0$ then $\sigma_f$ pretends to be a character (otherwise, such a sign pattern must occur with positive logarithmic density). However, going beyond this requires significant casework, which is all the more complicated when $f$ is allowed to vanish. We therefore leave this matter to another occasion.

<!-- def1-2: body_md -->
We say that an additive function $g: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ has \emph{finite concentration} if 
$$
\limsup_{x \rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{x}\sup_{a \in \mathbb{R}} |\{n \leq x : g(n)\in [a-1/2,a+1/2]\}| > 0.
$$
Erd\H{o}s showed (see \cite[Thm. V]{Erdos}) that this is equivalent to the statement that there exists\footnote{In the sequel we shall sometimes refer to such a parameter $\lambda$ as a \emph{minimiser} for $g$.} $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ with
$$
\sum_p \frac{\min\{(g(p)-\lambda\log p)^2,1\}}{p} < \infty.
$$
<!-- end def1-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $M = M(\omega) \geq 2$ be the minimum positive integer such that
$$
\prod_{p^k, \ k \geq 1} \left(1+\frac{1}{p^{kM}}\right) < \omega.
$$
Fix a prime $p_0$. For every prime power $p^k$, $p \neq p_0$, we define
$$
f_j(p^k) = p^{kM} + 1, \quad j = 1,2.
$$
Also, define $f_j(p_0^k) := c_k^{(j)}p_0^{kM}$, where for $k \geq 1$ set
$$
c_k^{(j)} := p_0^{M(-1)^{j-1}\lfloor \log k\rfloor}.
                                              
$$ 
Finally, extend $f_j$ to a multiplicative function. Since $\lfloor \log k\rfloor \leq k$ for all $k \geq 1$, $f_j$ clearly takes positive integer values. It is obviously true that for each $j = 1,2$ and any $k \geq 0$, $f_j(p) \neq p^k$ for all $p \neq p_0$, since for such primes $p \nmid f_j(p)$. \\
Writing $\nu_{p_0}(m)$ to denote the maximal $k$ such that $p_0^k | m$ and setting $c_0^{(j)} := 1$ for both $j = 1,2$, we find that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fgenForm}
f_j(n) = n^Mc_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}^{(j)}\prod_{\substack{p^k || n \\ p \neq p_0}} \left(1+\frac{1}{p^{kM}}\right).
\end{equation}
Obviously when $p_0\nmid n$ we have
$$
1 \leq f_j(n)/n^M = \prod_{\substack{p^k || n}} \left(1+\frac{1}{p^{kM}}\right) \leq \prod_{p^k, k \geq 1} \left(1+\frac{1}{p^{kM}}\right) < \omega, 
$$
which verifies (a). Moreover, we have $c_k^{(j)} = f_j(p_0^k)/p_0^{Mk}$, which tends to $\infty$ as $k \rightarrow \infty$ if $j =1$, and tends to $0$ as $k \rightarrow \infty$ if $j = 2$, 
     
    
                                         
    
                    
verifying (b). \\
Next, we show that $|\mathcal{S}_{f_1}(\omega)| < \infty$. Let us choose $n_0(\omega)$ minimally so that for $n \geq n_0(\omega)$, 
$$
\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{M} \prod_{p^k, \ k \geq 1}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{kM}}\right) < \omega.
$$
Using \eqref{eq:fgenForm}, we see that
                                
\begin{align*}
\min\{f_1(n - 1), f_1(n+1)\} &= \min_{\varepsilon = \pm 1} (n+\varepsilon)^M c_{\nu_{p_0}(n+\varepsilon)}^{(1)} \prod_{p^k||(n+\varepsilon)} \left(1+\frac{1}{p^{Mk}}\right) \\
&\leq n^M \left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^M \prod_{p^k, \ k \geq 1} \left(1+\frac{1}{p^{Mk}}\right) \min\{c_{\nu_{p_0}(n-1)}^{(1)}, c_{\nu_{p_0}(n+1)}^{(1)}\} \\
&< \omega f_1(n)\min\left\{\frac{c_{\nu_{p_0}(n-1)}^{(1)}}{c_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}^{(1)}}, \frac{c_{\nu_{p_0}(n+1)}^{(1)}}{c_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}^{(1)}}\right\}.
\end{align*}
Observe that either $p_0|n$, in which case $c_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}^{(1)} \geq 1$ whilst $c_{\nu_{p_0}(n\pm 1)}^{(1)} = c_0^{(1)} = 1$, or else $p_0 \nmid n$, in which case $0 \leq \min\{\nu_{p_0}(n-1),\nu_{p_0}(n+1)\} \leq 1$. Because $c^{(1)}_0 = c^{(1)}_1 = 1$ it follows that the bracketed expression is $\leq 1$, and thus
$$
\min\{f_1(n-1),f_1(n+1)\} < \omega f_1(n)
$$
for all $n \geq n_0(\omega)$. We conclude that $|\mathcal{S}_{f_1}(\omega)| < \infty$. \\
The proof that $|\mathcal{L}_{f_2}(\omega)| < \infty$ is similar. In this case, note that $c_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}^{(2)} \leq 1$ for all $n$, and that as $c_0^{(2)} = c_1^{(2)} = 1$, 
$$
\max\left\{\frac{c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n-1)}}{c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}}, \frac{c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n+1)}}{c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}}\right\} \geq 1. 
$$
If $n_1(\omega)$ is now chosen minimally so that
$$
\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{-M} \prod_{p^k,k \geq 1} \left(1+\frac{1}{p^{kM}}\right) < \omega,
$$
then we deduce from \eqref{eq:fgenForm} that for all $n \geq n_1(\omega)$,
\begin{align*}
\max\{f_2(n-1),f_2(n+1)\} \geq n^M c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n)} &\cdot \prod_{\substack{p^k || n \\ p \neq p_0}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{kM}}\right) \cdot \left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^M \prod_{p^k,k \geq 1} \left(1+\frac{1}{p^{kM}}\right)^{-1} \\
&\cdot \max\left\{\frac{c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n-1)}}{c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}}, \frac{c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n+1)}}{c^{(2)}_{\nu_{p_0}(n)}}\right\} > \frac{1}{\omega} f_2(n),
\end{align*}
whence the claim follows.

<!-- thm-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:redtoFinConc}
Let $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow (0,\infty)$ be a multiplicative function. Suppose that there is $\omega > 0$ such that $\delta(\mathcal{L}_f(\omega))\delta(\mathcal{S}_f(\omega)) = 0$. Set $g:= \log f$. Then $g$ has finite concentration, i.e., there exists $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that
$$
\sum_p \frac{\min\{1,(g(p)-\lambda \log p)^2\}}{p} < \infty.
$$
<!-- end thm-4 -->
<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:gapConc}
Let $g$ be a real-valued additive function, let $d \in \mathbb{N}$ and let $h > 0$. Then
$$
\tilde{Q}_g(x;d,h) \ll_{d,h} \frac{1}{\sqrt{U_g(x)}} + \left(\frac{\log\log \log x}{\log\log x}\right)^{1/2}.
$$
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:clusters}
Let $\omega \geq 1$. Assume that $g$ does not have finite concentration. Then $\mathcal{M}_g(\omega)$ has logarithmic density $0$. Consequently, there is an infinite subsequence $(x_k)_k$ on which
$$
|\mathcal{M}_g(x_k;\omega)| = o_{k \rightarrow \infty}(x_k).
                    
$$
<!-- end cor-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Note that the condition 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:midRange}
\max\{g(n+1),g(n-1)\} \leq g(n) \leq \max\{g(n+1),g(n-1)\} + \Omega
\end{equation}
implies that for one of $\varepsilon \in \{-1,+1\}$,
$$
0 \leq g(n)-g(n+\varepsilon) \leq \Omega.
$$
By the union bound, we deduce that
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\log x} \sum_{\substack{n \leq x \\ \eqref{eq:midRange} \text{ holds for $n$}}} \frac{1}{n} \leq \frac{2}{\log x} \sum_{\substack{n \leq x \\ |g(n+1)-g(n)| \leq \Omega}} \frac{1}{n} + o(1) \leq 2\tilde{Q}_g(x;1,2\Omega) + o(1).
\end{equation}
By assumption, we have $U_g(x) \rightarrow \infty$ as $x \rightarrow \infty$, so that by Proposition \ref{prop:gapConc}, 
            
$$
\frac{1}{\log x} \sum_{\substack{n \leq x \\ \eqref{eq:midRange} \text{ holds for $n$}}} \frac{1}{n} = o(1), \quad x \rightarrow \infty.
$$

\label{lem:valleys}
Let $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow (0,\infty)$ be a multiplicative function for which $\delta(\mathcal{L}_f(\omega))= 0$. Let $(x_k)_k$ be an infinite subsequence, and write 
$$
N_k := |\mathcal{M}_g(x_k;\omega)|, \quad \mathcal{M}_g(x_k;\omega) = (m_j)_{1 \leq j \leq N_k}.
$$
        
    
                                                                                                                                                  
    
Set also $m_0 := 0$. Then for every $k \geq 1$ and $0 \leq j \leq N_k-1$ 
                                                      
there is $\ell_j \in (m_j,m_{j+1}]$ such that
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item $g(n+1) < g(n) \text{ for all } m_j < n <\ell_j$
\item $g(n) \leq g(n+1) \text{ for all } \ell_j \leq n < m_{j+1}.$
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By assumption, for each $n \in \mathbb{N} \backslash \mathcal{L}_f(\omega)$ we have
$$
g(n)\leq \max\{g(n-1),g(n+1)\} + \Omega.
$$
Therefore, if $m_j < n < m_{j+1}$ for some $j \geq 0$ then we get the stronger upper bound $g(n) < \max\{g(n-1),g(n+1)\}$. Now 
                                                                   
define $\ell_j \in (m_j,m_{j+1}]$ to be the minimal $n$ such that $g(n+1) \geq g(n)$ in the interval (if $f$ is decreasing in the entire interval $(m_j,m_{j+1})$ then set $\ell_j = m_{j+1}$). Now, we argue by induction that $g(n) \leq g(n+1)$ for all $\ell_j \leq n < m_{j+1}$ (the claim being vacuous in the case that $\ell_j = m_{j+1}$). \\
The base case is $g(\ell_j) \leq g(\ell_j+1)$, which holds by construction. Next, assume the same relation holds for each $\ell_j \leq n < m_{j+1}-1$, and let $\ell_j< n < m_{j+1}$. As $g(n) \geq g(n-1)$ and $g(n) < \max\{g(n-1),g(n+1)\}$ it follows that $g(n) < g(n+1)$. The claim then follows by induction.

\label{lem:MRclipped}

By Corollary \ref{cor:clusters}, we obtain a sequence $(x_k)_k$ such that

By Lemma \ref{lem:valleys}, we may decompose $[0,x]$ as a union
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
[m_{N}, \min\{x+1,\ell_N\}) \cup [\min\{x+1,\ell_N\},x] \cup \bigcup_{0 \leq j \leq N-1} [m_j,\ell_j) \cup [\ell_j,m_{j+1}),
\end{equation}
such that $f$ is decreasing on $(m_j,\ell_j)$ and increasing on $[\ell_j, m_{j+1})$ for each $0 \leq j \leq N-1$, and similarly on the two remaining intervals in \eqref{eq:split}. \begin{align*}
h_{x}(n+1) - h_{x}(n) &= \phi_K(g_{\lambda_{x}}(n+1)/s_x - a_{x}) - \phi_K(g_{\lambda_{x}}(n)/s_x-a_{x}) \\
&= \phi_K(g(n+1)/s_{x} - c_{x}\log n - a_{x}) - \phi_K(g(n)/s_{x} - c_{x}\log n - a_{x}) + O\left(\frac{|c_{x}|}{n}\right),
\end{align*}
wherein the main term on the RHS is non-negative. Similarly, if $g(n) \geq g(n+1)$ then for $n \geq 1$,
\begin{align*}
h_{x}(n) - h_{x}(n+1) &= \phi_K(g_{\lambda_{x}}(n)/s_x - a_{x}) - \phi_K(g_{\lambda_{x}}(n+1)/s_x-a_{x}) \\
&= \phi_K(g(n)/s_{x} - c_{x}\log n - a_{x}) - \phi_K(g(n+1)/s_{x} - c_{x}\log n - a_{x}) + O\left(\frac{|c_{x}|}{n}\right),
\end{align*}
and now the main term on the RHS is also non-negative. We deduce, therefore, that
\begin{align} \label{eq:gtohSign}
|h_{x}(n+1)-h_{x}(n)| = (h_{x}(n+1)-h_{x}(n)) \text{sign}(g(n+1)-g(n)) + O\left(\frac{|c_x|}{n}\right).
\end{align}

\begin{align*}
&\frac{1}{x} \sum_{n\leq x} |h_{x}(n+1) - h_{x}(n)|  \\
&\leq \frac{1}{x}\sum_{0 \leq j \leq N} \left(\sum_{m_j < n < \min\{x+1,\ell_{j}\}} (h_{x}(n)-h_{x}(n+1)) + \sum_{\min\{x+1,\ell_{j}\} \leq n \leq x} (h_{x}(n+1) - h_{x}(n))\right) \\
& \qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad \qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+ \frac{2K(N+1) + O(|c_{x}|\log x)}{x} \\
&= \frac{1}{x} \sum_{0 \leq j \leq N} \left(h_{x}(m_j+1) + h_{x}(\lfloor x\rfloor + 1) - 2h_{x}(\min\{\ell_j,\lfloor x\rfloor+1\})\right) + \frac{2K(N+1) + O(|c_{x}|\log x)}{x} \\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad \qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ll \frac{KN + |c_{x}| \log x}{x}.
\end{align*}
                                                                                                                                                                                                    
We now revert to the notation $x_k$ and $N_k$ in place of $x$ and $N$, to make the dependence on $k$ more explicit. Let $H_k = H(x_k)$ be a parameter to be chosen shortly.

\begin{align*}
&\frac{1}{x_k} \sum_{H_k < n \leq x_k} \left|h_{x_k}(n)-\frac{1}{H_k}\sum_{1 \leq j \leq H_k} h_{x_k}(n-j)\right|^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{x_kH_k^2} \sum_{H_k < n \leq x_k} \left|\sum_{1 \leq j \leq H_k} \sum_{0 \leq i \leq j-1} (h_{x_k}(n-i)-h_{x_k}(n-i-1))\right|^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{x_kH_k^2} \sum_{H_k < n \leq x_k} \left|\sum_{0 \leq i \leq H_k-1} (H_k-i)(h_{x_k}(n-i)-h_{x_k}(n-i-1))\right|^2 \\
&\leq \frac{H_k^2}{x_k} \sum_{n \leq x_k} |h_{x_k}(n) - h_{x_k}(n+1)|^2 \ll KH_k^2 \frac{KN_k + |c_{x_k}| \log x_k}{x_k}.
\end{align*}

$$
H_k := x_k^{1/3}(KN_k + \log x_k)^{-1/3},
$$

\begin{align*}

\end{align*}
as claimed.

\label{lem:smallDev}
Let $h$ be a real-valued additive function, such that there are parameters $M,T \geq 1$ with:
\begin{itemize}
\item $|h(p)|\leq M$ for all $p$, 
\item $h(p^k) = 0$ whenever $k \geq 2$ and $p^k > T$, and
\item $\sum_{p} h(p)^2/p < \infty$.
\end{itemize}
                                                        
Finally, assume that $x \geq x_0(M,T)$. Then, uniformly over $Q \leq \sqrt{x}$ and $1 \leq a \leq Q$ with $(Q,a) \leq T$,
$$
\frac{1}{x} \sum_{n \leq x} \left|h(Qn+a) - A_h(Qx) + \sum_{\substack{p | Q/(Q,a)}} \frac{h(p)}{p} \right|^2 \ll_{M,T} 1.
$$

Define $\tilde{h}(n) := \sum_{p^k || n} h(p^k) 1_{p \leq \sqrt{x}}$, and set $Q' := Q/(Q,a)$, $a' := a/(Q,a)$. If $x > T^2$ then as $Qn+a \leq 2x^{3/2}$, we have 
$$
h(Qn+a) = \tilde{h}(Qn+a) + O_M(1) = \tilde{h}(Q'n+a') + O_{M,T}(1).
$$
Moreover, note that
$$
\left|A_h(Qx) - A_{\tilde{h}}(\sqrt{x})\right| \leq M\sum_{\substack{\sqrt{x} < p \leq x^{3/2}}} \frac{1}{p} \ll_M 1,
$$
as well as
$$
\sum_{\substack{p^k \leq x \\ k \geq 2, \ p|Q'}} \frac{h(p^k)}{p^k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right) + \sum_{p|Q'} \frac{h(p)}{p^2} \leq \sum_{\substack{p^k \leq T \\ k \geq 2}} \frac{h(p^k)}{p^k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right) + O_M(1) \ll_{M,T} 1.
$$
Therefore, writing
$$
A^{(Q')}_{\tilde{h}}(\sqrt{x}) := A_{\tilde{h}}(\sqrt{x}) - \sum_{\substack{p^k \leq x \\ p | Q'}} \frac{h(p^k)}{p^k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right) = \sum_{\substack{p^k \leq x \\ p \nmid Q'}} \frac{h(p^k)}{p^k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)
$$
and applying Cauchy-Schwarz, it suffices to show that
\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n \leq x} \left|\tilde{h}(Q'n+a') - A^{(Q')}_{\tilde{h}}(\sqrt{x})\right|^2 
                                                                                       
\ll_{M,T} 1.
\end{align*}
We now expand the square, noting again that if $x > T^2$ then $\tilde{h}(p^k) = 0$ for $p^k > \sqrt{x}$ and all $k \geq 1$. Thus,
       
\begin{align*}
&\sum_{\substack{p_1^{k_1}, p_2^{k_2} \leq \sqrt{x} \\ (p_1p_2,Q') = 1}} h(p_1^{k_1})h(p_2^{k_2}) \frac{1}{x}\sum_{\substack{n \leq x \\ p_j^{k_j}||Q'n+a', \ j = 1,2}} 1 - 2A^{(Q')}_{\tilde{h}}(\sqrt{x})\sum_{\substack{p^k \leq \sqrt{x} \\ p \nmid Q}} h(p^k) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{p^k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right) + O(1)\right) + A^{(Q')}_{\tilde{h}}(\sqrt{x})^2\\
&= \sum_{\substack{p_1^{k_1},p_2^{k_2} \leq \sqrt{x} \\ (p_1p_2,Q') = 1}} h(p_1^{k_1})h(p_2^{k_2})\left(\frac{\prod_{p|p_1p_2}(1-1/p)}{[p_1^{k_1},p_2^{k_2}]} - \frac{\prod_{j = 1,2}(1-1/p_j)}{p_1^{k_1}p_2^{k_2}}\right) \\
&+ O\left(\frac{1}{x}\left(\sum_{p^k \leq \sqrt{x}} |h(p^k)|\right)\left(A_{|\tilde{h}|}(\sqrt{x}) + \sum_{p^k \leq \sqrt{x}} |h(p^k)|\right)\right).
\end{align*}
Using the facts that $|h(p)| \leq M$ and $h(p^k) = 0$ for $k \geq 2$ and $p^k > T$, the error term is $O_{M,T}\left(\tfrac{1}{(\log x)^2}\right)$. The main term cancels all but the terms for which $p_1 = p_2$, in which case we get an upper bound
$$
\sum_{p \leq \sqrt{x}} \frac{h(p)^2}{p} + \sum_{\substack{p^\ell\leq p^k \leq T \\ k \geq 2}} \frac{|h(p^k)h(p^\ell)|}{p^k} \ll_{M,T} 1.
$$
The claim follows.

\max_{\eta \in \{-1,+1\}}|g_{\lambda}(Wqn + 1) - g_{\lambda}(Wq(Wq n+1) + \eta)| \leq B

But for each of these $n$, if we write $m = Wqn+1$ it follows that $(Wq,m) = 1$, and by additivity,
\begin{align*}
g_{\lambda}(Wqm) &\geq g_{\lambda}(q) - |g_{\lambda}(W)| + g_{\lambda}(m) \geq 1+B + \Omega + \max\{g_{\lambda}(Wqm - 1), g_{\lambda}(Wqm+1)\} - B \\
&= 1 + \Omega + \max\{g_{\lambda}(Wqm - 1), g_{\lambda}(Wqm+1)\}.
\end{align*}
Adding $\lambda \log(Wqm)$ to both sides and noting that $m \gg x$, it follows that if $x$ is large enough we get
$$
g(Wqm) \geq \frac{1}{2} + \Omega + \max\{g(Wqm-1),g(Wqm+1)\} > \Omega + \max\{g(Wqm-1),g(Wqm+1)\}.
$$
Since this holds for a positive proportion of $n \leq x$, we obtain a contradiction to $\delta(\mathcal{L}_f(\omega)) = 0$. \\
\underline{Case 2:} We may assume now that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bddPos}
\sup_{q \geq 1} g_{\lambda}(q) < \infty.
\end{equation}
We claim that there exists a threshold $C> 0$ depending only on $\omega$ and $g$, such that for any two coprime $q_1,q_2 \in \mathbb{N}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduced}
\min\{-g_{\lambda}(q_1),-g_{\lambda}(q_2)\} \leq C.
\end{equation}
Assume this for the moment. Given our assumption \eqref{eq:bddPos}, it follows, necessarily, that for some possibly larger threshold $C' > 0$, for every pair of coprime $q_1,q_2 \in \mathbb{N}$ we in fact have
$$
\min\{|g_{\lambda}(q_1)|,|g_{\lambda}(q_2)|\} \leq C'.
$$
By Lemma \ref{lem:easyVer}, we obtain the desired claims: 
\begin{itemize}
\item $|\mathcal{B}_{g_{\lambda}}| \leq 1$, and 
\item there is $P \in \mathbb{N}$ with $\Omega(P) \leq 1$ such that $\sup_{(n,P) = 1} |g_{\lambda}(n)| < \infty$. \end{itemize}
It therefore remains to show \eqref{eq:reduced}, which we will do by contradiction. \\
                                                                                                       
In preparation, set $D = 24$, $M := 1 + \max_{1 \leq d \leq D} |g_{\lambda}(d)|$, $\varepsilon= 1/3$, $B = B(\varepsilon,D)$ and $T = T(\varepsilon,D) \geq 24$ chosen as in Lemma \ref{lem:unifClose}, and set $W = W_{T,M}$. Take $C := 1 + M + B + \Omega$, and assume that \eqref{eq:reduced} does not always hold, i.e., we can find coprime integers $q_1,q_2 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
                                                                  
$$
\min\{-g_{\lambda}(q_1), -g_{\lambda}(q_2)\} > C.
$$
 We choose $n = \tilde{W}(q_1q_2)^2m + b$, where $\tilde{W} := W/(W,q_1q_2)$ and $1 \leq b \leq \tilde{W}(q_1q_2)^2$ is chosen so that 
$$
b \equiv 1 + q_1 \pmod{q_1^2}, \quad b \equiv -1 + q_2 \pmod{q_2^2}, \quad (b^3-b,\tilde{W}/(6,\tilde{W})).
$$
Note that the latter condition can be guaranteed by the Chinese remainder theorem, since it holds modulo each prime $p|\tilde{W}$, $p > 3$. The first two conditions are then compatible with this choice because the squarefreeness of $W$ implies that $(\tilde{W},(q_1q_2)^2) = 1$. \\
We now observe that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GCDcond}
\max\{(b,\tilde{W}(q_1q_2)^2) , ((b-1)/q_1,\tilde{W}q_1q_2^2), ((b+1)/q_2,\tilde{W}q_2q_1^2)\} \leq D.
\end{equation}
Indeed, since $(b,q_1q_2) = ((b-1)/q_1,q_1) = ((b+1)/q_2,q_2) = 1$ and $(b^3-b,\tilde{W}) \leq 6$, it suffices to show that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:redGCD}
                                                      
\left(\frac{b-1}{q_1}, q_2^2\right)\left(\frac{b+1}{q_2},q_1^2\right) = (b-1,q_2) (b+1,q_1) \leq 4.
\end{equation}
Since $q_2|(b+1)$, $(b-1,q_2)$ divides $(b-1,b+1)$, which divides $2$. The same argument shows that $(b+1,q_1) \leq 2$. Since $q_1,q_2$ are coprime, at most one of these is equal to $2$, so as $(d_1,d_2^2)$ divides $(d_1,d_2)^2$ for all $d_1,d_2 \in \mathbb{N}$, \eqref{eq:redGCD} follows. \\
    
                                                             
    
We now take $Q_1 := \tilde{W}(q_1q_2)^2$, $a_1 = b$, $Q_2 := \tilde{W}q_1q_2^2$, $a_2 = (b-1)/q_1$ and $Q_3 := \tilde{W}q_1^2q_2$, $a_3 = (b+1)/q_2$, so that $(Q_j,a_j) \leq D$ for all $j =1,2,3$, and $Q_i/(Q_i,a_i) \triangle Q_j/(Q_j,a_j) \leq D$ for each pairs $i\neq j$. We may assume that $x$ is large enough that $(q_1q_2)^2 \tilde{W} \leq \sqrt{\log x}$. Arguing as in the previous proof, with the aforementioned choices of $T$ and $B$ there is a proportion $\geq 1/6$ of integers $x/6 < m \leq x$ such that 
\begin{align*}
\max\{|g_{\lambda}(Q_1m + a_1) - g_{\lambda}(Q_2m + a_2)|, |g_{\lambda}(Q_1m + a_1) - g_{\lambda}(Q_3m + a_3)|\} \leq B + M,
\end{align*}
where the additional $M$ comes from trivially bounding the terms 
$$
\sum_{\substack{p|\tfrac{Q_1}{(Q_1,a_1)} \triangle \frac{Q_j}{(Q_j,a_j)} \\ |g_{\lambda}(p)|\leq M}}\frac{g_{\lambda}(p)}{p} \leq M\sum_{p|24} \frac{1}{p} < M, \quad j=2,3.
$$
Since $q_2(Q_3m + a_3) = Q_1m+a_1 + 1$ and $q_1(Q_2m + a_2) = Q_1m+a_1 - 1$, we find that
\begin{align*}
&\max\{g_{\lambda}(Q_1m + a_1-1), g_{\lambda}(Q_1m + a_1 + 1)\} + \Omega \\
&\leq \max\{g_{\lambda}(q_1) +  g_{\lambda}(Q_2m + a_2), g_{\lambda}(q_2) + g_{\lambda}(Q_3m + a_3)\} + \Omega \\
&\leq -(1+M+B +\Omega) + g_{\lambda}(Q_1m + a_1) + B + M + \Omega = g_{\lambda}(Q_1m+a_1) - 1. 
\end{align*}
Adding $\lambda \log(Q_1m+a_1)$ to both sides once again, and using the fact that $m > x/6$, we deduce that when $x$ is large enough,
$$
\max\{g(Q_1m + a_1-1), g(Q_1m + a_1 + 1)\} + \Omega \leq g(Q_1m+a_1) - 1/2 < g(Q_1m+a_1).
$$
Since this holds for a positive proportion of $m \leq x$, as $x \rightarrow \infty$ this contradicts the hypothesis that $\delta(\mathcal{L}_f(\omega)) = 0$. Thus, \eqref{eq:reduced} holds after all, and as discussed earlier the proof is complete.

Assume that $f$ is completely multiplicative and takes positive integer values. As remarked earlier, it suffices to take $\omega \geq 1$. Assume that $\delta(\mathcal{L}_f(\omega))\delta(\mathcal{S}_f(\omega)) = 0$. Theorem \ref{thm:redtoFinConc} allows us to assume that $g = \log f$ satisfies

By Theorem \ref{thm:PowBad}, we see that there exists at most a single prime $p_0$ such that 
$$
\sup_{\substack{n \in \mathbb{N} \\ p_0 \nmid n}} |g_{\lambda}(n)| < \infty.
$$
But as $g$ is completely additive, on selecting $p \neq p_0$ we see that $g_{\lambda}(p) = 0$, since otherwise
$$
\limsup_{k \rightarrow \infty} |g_{\lambda}(p^k)| = |g_{\lambda}(p)| \limsup_{k \rightarrow \infty} k = \infty,
$$
a contradiction. Therefore, $f(p) = p^{\lambda}$ for all $p \neq p_0$. This will yield a contradiction to S\'{a}rk\"{o}zy's hypothesis, provided we can show that $\lambda \in \mathbb{N}_0$. \\
To begin with, since $f(p) \geq 1$ we clearly have $\lambda \geq 0$. 
                                                                                              
Next, note that if $\lambda \notin \mathbb{Q}$ then by the Gelfond-Schneider theorem (see e.g. \cite[Ch. 2]{Baker}), $p^{\lambda}$ would be transcendental, which contradicts the fact that $f(p) \in \mathbb{N}$. Thus, we find that $\lambda = a/b$ for some coprime integers $a,b$ with $b \geq 1$ and $a \geq 0$. \\
If $a = 0$ then $\lambda = 0 \in \mathbb{N}_0$ as required. Otherwise, suppose $a \geq 1$. If $b > 1$ then from the identity $f(p)^b = p^a$ and unique factorisation of integers, we deduce that $f(p) = p^\nu$ for some $\nu \geq 1$. It follows that $a = \nu b$, so that $a$ and $b$ are not coprime, a contradiction. This implies that $b = 1$ as claimed, and so $\lambda \in \mathbb{N}$. The conclusion follows.

$$

\label{lem:clipLg}
Suppose there exists $P$ with $\Omega(P) \leq 1$ and that $\sup_{(n,P) = 1} |g_{\lambda}(n)| < \infty$. Then there exists an additive function $h$ with $\sup_n |h_{\lambda}(n)| < \infty$ and $\mathcal{L}_h \subseteq \mathcal{L}_g$.

<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
If $P = 1$ then the claim is immediate from taking $h = g$. Thus, we may assume in the sequel that $P = p_0$. \\
                              
Let 
$$
C := \sup_{p_0 \nmid n} |g_{\lambda}(n)| + 100 |\lambda|,
                     
\quad K := 2C + 1.
$$ 
Letting $\phi_K(t) := \text{sign}(t) \min\{K,|t|\}$ as before, we define the additive function $h$ on prime powers via 
$$
h(p^k) := \begin{cases} g(p^k) &\text{ if } p \neq p_0 \\ \lambda \log p_0^k + \phi_K(g_{\lambda}(p_0^k)) &\text{ if } p = p_0. \end{cases}
$$
Thus, we clearly have 
$$
\sup_n |h_{\lambda}(n)| \leq \sup_{p_0 \nmid n} |g_{\lambda}(n)| + \sup_{k \geq 1} |h_{\lambda}(p_0^k)| \leq C + K < \infty.
$$
Next, let $n \in \mathcal{L}_h$, and consider first that $p_0^\nu||n$ for some $\nu \geq 1$. Then $g(n\pm 1) = h(n\pm 1)$ and $g(n/p_0^{\nu}) = h(n/p_0^{\nu})$. Therefore, we have
$$
h(p_0^\nu) + g(n/p_0^\nu) > \max\{g(n-1),g(n+1)\}.
$$
Subtracting $\lambda \log n$ from both sides and rearranging, we equivalently have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p_0nCase}
h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu) > \max\{g_{\lambda}(n-1) + \lambda\log(1-1/n) , g_{\lambda}(n+1) + \lambda \log(1+1/n)\} - g_{\lambda}(n/p_0^\nu).
                 
\end{equation}
In particular, uniformly over $n$, there is an absolute constant $c \in (0,100)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:withc}
                    
h_{\lambda}(p_0^{\nu}) > \max\{g_{\lambda}(n-1) - g_{\lambda}(n/p_0^\nu), g_{\lambda}(n+1)-g_{\lambda}(n/p_0^\nu)\} - c|\lambda|. 
\end{equation}
Note that $h_{\lambda}(p_0^{\nu})$ has the same sign as $g_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)$, and if the latter is $> K$ in absolute value then $|h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)| = K$. Consider the following (exhaustive) subcases: 
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item if the RHS of \eqref{eq:p_0nCase} is positive then so is $h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)$. As $\phi_K(t) \leq t$ for all $t \geq 0$, 
                                                                        
    
                                                                                                                                                                               
    
we can replace $h$ by $g$ in \eqref{eq:p_0nCase}. In particular, adding $\lambda \log n$ back and rearranging, we get that
$$
g(n) > \max\{g(n-1),g(n+1)\},
$$
i.e., $n \in \mathcal{L}_g$.
\item If the RHS of \eqref{eq:p_0nCase} is negative and the LHS is non-negative then replacing $h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)$ by $g_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)$ maintains the non-negativity of the LHS, and the inequality still holds. We obtain $n \in \mathcal{L}_g$ as in (a).
\item Finally, if the RHS of \eqref{eq:p_0nCase} is negative and the LHS is also negative, then because $c < 100$ the RHS in \eqref{eq:withc} has absolute value $< 2C$. It follows on multiplying both sides of \eqref{eq:p_0nCase} by $-1$ that
$$
0 < |h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)| < 2C < K
$$
Since $\phi_K(t) = t$ whenever $|t| \leq K$, we find that $g_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu) = h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)$, and so \eqref{eq:p_0nCase} continues to hold with $g$ replaced by $h$. 
\end{enumerate}
Thus, whenever $p_0 | n$ and $n \in \mathcal{L}_h$, $n \in \mathcal{L}_g$ as well. \\
Next, we suppose $p_0|(n^2-1)$. Without loss of generality we may assume that $p_0|(n+1)$; the proof is the same if $p_0|(n-1)$ instead (in the case where $p_0 = 2$, we may similarly only focus on $n+1$). In this case, in place of \eqref{eq:p_0nCase}, whenever $n \in \mathcal{L}_h$ we have
\begin{equation*}
                                               
g_{\lambda}(n) > g_{\lambda}((n+1)/p_0^\nu) + h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu) + \lambda \log(1+1/n).
\end{equation*}
Rearranging, we then get that
                           
\begin{align}\label{eq:p_0NotnCase}
h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu) \leq g_{\lambda}(n) - g_{\lambda}((n+1)/p_0^\nu) - \lambda\log(1+1/n).
\end{align}
We do similar case work as above. 
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item If the RHS of \eqref{eq:p_0NotnCase} is positive and the LHS is non-positive then replacing $h$ by $g$ preserves the inequality.
\item If the RHS of \eqref{eq:p_0NotnCase} is positive and the LHS is positive then (again since $c < 100$) we have
$$
0 < h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu) < 2C < K,
$$
and so $g_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu) = h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)$ in \eqref{eq:p_0NotnCase}.
\item If the RHS of \eqref{eq:p_0NotnCase} is negative then the LHS is negative, and on multiplying both sides by $-1$, we have
$$
|h_{\lambda}(p_0^\nu)| > |g_{\lambda}(n) - g_{\lambda}((n+1)/p_0^\nu)- \lambda\log(1+1/n)|.
$$
Since $h_{\lambda}$ and $g_{\lambda}$ share the same sign and $|h_{\lambda}| \leq |g_{\lambda}|$ by Lemma \ref{lem:clipProp}, the inequality \eqref{eq:p_0NotnCase} continues to hold with $h$ replaced by $g$.
                                                                                                     
\end{enumerate}
We deduce that when $p_0|(n+1)$, if $n \in \mathcal{L}_h$ then $n \in \mathcal{L}_g$. The claim with $n-1$ in place of $n+1$ is the same. The proof is now complete.
