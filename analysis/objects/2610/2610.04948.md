<!-- conjecture-1: body_md -->
\label{conj:optimal-strong}
For every finite simple unweighted graph $G$ and every
integer $k\ge3$, every optimal coloring of the Max-$k$-Cut
game on $(G,k)$ is a strong equilibrium.
<!-- end conjecture-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:optimal-not-strong}
For every integer $k\ge3$, there exists a finite simple,
connected, unweighted graph that admits an optimal
$k$-coloring which is not a strong equilibrium.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:multipartite}
Let $H$ be the complete $k$-partite graph with
$n\ge1$ vertices in each part.
Every coloring $\rho\colon V(H)\to[k]$ is either
optimal or has at least $n$ monochromatic edges.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
A coloring with no monochromatic edges is optimal, since assigning
a distinct color to each part cuts every edge. Suppose $\rho$ has
fewer than $n$ monochromatic edges. In each part $R_i$, at most
one vertex per monochromatic edge is incident to such an edge.
Thus there is a vertex $v_i\in R_i$ incident to no monochromatic
edge. The vertices $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ form a clique, so their colors
are distinct; relabel them so that $\rho(v_i)=i$.
Every vertex of $R_j$ is adjacent to $v_i$ for each $i\ne j$,
and therefore cannot have color $i$. Hence all vertices of
$R_j$ have color $j$, and no edge is monochromatic.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:allowed-colors}
Suppose the anchor sets are optimally colored,
with $R_j$ assigned color $j$.
If a core vertex uses a forbidden color, it can
be recolored with an allowed color so as to
strictly decrease the total number of
monochromatic edges.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $\rho$ be the current coloring and fix a core vertex $v$ using
a forbidden color $c$. Keep all other colors fixed. Assigning
color $a$ to $v$ creates exactly $m_v(a;\rho)$ incident
monochromatic edges. Every forbidden color has six neighbors of
$v$ in the corresponding anchor set, so $m_v(c;\rho)\ge6$.
We show that at least one allowed color gives
$m_v(a;\rho)\le5$.

Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be the two allowed colors
of $v$.
A core neighbor can have color $\alpha$ or color
$\beta$, but not both.
Consequently, the total contribution of core
neighbors to $m_v(\alpha;\rho)+m_v(\beta;\rho)$ is at most
the core degree of $v$.
The anchor contributions are fixed by the construction.
Therefore,
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{Group}
&
\text{Core degree}
&
\text{Anchor costs}
&
m_v(\alpha;\rho)+m_v(\beta;\rho)\text{, at most}
\\ \hline
A & 8 & 0,\ 3 & 11\\
B & 8 & 1,\ 0 & 9\\
C & 3 & 1,\ 0 & 4\\
D & 7 & 0,\ 3 & 10
\end{array}
\]
Since these counts are integers,
\[
  \min\{m_v(\alpha;\rho),m_v(\beta;\rho)\}
  \le
  \left\lfloor
    \frac{m_v(\alpha;\rho)+m_v(\beta;\rho)}{2}
  \right\rfloor.
\]
Hence an allowed color yields at most $5$, $4$,
$2$, or $5$ incident monochromatic edges for a
vertex in $A$, $B$, $C$, or $D$, respectively.
In every case, this is fewer than the at least six
conflicts under its current forbidden color.

Finally, recoloring $v$ changes only edges incident
to $v$.
The strict decrease in its incident monochromatic
edges is therefore also a strict decrease in the
total number of monochromatic edges.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:optimality-certificate}
Suppose every vertex of $R_j$ has color $j$, and
every core vertex uses an allowed color.
Then the resulting coloring $\rho$ satisfies
\[
  M(\rho)\ge24.
\]
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Write
\[
  x:=|\{a_i:\rho(a_i)=1\}|,\qquad
  y:=|\{b_i:\rho(b_i)=1\}|,
\]
and let
\[
  z:=|\{i:\rho(a_i)=\rho(b_i)=1\}|.
\]
Thus $0\le x,y\le4$.
We partition the potentially monochromatic edges
into three groups.

\medskip
\noindent
\emph{Edges between $A$ and $B$, and their anchor edges.}
The only common allowed color of $A$ and $B$ is $1$.
Since $a_i b_j$ is an edge precisely when $i\ne j$,
there are $xy-z$ monochromatic edges between these sets.
The monochromatic anchor edges incident to $A$ and $B$
number $3(4-x)$ and $4-y$, respectively.
Their combined contribution is therefore
\[
  16-3x-y+xy-z.
\]

\medskip
\noindent
\emph{Edges incident to $C$.}
First assign color $2$ to every vertex of $C$.
The resulting number of conflicts on edges incident to $C$
is $2(4-y)$: each of the $4-y$ vertices of $B$ with
color $2$ has exactly two neighbors in $C$.

Now consider recoloring $c_i$ from $2$ to $3$.
Its number of conflicts changes from the number of
color-$2$ vertices among $b_i,b_{p(i)}$ to one if
$\rho(a_i)=1$ (due to the conflict with the anchor),
and two if $\rho(a_i)=3$.
Thus assigning color $2$ to $c_i$ creates at most two
conflicts, while assigning color $3$ creates at least one.
Hence a strict reduction is possible only when both
$b_i$ and $b_{p(i)}$ have color $2$ and $\rho(a_i)=1$;
in that case, the saving is exactly one.

We now bound the total saving from recoloring vertices
of $C$. Each vertex $c_i$ can save at most one conflict,
and a saving requires, in particular,
$\rho(a_i)=1$ and $\rho(b_i)=2$.
Of the $x$ indices with $\rho(a_i)=1$, exactly $z$
have $\rho(b_i)=1$, leaving $x-z$ with $\rho(b_i)=2$.
Thus at most $x-z$ vertices of $C$ can save a conflict.

Because no two vertices of $C$ are adjacent, recoloring
one does not change the conflicts incident to another.
Starting from the $2(4-y)$ conflicts obtained by coloring
all of $C$ with color $2$, we can therefore remove at
most $x-z$ conflicts. Hence every allowed coloring of
$C$ leaves at least
\[
  2(4-y)-(x-z)=8-2y-x+z
\]
conflicts on edges incident to $C$.

                                         
                                            
                                                    
                                
   
                            
                
   

\medskip
\noindent
\emph{Edges incident to $D$.}
If $\rho(b_i)=1$, assigning color $1$ or color $2$
to $d_i$ produces, respectively,
\[
  x+y-1
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  7-y
\]
monochromatic edges, including its anchor edges.
If $\rho(b_i)=2$, the corresponding counts are
\[
  x+y
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  6-y.
\]
Indeed, $d_i$ is adjacent to every vertex of $A$,
to every vertex of $B$ except $b_i$, and to
three vertices of $R_2$.

There are $y$ indices of the first type and $4-y$
of the second. Also, combining the contributions counted
above, the total number of conflicts on edges incident
to $A\cup B\cup C$ is at least
\[
  (16-3x-y+xy-z)+(8-2y-x+z)
  =24-4x-3y+xy.
\]


Hence
\begin{align*}
  M(\rho)\ge L(x,y)
  &:= 
  24+x(y-4)-3y\\
  &\quad
  +y\min\{x+y-1,\,7-y\}\\
  &\quad
  +(4-y)\min\{x+y,\,6-y\}.
\end{align*}

Since $x,y\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}$, we can evaluate this
bound directly:

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
  x\backslash y & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4\\
  \hline
  0 & 24 & 24 & 24 & 24 & 24\\
  1 & 24 & 25 & 26 & 26 & 24\\
  2 & 24 & 26 & 28 & 28 & 24\\
  3 & 24 & 27 & 28 & 27 & 24\\
  4 & 24 & 28 & 28 & 26 & 24
\end{array}
\]

Every entry is at least $24$, so $M(\rho)\ge24$.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Fix $k\ge3$. The graph $G_k$ constructed in \Cref{sec:counterexample} is finite,
simple, connected, and unweighted.

We first show that every coloring of $G_k$ has at least
$24$ conflicts. Suppose, to the contrary, that a coloring
$\rho$ has fewer than $24$ conflicts.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:multipartite}, the anchor sets must
receive distinct colors. Relabeling the colors, we may
assume that their colors agree with those in the construction.
We can then apply Lemma~\ref{lem:allowed-colors} repeatedly
to give every core vertex an allowed color without increasing
the number of conflicts. The resulting coloring still has
fewer than $24$ conflicts, contradicting
Lemma~\ref{lem:optimality-certificate}.

Since $M(\sigma)=M(\tau)=24$, both $\sigma$ and $\tau$
minimize the number of conflicts and hence maximize
the cut size. However, $\tau$ agrees with $\sigma$ outside $Q$, and
Equation~\eqref{eq:coalition-gain} shows that every
member of $Q$ strictly gains by deviating to $\tau$.
Thus $\sigma$ is optimal but not a strong equilibrium.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The construction shows that an optimal coloring
need not be a strong equilibrium.
It does not rule out the existence of another
optimal coloring of $G_k$ that is a strong
equilibrium, and therefore leaves the general
existence question unresolved.
Also, we make no claim that the graph order or the
deviating coalition size is smallest possible.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:deviation-identity}
Let $\sigma$ and $\tau$ be colorings that agree outside
$S\subseteq V$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:deviation-identity}
\mathcal C(\tau)-\mathcal C(\sigma)
=
\sum_{v\in S}\bigl(u_v(\tau)-u_v(\sigma)\bigr)
-
\bigl(M_S(\sigma)-M_S(\tau)\bigr).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $B_S(\rho)$ denote the number of bichromatic edges
with exactly one endpoint in $S$ under $\rho$. Edges
outside $S$ are unchanged, so
\[
\mathcal C(\tau)-\mathcal C(\sigma)
=
B_S(\tau)-B_S(\sigma)
+
M_S(\sigma)-M_S(\tau).
\]
The sum of the coalition's utilities counts each
bichromatic boundary edge once and each bichromatic
internal edge twice. Hence
\[
\sum_{v\in S}\bigl(u_v(\tau)-u_v(\sigma)\bigr)
=
B_S(\tau)-B_S(\sigma)
+
2\bigl(M_S(\sigma)-M_S(\tau)\bigr).
\]
Subtracting gives~\eqref{eq:deviation-identity}.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:necessary-conflicts}
If $\sigma$ is an optimal coloring and $(S,\tau)$ is a
strong deviation from $\sigma$, then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:necessary-conflicts}
M_S(\sigma)
\ge
M_S(\tau)+|S|
\ge
|S|.
\end{equation}
Consequently, an optimal coloring $\sigma$ is a strong
equilibrium whenever
\[
M_S(\sigma)<|S|
\qquad
\text{for every nonempty }S\subseteq V.
\]
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Every deviating player gains at least one unit of utility,
since the graph is unweighted. By optimality and
Lemma~\ref{lem:deviation-identity},
\[
0
\ge
\mathcal C(\tau)-\mathcal C(\sigma)
\ge
|S|-\bigl(M_S(\sigma)-M_S(\tau)\bigr),
\]
which proves~\eqref{eq:necessary-conflicts}. The final
statement follows immediately.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:bounded-degree}
If $\Delta(G)\le 2k-1$, then every optimal coloring of
the Max-$k$-Cut game on $(G,k)$ is a strong equilibrium.
In particular, this holds whenever $\Delta(G)\le 5$
and $k\ge 3$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let $\sigma$ be an optimal coloring, and hence a Nash
equilibrium.
At a Nash equilibrium, the current color of $v$ minimizes
the number of its neighbors assigned that color. Therefore,
\[
m_v(\sigma(v);\sigma)
\le
\left\lfloor\frac{d(v)}{k}\right\rfloor
\le 1.
\]
For every nonempty $S\subseteq V$,
\[
2M_S(\sigma)
\le
\sum_{v\in S}m_v(\sigma(v);\sigma)
\le |S|,
\]
so $M_S(\sigma)\le\lfloor |S|/2\rfloor<|S|$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:necessary-conflicts} now applies.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:post-conflicts}
Let $\sigma$ be a Nash equilibrium and $(S,\tau)$ a strong deviation.
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:post-conflict-bound}
  M_S(\tau)\le
  \left\lfloor\frac{2e(S)-|S|}{4}\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
The bound is attained for arbitrarily large coalitions, for every
fixed $k\ge2$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
For $v\in S$, let $\sigma^{v}$ be obtained from $\sigma$ by
recoloring only $v$ to $\tau(v)$. Since $\sigma$ is a Nash equilibrium,
$u_v(\sigma^{v})\le u_v(\sigma)$. Hence
\[
1\le u_v(\tau)-u_v(\sigma)
\le u_v(\tau)-u_v(\sigma^{v})
=\sum_{w\in N(v)\cap S}
 \bigl(\mathbf 1_{\{\sigma(w)=\tau(v)\}}
       -\mathbf 1_{\{\tau(w)=\tau(v)\}}\bigr).
\]
After summing over $v\in S$, each internal edge monochromatic
under $\tau$ contributes exactly $-2$: both its endpoints
change color, so neither positive indicator is one.
Every other internal edge contributes at most $2$.
Consequently,
\[
  |S|\le 2\bigl(e(S)-M_S(\tau)\bigr)-2M_S(\tau)
        =2e(S)-4M_S(\tau),
\]
which proves the bound. The following construction proves sharpness.
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:post-conflicts}
Fix $k\ge2$ and an integer $t\ge1$. Let $S=X\cup Y$ induce a
clique on $2t$ vertices, with $|X|=|Y|=t$.
Add disjoint anchor sets $T_1,\ldots,T_k$, each of size $t$,
and join distinct anchor sets completely. Join every vertex of
$X$ to one fixed vertex of $T_1$, and every vertex of $Y$ to
one fixed vertex of $T_2$. Join every vertex of $S$ to every
vertex of $T_j$ for $j\ge3$. There are no further edges.

Under $\sigma$, color $X$ and $T_1$ with $1$, color $Y$ and
$T_2$ with $2$, and color each remaining $T_j$ with $j$.
Each core vertex has exactly $t$ neighbors of each available
color, so no unilateral move improves its utility.
An anchor vertex has either zero or $t$ same-colored neighbors
and at least $t$ neighbors of every other color.
Thus $\sigma$ is a Nash equilibrium.

Now interchange colors $1$ and $2$ on $S$, keeping the anchors
fixed, to obtain $\tau$. Each core vertex retains $t-1$
monochromatic clique edges and loses its one monochromatic
anchor edge, so every member of $S$ gains exactly one unit.
Moreover,
\[
 |S|=2t,\qquad e(S)=\binom{2t}{2},\qquad
 M_S(\tau)=2\binom{t}{2}=t(t-1)
          =\frac{2e(S)-|S|}{4}.
\]
For $t\ge4$, this gives $M_S(\tau)>|S|$; the ratio
$M_S(\tau)/|S|=(t-1)/2$ is unbounded.
These coalitions are not minimal when $t>1$: one vertex of
$X$ and one of $Y$ can already profit by swapping colors.
Accordingly, the example concerns arbitrary strong deviations
and makes no claim about a bound for minimal coalitions.
<!-- end example-1 -->
<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:density}
Let $\sigma$ be a Nash equilibrium and let $(S,\tau)$
be a strong deviation from $\sigma$. If
\[
\frac{2e(S)}{|S|}\le 5,
\]
then $M_S(\tau)\le |S|$. Conversely, if
$M_S(\tau)>|S|$, then
\[
2e(S)\ge 5|S|+4
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
|S|\ge 7.
\]

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
By~\eqref{eq:post-conflict-bound}, if $2e(S)\le 5|S|$,
then
\[
M_S(\tau)
\le \frac{2e(S)-|S|}{4}
\le |S|.
\]
Conversely, $M_S(\tau)>|S|$ implies
$M_S(\tau)\ge |S|+1$, and hence
\[
2e(S)\ge 4M_S(\tau)+|S|\ge 5|S|+4.
\]
Writing $s=|S|$ and using $2e(S)\le s(s-1)$ gives
$s^2-6s-4\ge 0$, so $s\ge 7$.
