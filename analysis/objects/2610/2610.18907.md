<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For every positive integer $k$, the existence of a proper $k$-coloring of $\mathbb R^3$ implies
the existence of a proper Lebesgue-measurable $k$-coloring. Consequently,
\[
  \chi(\mathbb R^3) = \chi_m(\mathbb R^3).
\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:bounds}
\[
  7 \le \chi(\mathbb R^3) = \chi_m(\mathbb R^3) \le 15.
\]
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- head-1: body_md -->
The first and crucial stage replaces an arbitrary proper coloring by a measurable partition for which monochromatic unit pairs occur on a set of measure zero in position and direction. The second stage removes those exceptional pairs without adding colors, which results in the proper measurable coloring.                                                                                                                                                                                                           
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  

For the first stage, the original spatial color indicators cannot be integrated: no measurability was assumed. Instead restrict the coloring to the countable dense set of algebraic points and consider the space of \emph{all} proper colorings of that set. Averaging translates and rotations of one coloring gives an invariant probability distribution on whole colorings. The random variable $f_i$ asks whether the origin has color $i$. Its translate asks the same question at another point. Properness becomes an identity saying that the expected product of these two indicators is zero at every algebraic unit displacement.

We first retain the observations that change continuously in expected squared error under small horizontal translations. The resulting functions $p_i$ are nonnegative weights summing to one. The frequency description of this operation lets rigidity identify what was discarded, and shows that its removal preserves zero correlations at nonvertical unit displacements. Positivity alone would not give this preservation; it is where rigidity is used.

To handle the height coordinate, start with a nonvertical unit vector and change its height slightly. Compensate by changing the horizontal length so the vector remains on the unit sphere. The compensated displacement still has zero correlation, and horizontal continuity controls the cost of undoing the compensation. This estimate permits averaging over small algebraic height shifts. The resulting weights $a_i$ are continuous in expected squared error under all translations, and their unit correlations remain zero. After choosing representatives measurable in position and in the random coloring, one probability sample yields the required spatial partition.

For the second stage, replace each spatial class by its density-one points: points at which the class occupies a proportion tending to one of every shrinking ball. These sets avoid unit distance and cover almost all of space. For any finite set of vertices, many sufficiently small common translations preserve the colors already prescribed at its density-one points and put all other vertices into the covered set. Translating back gives a compatible coloring of that finite set. Compactness then supplies a global coloring preserving all the prescribed colors. The translation may depend on the finite set; no single translation is required to work everywhere.

The invariant probability model and sampling argument follow the planar transfer method of~\cite[Proposition~4.2]{OpenAI}; the continuous-observation identification has its counterpart in~\cite[Lemma~4.1]{OpenAI}. Our external rigidity input is the statement of~\cite[Theorem~2.3]{OpenAI}, and the algebraic averaging construction follows~\cite[Section~2.2]{OpenAI}. The density and completion arguments are related to~\cite[Lemma~B.1 and proof of Lemma~B.2]{Davies}, with the density-point step credited there to~\cite[proof of Lemma~5.6]{DOV}. 

The main proof introduces each object when it becomes useful. Appendix~\ref{app:probability} gives the probability and function-space background and proves the representative lemma. Appendix~\ref{app:frequencies} proves the identification of continuous observations with continuous frequencies. Appendix~\ref{app:height-averaging} constructs the algebraic-height average used at the sphere step.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:factor}
Let a countable dense additive subgroup $G\subset\mathbb R^r$ act by
measure-preserving transformations on a compact metric space with
a completed Borel probability $\mu$. For its translation operators
$T_g$, put
\[
 H_G=\{v\in L^2(\mu):\lVert T_gv-v\rVert_2\to0
                 \text{ as }g\in G,\ g\to0\text{ in }\mathbb R^r\}.
\]
There is a completed sub-sigma-algebra $\mathcal F_G$ of measurable
events such that $H_G=L^2(\mathcal F_G)$. The orthogonal projection
onto $H_G$ is conditional expectation
$Pv=\mathbb E[v\mid\mathcal F_G]$. In particular, it preserves
nonnegative functions and the constant $1$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:smoothing}
Let $(V_t)_{t\in F}$ be linear operators on a complex Hilbert space $H$
which preserve inner products and satisfy $V_0=\operatorname{Id}$ and
$V_sV_t=V_{s+t}$. No continuity in $t$ is assumed. Let $Q$ be the
orthogonal projection onto the closed subspace
\[
 H_v=\left\{v\in H:\lVert V_tv-v\rVert\longrightarrow0
       \text{ as }t\in F,\ t\longrightarrow0\text{ in }\mathbb R\right\}.
\]
There is a complex-linear functional $I_{\mathbb C}$ on all bounded functions
$h:F\to\mathbb C$ which vanish outside some bounded real interval. It is
positive, meaning $I_{\mathbb C}(h)\geq0$ if $h\geq0$, and satisfies
\[
 I_{\mathbb C}(h(\,\cdot+s))=I_{\mathbb C}(h)\quad(s\in F),
 \qquad
 I_{\mathbb C}(\varphi|_F)=\int_{\mathbb R}\varphi(t)\,dt
       \quad(\varphi\in C_c(\mathbb R)).
\]
Here $C_c(\mathbb R)$ denotes the continuous functions which vanish outside a
bounded interval. For every bounded map $W:F\to H$ vanishing outside
such an interval, there is a unique vector $I_H(W)$ such that
\[
 \langle w,I_H(W)\rangle
   =I_{\mathbb C}\bigl(t\mapsto\langle w,W(t)\rangle\bigr)
       \quad(w\in H),
 \qquad
 \lVert I_H(W)\rVert\leq I_{\mathbb C}\bigl(t\mapsto\lVert W(t)\rVert\bigr).
\]
Inner products are linear in their second argument. The vector map is
linear and translation-invariant in $t$, and every bounded linear
operator $L:H\to H$ can be moved through it:
\[
 L I_H(W)=I_H(t\mapsto L W(t)).
\]
For every bounded $h:F\to\mathbb C$ and every nonnegative
$\varphi\in C_c(\mathbb R)$ of integral one,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:height-support-bound}
 \left|I_{\mathbb C}(\varphi h)\right|
 \leq\sup_{t\in F\cap\operatorname{supp}\varphi}|h(t)|.
\end{equation}
The product $\varphi h$ belongs to the domain of $I_{\mathbb C}$ even when
$h$ itself does not.

For $\varphi\in C_c(\mathbb R)$ define
\[
 S_\varphi v=I_H(t\mapsto\varphi(t)V_tv).
\]
This operator has range in $H_v$, vanishes on $H_v^\perp$, and satisfies
$\lVert S_\varphi\rVert\leq\int_{\mathbb R}|\varphi|$. If
$\varphi_\varepsilon\in C_c(\mathbb R)$, $\varphi_\varepsilon\geq0$,
$\int_{\mathbb R}\varphi_\varepsilon=1$, and
$\operatorname{supp}\varphi_\varepsilon\subset[-\delta_\varepsilon,
\delta_\varepsilon]$ with $\delta_\varepsilon\to0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smoothing}
 \lVert S_{\varphi_\varepsilon}v-Qv\rVert\longrightarrow0
 \qquad(v\in H).
\end{equation}
Finally, if $H=L^2(\Omega,\mu)$ for a completed Borel probability on a
compact metric space and the $V_t$ come from measure-preserving
transformations, then $Q$ preserves nonnegative functions and $Q1=1$.

\label{lem:completion}

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $B_1,\ldots,B_k$ be the given color classes. Choose Borel
representatives modulo null sets, make them disjoint in order, and
assign any remaining points to the last class. This gives a Borel
partition differing from the original only on a null set. We continue
to call its classes $B_i$. As observed after~\eqref{eq:weak}, this
change preserves weak properness: for each fixed direction $u$,
the pairs whose initial or final point meets a spatial null set have
only a null set of initial points.

\paragraph{An overlap identity in every direction.}
Define
\[
 g_i(x)=e^{-\lVert x\rVert^2}\mathbf 1_{B_i}(x),
 \qquad
 G_i(v)=\int_{\mathbb R^d}g_i(x)g_i(x+v)\,dx.
\]
The positive Gaussian puts $g_i$ in $L^2(\mathbb R^d)$ even when $B_i$ has
infinite volume. Its positivity also means that $G_i(v)>0$ exactly
when $B_i$ and $B_i-v$ overlap on a set of positive volume.

Ordinary spatial translations are continuous in $L^2$: if
$g\in L^2(\mathbb R^d)$, then $\lVert g(\cdot+h)-g\rVert_2\to0$ as $h\to0$.
To see this, approximate $g$ in $L^2$ by a continuous function of
compact support. Uniform continuity proves the assertion for the
approximation, and translations preserve the norm of the error.
Consequently, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
 |G_i(v)-G_i(w)|
 \le \lVert g_i\rVert_2
       \lVert g_i(\cdot+v)-g_i(\cdot+w)\rVert_2,
\]
so the nonnegative function $G_i$ is continuous.

Weak properness on all positive integer-radius balls, followed by
Tonelli's theorem, gives
$\mathbf 1_{B_i}(x)\mathbf 1_{B_i}(x+u)=0$ for almost every position--direction
pair. Multiplying by the Gaussian factors and integrating shows that
$G_i(u)=0$ for almost every unit direction. In dimensions $d\ge2$,
every nonempty relatively open part of the unit sphere has positive
surface measure. A continuous nonnegative function on that sphere
which vanishes almost everywhere must therefore vanish everywhere.
For $d=1$, almost-everywhere vanishing already includes both
directions, since each has positive mass. Thus, in all dimensions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:completion-overlap}
 G_i(u)=0\qquad(\lVert u\rVert=1).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Independent sets of density-one points.}
A point $x$ has \emph{density one} in a measurable set $B$ if
\[
 \lim_{r\downarrow0}
 \frac{\operatorname{vol}(B\cap B(x,r))}{\operatorname{vol}(B(x,r))}=1.
\]
This says that $B$ occupies nearly all sufficiently small balls
about $x$; it requires neither that $x\in B$ nor that $B$ contain
a neighborhood of $x$. Let $C_i$ be the set of density-one points
of $B_i$.

These sets are Borel. Indeed, for each fixed $r>0$ the ball average
\[
 A_{i,r}(x)=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(B_i\cap B(x,r))}{\operatorname{vol}(B(0,r))}
\]
is continuous in $x$: its change between two centers is bounded
by the volume of the symmetric difference of their balls, divided
by $\operatorname{vol}(B(0,r))$, and that bound tends to zero as the centers
approach one another. Moreover,
\[
 C_i=\{x:\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{i,1/n}(x)=1\}.
\]
The sequence $1/n$ detects the full density limit because any
intermediate radius lies between $1/(n+1)$ and $1/n$, whose ball
volumes have ratio tending to one. The displayed limit condition
is Borel, since it uses only countably many continuous functions.

The Lebesgue density theorem states that almost every point of
$B_i$ has density one in $B_i$, and almost every point of its
complement has density zero in $B_i$. It follows that
\[
 \operatorname{vol}(C_i\mathbin{\triangle}B_i)=0.
\]
The $C_i$ are disjoint: two disjoint classes cannot both occupy a
proportion tending to one of the same shrinking ball. Their union
$C_*=\bigcup_{i=1}^k C_i$ is conull, meaning that its complement has
measure zero. Also, every point of $C_i$ has density one in $C_i$
itself, because changing a set on a null set leaves every ball
volume unchanged.

Each $C_i$ is independent in the unit-distance graph. Suppose,
to the contrary, that $x,x+u\in C_i$ and $\lVert u\rVert=1$. Then both
$B_i$ and $B_i-u$ have density one at $x$. In a sufficiently small
ball about $x$, each misses less than one quarter of the volume.
Their intersection therefore occupies more than half the ball.
On this positive-volume intersection,
$g_i(q)g_i(q+u)>0$, contradicting~\eqref{eq:completion-overlap}.
We have obtained a proper coloring on $C_*$, with color $i$ on $C_i$.

\paragraph{A compatible coloring of each finite set.}
The remaining null set may have unit edges both to $C_*$ and
within itself, so we must extend the coloring while retaining its
prescribed values on $C_*$. Fix a finite set $V\subset\mathbb R^d$.
We seek one small translation $t$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:completion-shift}
 x+t\in C_i\quad(x\in V\cap C_i),
 \qquad
 x+t\in C_*\quad(x\in V\setminus C_*).
\end{equation}
We could then color each $x\in V$ by the color of $x+t$ in $C_*$.
This would respect every prescribed color on $V\cap C_*$, and
it would be proper because translation preserves distances.

Let $m=|V\cap C_*|$. If $m>0$, density one at each prescribed
point gives a radius $r>0$ such that, for every $x\in V\cap C_i$,
the bad shifts
$\{t\in B(0,r):x+t\notin C_i\}$ occupy less than
$1/(2m)$ of the ball. A common radius exists because there are
only finitely many prescribed points. The union bound leaves
more than half the ball satisfying all these requirements.
If $m=0$, begin with any ball about zero.
For each remaining $x\in V\setminus C_*$, the shifts failing
$x+t\in C_*$ form a null set. Removing these finitely many null
sets still leaves a suitable $t$. The radius can be chosen
arbitrarily small, but it and the translation may depend on $V$.

\paragraph{Compactness with the prescribed colors.}
Consider the compact product
$X=\{1,\ldots,k\}^{\mathbb R^d}$, with the finite color set discrete.
An element of $X$ assigns a color to every spatial point.
For every unit edge $\{x,y\}$ impose the constraint $c(x)\ne c(y)$;
for every $x\in C_i$ impose $c(x)=i$.
Each constraint defines a closed subset of $X$, because it
depends on finitely many coordinates. Every finite collection
of constraints involves a finite vertex set $V$, and the
translation argument gives a coloring of $V$ satisfying them.
Extend that finite assignment arbitrarily to the other
coordinates. Thus every finite collection of these closed sets
has nonempty intersection. Compactness gives a point in their
total intersection: a proper coloring of all $\mathbb R^d$ retaining
the color of every point in $C_*$.
This is the graph-compactness argument of
de Bruijn and Erd\H{o}s~\cite{dBE}, with fixed-color constraints
included. No limiting translation is needed.

The final color classes have the form
\[
 \widetilde B_i=C_i\cup N_i,
 \qquad N_i\subseteq\mathbb R^d\setminus C_*.
\]
The sets $C_i$ are Borel, and the remainder is a Borel null set.
Every subset $N_i$ of that remainder is measurable for completed
Lebesgue measure. Hence the $\widetilde B_i$ are Lebesgue measurable,
as required. The compactness argument does not assert that these
final classes are Borel.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:conditional-expectation}
Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu)$ be a probability space and
$\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F$ a sub-$\sigma$-algebra. For every
integrable $v$ there is a $\mathcal G$-measurable integrable function
$\mathbb E[v\mid\mathcal G]$, unique almost everywhere, such that
\[
 \int_B\mathbb E[v\mid\mathcal G]\,d\mu=\int_Bv\,d\mu
 \qquad(B\in\mathcal G).
\]
The operation is linear, preserves nonnegativity and the constant
$1$, and preserves expectations. For $v\in L^2(\mu)$ it is the
orthogonal projection onto the closed subspace $L^2(\mathcal G)$.

First, every completed-measurable $[0,1]$-valued function on $\Omega$
has a Borel representative. Indeed, approximate it by finite simple
functions, for example by rounding down to successive dyadic values.
Each measurable level set differs from a Borel set by a null set.
Replacing those sets gives Borel simple functions with the same
values outside the union of countably many null exceptions. Take
their limit on the Borel set where they converge, put zero elsewhere,
and truncate to $[0,1]$. This gives the required Borel representative.

Let $Q_m=[-m,m]^3$, set $Q_0=\varnothing$, and put
$W_m=Q_m\setminus Q_{m-1}$. These are disjoint bounded Borel sets
whose union is $\mathbb R^3$. Fix $m$. Norm continuity of $x\mapsto u_x$
is uniform on the compact cube $Q_m$. For each $n$, a sufficiently
fine finite Borel partition $W_m=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{r_n}E_{n,\ell}$,
with sample points $x_{n,\ell}\in E_{n,\ell}$, therefore gives
\[
 \lVert u_x-u_{x_{n,\ell}}\rVert_2\leq2^{-n}
 \qquad(x\in E_{n,\ell}).
\]
Empty cells can be discarded. Choose Borel $[0,1]$-valued
representatives $h_{n,\ell}$ of these finitely many sample vectors
and define
\[
 H_n(x,\omega)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r_n}
       \mathbf 1_{E_{n,\ell}}(x)h_{n,\ell}(\omega).
\]
This is jointly Borel on $W_m\times\Omega$ and takes values in
$[0,1]$. Each section satisfies
$\lVert H_n(x,\cdot)-u_x\rVert_2\leq2^{-n}$. Consequently
\[
 \lVert H_n-H_r\rVert_{L^2(W_m\times\Omega)}
 \leq\operatorname{vol}(W_m)^{1/2}(2^{-n}+2^{-r}).
\]
Thus the sequence has a limit $V$ in the product $L^2$ space.
Choose a subsequence $H_{n_j}$ such that
$\sum_j\lVert H_{n_j}-V\rVert_{L^2(W_m\times\Omega)}^2<\infty$.
Tonelli gives
\[
 \sum_j|H_{n_j}(x,\omega)-V(x,\omega)|^2<\infty
 \quad\text{for almost every }(x,\omega),
\]
after choosing any measurable representative of $V$. In particular,
$H_{n_j}$ converges almost everywhere on the product.

The convergence set of this sequence of Borel scalar functions is
Borel. Define $H^{(m)}$ to be its limit there and zero elsewhere.
Then $H^{(m)}$ is jointly Borel and $[0,1]$-valued. Fubini shows that,
for almost every fixed $x\in W_m$, the convergence holds for
$\mu$-almost every $\omega$. Bounded convergence on the probability
space then gives
$H_{n_j}(x,\cdot)\to H^{(m)}(x,\cdot)$ in $L^2(\mu)$.
Since the same sections tend to $u_x$ in that norm,
$H^{(m)}(x,\cdot)$ represents $u_x$ for almost every $x\in W_m$.

Finally, use $H^{(m)}$ on $W_m\times\Omega$ for each $m$. A
countable combination on these disjoint Borel regions gives one
jointly Borel function $H:\mathbb R^3\times\Omega\to[0,1]$. Enlarge each
exceptional spatial null set to a Borel null set and take their
countable union $N$. For every $x\notin N$, the section $H(x,\cdot)$
represents $u_x$, as required.

<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{app:spectral-theorem}
Let $g\mapsto T_g$ be a unitary representation of a countable discrete abelian group $G$ on a complex Hilbert space $H$. There is a unique projection-valued Borel measure $\Pi$ on $D_G$ such that
\[
 T_g=\int_{D_G}d(g)\,d\Pi(d).
\]
Precisely, each $\Pi(S)$ is an orthogonal projection,
\[
 \Pi(D_G)=\operatorname{Id},\qquad
 \Pi(S)\Pi(B)=\Pi(S\cap B),
\]
and for disjoint Borel sets $S_n$,
\[
 \Pi\Bigl(\bigcup_n S_n\Bigr)v=\sum_n\Pi(S_n)v
 \quad(v\in H),
\]
where the sum converges in Hilbert norm. For every $v\in H$, the finite positive Borel measure
\[
 \lambda_v(S)=\langle v,\Pi(S)v\rangle=\lVert \Pi(S)v\rVert^2
\]
satisfies
\[
 \langle v,T_gv\rangle=\int_{D_G}d(g)\,d\lambda_v(d),
 \qquad \lambda_v(D_G)=\lVert v\rVert^2.
\]
It is uniquely determined by these Fourier coefficients.

<!-- theorem-9: body_md -->
\label{app:bochner}
A continuous function $c:\mathbb R^r\to\mathbb C$ is positive definite if and only if there is a finite positive Borel measure $\nu$ on $\mathbb R^r$ such that
\[
 c(x)=\int_{\mathbb R^r}e^{i\xi\cdot x}\,d\nu(\xi).
\]
The measure is unique and has total mass $c(0)$.

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
First, $H_c$ is a closed linear subspace. Linearity follows from the triangle inequality. If $v_n\in H_c$ and $v_n\to v$ in $L^2$, then
\[
 \lVert T_gv-v\rVert_2
 \leq 2\lVert v-v_n\rVert_2+\lVert T_gv_n-v_n\rVert_2.
\]
Choose $n$ first and then make $g$ small. This proves that $v\in H_c$.

Its bounded elements also form an algebra. Here bounded means bounded outside a probability-null set, and $\lVert v\rVert_\infty$ is the least such bound. Constants and complex conjugates belong to $H_c$. For bounded $v,w\in H_c$, preservation of products and of these bounds gives
\[
 \begin{split}
 \lVert T_g(vw)-vw\rVert_2
 &\leq \lVert v\rVert_\infty\lVert T_gw-w\rVert_2
       +\lVert w\rVert_\infty\lVert T_gv-v\rVert_2
       \longrightarrow0.
 \end{split}
\]

Bounded elements suffice to recover the whole subspace. For $M>0$ let
\[
 \theta_M(z)=
 \begin{cases}
 z,&|z|\leq M,\\
 Mz/|z|,&|z|>M.
 \end{cases}
\]
This clips a complex value to the disk of radius $M$. As the nearest-point map onto a closed convex disk, it is $1$-Lipschitz. Composition with $\theta_M$ commutes with $T_g$, and consequently
\[
 \lVert T_g\theta_M(v)-\theta_M(v)\rVert_2
 \leq\lVert T_gv-v\rVert_2.
\]
Thus $v\in H_c$ implies $\theta_M(v)\in H_c$. Moreover,
\[
 |v-\theta_M(v)|^2
 \leq |v|^2\mathbf 1_{\{|v|>M\}},
\]
so dominated convergence gives $\theta_M(v)\to v$ in $L^2$.

We now identify the event information represented by this subspace. Let $\mathcal A$ be the collection of all everywhere bounded, $\mathcal F$-measurable real functions whose $L^2$ classes lie in $H_c$. Including every bounded measurable representative of each class makes $\mathcal A$ a pointwise algebra, rather than merely an algebra of equivalence classes. Define $\mathcal F_c$ to be the completion by ambient null sets of
\[
 \sigma(\mathcal A)
 =\sigma\bigl(\{v^{-1}(B):v\in\mathcal A,
                              \ B\subseteq\mathbb R\text{ Borel}\}\bigr).
\]
These are the events determined by the bounded continuous observations. The inclusion of null-set modifications makes this definition independent of the representatives chosen.

The needed passage from an algebra to all its measurable functions is the \emph{functional monotone class theorem}: if an algebra $\mathcal A$ of bounded real functions contains the constant $1$ and is contained in a real vector space $\mathcal M$ of bounded functions closed under pointwise increasing limits of uniformly bounded sequences, then $\mathcal M$ contains every bounded $\sigma(\mathcal A)$-measurable real function. Apply it here with $\mathcal M=\mathcal A$. If $v_n\in\mathcal A$ increase pointwise and are uniformly bounded, their limit $v$ is measurable and $v_n\to v$ in $L^2$ by dominated convergence on the probability space. Closedness of $H_c$ gives $v\in\mathcal A$. The theorem therefore shows that every bounded $\sigma(\mathcal A)$-measurable function belongs to $H_c$. Completion changes representatives only on null sets. Taking real and imaginary parts, and then bounded truncations, yields
\[
 L^2(\mathcal F_c)\subseteq H_c.
\]
Conversely, the real and imaginary parts of each $\theta_M(v)$, for $v\in H_c$, belong to $\mathcal A$ after choosing bounded representatives. Hence $\theta_M(v)$ is $\mathcal F_c$-measurable. Its $L^2$ limit is also $\mathcal F_c$-measurable up to a null set: one may take an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence and use completion. We have proved
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-information}
 H_c=L^2(\mathcal F_c).
\end{equation}
By the conditional-expectation result, Theorem~\ref{thm:conditional-expectation}, the orthogonal projection $P$ onto this space is
\[
 Pv=\mathbb E[v\mid\mathcal F_c].
\]
It therefore preserves nonnegativity and the constant $1$, as asserted in Lemma~\ref{lem:factor}.

Next we extend the parameters on $H_c$. Commutativity and unitarity show that $T_gH_c=H_c$ for every $g\in G$; the reverse inclusion follows by using $-g$. For $x\in\mathbb R^r$, choose $g_n\in G$ with $g_n\to x$. If $v\in H_c$, then
\[
 \lVert T_{g_n}v-T_{g_m}v\rVert_2
 =\lVert T_{g_n-g_m}v-v\rVert_2\longrightarrow0.
\]
Define $T_xv$ to be the limit in the closed space $H_c$. Comparing two approximating sequences by the same estimate proves that this definition is independent of the choice. Linearity, norm preservation and the group law pass to limits. The inverse is $T_{-x}$, so every $T_x$ is unitary. Continuity at zero on $G$ passes to real parameters by density; the group law then gives continuity at every $x$. Thus the extension is \emph{strongly continuous}: for each fixed $v\in H_c$, the map $x\mapsto T_xv$ is continuous in $L^2$ norm. The nonnegative cone of $L^2$ is closed, so these limiting operators preserve nonnegativity; they also fix $1$. This constructs an action on $H_c$, without asserting a pointwise action of real translations on every $\omega\in\Omega$.

It remains to identify this same subspace by frequencies. Let $\Pi$ be the spectral measure of the original $G$-representation. The spectral theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-spectral-norm}
 \lVert T_gv-v\rVert_2^2
 =\int_{D_G}|d(g)-1|^2\,d\lambda_v(d).
\end{equation}
If $\lambda_v(D_G\setminus C_G)=0$, the integrand tends to zero as $g\to0$ at every character carrying the measure and is bounded by $4$. Dominated convergence implies $v\in H_c$.

For the converse, take $v\in H_c$ and use the real-parameter extension just obtained. The function
\[
 c(x)=\langle v,T_xv\rangle,\qquad x\in\mathbb R^r,
\]
is continuous. It is positive definite because
\[
 \sum_{j,\ell}\overline{\alpha_j}\alpha_\ell c(x_\ell-x_j)
 =\lVert \sum_\ell\alpha_\ell T_{x_\ell}v\rVert_2^2\geq0.
\]
Bochner's theorem supplies a finite positive measure $\nu$ on $\mathbb R^r$ with
\[
 c(x)=\int_{\mathbb R^r}e^{i\xi\cdot x}\,d\nu(\xi).
\]
Map this measure into $D_G$ by $j$: explicitly, set
\[
 \widetilde\nu(S)=\nu(j^{-1}(S))
 \qquad(S\subseteq D_G\text{ Borel}).
\]
This is the distribution after applying the map $j$, also called its pushforward. It gives $D_G\setminus C_G$ mass zero. For $g\in G$ its Fourier coefficients satisfy
\[
 \int_{D_G}d(g)\,d\widetilde\nu(d)
 =\int_{\mathbb R^r}e^{i\xi\cdot g}\,d\nu(\xi)
 =c(g)=\int_{D_G}d(g)\,d\lambda_v(d).
\]

For completeness, these equalities determine the measures. Finite complex linear combinations of the evaluation functions $d\mapsto d(g)$ form an algebra: multiplying two evaluations adds their arguments. The algebra contains constants, is closed under complex conjugation because $\overline{d(g)}=d(-g)$, and separates distinct points of $D_G$. The complex Stone--Weierstrass theorem therefore makes it uniformly dense in the continuous functions on $D_G$. Equality of its integrals implies equality of integrals of all continuous functions, because both measures are finite. Finite Borel measures on a compact metric space are regular and are uniquely determined by those continuous tests. Hence $\widetilde\nu=\lambda_v$, and $\lambda_v$ is carried by $C_G$.

We have proved both implications in
\[
 v\in H_c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \lambda_v(D_G\setminus C_G)=0.
\]
The right side describes the range of $\Pi(C_G)$: its complementary projection has squared norm $\lambda_v(D_G\setminus C_G)$. Thus the two orthogonal projections onto the same subspace coincide,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous-projection-identification}
 P=\Pi(C_G).
\end{equation}
In particular, $v\perp H_c$ implies
\[
 \lambda_v(C_G)=\lVert \Pi(C_G)v\rVert_2^2=0.
\]
This proves Lemma~\ref{lem:frequencies} for precisely the subspace already identified in Lemma~\ref{lem:factor}.
