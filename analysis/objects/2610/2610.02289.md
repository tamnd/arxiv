<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
For every integer $n\ge5$, put
$\varepsilon_n=0$ if $n$ is odd and $\varepsilon_n=1$ if $n$ is even. Then

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\frac{n(n-3)}2-\varepsilon_n
\ \le\ p_n\ \le\ \binom{n+1}{2}-4.}
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
Suppose a copositive $S$ has exactly
$q<\infty$ nonnegative zero rays, represented by
$u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(q)}$. If

\begin{equation}
h=\dim\operatorname{span}\{u^{(a)}u^{(a)\mathsf T}:1\le a\le q\},
\end{equation}

then the maximum cp-rank of matrices in $F(S)$ is $h$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
If $q=0$, then $F(S)=\{0\}$ and $h=0$. Otherwise, choose a
factorization $A=BB^{\mathsf T}$ for $A\in F(S)$. Copositivity makes
every summand's pairing with $S$ nonnegative, so each nonzero column of
$B$ lies on a listed zero ray. Thus $F(S)$ is generated by their outer
products. The
conic form of Carath\'eodory's theorem says that any point of a cone
spanning an $h$-dimensional space is a nonnegative combination of at
most $h$ generators, so cp-rank is at most $h$. A point of $F(S)$
outside the finitely many spans of fewer than $h$ generators has
cp-rank $h$. Such a point exists because a finite union of proper
linear subspaces cannot cover an $h$-dimensional cone.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
Let $T=\{v_1,v_2,v_3\}$ be a
surrounding triple, with its vertices listed in cyclic order and gaps
$k,\ell,r$ as above. Up to multiplication by a positive scalar, there
is exactly one nonnegative zero $u$ of $S$ with
$\operatorname{supp}(u)=T$. A representative, with all other
coordinates zero, is

\begin{equation}
u_{v_1}=\sin\phi_\ell,\qquad
u_{v_2}=\sin\phi_r,\qquad
u_{v_3}=\sin\phi_k,
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For the vertices in cyclic order, take planar unit vectors
$w_1,w_2,w_3$ at angles $0,\phi_k,\phi_k+\phi_\ell$.
Their \textbf{Gram matrix}, the matrix $(w_a\cdot w_b)_{a,b=1}^3$ of pairwise
inner products, is $S_T$, and
$\sin\phi_\ell w_1+\sin\phi_r w_2+\sin\phi_k w_3=0$:
the vertical coordinate is
$\sin\phi_k(\sin\phi_r+\sin(\phi_k+\phi_\ell))=0$,
and the horizontal coordinate is
$\sin\phi_\ell+\sin(\phi_k+\phi_r)=0$, using
$\phi_k+\phi_\ell+\phi_r=2\pi$.
Thus (3.3) is the unique nonnegative kernel ray of the rank-two
positive semidefinite matrix $S_T$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
A vertex set containing no
surrounding triple lies in an open semicircle, hence in a cyclic interval
of at most $m$ steps. A four-vertex set containing a surrounding triple
contains exactly two such triples. They share a pair $\{i,j\}$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
A surrounding triple is precisely a triple of polygon vertices
whose convex hull contains the origin: three points on a circle enclose
its center exactly when they do not fit in an open semicircle, equivalently
when none of their cyclic gaps exceeds $m$. If the origin were in the convex
hull of a vertex set, planar Carath\'eodory's theorem would put it in the
convex hull of at most three of those vertices. One vertex cannot
contain the origin, and a segment between two vertices could contain
it only if they were antipodal, impossible for odd $n$. Hence a set
with no surrounding triple has a convex hull omitting the origin.
A linear functional strictly positive on this convex hull places all
its vertices in an open half-plane through the origin, hence in an open
semicircle spanning at most $m$ cyclic steps.

For four vertices let $g_1,g_2,g_3,g_4$ be their cyclic gaps, summing
to $2m+1$. If any $g_i>m$, no triple is surrounding. Otherwise the
triple obtained by omitting a vertex is surrounding exactly when the
two gaps merged by that omission sum to at most $m$. The sums
$g_1+g_2$ and $g_3+g_4$ add to $2m+1$, so exactly one is at most $m$;
the same holds for $g_2+g_3$ and $g_4+g_1$. Hence exactly two triples
are surrounding. They share a pair.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
If $u,v$ represent the rays of the two
surrounding triples in a four-set, then $u^{\mathsf T}Sv>0$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Because $S$ depends only on cyclic distance, rotations and
reflections preserve it. Relabel the common pair as $\{0,k\}$ with
$1\le k\le m$. A third vertex of a surrounding triple through this
pair lies in $\{m+1,\ldots,m+k\}$. Write the third vertices of
$u,v$ as $p,q$, respectively, and set
$d=d(p,q)=|p-q|$, so $1\le d\le m-1$.
Since $1\le p-k,n-p\le m$, we have
$ap-2t=\phi_k+\phi_{p-k}=2\pi-\phi_{n-p}$, and likewise for $q$.
Choose planar unit vectors at angles
$0,\phi_k,ap-2t,aq-2t$, indexed by $0,k,p,q$, and let $G$ be their
Gram matrix. Since $an=2\pi+3t$, its entries agree with $S$ on each
surrounding triple, while $G_{pq}=\cos(ad)$. Each of $u,v$ is a
dependence among its triple's planar vectors, so $u^{\mathsf T}Gv=0$.
The only entry at which $S$ and $G$ can differ in $u^{\mathsf T}Sv$
is the pair $\{p,q\}$:

\begin{equation}
u^{\mathsf T}Sv=u_pv_q[\cos(ad-t)-\cos(ad)]
=2u_pv_q\sin(ad-t/2)\sin(t/2)>0.
\end{equation}

Here $ad-t/2>0$ follows from $a>t$, and $ad<\pi$ follows from
$d\le m-1$ and $t<2\pi/(n-3)$, a weaker consequence of (3.1).
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
The matrix $S$ is copositive,
and its nonnegative zero rays are exactly those in Lemma 3.1.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Minimize $x^{\mathsf T}Sx$ on the simplex
$\Delta=\{x\ge0:\sum_i x_i=1\}$, and let $I$ be the support of a
minimizer. If $I$ contains no surrounding triple, Lemma 3.2 places it
in a cyclic interval $\{s,s+1,\ldots,s+L\}$ modulo $n$, with $L\le m$.
For each $i\in I$, choose the unique $r_i\in\{0,\ldots,L\}$ with
$i\equiv s+r_i\pmod n$, and let
$G_{ij}=\cos(a(r_i-r_j))$, a planar Gram matrix. For $i\ne j$ in
the interval, $d=|r_i-r_j|\le m$ and

\begin{equation}
S_{ij}-G_{ij}=2\sin(ad-t/2)\sin(t/2)>0.
\end{equation}

Indeed $a>t$ and
$am-t/2=\pi-\pi/n+(2n-3)t/(2n)<\pi$. The diagonals agree.
Since $G$ is positive semidefinite, the off-diagonal correction gives
$x^{\mathsf T}Sx>0$ for supports of size at least two;
singleton supports have value one.

If $I$ is a surrounding triple, $S_I$ is positive semidefinite by
Lemma 3.1. If $I$ contains a surrounding triple and at least one more
vertex, choose a four-set inside $I$ containing it. Let $u,v$ be its two
triangle rays, each normalized to have coordinate sum one. Then
$y=u-v$ has coordinate sum zero, and Lemma 3.3 gives
$y^{\mathsf T}Sy=-2u^{\mathsf T}Sv<0$.
Both $x+\epsilon y$ and $x-\epsilon y$ remain in $\Delta$ for small
$\epsilon>0$, but the average of their quadratic values is
$x^{\mathsf T}Sx+\epsilon^2 y^{\mathsf T}Sy<x^{\mathsf T}Sx$,
contradicting minimality. Hence the minimum is nonnegative and $S$ is
copositive.

A normalized zero minimizes on $\Delta$, so the cases force its
support to be a surrounding triple; $S_I$ then forces (3.3).
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
$\operatorname{rank}H=\operatorname{rank}M$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
For a triple $T=\{i,j,k\}$, let
$g_T=\sin\phi_{d(i,j)}\sin\phi_{d(i,k)}\sin\phi_{d(j,k)}>0$.
For a pair $e=\{a,b\}$ write $d(e)=d(a,b)$, and let $H_{\mathrm{off}}$
be the block of $H$ with only off-diagonal columns. If $e\subset T$,
equation (3.3) gives $u_au_b\sin\phi_{d(e)}=g_T$. Thus

\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{off}}=\operatorname{diag}(g_T)\,M\,
\operatorname{diag}\bigl(1/\sin\phi_{d(e)}\bigr),
\end{equation}

Both diagonal factors are invertible, so this block has rank
$\operatorname{rank}M$. For every zero $u$ and vertex $i$ with
$u_i>0$, varying that coordinate slightly in both directions preserves
nonnegativity. Copositivity and $u^{\mathsf T}Su=0$ therefore give
$(Su)_i=0$. If $u_i=0$, the following equation is trivial. Hence

\begin{equation}
u_i^2=-\sum_{j\ne i}S_{ij}u_iu_j.
\end{equation}

Each diagonal column of $H$ is a fixed linear combination of its
off-diagonal columns. It adds no rank.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
For every odd $n=2m+1\ge5$,

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{rank}M=\binom n2-n=\frac{n(n-3)}2.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Because $n$ is odd, every unordered pair has a unique form
$\{i,i+k\}$ with $1\le k\le m$. Label it by $(i,k)$ and write
$x_{i,k}$ for its coordinate.
For a surrounding triple with directed cyclic gaps $k,\ell,r\le m$
and $k+\ell+r=n$, the equation $Mx=0$ is

\begin{equation}
x_{i,k}+x_{i+k,\ell}+x_{i+k+\ell,r}=0.
\end{equation}

Over $\mathbb C$, the functions $i\mapsto z^i$ for $z^n=1$ form a
basis of functions on $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$. Thus every array $x$ has
the Fourier expansion

\[
x_{i,k}=\sum_{z^n=1}z^if_k(z),\qquad
f_k(z)=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}z^{-i}x_{i,k}.
\]

Substitution into (4.4) and comparison of the independent functions
$i\mapsto z^i$ show that the complex kernel splits into \textbf{modes}
$x_{i,k}=z^if_k$, one for each $z$. The equations for a mode are

\begin{equation}
f_k+z^kf_\ell+z^{k+\ell}f_r=0
\qquad(k+\ell+r=n;\ 1\le k,\ell,r\le m).
\end{equation}

For $z\ne1$, subtract the equations for $(k,m+1-k,m)$ and
$(k,m,m+1-k)$; the result is (4.6).

\begin{equation}
(1-z^m)f_{m+1-k}=(1-z^{m+1-k})f_m.
\end{equation}

Since $\gcd(m,n)=1$, $z^m\ne1$. As $k$ varies, (4.6) fixes every
$f_j$ as a multiple of $1-z^j$. Conversely these values satisfy
(4.5), whose left side telescopes to $1-z^n=0$. Thus the kernel in
each nontrivial mode has dimension one.

For $z=1$, compare the equations for
$(k,m+1-k,m)$ and $(k+1,m+1-k,m-1)$,
$1\le k\le m-1$. They give
$f_{k+1}-f_k=f_m-f_{m-1}$, so $f_k=Ak+B$. Every equation (4.5) then
reduces to $nA+3B=0$. This mode also has dimension one. There are $n$
values of $z$, so $\dim_{\mathbb C}\ker M=n$. The matrix $M$ is
real, hence its real nullity is $n$ as well. With $\binom n2$ columns,
(4.3) follows.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
For every $m\ge3$,

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{rank}M'=\binom{2m}{2}-2m-1
=\frac{N(N-3)}2-1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Divide the columns of the full odd-order matrix $M$ into
$P_0$, the pairs avoiding $0$, and $P_\bullet$, the pairs through $0$.
Divide its rows into those avoiding $0$, whose $P_0$ block is $M'$,
and the rows $R_0$ of surrounding triples through $0$. Write $U$ for
the row space of $M'$
padded by zeros on $P_\bullet$, and $W$ for the row space of $R_0$.
Since $\operatorname{rowspace}M=U+W$,

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{rank}M'=\operatorname{rank}M-\operatorname{rank}R_0
+\dim(U\cap W).
\end{equation}

A triple $\{0,j,k\}$ with $1\le j<k\le2m$ is surrounding exactly when
$j\le m<k$ and $k-j\le m$. Encode these triples by the edges of a
bipartite graph $B$ with left vertices $1,\ldots,m$ and right
vertices $m+1,\ldots,2m$, where $j$ is adjacent to
$m+1,\ldots,j+m$. Write $E(B)$ for its edge set. The graph is
connected and has

\begin{equation}
|E(B)|=\sum_{j=1}^m j=\frac{m(m+1)}2.
\end{equation}

Each row of $R_0$ contains the $P_0$ coordinate indexed by its pair
$\{j,k\}$, which occurs in no other row of $R_0$. Thus its rows are
independent and $\operatorname{rank}R_0=|E(B)|$.
For a pair $\{a,b\}$, let $b_{ab}$ be its unit coordinate vector in
the space indexed by all unordered pairs. An element of $W$ is
$\sum_{jk\in E(B)}\lambda_{jk}(b_{0j}+b_{0k}+b_{jk})$. Its
$P_\bullet$ coordinates vanish precisely when
$\sum_{f\ni v}\lambda_f=0$ at each vertex $v$ of $B$. Define
$Z(B)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb R^{E(B)}:\sum_{f\ni v}\lambda_f=0
\text{ for every vertex }v\}$, and use the same definition for
subgraphs $G$ of $B$. For an edge weighting $\lambda\in\mathbb R^{E(G)}$,
its support is $\{e\in E(G):\lambda_e\ne0\}$. Orient each edge of $G$
from left to right, and let $D_G$ be its vertex-edge matrix, with
$-1$ at the left endpoint, $+1$ at the right endpoint, and zero
elsewhere. The equations $D_G\lambda=0$ differ from the defining
vertex-sum equations only by signs, so $Z(G)=\ker D_G$ is the real
cycle space of $G$. The map
$\pi(\lambda)=\sum_{jk\in E(B)}\lambda_{jk}b_{jk}$ is injective, so
$\dim(U\cap W)$ equals the dimension of the $\lambda\in Z(B)$ for
which $\pi(\lambda)\in U$.

The edge $e_*=\{m,m+1\}$ belongs to $B$ but its column in $M'$ is
zero. Indeed no surrounding triple avoiding $0$ contains both
$m$ and $m+1$: a third vertex on either complementary arc makes one
cyclic gap exceed $m$. Hence $\pi(\lambda)\in U$ implies
$\lambda_{e_*}=0$. Let $B-e_*$ be the graph obtained by deleting
this edge, and regard $Z(B-e_*)$ as a subspace of $\mathbb R^{E(B)}$
by assigning zero to its $e_*$ coordinate. This gives the upper
inclusion

\begin{equation}
\{\lambda\in Z(B):\pi(\lambda)\in U\}\subseteq Z(B-e_*),
\end{equation}

We prove the reverse inclusion by four-cycles. For any cycle in a
bipartite graph, its \textbf{cycle vector} assigns alternating $+1$ and
$-1$ to successive edges and zero to all other edges. A four-cycle in $B$
has vertices $j<j'\le m<k<k'$ with $k'-j\le m$ and alternating
pair-coordinate vector, the image of its cycle vector under $\pi$,
$v=b_{jk}-b_{j'k}+b_{j'k'}-b_{jk'}$.
It avoids $e_*$ exactly when $j'\le m-1$ or $k\ge m+2$. In the first
case set $\ell=j'+m+1>k'$; in the second set
$\ell=k-m-1<j$. The defining inequalities of $B$ show in either
case that all four triples $\{j,k,\ell\}$,
$\{j',k,\ell\}$, $\{j',k',\ell\}$, and
$\{j,k',\ell\}$ are surrounding and avoid $0$. Indeed, for
$x\in\{j,j'\}$ and $y\in\{k,k'\}$ the cyclic gaps of
$\{x,y,\ell\}$ are, in the first case,
$y-x\le m$, $\ell-y\le m$, $n-\ell+x=m+x-j'\le m$;
in the second case they are $x-\ell\le m$, $y-x\le m$,
$n-y+\ell=m+k-y\le m$. If $T_{xyz}$
denotes the incidence row of $\{x,y,z\}$, cancellation of the pairs through
$\ell$ gives

\begin{equation}
T_{jk\ell}-T_{j'k\ell}+T_{j'k'\ell}-T_{jk'\ell}=v.
\end{equation}

Thus every four-cycle vector of $B-e_*$ maps into $U$.

These four-cycles span $Z(B-e_*)$. For any nonzero $\lambda$ in this
space, a vertex incident with exactly one edge in its support would
violate the vertex-sum equation. Thus every vertex incident with a
support edge has degree at least two there, and the finite support
subgraph contains a cycle. Subtract a multiple of that
cycle's vector to eliminate one support edge, without adding edges to
the support; finitely many repetitions show that cycle vectors span
the space. Now consider a cycle $C$ of length at least six.
Choose its largest left vertex $j_*$ other
than $m$. Every right vertex $k$ on $C$ has a cycle-neighbor
$j_a\ne m$, so $k\le j_a+m\le j_*+m$. Thus $j_*$ is adjacent in
$B-e_*$ to every right vertex of $C$; this edge cannot be $e_*$, since
$j_*\ne m$. There are at least three right vertices but only two
cycle-neighbors of $j_*$, so $C$ has a chord, an edge joining two
nonconsecutive vertices of $C$. It divides $C$ into two shorter cycles.
Choose the signs of their alternating cycle vectors so their chord
entries cancel; their sum is the cycle vector of $C$. Repeating this
split expresses that vector as a sum of four-cycle vectors. Also,
$B-e_*$ remains connected when $m\ge3$: $m$ meets
every right vertex except $m+1$, the latter meets every left vertex
except $m$, and $m,m+1$ are joined by the path
$m,(m+2),(m-1),(m+1)$. For $G=B-e_*$, the equation
$D_G^{\mathsf T}c=0$ makes the vertex labels $c$ equal across every
edge. Connectedness makes $c$ constant, so $\operatorname{rank}D_G=2m-1$
and

\begin{equation}
\dim Z(B-e_*)=\bigl(|E(B)|-1\bigr)-2m+1
=\frac{m(m-3)}2.
\end{equation}

Equations (5.4)--(5.6) show that $\dim(U\cap W)=m(m-3)/2$.
Substitute this, (5.3), and
$\operatorname{rank}M=\binom{2m+1}{2}-(2m+1)$ from Lemma 4.2 into
(5.2). The result is
$\operatorname{rank}M'=2m^2-3m-1$, which is (5.1).

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The odd lower bound follows after Lemma 4.2.
For even $n=N\ge6$, the rank transfer of Lemma 4.1 applies to the
surviving zero rays, so Proposition 5.1 and Lemma 2.1 give a matrix
in the exposed face $F(S')\subseteq\operatorname{CP}_N$ of cp-rank
$N(N-3)/2-1$. The upper bound is
\cite[Corollary~5.2]{ref2}. Subtracting the two expressions in (1.2)
gives $2n-4+\varepsilon_n$.
