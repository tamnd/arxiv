<!-- sqp-1: body_md -->
Given an infinite-dimensional Banach space $X$, does there exist a closed infinite-dimensional subspace $Y$ of $X$ such that $X/Y$ is an infinite-dimensional separable Banach space?
<!-- end sqp-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{t1}
The following statements are equivalent for an infinite-dimensional Banach space \( X \):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \( X \) contains a dense non-barreled subspace.
\item \( X \) admits a strictly increasing sequence of closed subspaces whose union is dense in \( X \).
\item \( X^* \) admits a strictly decreasing sequence of weak$^*$ ($\mathrm{w}^*$ for short)-closed subspaces whose intersection consists only of the zero element.
\item \( X \) has a separable quotient.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{t2.2}
Let \( X \) be an infinite-dimensional Banach space. The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \( X \) has a separable quotient.
\item \( X^* \) has a strongly normal sequence.
\item \( X^* \) has a basic sequence in the $\mathrm{w}^*$ topology.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{p2.4}
A Banach space \( X \) has a quotient isomorphic to \( \ell_1 \) if and only if \( X^* \) contains a normal sequence \( (y_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}} \) such that \( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |y_n(x)| < \infty \) for all \( x \in X \).
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{t2.9}
A Banach space has a separable quotient if and only if it has a proper and dense operator range.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
A semigroup of linear operators on $X$ is a family $(T(t))_{t\geq 0} \subset \mathcal{L}(X)$, denoted simply by $T$ when there is no risk of confusion, that satisfies:
\begin{itemize}
\item $T(0) = I$, where $I$ is the identity operator on $X$;
\item $T(t + s) = T(t)T(s)$, for all $t, s \geq 0$. 
\end{itemize}
If, furthermore, we have
\begin{itemize}
\item $\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 0^+} \|T(t) - I\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)} = 0$, we say that the semigroup is \textit{uniformly continuous};
\item $\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 0^+} \|T(t)x - x\|_X = 0$, for each $x \in X$, we say that the semigroup is \textit{strongly continuous}, or a $C_0$-\textit{semigroup}. If $T \subset \mathcal{L}(X)$ is a $C_0$-semigroup, its infinitesimal generator is the operator $A : D(A) \subset X \to X$ defined by
\[
Ax = \lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{T(t)x - x}{t},
\]
where
\[
D(A) = \left\{ x \in X : \lim_{t \to 0^+} \frac{T(t)x - x}{t} \text{ exists} \right\}.
\]
\end{itemize}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
Let $X$ be an infinite-dimensional Banach space. Then there exists a semigroup of continuous operators on $X$ that is not uniformly continuous.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
From the Josefson-Nissenzweig Theorem \cite{josefson1975,nissenzweig1975} and by \cite[Lemma 3.1.19.]{gonzalez2010tauberian}, we obtain a generalized biorthogonal system $(X_n,P_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ with
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty}\|P_n(x)\|=0
\]
for all $x\in X$. Now, take $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in \bigcap_{p>0}\ell_{p}$ a sequence of positive terms ($a_n=c^n$ for $0<c<1$ is an example). Let us consider $\alpha_n:[0,\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}$ given by $\alpha_n(t)=a_n^t$. Note that for each $t>0$ and $x\in X$, we have
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|\alpha_n(t)P_n(x)\|\leq \sup_{n\in \mathbb{N}}\|P_n(x)\| \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_n|^t<\infty.
\]
Since $X$ is Banach, the family of operators $(T(t))_{t>0}\subset\mathcal{L}(X)$ given by
\[
T(t)(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_n(t)P_n(x)
\]
is well defined. If $t,s>0$, then for $x\in X$ we have
\begin{align*}
T(t)T(s)(x)&=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_i(t)P_i\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_j(s)P_j(x)\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_n(t)\alpha_n(s)P_n(x)\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_n(t+s)P_n(x)=T(t+s)(x).
\end{align*}
Therefore, defining $T(0)=I$, we have that the family $(T(t))_{t\geq 0}$ is a semigroup of continuous operators on $X$. Now observe that for each $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and each $x\in X$, we have
\[
\lim_{t \to 0^+}\frac{T(t)\left(P_n(x)\right)-P_n(x)}{t}=\lim_{t \to 0^+}\frac{\alpha_n(t)-1}{t}P_n(x)=\ln(a_n)P_n(x).
\]

If the semigroup $(T(t))_{t\geq 0}$ were uniformly continuous, we would have $A\left(P_n(x)\right))=\ln(a_n)P_n(x)$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and for all $x\in X$, whence it would follow $|\ln(a_n)|\leq \|A\|$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$. But since $\lim_{n \to \infty}a_n=0$, it follows that $\|A\|=\infty$, which is a contradiction.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
We say that a Banach space $X$ has the Lotz property if every $C_0$-semigroup on $X$ is uniformly continuous
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{main}
Every infinite-dimensional Banach space that does not possess the Lotz property admits a separable quotient.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let \( X \) be a Banach space that does not possess the Lotz property, and let \( T \subset \mathcal{L}(X) \) be a \( C_0 \)-semigroup that is not uniformly continuous. Denote by \( A \) the infinitesimal generator of \( T \).

It is well known that \( A \) is a densely defined linear operator; that is, \( D(A) \) is dense in \( X \). Moreover, since \( A \) is closed, \( D(A) \), endowed with the graph norm
\[
\|x\|_{G(A)} := \|x\| + \|Ax\|,
\]
is itself a Banach space. Since \(\|x\|_{G(A)} \ge \|x\|\) for all \(x \in D(A)\), the inclusion map
\[
i : (D(A), \|\cdot\|_{G(A)}) \longrightarrow (X, \|\cdot\|)
\]
is continuous. Therefore, \( D(A) \) can be regarded as the image of a continuous linear operator between Banach spaces, and thus as an operator range.
	
Finally, recall that a \( C_0 \)-semigroup is uniformly continuous if and only if its infinitesimal generator is bounded. Since \( T \) is not uniformly continuous, its generator \( A \) must be unbounded, implying that \( D(A) \) is a proper subspace of \( X \). Indeed, assume (by  contradiction) that \( D(A) = X \). In that case, the inclusion map \( i \) would be a continuous and surjective linear operator between Banach spaces. By the \textit{Closed Graph Theorem}, its inverse would also be continuous, meaning that the graph norm \(\|\cdot\|_{G(A)}\) would be equivalent to the original norm on \(X\). Consequently, \( A \) would be bounded, contradicting the assumption that \(T\) is not uniformly continuous. Thus, \( D(A) \) is indeed a proper subspace of \( X \).

Therefore, \( D(A) \subset X \) is a proper and dense operator range. From Theorem \ref{t2.9}, we conclude that \( X \) admits a separable quotient, which completes the proof.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The converse of the previous theorem does not hold. In fact, the result of Lotz \cite{lotz} provides the necessary counterexample since it can be shown that any Banach space that is either a Grothendieck space or has the Dunford--Pettis property admits a separable quotient. In fact, if the space is Grothendieck and $B_{X^*}$ is not sequentially compact in the $\mathrm{w}^*$ topology, we can  conclude that the space has a separable quotient by \cite{CKLS}. If $B_{X^*}$ is sequentially compact in the $\mathrm{w}^*$ topology, we have that $B_{X^*}$ is a reflexive space by the Erberlein--Smulian Theorem (see \cite{abk}), and then we guarantee the  separable quotient, for instance, by \cite{pelczynski1964}. Now, if the space has the Dunford--Pettis property, it is possible to prove that $X$ or $X^*$ contains a copy of $\ell_1$. Indeed, otherwise, by the Rosental's $\ell_1$-Theorem (see \cite{abk}), we would be able to construct sequences $(x_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ and $(x_n^*)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $X$ and $X^*$ respectively, with $x_n \overset{\mathrm{w}}{\to} 0 $, $x_n^* \overset{\mathrm{w}}{\to} 0 $ and $x_n^*(x_n) =1$, for all $n\in \mathbb{N}$.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
Every Banach lattice space with a quasi-interior point has a separable quotient.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{p3.7}
Let $X$ and $Y$ Banach spaces and $(T_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ a sequence in $\mathcal{L}(X,Y)$. If for each $x\in X$ the sequence $(T_n(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ converges weakly in $Y$, then the limit operator
\[
T(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}T_n(x),
\]
is continuous.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{onemain}
Let $X$ be an infinite-dimensional Banach space. If $X$ has quotient isomorphic to $\ell_1$, then $X$ fails to have the Lotz property; that is, there exists a semigroup \( T \) of continuous linear operators which is strongly continuous but not uniformly continuous.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By Proposition \ref{p2.4}, there exists $(\varphi_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset S(X^*)$ such that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(x)|<\infty$ for all $x\in X$. By \cite[Lemma 3.1.19.]{gonzalez2010tauberian}, taking a subsequence of $(\varphi_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ if necessary, we can assume that there exists a bounded sequence $(x_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ such that $\varphi_n(x_m)=\delta_{nm}$. Consider now the sequence $(P_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $\mathcal{L}(X)$ given by $P_n(x)=\varphi_n(x)x_n$. We obviously have $P_n P_m=\delta_{nm} P_{\min\{n,m\}}$ and
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|P_n(x)\|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(x)|\|x_n\|\leq \left(\sup_{n\in \mathbb{N}}\|x_n\|\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(x)|\right)<\infty
\]
for all $x\in X$. Let $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ a sequence of positive numbers such that $\lim_{n \to \infty}a_n=0$. Thus we have $\lim_{n \to \infty}|\ln (a_n)|=\infty$ and $\lim_{n \to \infty}\alpha_n(t)=-\alpha(t)$ for all $t>0$, where $\alpha_n$ is of the form given in \eqref{alphan} and $\alpha(t)=a^t$ for some $a>0$. Furthermore, there exists $K>0$ such that $|\alpha_n(t)|\leq K$ for all $t\in [0,1]$ and all $n\in\mathbb{N}$. Since for each $x\in X$ and $t>0$ the series
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_i(t)P_i(x)
\]
is absolutely convergent, it follows that \eqref{eq1} is well defined. Now, given $\epsilon>0$ and $x\in X$, take $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that
\[
\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\|P_n(x)\|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2K}.
\]
Now take $\delta>0$ such that
\[
|\alpha(t)-1|\leq \frac{\epsilon}{2N(\|x\|+1)}
\]
and
\[
|\alpha_n(t)|\leq \frac{\epsilon}{2N(\|P_n(x)\|+1)}
\]
for all $n< N$ and $t<\delta$. Therefore, for $t<\delta$ we have
\begin{align*}
\|T_t(x)-x\|&=\left\|(\alpha(t)-1)x+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_n(t)P_n(x)\right\|\leq |\alpha(t)-1|\cdot \|x\|+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\alpha_n(t)|\cdot\|P_n(x)\|\\
&\leq|\alpha(t)-1|\cdot \|x\|+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}|\alpha_n(t)|\cdot\|P_n(x)\|+\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}K\cdot\|P_n(x)\|\\
&\leq \frac{\epsilon\cdot\|x\|}{2N(\|x\|+1)}+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{\epsilon\cdot\|P_n(x)\|}{2N(\|P_n(x)\|+1)}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\leq N\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{2N}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon,
\end{align*}
that is, $\displaystyle \lim_{t \to 0^+}T_t(x)=x$ for each $x\in X$. We then have that $(T_t)_{t\geq 0}$ given by \eqref{eq1} is a $C_0$-semigroup. However, the fact that $\lim_{n \to \infty}|\ln(a_n)|=\infty$ guarantees us that $(T_t)_{t\geq 0}$ is not uniformly continuous, due to the inequality seen in \eqref{eq4}.

It is known that a semigroup $(T_t)_{t\geq0}$ is strongly continuous if and only if it is weakly continuous (see \cite{pazy1983}), that is,
\[
\lim_{t \to 0^+}\varphi(T_t(x)-x)=0
\]
for all $(x,\varphi)\in X\times X^*$.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{t3.10}
Let $X$ be a weakly sequentially complete Banach space. If there exists a sequence of projections $(P_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{L}(X)\setminus\{0\}$ such that $P_n P_m=\delta_{nm}P_{\min\{n,m\}}$ and
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi(P_n(x))|<\infty
\]
for all $(x,\varphi)\in X\times X^*$, then $X$ fails to have the Lotz property.
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Consider $\alpha(t)=a^{t}$ for any $a\in (0,\infty)$ and for each $n\in\mathbb{N}$ take $\alpha_n(t)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{t}-\alpha(t)$. For each $n\in\mathbb{N}$ consider $T_t^{(n)}\in \mathcal{L}(X)$ given by $T_t^{(n)}(x)=\alpha(t)x+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i(t)P_i(x)$. Let us see that the sequence $(T_t^{(n)}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ converges weakly for all $x\in X$ and all $t\geq 0$. Since the sequence $(\alpha_n(t))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is bounded, we have for $\varphi\in X^*$ that
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\alpha_i(t)\varphi\left(P_i(x)\right)|<\infty.
\]
Therefore $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_i(t)\varphi\left(P_i(x)\right)$ converges in $\mathbb{K}$. Thus
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty}\varphi\left(T_t^{(n)}(x)\right)=\lim_{n \to \infty}\left(\alpha(t)\varphi(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i(t)\varphi(P_i(x))\right)
\]
exists for any $\varphi\in X^*$. Since $X$ is weakly sequentially complete, we can conclude that there exists $T_t(x)\in X$ such that the sequence $(T_t^{(n)}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ converges weakly to $T_t(x)$. Proposition \ref{p3.7} assures us that $T_t:X\longrightarrow X$ is continuous.

Let us now see that
\[
T_t^{(n)} T_s^{(m)}=T_{t+s}^{(\min\{n,m\})}+\alpha(t)\left(T_{s}^{(m)}-T_{s}^{(\min\{n,m\})}\right)+\alpha(s)\left(T_{t}^{(n)}-T_{t}^{(\min\{n,m\})}\right)
\]
for any $t,s\geq 0$ and $n,m\in\mathbb{N}$. In fact, given $x\in X$, it follows that
\begin{align*}
&T_t^{(n)}\left(T_s^{(m)}(x)\right)=\alpha(t)T_s^{(m)}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i(t)P_i\left(T_s^{(m)}(x)\right)\\
&=\alpha(t)\left(\alpha(s)x+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_j(s)P_j(x)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i(t)P_i\left(\alpha(s)x+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_j(s)P_j(x)\right)\\
&=\alpha(t)\left(\alpha(s)x+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_j(s)P_j(x)\right)+\alpha(s)\left(\sum_ {i=1}^{n}\alpha_i(t)P_i(x)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{n,m\}}\alpha_i(t)\alpha_i(s)P_i(x)\\
&=\alpha(t+s)x+\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{n,m\}}\left(\alpha(t)\alpha_i(s)+\alpha(s)\alpha_i(t)+\alpha_i(t)\alpha_i(s)\right)P_i(x)\\
&\qquad+\alpha(t)\left(\sum_{i=\min\{n,m\}+1}^{m}\alpha_i(s)P_i(x)\right)+\alpha(s)\left(\sum_{i=\min\{n,m\}+1}^{n}\alpha_i(t)P_i(x)\right)\\
&=T_{t+s}^{(\min\{n,m\})}(x)+\alpha(t)\left(T_{s}^{(m)}(x)-T_{s}^{(\min\{n,m \})}(x)\right)+\alpha(s)\left(T_{t}^{(n)}(x)-T_{t}^{(\min\{n,m\})}(x)\right).
\end{align*}
Thus, for each $x\in X$ and $m\in\mathbb{N}$ we have
\begin{align*}
T_t\left(T_s^{(m)}(x)\right) &=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}T_t^{(n)}\left(T_s^{(m)}(x)\right)\\
&=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left[T_{t+s}^{(\min\{n,m\})}(x)+\alpha(t)\left(T_{s}^{(m)}(x)-T_ {s}^{(\min\{n,m\})}(x)\right)\right. \\
& \qquad\qquad \left. +\alpha(s)\left(T_{t}^{(n)}(x)-T_{t}^{(\min\{n,m\})}(x)\right)\right]\\
&=T_{t+s}^{(m)}(x)+\alpha(s)\left(T_t(x)-T_t^{(m)}(x)\right).
\end{align*}
If we make $m\rightarrow\infty$ in the last equality, we obtain $T_t\left(T_s(x)\right)=T_{t+s}(x)$. We then conclude that $(T_t)_{t\geq0}$ is a semigroup of continuous operators of $X$.

Now we will show that $(T_t)_{t\geq0}$ is strongly continuous using Remark \ref{ob3.9}. In fact, for $\varphi\in X^*$ and $x\in X$, given $\epsilon>0$ take $N\in \mathbb{N}$ such that
\[
\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}|\varphi(P_i(x))|<\frac{\epsilon}{2K} \ \text{ with } \ K=\sup\limits_{t\in[0,1]}|\alpha(t)|+1.
\]
Now take $0<\delta\leq 1$ such that for $0\leq t<\delta$ we have
\[
|\alpha(t)-1||\varphi(x)|+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\alpha_i(t)||\varphi(P_i(x))|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.
\]
Thus, for $0\leq t<\delta$ we have
\begin{align*}
|\varphi\left(T_t(x)-x\right)|&=\left|(\alpha(t)-1)\varphi(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_i(t)\varphi(P_i(x))\right|\\
&\leq|\alpha(t)-1||\varphi(x)|+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\alpha_i(t)||\varphi(P_i(x))|+\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}|\alpha_i(t)||\varphi(P_i(x))|\\
&\leq|\alpha(t)-1||\varphi(x)|+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\alpha_i(t)||\varphi(P_i(x))|+\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}K|\varphi(P_i(x))|\\
&<\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon,
\end{align*}
that is, we verify that $\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0}\varphi(T_t(x)-x)=0$ and hence $(T_t)_{t\geq0}$ is indeed a strongly continuous semigroup. It is easy to check that $\mathrm{Im}(P_n)\subset {D}\left(A\right)$ (where $A:{D}(A)\longrightarrow X$ is the infinitesimal generator of the semigroup $(T_t)_{t\geq0}$) for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and that $A(P_n(x))=\ln\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)P_n(x)$ for all $x\in X$. This shows us that $(T_t)_{t\geq0}$ is not uniformly continuous.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{t2.8}
Let $X$ be a Banach space. If $(p_\lambda)_{\lambda\in \Lambda}$ is a family of continuous seminorms, then the subspace $Y=\{x\in X: \sup_{\lambda\in \Lambda}p_\lambda(x)<\infty\}$ is an operator range.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Evidently $Y$ is a subspace of $X$. Define in $Y$ the following norm
\[
\|x\|_Y=\|x\|+\sup_{\lambda\in \Lambda}p_\lambda(x).
\]
Let us see that $(Y,\|\cdot\|_Y)$ is complete. Let $(y_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ be a Cauchy sequence in $(Y,\|\cdot\|_Y)$. We then have that $(y_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $X$, since $\|x\|_y\geq \|x\|$ is valid for all $x\in Y$. Let $y\in X$ be the limit of $(y_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $X$. As $(\|y_n\|_Y)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is bounded (since it is convergent), there is $K>0$ such that
\[
\|y_n\|+\sup_{\lambda\in \Lambda}p_\lambda(y_n)\leq K
\]
for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$. In particular, we have $p_\lambda(y_n)\leq K$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$, $\lambda\in \Lambda$. As each $p_\lambda$ is continuous and $y_n\longrightarrow y$ in $X$, we have that $p_\lambda(y)\leq K$ for all $\lambda\in \Lambda$, that is, $y\in Y$. Now, given $\epsilon>0$, there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that
\[
\|y_n-y_m\|_Y<\epsilon,\ \forall n,m\geq N.
\]
In particular, for each $\lambda\in \Lambda$ we have
\[
\|y_n-y_m\|+p_\lambda(y_n-y_m)<\epsilon,\ \forall n,m\geq N.
\]
Therefore, if $m\longrightarrow\infty$ in the last inequality, we have
\[
\|y_n-y\|+p_\lambda(y_n-y)\leq\epsilon
\]
for all $\lambda\in \Lambda$ and $n\geq N$, that is, $\|y_n-y\|_Y\leq \epsilon$ for $n\geq N$. Therefore $Y$ is complete with the norm $\|\cdot\|_Y$ and is the image of the inclusion $I:(Y,\|\cdot\|_Y)\longrightarrow (X,\|\cdot\|)$ which is continuous.

<!-- theorem-9: body_md -->
A Banach space $X$ has a separable quotient if and only if there exists a family of continuous seminorms $(p_\lambda)_{\lambda\in \Lambda}$ such that $Y=\{x\in X: \sup_{\lambda\in \Lambda}p_\lambda(x)<\infty\}$ is proper and dense.
<!-- end theorem-9 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The converse follows immediately from Theorem \ref{t2.9} and Lemma \ref{t2.8}. Now, assuming that $X$ has a separable quotient, we obtain by Theorem \ref{t1} a strictly increasing sequence $Y_1\subset Y_2\subset\cdots \subset Y_n\subset\cdots$ of subspaces of $X$ whose union is dense in $X$. Consider now the sequence of seminorms $(\hat{p}_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ given by $\hat{p}_n(x)=\|\pi_{Y_n}(x)\|$. Since $\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Y_n$ is a proper subspace of $X$ (thanks to Baire's Category Theorem), there exists $y\in X\setminus \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Y_n$, whence we have $\hat{p}_n(y)>0$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$. Consider now the new sequence of continuous seminorms $(p_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ given by
\[
p_n(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\hat{p}_i(x)}{\hat{p}_i(y)}.
\]
Let us now see that $\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Y_n\subset Y=\{x\in X: \sup_{n\in \mathbb{N}}p_n(x)<\infty\}$ and $y\notin Y$ to conclude the result. In fact, given $x\in \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Y_n$, there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $x\in Y_N$. Thus $\hat{p}_m(x)= 0$ for all $m\geq N$, where we obtain $p_N(x)=p_n(x)$ for all $n\geq N$. Therefore $\sup_{n\in \mathbb{N}}p_n(x)<\infty$, and then $x\in Y$. Finally, since $p_n(y)=n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$, we have that $y\notin Y$.

<!-- proposition-7: body_md -->
\label{p2.11}
Let $X$ be a Banach space, $p\in (1,\infty]$ and $(\varphi_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X^*$ a sequence of non-zero functionals. If
\[
Y=\bigcup\limits_{q\in [1,p)}\left\{x\in X: \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(x)|^q<\infty\right\}
\]
is a proper subspace of $X$, then for each $z\in X\setminus Y$ there exists an operator range $R_z$ such that $z\notin R_z$ and $Y\subset R_z$.
<!-- end proposition-7 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $(q_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ be a strictly increasing sequence in $[1,p)$ such that $q_n\rightarrow p$. Since
\[
\left\{x\in X: \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(x)|^r<\infty\right\}\subset\left\{x\in X: \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(x)|^q<\infty\right\}
\]
for $r<q$, we have $Y=\bigcup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{x\in X: \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_i(x)|^{q_n}<\infty\right\}$. Since $z\notin Y$, it follows that $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_i(x)|^{q_n}=\infty$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$, and so for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$ we can take $n_k\in \mathbb{N}$ satisfying
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}|\varphi_i(z)|^{q_k}\geq (\sqrt{k}-\|z\|)^{q_k}.
\]
Define in $X$ the norm
\[
\|x\|_k=\frac{1}{k}\left(\|x\|+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}|\varphi_i(x)|^{q_k}\right)^\frac{1}{q_k}\right),
\]
which is clearly equivalent to the original norm of $X$, since
\[
\frac{1}{k}\|x\|\leq \|x\|_k\leq \frac{1+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}\|\varphi_i\|^{q_k}\right)^\frac{1}{q_k}}{k}\|x\|.
\]

If we consider $R_z=\{x\in X: \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|x\|_k^2<\infty\}$, we have that $R_z$ is a Banach space if equipped with the norm
\[
|||x|||=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|x\|_k^2}
\]
and the inclusion of $R_z$ in $X$ is continuous. Given $y\in Y$, we have that $y\in\{x\in X: \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(x)|^{q_r}<\infty\}$ for some $r\in \mathbb{N}$ and $$\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(y)|^{q_s}\right)^\frac{1}{q_s}\leq\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_n(y)|^{q_r}\right)^\frac{1}{q_r}$$ for all $s\geq r$. So
\begin{align*}
&\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|y\|_k^2\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\frac{1}{k^2}\left(\|y\|+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}|\varphi_i(y)|^{q_k}\right)^\frac{1}{q _k}\right)^2+\sum_{k=r}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^2}\left(\|y\|+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}|\varphi_i(y)|^{q_k}\right)^\frac{1}{q_k}\right)^2\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\frac{1}{k^2}\left(\|y\|+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}|\varphi_i(y)|^{q_k}\right)^\frac{1}{q_k}\right)^2 +\sum_{k=r}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^2}\left(\|y\|+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}|\varphi_i(y)|^{q_r}\right)^\frac{1}{q_r}\right)^2\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}\frac{1}{k^2}\left(\|y\|+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}|\varphi_i(y)|^{q_k}\right)^\frac{1}{q_k}\right)^2+ \left(\|y\|+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_i(y)|^{q_r}\right)^\frac{1}{q_r}\right)^2\cdot\left(\sum_{k=r}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^2}\right)\\
&<\infty,
\end{align*}
that is, $y\in R_z$. On the other hand,
\begin{align*}
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|z\|_k^2 & =\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^2}\left(\|z\|+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_k}|\varphi_i(z)|^{q_k}\right)^\frac{1}{q_k}\right)^2 \\
& \geq \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^2}\left(\|z\|+\sqrt{k}-\|z\|\right)^2=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}=\infty.
\end{align*}

<!-- theorem-10: body_md -->
Let $(X,\|\cdot\|)$ and $(Y,\|\cdot\|_Y)$ be Banach spaces with $Y\subset\mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$, and $Y$ having the canonical sequence $(e_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ as Schauder basis. Let $(\varphi_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ be a linearly independent sequence in $X^*$. If $(\varphi_n(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in Y$ for all $x\in X$ and $$\sup_{(\varphi_n(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in S_Y}\left[\inf_{y\in \bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{ker}(\varphi_n)}\|x-y\|\right]<\infty,$$ then $X$ has quotient isomorphic to a subspace of $Y$.
<!-- end theorem-10 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Since the map $T:X\longrightarrow Y$ given by $T(x)=(\varphi_n(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is well defined and has a closed graph, it follows that there exists $C>0$ such that
\[
\|(\varphi_n(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_Y\leq C\|x\|,
\]
for all $x\in X$. Consider now the quotient space $\displaystyle W:=\frac{X}{\bigcap\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{ker}(\varphi_n)}$. For $\overline{x}\in W$ we define $\overline{\varphi}_n(\overline{x}):=\varphi_n(x)$ and this expression does not depend on the representative of the \textcolor{black}{equivalence} class $\overline{x}$. As for $y\in \bigcap\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{ker}(\varphi_n)$ we have
\[
|\overline{\varphi}_n(\overline{x})|=|\varphi_n(x)|=|\varphi_n(x-y)|\leq\|\varphi_n\|\cdot\|x-y\|,
\]
it follows that $\overline{\varphi}_n\in W^*$ with $\|\overline{\varphi}_n\|\leq\|\varphi_n\|$. It can be easily verified that the sequence $(\overline{\varphi}_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $W^*$ is linearly independent with $\bigcap\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{ker}(\overline{\varphi}_n)=\{0\}$, that is, $W^*=\overline{\mathrm{span}\{\overline{\varphi}_n:\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathrm{w}^*}$. Clearly we also have
\[
\|(\overline{\varphi}_n(\overline{x}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_Y\leq C\|\overline{x}\|,
\]
for all $\overline{x}\in W$. Since the sequence $(\overline{\varphi}_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ separates the points of $W$, it follows that
\[
\|(\overline{\varphi}_n(\overline{x}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_Y =0\Leftrightarrow \overline{x}=0.
\]
Hence, the expression $\|\overline{x}\|_W:=\|(\overline{\varphi}_n(\overline{x}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_Y$ defines a norm on $W$. Evidently the set $\{\overline{x}\in W:\ \|\overline{x}\|_W= 1\}$ is bounded in $W$ with the quotient norm, that is, there exists $K>0$ such that, if $\|\overline{x}\|_W= 1$, then $\|\overline{x}\|\leq K$. Thus, for $\overline{x}\neq 0$ we have
\[
\left\|\frac{\overline{x}}{\|\overline{x}\|_W}\right\|\leq K,
\]
whence we infer that $\|\overline{x}\|\leq K \|\overline{x}\|_W$. Since the norms $\|\cdot\|$ and $\|\cdot\|_W$ are equivalent in $W$, it follows that $(W,\|\cdot\|_W)$ is complete. Now, since the map $\overline{T}:(W,\|\cdot\|_W)\longrightarrow Y$, $\overline{T}(\overline{x}):=(\overline{\varphi}_n(\overline{x}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$, is an isometry, we conclude that $(W,\|\cdot\|)$ is isomorphic to a subspace of $Y$.
