		Let                                              
		\(n\geq 2\), \(0<\lambda<n\) and suppose that \(0<a\), \(q\in[1,\frac{n}{n-\lambda})\) and \(b\) are the exponents satisfying \(\frac{n+b}{q}=n-\lambda+a\). Further suppose \(a\notin {\mathbb N}\), then if \(\lambda\) is not an even integer or \(\floor{a}<\lambda\) the inequality

		the vanishing moment assumption: For all multi-indices \(|\gamma|\leq \floor{a}\), \mbox{\(\int_{{\mathbb R}^n} x^{\gamma}f(x) \ dx=0\)}.

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{necessity_mean_0}
	                                               
	Let \(n\geq 2\), \(0<\lambda<n\). Suppose that \(a>0\), \(q\in[1,\frac{n}{n-\lambda}]\) and \(b\) are parameters satisfying the scaling condition \(\frac{n+b}{q}=n-\lambda+a\). If \(f\in C^{\infty}_c({\mathbb R}^n)\) satisfies inequality \eqref{SW}. Then, unless \(\lambda\) is an even integer and \(\ceil{a}-1\geq\lambda\), it must be the case that for all multi-indices \(|\gamma|\leq \ceil{a}-1\) one has
	\begin{align*}
		\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}x^{\gamma}f(x)dx
		&=0.
	\end{align*}
	In the exceptional case where \(\lambda\) is an even integer and \(\ceil{a}-1\geq \lambda\) one instead has that for every \(\ell\leq \ceil{a}-1\), there exists a homogeneous polynomial \(W\) of degree \(\ell-\lambda\) such that
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{|\gamma|=\ell}                
		\frac{M_{\gamma}}{\gamma!}\xi^{\gamma}&=W(\xi)|\xi|^{\lambda}. \label{factored}
	\end{align}
	Here \(M_{\gamma}=\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}(-x)^{\gamma}f(x)dx\).

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{equiv}
	Suppose that \(\lambda\) is an even integer and \(\ell\geq \lambda\). Then the following are equivalent:
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
		\item\label{one} The order \(\ell\) moments of \(f\) satisfy the equation
		\begin{align*}
			\sum_{|\gamma|=\ell}\frac{M_{\gamma}}{\gamma!}\xi^{\gamma}&=W(\xi)|\xi|^{\lambda}
		\end{align*}
		for some homogeneous degree \(\ell-\lambda\) polynomial \(W\). Here \(M_{\gamma}=\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}(-x)^{\gamma}f(x)dx\).\\
		\item\label{two} \(\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}h(x)f(x)dx=0\) for all polyharmonic order \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\), homogeneous degree \(\ell\) \mbox{polynomials \(h\)}.\\                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   
		\item\label{three} When viewed as linear operators on homogeneous degree \(\ell\) polynomials \begin{align*}
		\ker(\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}})&\subseteq\ker\left(\sum_{|\gamma|=\ell}\frac{M_{\gamma}}{\gamma!}\partial^{\gamma}\right).
		\end{align*}
	\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
To simplify notation, we let \(P(\xi)=\sum_{|\gamma|=\ell}\frac{M_{\gamma}}{\gamma!}\xi^{\gamma}\), and for a homogeneous degree \(\ell\) polynomial \(h\) we write \(h(x)=\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}h_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\) where \(\{h_{\alpha}\}_{|\alpha|=\ell}\) are constants. The following calculation verifies that \(P(D)h=(-1)^{\ell}\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}h(x)f(x)dx\) for all homogeneous degree \(\ell\) polynomials, and is useful throughout:
	\begin{align*}
		                                                                                                             
		P(D)h&=\sum_{|\gamma|=\ell}\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{M_{\gamma}}{\gamma!}h_{\alpha}\partial^{\gamma}x^{\alpha}\\
		&=\sum_{|\gamma|=\ell}M_{\gamma}h_{\gamma}.
	\end{align*}
	The last line follows by computing \(\partial^{\gamma}x^{\alpha}\). Exchanging the integral in \(M_{\gamma}\) and the sum provides
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{|\gamma|=\ell}M_{\gamma}h_{\gamma}&=\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}\sum_{|\gamma|=\ell}h_{\gamma}(-x)^{\gamma}f(x)dx\\
		&=(-1)^{\ell}\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}h(x)f(x)dx.
	\end{align*}
	                                                                                                    
	We start with \ref{one} implies \ref{two}, let \(h\) be polyharmonic of order \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\), we have from \ref{one} that 
	\begin{align*}
		P(D)h&=W(D)\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}h\\
		&=0.
	\end{align*}
	Combining this with our earlier identity \mbox{\(P(D)h=(-1)^{\ell}\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}h(x)f(x)dx\)} gives the desired conclusion.\\
	\\
	Next, \ref{two} implies \ref{three} also follows from the identity \mbox{\(P(D)h=(-1)^{\ell}\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}h(x)f(x)dx\)}. Indeed, if \(h\) is polyharmonic order \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\), homogeneous degree \(\ell\) then \ref{two} states that \(\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}h(x)f(x)dx=0\), and so our identity gives \(P(D)h=0\).\\
	\\
	Finally, in order to demonstrate \ref{three} implies \ref{one}, we use the Fischer inner product which is defined as                                                                                                                                                                                                               
                                                                                                                                                        
                                                                                                                                                                      
                 
                                        
              
                                                                                                   
	\begin{align*}
		\langle p,q\rangle_F&:=p(D)q(0)
	\end{align*}
	where \(p,q\) are homogeneous degree \(\ell\) polynomials. A calculation shows
	\begin{align*}
		\langle |x|^{\lambda}w(x), q\rangle_F&=\langle w(x), \Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}q\rangle_F
	\end{align*}
	and so, viewing \(\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}\) as a linear operator on homogeneous degree \(\ell\) polynomials, we have that the adjoint is multiplication by \(|x|^{\lambda}\). Since \ref{three} was \(\ker(\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}})\subseteq \ker(P(D))\), one has for all \(h\in\ker(\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}})\), \(P(D)h=0\). This implies
	\begin{align*}
		\langle P, h \rangle_F&=0
	\end{align*} 
	and hence \(P\) is orthogonal to \(\ker(\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}})\). Noting that the orthogonal complement of \(\ker(\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}})\) is the image of multiplication by \(|x|^{\lambda}\) we conclude \(P(x)=|x|^{\lambda}W(x)\) for some homogeneous degree \(\ell-\lambda\) polynomial \(W\), as desired.\\

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{Error_term_lemma}
	Suppose that \(\lambda<n\), \(a>0\), \(q\in[1,\frac{n}{n-\lambda}]\) and \(b\) are parameters satisfying the scaling condition \(\frac{n+b}{q}=n-\lambda+a\). Let \(R>0\), and suppose that \(y\in B(0,\frac{R}{2})\) and \(x\in B(0,2R)\setminus B(0,R)\). Then for all \(a>0\), set \(m:=\ceil{a}-1\) one has the estimate
	\begin{align}
		\left||x-y|^{\lambda-n}-\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}|x|^{\lambda-n}}{\alpha!}\right|&\lesssim |y|^aR^{\lambda-n-a}                                         
		\label{error_estimate}
	\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
\(\)\\
	                                                                                                                                           
	               
	                                                                                                  
	             
	                            
	This follows by Taylor expanding \(|x-y|^{\lambda-n}\), which immediately gives the naive estimate
	\begin{align*}
		\left||x-y|^{\lambda-n}-\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}|x|^{\lambda-n}}{\alpha!}\right|&\lesssim |y|^{m+1}|z|^{\lambda-n-m-1}                              
	\end{align*}
	for some \(z\) on the line segment between \(x\) and \(x-y\). We estimate the \(|z|\) term (which has a negative exponent) first: By using the fact that \(|x|>2|y|\) observe that any point on the line segment between \(x\) and \(x-y\) must satisfy both \(|y|<|z|\) and \(\frac{|x|}{2}\leq |z|\). Furthermore, since \(|x|\geq R\) it follows that \(|z|\gtrsim R\) and so                                                              
	               
	                            
	             
	                                                 
	\eqref{error_estimate} would be implied by
	\begin{align*}
		|y|^{m+1}R^{\lambda-n-m-1}\lesssim |y|^aR^{\lambda-n-a}.
	\end{align*}
	                                  
	               
	                                                                  
	             
	This follows by using that \(|y|\lesssim R\), and \(a\leq m+1\) to deduce \(|y|^{m+1-a}\leq R^{m+1-a}\). Hence
	               
	                                                          
	             
	               
	the desired estimate follows.\\

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{asymptotic_L^q}
	                                               
	Let \(n\geq 2\), \(0<\lambda<n\). Suppose that \(a>0\), \(q\in[1,\frac{n}{n-\lambda}]\) and \(b\) are parameters satisfying the scaling condition \(\frac{n+b}{q}=n-\lambda+a\). Let \(f\in C^{\infty}_c({\mathbb R}^n)\) satisfy the inequality
	\begin{align*}
		\norm{{\mathcal I}_{\lambda} f}_{L^q(|x|^b, dx)} &\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a |f(y)|dy
	\end{align*}
	Set \(A_R:=B(0,2R)\setminus B(0,R)\), \(R>2\sup\{|y|:y\in \emph{supp}f\}\), \(M_{\alpha}:=\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}(-y)^{\alpha}f(y)dy\), and \(M_0:=\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}f(y)dy.\) Then one has
		\begin{align}
		\left|\left|\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}\right|\right|_{L^q(|x|^b,A_R)} \ &\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a |f(y)|dy \label{asymptotic_L^q_bound}
	\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Again let \(m:=\ceil{a}-1\), note the immediate identity
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}f(y)&=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}f(y)-|x-y|^{\lambda-n}f(y)+|x-y|^{\lambda-n}f(y) \label{splitting}
	\end{align}
	and if \(m=0\) we use the convention
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{|\alpha|\leq 0}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}&=|x|^{\lambda-n}.
	\end{align*}
	We use the following notation for simplicity
	\begin{align*}
		K(x,y)&                                                                                                    
		:=|x-y|^{\lambda-n}-\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}
	\end{align*}
	and we also define
	\begin{align*}
		\mathcal{K}f(x)&:=\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}K(x,y)f(y)dy\\
		                                                           
                                                                                                  
                           
	\end{align*}
	If we integrate both sides of \eqref{splitting} in \(y\), and then take weighted \(L^q\) norm of the resulting function of \(x\) on the annulus \(A_R:=B(0,2R)\setminus B(0,R)\), \(R>0\) we arrive at
	\begin{align}
		\left|\left|\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}\right|\right|_{L^q(|x|^b,A_R)} \ &\leq \norm{{\mathcal I}_{\lambda} f}_{L^q(|x|^b,A_R)}+\norm{\mathcal{K}f}_{L^q(|x|^b,A_R)}. \label{useful_bound}
	\end{align}
	The right hand side is controlled by \(\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy\), first inequality \eqref{SW} would imply
	\begin{align*}
		\norm{{\mathcal I}_{\lambda} f}_{L^q(|x|^b,A_R)}\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy
	\end{align*}
	and so we just need to verify
	\begin{align}
		\norm{\mathcal{K}f}_{L^q(|x|^b,A_R)}&\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy \label{key_error_term}.
	\end{align}
	This follows from Lemma \ref{Error_term_lemma} via the following argument: 
	                               
	                
		                                                                                                
		                                                  
		              
	Recall that \(x\in A_R\). Restricting \(R\) to \(\frac{R}{2}>\sup\{|y|:y\in \text{supp} f\}\), which is finite since \(f\) is compactly supported, we observe that every \(y\in \text{supp} f\) and \(x\in A_R\) meet the requirement of Lemma \ref{Error_term_lemma} and so we conclude
	\begin{align*}
		|K(x,y)|\lesssim |y|^aR^{\lambda-n-a}.
	\end{align*}
	Estimating \(\norm{\mathcal{K}f}_{L^q(|x|^b,A_R)}\) using Minkowski's integral inequality and the above bound, we conclude
	\begin{align*}
		\norm{\mathcal{K}f}_{L^q(|x|^b,A_R)}&\lesssim R^{\lambda-n-a}\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy\left(\int_{A_R}|x|^bdx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\\
		&\lesssim R^{\lambda-n-a}R^{\frac{b+n}{q}}\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy.
	\end{align*}
	Using the scaling condition, one has \(\frac{n+b}{q}=n-\lambda+a\) and so the \(R\) terms cancel which verifies \eqref{key_error_term} for all \(R\) sufficiently large.\\

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{linear_independence}
	Suppose that \(0<\lambda<n\) and assume
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}|x|^{\lambda-n}&=0\label{look_for_trivial}
	\end{align} 
	for all \(x\in{\mathbb R}^n\setminus \{0\}\). Then, unless \(\lambda\) is an even integer and \(\ell\geq\lambda\), one has 	
	\begin{align}
		c_{\alpha}&=0 \label{conc_2}
	\end{align}
	for all \(|\alpha|=\ell\). In the exceptional case where \(\lambda\) is an even integer, and \(\ell\geq \lambda\), there exists a homogeneous degree \(\ell-\lambda\) polynomial \(W\) such that
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}&=W(\xi)|\xi|^{\lambda} \label{conc_1b}.
	\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
\(\)\\
	The assumption \eqref{look_for_trivial} on \({\mathbb R}^n\setminus\{0\}\) implies that the distribution on the left hand side is supported only at \(\{0\}\). Hence, (see Theorem 2.3.4 in \cite{hormander}) it is a finite linear combination (possibly zero) of derivatives of the delta distribution \(\delta_0\). Explicitly, for some \(k\in {\mathbb N}\) and constants \(\{w_{\beta}\}_{|\beta|\leq k}\) \eqref{look_for_trivial} is equivalent to
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}|x|^{\lambda-n}&=\sum_{|\beta|\leq k}w_{\beta}\partial^{\beta}\delta_0(x) \label{look_for_trivial_2}
	\end{align}
	as distributions on \({\mathbb R}^n\). It is easier to analyse the distributional Fourier transform of the above identity. Since \(0<\lambda<n\),  the distribution \(|x|^{\lambda-n}\) and all of its derivatives have a well defined (distributional) Fourier transform on \({\mathbb R}^n\), which can be identified with the following function (see Lemma 2 in Section 1, Chapter 5 in \cite{stein70}):
	\begin{align*}
		\widehat{|\cdot|^{\lambda-n}}&=C_{n,\lambda}|\xi|^{-\lambda}
	\end{align*}
	for some dimensional constant \(C_{n,\lambda}\), which we may safely ignore. With this in mind, \eqref{look_for_trivial_2} becomes                                                                                                                                                
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}|\xi|^{-\lambda}&=\sum_{|\beta|\leq k}w_{\beta}\xi^{\beta}.
	\end{align*}
	After multiplying both sides by \(|\xi|^{\lambda}\), we arrive at the following equation
		\begin{align*}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}&=\sum_{|\beta|\leq k}w_{\beta}\xi^{\beta}|\xi|^{\lambda}.
	\end{align*}
	The left hand side is either zero, or homogeneous of degree \(\ell\), the right hand side must also be zero, or homogeneous of degree \(\ell\). This gives \(\ell\geq \lambda\) and
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}&=\sum_{|\beta|= k}w_{\beta}\xi^{\beta}|\xi|^{\lambda} \label{W_defn}
	\end{align}
	where \(\ell=k+\lambda\). Furthermore, testing \(-\xi\) in the above equation reveals that \(\ell\) and \(k\) must have the same parity, or both sides are \(0\). Hence both sides are non-zero precisely when \(\ell\geq \lambda\) and \(\lambda\) is an even integer. In this exceptional case, \eqref{W_defn} is the desired conclusion.\\
	\\
	Suppose now that \(\ell<\lambda\) or \(\lambda\) is not an even integer. Then both sides of \eqref{W_defn} must be zero. By the linear independence of polynomials we are forced to conclude \(c_{\alpha}=0\) for all \(|\alpha|=\ell\), and so \eqref{conc_2} follows.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
\(\)\\
	Assume for a contradiction that there exists a \(\gamma\) of minimal degree such that
	\begin{align*}
		M_{\gamma}:=\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}(-y)^{\gamma}f(y)dy
	\end{align*}
	is nonzero. That is, if there exists another \(\beta\) such that \(M_{\beta}\neq 0\) then
	\begin{align*}
		|\gamma|\leq |\beta|.
	\end{align*}
	Let \(\ell:=|\gamma|\), \(m=\ceil{a}-1\) and assume that \(\ell\leq m\) (otherwise there is nothing to prove). By assumption, since every multi-index of degree less than \(\ell\) is zero, we begin by observing that
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}&=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}-\sum_{\ell<|\alpha|\leq m}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}.
	\end{align*}
	Taking weighted \(L^q\) norms on \(A_R\) and applying Lemma \ref{asymptotic_L^q} to the first term on the right hand side, we conclude
	\begin{align}
			\left|\left|\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}\right|\right|_{L^q(|x|^b, A_R)}&\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy+\sum_{\ell<|\alpha|\leq m}R^{\frac{n+b}{q}+\lambda-n-|\alpha|}|M_{\alpha}|. \label{intermediate_bound}
	\end{align}
                                                                                                     
                
                                                                                           
              
           
                                                                                                                                    
                
                                                                                                                                                                     
              
	In order to leverage Lemma \ref{linear_independence}, set
	\begin{align*}
		Q(x):=\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}.
	\end{align*}
	Since \(\partial^{\alpha}|x|^{\lambda-n}\) is a homogeneous degree \(\lambda-n-\ell\) function, so is \(Q\). With this in mind, 
	                                                                                                                      
	we compute the left hand side of \eqref{intermediate_bound} using spherical coordinates:
	\begin{align*}
		\left|\left|\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}\right|\right|_{L^q(|x|^b, A_R)}^q                                                                                    
		&=\int_{R}^{2R}\int_{S^{n-1}}r^{b+n-1+q(\lambda-n-\ell)}|Q(\omega)|^qd\omega dr\\
		&\gtrsim R^{b+n+q(\lambda-n-\ell)}\int_{S^{n-1}}|Q(\omega)|^qd\omega.
	\end{align*}
	Inserting this lower bound into \eqref{intermediate_bound}, and using the scaling condition to simplify the powers of \(R\), we conclude
	\begin{align*}
		R^{a-\ell}\left(\int_{S^{n-1}}|Q(\omega)|^qd\omega\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}&\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy+\sum_{\ell<|\alpha|\leq m}R^{a-|\alpha|}|M_{\alpha}|.
	\end{align*}
	Note that the largest power of \(R\) appears on the left hand side, and hence for the inequality to be valid for all large \(R\) one must have
	\begin{align}
		\int_{S^{n-1}}|Q(\omega)|^qd\omega&=0. \label{vanished_poly}
	\end{align}
	This establishes that \(Q(x)=0\) on \({\mathbb R}^n\setminus\{0\}\) and so Lemma \eqref{linear_independence} applies. We conclude that, unless \(\lambda\) is an even integer and \(\ell\geq \lambda\), we must have \(M_{\alpha}=0\) for all \(|\alpha|=\ell\). When \(\lambda\) is even but \(\ell<\lambda\) we still have \(M_{\alpha}=0\) for all \(|\alpha|=\ell\). Lastly, when both \(\lambda\) and \(\ell\geq \lambda\), 
	                                                                                                                                 
	we conclude that there exists a homogeneous polynomial \(W\) of degree \(\ell-\lambda\) such that
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{M_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\xi^{\alpha}&=W(\xi)|\xi|^{\lambda}.
	\end{align*} 
	Furthermore, the same conclusion can be obtained for order \(k\) moments for any \(\ell<k\leq m\) by modifying the previous argument in the following manner: Instead of requiring a non-zero minimal degree moment, we suppose for a contradiction that there is a minimal \(k\) satisfying \(\ell<k\leq m\) such that the rational function
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\frac{M_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\xi^{\alpha}|\xi|^{-\lambda}
	\end{align*}
	is not a homogeneous polynomial. We use the identity
	\begin{align}
		\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}&=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}-\sum_{k<|\alpha|\leq m}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}-\sum_{\ell\leq|\alpha|< k}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha} \label{identity_2}
	\end{align}
	and note that by the minimality on \(k\) assumption, there exists a polynomial \(R\) of degree strictly lower than \(k-\lambda\) (obtained by summing the homogeneous polynomials) such that
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{\ell\leq|\alpha|< k}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}&=R(D)\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}|x|^{\lambda-n}.
	\end{align*}
	The distribution on the right hand side is \(R(D)\delta_0\), and this vanishes when tested against any function supported away from the origin. Hence we conclude
	\begin{align*}
		\left|\left|\sum_{\ell\leq|\alpha|< k}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}\right|\right|_{L^q(|x|^b, A_R)}&=0.
	\end{align*}
	For the remaining terms in \eqref{identity_2}, we repeat the arguments leading to \eqref{intermediate_bound} and we obtain
	\begin{align*}
			\left|\left|\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}|x|^{\lambda-n}M_{\alpha}\right|\right|_{L^q(|x|^b, A_R)}&\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy+\sum_{k<|\alpha|\leq m}R^{\frac{n+b}{q}+\lambda-n-|\alpha|}|M_{\alpha}|.
	\end{align*}
	Following the same steps as before to invoke Lemma \ref{linear_independence}, we arrive at a contradiction. Hence for all \(\lambda\leq \ell \leq m\), the function \(\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{M_{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\xi^{\alpha}|\xi|^{-\lambda}\) must be a homogeneous polynomial.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{technical_reduction}       
	Let                                                 
	\(n\geq 2\), \(0<\lambda<n\) and suppose that \(a\geq 0\), \(b\in {\mathbb R}\), and \(q\in[1,\frac{n}{n-\lambda})\) satisfy \(\frac{n+b}{q}=n-\lambda+a\). Let \(m=\floor{a}\) denote the floor of \(a\). If \(\rho\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb R})\) is identically \(1\) on \(\left[0,\frac{1}{2r}\right]\) and compactly supported in \(\left[0,\frac{1}{r}\right]\) for some \(r>1\), and if \(G\in C^{m+1}({\mathbb R}^n\setminus\{0\})\) is positively homogeneous of degree \(\lambda-n\), define                                                                                                                                                                                                     
	\begin{align*}
		H(x,y)&:=\rho\left(\frac{|y|}{|x|}\right)\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial_x^{\alpha}G(x)}{\alpha!}.
	\end{align*}
	Then the inequality
	\begin{align*}
		\left(\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|x|^b \left|G*f(x)\right|^qdx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}&\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|x|^a|f(x)|dx+\left(\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|x|^{b}\left|\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}H(x,y)f(y)dy\right|^q dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}
	\end{align*}
	holds for all \(f\in C^{\infty}_c({\mathbb R}^n)\) with implied constant independent of \(f\) (here \(*\) denotes convolution).

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
To simplify notation we set \(\rho=\chi_{[0,\frac{1}{4}]}\). Let \(m:=\floor{a}\) and write
\begin{align*}
	H(x,y)&=\rho\left(\frac{|y|}{|x|}\right)\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial_x^{\alpha}(|x|^{\lambda-n})}{\alpha!}.
	                                                                                                                                  
\end{align*}
As before, when \(m=0\) we mean \(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial_x^{\alpha}(|x|^{\lambda-n})}{\alpha!}=|x|^{\lambda-n}\). By Lemma \ref{technical_reduction} (with \(G(x)=|x|^{\lambda-n}\)) it suffices to check
\begin{align}
	\left(\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|x|^{b}\left|\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}H(x,y)f(y)dy\right|^q dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}&\lesssim \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|y|^a|f(y)|dy \label{Hbound}.
\end{align}
To simplify notation we write
\begin{align*}
	J(|x|)&:= \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}H(x,y)f(y)dy\\
	\tilde{J}(|x|)&:= \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial_x^{\alpha}(|x|^{\lambda-n})}{\alpha!}f(y)dy
\end{align*}
and we divide our analysis into two terms, \(|J-\tilde{J}|\), and \(|\tilde{J}|\). For the difference term, we observe
\begin{align*}
	|J(|x|)-\tilde{J}(|x|)|&=\left|\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}\left(1-\rho\left(\frac{|y|}{|x|}\right)\right)\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial_x^{\alpha}(|x|^{\lambda-n})}{\alpha!}f(y)dy\right|.
\end{align*}
The support of \(1-\rho\left(\frac{|y|}{|x|}\right)\) is the region \(4|y|>|x|\), and combining the above identity for \(|J-\tilde{J}|\) with Minkowski's integral inequality (and triangle inequality) gives,
\begin{align*}
	\left(\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|x|^{b}|J(|x|)-\tilde{J}(|x|)|^{q}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}&\lesssim\sum_{\ell\leq m} \int_{{\mathbb R}^n}|f(y)||y|^{\ell}\left(\int_{\{y\in{\mathbb R}^n:4|y|>|x|\}}|x|^{b+q(\lambda-n-\ell)}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}dy.
\end{align*}
The integral in \(x\) on the RHS is bounded by a constant multiple of \(|y|^{a-\ell}\), to see this use the scaling condition to replace \(b\) in the exponent of \(|x|\) and realise that \(|x|^{q(a-\ell)-n}\) is locally integrable because \(a>\ell\). This means \eqref{Hbound} reduces to analysing the subtracted quantity \(\tilde{J}\). First, we realise that the polyharmonic moment assumption provides (via Lemma \ref{equiv})
               
                                                                                                                         
                                                                                                               
                                                 
             
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             
                                                                 
a polynomial \(R\) (obtained by adding the homogeneous polynomials) which satisfies
\begin{align*}
	\int_{{\mathbb R}^n}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}(-y)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial_x^{\alpha}(|x|^{\lambda-n})}{\alpha!}f(y)dy&=R(D)\Delta^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}|x|^{\lambda-n}.
\end{align*}
When viewed as distributions, the term on the right is \(R(D)\delta_0(x)\). Using the scaling condition, for any \(0\leq \ell\leq m\) we deduce that \(\frac{b}{q}-\ell+\lambda=a-\ell+n-\frac{n}{q}\) is non-negative, since \(a>\ell\) and \(1\leq q\). This implies that
\begin{align*}
	|x|^{\frac{b}{q}}R(D)\delta_0(x)&=0
\end{align*}
in the distributional sense. Hence, interpreting the \(L^q\) norm using duality, we conclude
\begin{align*}
	\norm{|\cdot|^{\frac{b}{q}} \ R(D)\delta_0}_{L^q}&=                                                                                                    
	0.
\end{align*}
