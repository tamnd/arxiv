<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:triangular}
  Let $\nu = \nu_X \otimes \nu_Y \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})$ be a product
  reference measure and $\pi \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})$ a target measure.
  Let $T \colon \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{S}$ be a block-triangular map of
  the form
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:blocktriangular}
    T(x, y) =
    \begin{pmatrix}
      T_X(x; y) \\ y
    \end{pmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  If $T_\ast \nu = \pi$, then $\nu_Y = \pi_Y$ and
  \begin{equation*}
    T_X(\cdot\,; y)_\ast\, \nu_X = \pi(\cdot  \hspace{0.1em}\vert\hspace{0.1em} y)
    \qquad \text{for } \pi_Y\text{-a.e. } y \in \mathcal{Y}.
  \end{equation*}
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Since $T$ leaves the second component unchanged, $T_\ast \nu = \pi$
  implies that the $\mathcal{Y}$-marginals coincide, hence $\nu_Y = \pi_Y$.
  For $B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S})$ and $y \in \mathcal{Y}$, define the slice
  $B_y \coloneqq \{x \in \mathcal{X} : (x, y) \in B\}$. 
  The triangular structure of $T$ gives
  $[T^{-1}(B)]_y = T_X(\cdot\,; y)^{-1}(B_y)$, and hence, by
  $T_\ast \nu = \pi$, the product structure of $\nu$, and Fubini's theorem,
  \begin{equation*}
    \pi(B) = \nu\bigl(T^{-1}(B)\bigr)
    = 
    \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \nu_X\bigl( T_X(\cdot\,; y)^{-1}(B_y) \bigr)
      \, \nu_Y(\mathrm{d}y)
    = 
    \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \bigl( T_X(\cdot\,; y)_\ast\, \nu_X \bigr)(B_y)
      \, \pi_Y(\mathrm{d}y).
  \end{equation*}
  On the other hand, the disintegration of $\pi$ with respect to its
  $\mathcal{Y}$-marginal yields
  \[
    \pi(B) = \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \pi(B_y  \hspace{0.1em}\vert\hspace{0.1em} y)\, \pi_Y(\mathrm{d}y),
  \]
  and the ($\pi_Y$-a.e.) uniqueness of the disintegration  implies
  $T_X(\cdot\,; y)_\ast\, \nu_X = \pi(\cdot  \hspace{0.1em}\vert\hspace{0.1em} y)$ for $\pi_Y$-a.e.\ $y$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:likelihood_free}
  Both training and sampling require only draws from the joint distribution
  $\pi$, i.e., prior samples pushed through the forward model; the
  likelihood is never evaluated. Bayesian flow matching therefore applies
  verbatim to simulation-based (likelihood-free) inverse problems in which
  $\pi(y  \hspace{0.1em}\vert\hspace{0.1em} x)$ is intractable but forward simulation is feasible
  \cite{CranmerEtAl2020,WildbergerEtAl2023,baptista2024conditional}, such as the stochastic Lotka--Volterra model
  of Section~\ref{subsec:lotka_volterra}. In contrast, the Metropolization
  of Section~\ref{subsubsec:imh} and the diagnostics of
  Section~\ref{subsec:diagnostics} require the unnormalized posterior
  density and are hence available only for tractable likelihoods.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:imh_tv}
  Let the target $\pi^y$ and the proposal $q$ have densities $p$ and $q$,
  respectively, and let
  \begin{equation*}
    \bar{\alpha}
    \coloneqq {\boldsymbol{\mathsf  E}}_{X \sim \pi^y,\, \widetilde{X} \sim q} \left[ \alpha(X, \widetilde{X}) \right]
  \end{equation*}
  denote the mean acceptance rate of the stationary IMH chain. Then
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:imh_sandwich}
    1 - 2\, d_{\mathrm{TV}}(q, \pi^y)
    \;\le\; \bar{\alpha} \;\le\;
    1 - d_{\mathrm{TV}}(q, \pi^y).
  \end{equation}
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The term $N \sum_{i=1}^N \bar{W}_i^2 \geq 1$ is related to the so-called \emph{effective sample size} of importance sampling.
It is defined by $(\sum_{i=1}^N \bar{W}_i^2)^{-1}$ and quantifies the number of independent samples from $\pi^y$ which yield the same statistical efficiency for Monte Carlo as the importance weighted samples taken from the proposal distribution.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
In many machine learning applications such as image generation the quality of the generative model is evaluated via the Fr\'echet inception distance, which is, in fact, the 2-Wasserstein distance between two Gaussian measures. That is, it is implicitly assumed that the target $\pi^y$ and $\pi_\theta^y$ are Gaussian. 
In image generation this seems to be a moderate assumption (specifically, when considered in latent spaces), but for our setting, i.e., non-Gaussian posteriors in Bayesian inverse problems, this assumption seems rather far-fetched.
