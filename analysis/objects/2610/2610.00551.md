<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
With regard to the expansion in \eqref{normalized}, since \(x_{11}(q)=q+O(q^2)\), this expansion determines the 
 sequence $(c_n : n \in \mathbb{N}_{0})$  uniquely.
 The uniqueness asserted here is relative to     the pair       $(a_{11}(q),   x_{11}(q))$;  
      it is not a claim
that the  level \(11\), independently of all choices of
modular groups, Hauptmoduls,  and   modular forms,        determines
a unique coefficient sequence. This contrasts with Cooper's
level-\(10\) theory, in which the three distinct modular pairs
\[
(X_A,Z_A),\qquad (X_B,Z_B),\qquad (X_C,Z_C)
\]
give rise to the three coefficient sequences \(T_A(n)\), \(T_B(n)\),
and \(T_C(n)\)
\cite[Chap.~10, Ex.~9]{Cooper2017}.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:recurrence}
 The period coefficients $b_n$ satisfy the recurrence \eqref{splitrec}, with $b_0=1$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 b_n=c_n\qquad(n\geq 0).
 \label{eq:periodequalssequence}
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $ \vartheta:=t\frac{d}{dt}$. 
Fei \cite[Eq.~(1.1)]{Fei} writes the relevant $D3$ operator in the form
\begin{multline*}
 \vartheta^3
 +t\Bigl(\vartheta+\tfrac12\Bigr)
 \bigl(\alpha_3(\vartheta^2+\vartheta)+\beta_1\bigr) 
 +t^2(\vartheta+1)
 \bigl(\alpha_2(\vartheta+1)^2+\beta_0\bigr) + \\
 \alpha_1t^3(\vartheta+2)
 \Bigl(\vartheta+\tfrac32\Bigr)(\vartheta+1)
 +\alpha_0t^4(\vartheta+3)(\vartheta+2)(\vartheta+1).
\end{multline*}
 The heading of the last column of Fei's
Table~1 is
$-(\alpha_3,\alpha_2,\alpha_1;\beta_1,\beta_0)$, and the $V_{22}$ row is
\[
 (20,-56,44;8,-8),
\]
with $\alpha_0=0$ \cite{Fei}. Hence the operator
annihilating $P(t)$ is
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
 \mathcal L_{11}:={} 
 & \vartheta^3
 -t\Bigl(\vartheta+\tfrac12\Bigr)
 \bigl(20(\vartheta^2+\vartheta)+8\bigr) 
 +t^2(\vartheta+1)
 \bigl(56(\vartheta+1)^2+8\bigr) \\
 & \quad -44t^3(\vartheta+2)
 \Bigl(\vartheta+\tfrac32\Bigr)(\vartheta+1), 
\end{split}\label{eq:D3operator}
\end{align}
 so that $\mathcal L_{11}P=0$.

 Here and below, differential operators act on expressions to their right; thus
\[
t^jQ(\vartheta)F(t):=t^j\bigl(Q(\vartheta)F(t)\bigr).
\]
In particular, the displayed powers of \(t\) are not commuted through
the factors involving \(\vartheta\). 
 Taking the coefficient of \(t^m\) in
\(\mathcal L_{11}P(t)=0\), and setting \(b_j=0\) for \(j<0\), gives 
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
 0={}&m^3b_m
 -2(2m-1)(5m^2-5m+2)b_{m-1} 
 + 8 (m- \\
 & 1)\bigl(7(m-1)^2+1\bigr)b_{m-2} - 22(m-1)(m-2)(2m-3)b_{m-3}, 
\end{split}\label{eq:bmrecurrence}
\end{align}
 and this can be verified, for example, 
 by writing 
\[
 \Bigl(m-\tfrac12\Bigr)\bigl(20m(m-1)+8\bigr)
 =2(2m-1)(5m^2-5m+2),
\]
 and by rewriting the other two shifted factors similarly.

Now, put $m=n+1$ in~\eqref{eq:bmrecurrence} and move all terms except
$(n+1)^3b_{n+1}$ to the right. The result is exactly
\eqref{splitrec} with $b$ in place of $c$. Also,
\[
 b_0=\operatorname{CT}_{x,y,z}1=1.
\]
Since the coefficient $(n+1)^3$ never vanishes for $n\geq 0$, the
recurrence and the initial value determine the sequence uniquely.
Therefore $b_n=c_n$ for all $n\geq 0$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:firstsum}
For every $n\geq 0$,
\begin{equation}
 b_n=
 \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}
 \sum_{j=0}^{r}
 \binom{r}{j}^{2}
 \binom{2j}{n-r+j}
 \binom{2r-2j}{n-j}.
 \label{eq:rjsum}
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
By the binomial theorem,
\begin{align}
 b_n
 &=\operatorname{CT}_{x,y,z}
 \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}
 \left(\frac{AB}{xyz}\right)^r
 \left(\frac{1}{yz}\right)^{n-r}\notag\\
 &=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}
 [x^ry^nz^n]A^rB^r.
 \label{eq:ct1}
\end{align}
From~\eqref{eq:Adef} and~\eqref{eq:Bdef},
\begin{align}
 A^r
 &=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}
 x^jy^{r-j}(1+y)^j(1+z)^{r-j},
 \label{eq:Ar}\\
 B^r
 &=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\binom{r}{\ell}
 x^\ell z^{r-\ell}(1+z)^\ell(1+y)^{r-\ell}.
 \label{eq:Br}
\end{align}
In the product $A^rB^r$, the extraction of $x^r$ forces
$j+\ell=r$, hence $\ell=r-j$. Therefore
\begin{align*}
 [x^ry^nz^n]A^rB^r
 &=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}^{2}
 [y^{n-r+j}](1+y)^{2j}
 [z^{n-j}](1+z)^{2r-2j}\\
 &=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}^{2}
 \binom{2j}{n-r+j}
 \binom{2r-2j}{n-j}.
\end{align*}
Substitution in~\eqref{eq:ct1} proves~\eqref{eq:rjsum}.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
The binomial sum formula for $c_{11}(n)$ in \eqref{s11Aexplicit}
 holds for all nonnegative integers.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:recurrence}, $c_n=b_n$. Starting from
\eqref{eq:rjsum}, make the change of variables
\begin{equation}
 a:=n-r,\qquad b:=j-a.
 \label{eq:change}
\end{equation}
Thus
\[
 r=n-a,\qquad j=a+b.
\]
We now determine the exact nonzero range.

The factor
\[
 \binom{2j}{n-r+j}=\binom{2j}{a+j}
\]
is nonzero only when $a\leq j$, which is equivalent to $b\geq 0$.
The factor
\[
 \binom{2r-2j}{n-j}
\]
is nonzero only when
\[
 n-j\leq 2r-2j.
\]
Using $r=n-a$ and $j=a+b$, this inequality is equivalent to
\[
 b\leq n-3a.
\]
Thus the nonzero terms are exactly those with
$$
 0\leq a\leq \left\lfloor\frac n3\right\rfloor,
 \qquad 0\leq b\leq n-3a.
$$
On this range,
\begin{align*}
 \binom{n}{r}&=\binom{n}{a},\\
 \binom{r}{j}&=\binom{n-a}{a+b},\\
 \binom{2j}{n-r+j}
 &=\binom{2a+2b}{2a+b}
 =\binom{2a+2b}{b},\\
 \binom{2r-2j}{n-j}
 &=\binom{2n-4a-2b}{n-a-b}\\
 &=\binom{2n-4a-2b}{n-3a-b},
\end{align*}
where the last equality uses symmetry of binomial coefficients.
 Substituting these identities into~\eqref{eq:rjsum} gives
\eqref{s11Aexplicit}, completing the proof.
