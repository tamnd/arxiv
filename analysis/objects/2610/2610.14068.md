<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:nilinterval}
Let $A$ be a finite ai-semiring and let $\mathcal W$ be an ai-semiring
variety such that
\[
 \mathcal N\subseteq\mathcal W\subseteq\mathsf V(A).
\]
For every positive integer $n$, there is a finite commutative flat
semiring $B_n$, with nilpotent multiplicative reduct and satisfying
$x^2\approx y^2$, such that
\begin{enumerate}[$(1)$]
\item every subsemiring of $B_n$ generated by at most $n$ elements
belongs to $\mathcal N$;
\item $B_n\notin\mathsf V(A)$.
\end{enumerate}
Thus $B_n$ satisfies every identity of $\mathcal W$ in at most $n$
variables but does not belong to $\mathcal W$. In particular,
$\mathcal W$ has infinite axiomatic rank.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $A$ be a finite ai-semiring. If $S_7\in\mathsf V(A)$, then $\mathsf V(A)$
has infinite axiomatic rank. Consequently, $S_7$ is strongly
nonfinitely based.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The hypothesis gives
$\mathcal N\subseteq\mathsf V(S_7)\subseteq\mathsf V(A)$.
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:nilinterval} with $\mathcal W=\mathsf V(A)$.
Every finite identity basis uses only finitely many variables, so
infinite axiomatic rank excludes such a basis.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:localglobal}
Let $\mathcal U\subseteq\mathcal W$ be varieties of ai-semirings.
Suppose that for each positive integer $n$ there is an ai-semiring $B_n$
outside $\mathcal W$ such that every subsemiring of $B_n$ generated by
at most $n$ elements belongs to $\mathcal U$.
Then $B_n$ satisfies $\operatorname{Id}_n(\mathcal W)$, and $\mathcal W$ has infinite
axiomatic rank.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $u\approx v$ belong to $\operatorname{Id}_n(\mathcal W)$, and assign its variables
arbitrarily to elements of $B_n$. These values generate a subsemiring
$C$ belonging to $\mathcal U\subseteq\mathcal W$. Since $u\approx v$
holds in $C$, it holds under the given assignment. Thus $B_n$ satisfies
every identity in $\operatorname{Id}_n(\mathcal W)$.
If these identities implied all identities of $\mathcal W$, Birkhoff's
theorem would give $B_n\in\mathcal W$, a contradiction. This applies
to every positive integer $n$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:powersum}
Let $A$ be a finite ai-semiring with multiplicative reduct $M$.
The map
\[
 \pi:\mathcal P^{+}(M)\longrightarrow A,\qquad
 \pi(X)=\sum_{x\in X}x,
\]
is an onto homomorphism. In particular, every identity of $\mathcal P^{+}(M)$
holds in $A$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The sum defining $\pi$ is independent of the order of its summands.
Additive idempotence gives $\pi(X\cup Y)=\pi(X)+\pi(Y)$. By the two
distributive laws,
\[
 \pi(XY)=\sum_{x\in X,\,y\in Y}xy
 =\left(\sum_{x\in X}x\right)\left(\sum_{y\in Y}y\right)
 =\pi(X)\pi(Y).
\]
If different pairs give the same product, repeated copies of that
product are removed by additive idempotence. Finally,
$\pi(\{a\})=a$ for each $a\in A$, so $\pi$ is onto.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:semiring}
The stated identifications form a semigroup congruence. The quotient is
$0$-cancellative, and $B(\mathcal H)$ is a finite commutative ai-semiring.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Any set of size at least $k-d+1$ that is contained in a block is
contained in exactly one block, since $k-d+1>d-1$.
Thus a subset in one of the prescribed complement classes has a
unique host block and a unique complement label. In particular, two
such classes cannot overlap unless their labels agree. The full-block
class has degree $k$ and the ordinary singleton classes have degree
at most $k-d$, so these are disjoint from the complement classes as
well. The zero class is the singleton $\{0\}$.

If a product
$(E\setminus R)U$ is nonzero in $F(\mathcal H)$, its containing block must be
$E$. Hence $U\subseteq R$, and its class is $b_{R\setminus U}$.
Conversely, this product is nonzero whenever $U\subseteq R$.
The output class is therefore independent of the representative
$E\setminus R$. Full blocks annihilate every nonempty factor.
These observations prove compatibility with multiplication by an
arbitrary element of $F(\mathcal H)$ on either side, and hence prove that
the stated classes form a semigroup congruence. In particular, a
product of ordinary representatives is zero in $F(\mathcal H)$ if and only
if its image is zero in the quotient. We have formed a quotient of
the multiplicative semigroup; flat addition is imposed on that
quotient afterwards.

For reference, the quotient multiplication is as follows. The product
$a_Ua_W$ is zero unless $U,W$ are disjoint and $L=U\cup W$ lies in a
block. Otherwise it is $a_L$ if $|L|\leqslant k-d$, $b_{E\setminus L}$ if
$k-d<|L|<k$, and $p$ if $|L|=k$. In the middle case, $E$ is unique.
Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complementproduct}
 b_Ra_U=
 \begin{cases}
 b_{R\setminus U},&U\subseteq R,\\
 0,&U\not\subseteq R.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Two elements of type $b_R$ multiply to zero, because their degrees sum
to more than $k$, and $p$ annihilates every element. Degrees add whenever
a product is nonzero.

To prove $0$-cancellation, fix a left factor and a nonzero output. We
show that there is at most one right factor.

If the left factor is $a_U$ with $|U|\geqslant d$, its containing block $E$
is unique. The right factor must be ordinary, since a factor $b_R$
would make the total degree exceed $k$. Outputs $a_L$, $b_R$, and $p$
respectively determine the right supports
\[
 L\setminus U,\qquad E\setminus(R\cup U),\qquad E\setminus U.
\]
This proves uniqueness whenever such a product exists.

Suppose $1\leqslant |U|\leqslant d-1$. An output $a_L$ determines
$a_{L\setminus U}$, and output $p$ determines $b_U$. For output $b_R$,
one must have $U\cap R=\varnothing$. If $|U\cup R|\geqslant d$, there is at
most one block containing $U\cup R$, and it determines the right
factor $a_{E\setminus(U\cup R)}$. If $|U\cup R|<d$, the right factor
is necessarily $b_{U\cup R}$. Indeed, all possible representing
complements have this label, whereas an ordinary right factor would
have degree $k-|U\cup R|>k-d$.

Finally, a left factor $b_R$ and output $b_Q$ determine
$a_{R\setminus Q}$; output $p$ determines $a_R$. There are no nonzero
products with left factor $p$. Empty or inadmissible supports in these
formulas simply give no product. We have proved
$xy=xz\ne0\Rightarrow y=z$.

Flat addition is associative, commutative, and idempotent. If $y\ne z$,
then $xy,xz$ cannot be equal and nonzero, so
$xy+xz=0=x(y+z)$. If $y=z$, distributivity follows from idempotence.
Commutativity of multiplication gives the other distributive law.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:link}
Let $r=b_R$, with $1\leqslant s=|R|\leqslant d-1$. The Rees quotient obtained by
collapsing its nondivisors is isomorphic to $B(\mathcal H_R)$, where
\[
 \mathcal H_R=\{E\setminus R:E\in\mathcal E,\ R\subseteq E\}.
\]
The vertex set is the union of these blocks, the parameters are
$k'=k-s$ and $d'=d-s$, and $b_R$ corresponds to the common block product.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
An ordinary element $a_U$ divides $b_R$ precisely when $U\cap R$ is
empty and $U\cup R$ lies in a block. Necessity follows from the product
formulas above. For sufficiency, choose a block $E\supseteq U\cup R$
and multiply $a_U$ by the class of $E\setminus(U\cup R)$. This
complement is nonempty, since $|U|+|R|\leqslant k-1$.
These divisors are exactly the ordinary elements of $B(\mathcal H_R)$,
because $k'-d'=k-d$.

The element $b_Q$ divides $b_R$ precisely when $R\subseteq Q$.
For $Q\ne R$ it corresponds to the link element $b_{Q\setminus R}$;
the element $b_R$ itself corresponds to the common block product.
The element $p$ does not divide $b_R$. This gives a bijection between
the nonzero elements of the two algebras.

Formula~\eqref{eq:complementproduct} is preserved by this bijection:
for a retained $b_Q$ and an ordinary divisor $a_U$, the product
$b_Qa_U=b_{Q\setminus U}$ survives precisely when
$U\subseteq Q$ and $R\subseteq Q\setminus U$. Since
$U\cap R=\varnothing$, this is equivalent to
$U\subseteq Q\setminus R$, the corresponding condition in the link.

Consider next two retained ordinary elements $a_U,a_W$. If their
supports overlap, their product is zero in both constructions.
Suppose that the supports are disjoint and put $L=U\cup W$.
When $|L|\leqslant k-d$, their product survives precisely when $L$ is a
divisor support, that is, when $L\cap R=\varnothing$ and $L\cup R$
lies in a block. When $|L|>k-d$, a nonzero original product has a
unique containing block $E$ and is labelled by $E\setminus L$,
with the empty label allowed for $p$. It survives precisely when
$R\subseteq E\setminus L$. This is again equivalent to saying that
$L$ is disjoint from $R$ and is contained in a block containing $R$.
In the surviving case its link label is
$(E\setminus L)\setminus R=(E\setminus R)\setminus L$, as required;
the label is empty precisely when $L=E\setminus R$.
These are exactly the nonzero-product conditions and outputs in
$B(\mathcal H_R)$. Products of two large elements are zero in both algebras.
The bijection therefore preserves multiplication. It preserves flat
addition as well.

Distinct link blocks intersect in fewer than $d-s$ points. Finally,
\[
 k'-2(d'-1)=k-2(d-1)+s>0.
\]
Here $d'\geqslant1$ and $k'\geqslant k-d+1\geqslant d\geqslant2$, so the construction applies,
including the case $d'=1$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:topfibre}
Assign distinct variables to at most $n$ distinct nonzero elements of
$B(\mathcal H)$, under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:local}. Suppose
every monomial of $u$ has value $p$. Let $w$ be a word using only
variables from $\operatorname{c}(u)$. If every $Z\in\delta(u)$ meets $w$ in
exactly one occurrence, then $w$ has value $p$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Retain only generators whose variables occur in $u$.

\smallskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 1.} The possible monomials and their transversals.
Call an ordinary generator $a_U$ small if $|U|\leqslant d-1$; otherwise
$|U|\geqslant d$, and it has a unique containing block $E(U)$.
Let $D_0$ be the union of the small supports. Then
$|D_0|\leqslant n(d-1)<k$.

For a word consisting of ordinary factors, take their actual supports
as representatives in $F(\mathcal H)$. The quotient map is a semigroup
homomorphism and its zero class is a singleton. The ordinary word
therefore has nonzero value in the quotient precisely when its
representatives have nonzero product in $F(\mathcal H)$. Since the preimage
of $p$ consists exactly of the full blocks, such a word has value $p$
precisely when its supports are pairwise disjoint and their union
is a block. A nonzero word involving $b_R$ has only
ordinary remaining factors, and~\eqref{eq:complementproduct} shows that
their supports must partition $R$ in order to give $p$.
A word involving the factor $p$ has value $p$ only if it has no other
factor. We have therefore obtained exactly three possibilities:
$p$ alone, a factor $b_R$ followed by small factors whose supports
partition $R$, or ordinary factors whose supports partition a block
$E$. In the second case $R\subseteq D_0$. In the third case at least
one factor is not small, since $E\not\subseteq D_0$.
Call the blocks occurring in the third case active. Every nonsmall
ordinary generator occurring in $u$ belongs to a unique active block.

Choose independently $c_E\in E\setminus D_0$ for each active block.
Select the variables corresponding to $p$ and all $b_R$, select no
small variable, and select a nonsmall ordinary variable $a_U$ exactly
when $c_{E(U)}\in U$. Each monomial of $u$ has exactly one selected
occurrence: in the first two forms it is the distinguished factor;
in the third it is the unique part containing $c_E$. Thus these
selections belong to $\delta(u)$.

There is a second selection for each $x\in D_0$. Select $p$, select
$b_R$ when $x\notin R$, and select a small $a_U$ when $x\in U$.
For each active block containing $x$, set $c_E=x$; for every other
active block choose $c_E\in E\setminus D_0$. Select the nonsmall
ordinary variables by membership of $c_E$ in their support.
For a word involving $b_R$, either that factor is selected or exactly
one part of its partition of $R$ is selected. For an ordinary block
partition, exactly one part contains $c_E$; if $x\notin E$, none of
its small parts is selected. Hence these selections also lie in
$\delta(u)$.

\smallskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 2.} Two counting equations.
Let $m_g$ be the number of occurrences in $w$ of the variable naming
generator $g$; write $m_p=0$ if $p$ is absent. All sums over $R$ below
range over complement generators occurring in
$u$, and all sums over $E$ range over active blocks. For an active block $E$,
put
\[
 f_E(x)=\sum_{\substack{g=a_U,\ |U|\geqslant d\\E(U)=E,\ x\in U}}m_g.
\]
The first selections give
\[
 m_p+\sum_Rm_{b_R}+\sum_Ef_E(c_E)=1.
\]
Fix an active block $E$ and hold the choices for all other active
blocks fixed. Each point of $E\setminus D_0$ can be used as $c_E$,
and each resulting selection is a transversal of $u$. Comparing the
displayed equality for two such choices shows that $f_E$ takes the
same value at those points. Thus $f_E$ is constant on
$E\setminus D_0$. Denote its constant value by $d_E\in\mathbb N_0$. The
argument applies to every active block separately, giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:base}
 m_p+\sum_Rm_{b_R}+\sum_Ed_E=1.
\end{equation}
For $x\in D_0$, let
\[
 C_w(x)=\sum_{\substack{g=a_U\\x\in U}}m_g.
\]
The second selection at $x$ gives
\[
 m_p+\sum_{R\not\ni x}m_{b_R}+C_w(x)
       +\sum_{E\not\ni x}d_E=1.
\]
Indeed, all ordinary factors whose supports contain $x$ are selected;
each active block missing $x$ contributes its constant $d_E$ instead.
Subtracting~\eqref{eq:base} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:point}
 C_w(x)=\sum_{R\ni x}m_{b_R}+\sum_{E\ni x}d_E
 \qquad(x\in D_0).
\end{equation}

\smallskip\noindent
\textbf{Step 3.} Determine the value of $w$.
The nonnegative integers in~\eqref{eq:base} sum to one, so exactly
one of the following cases occurs.

If $m_p=1$, all $m_{b_R}$ and $d_E$ vanish. Every nonsmall ordinary
support occurring in $w$ must lie in $D_0$, since its block contributes
zero outside $D_0$. But~\eqref{eq:point} gives $C_w(x)=0$ throughout
$D_0$, excluding all ordinary factors. Thus $w=p$.

If $m_{b_R}=1$ for some $R$, all other terms in~\eqref{eq:base} vanish.
Again every ordinary support occurring in $w$ lies in $D_0$, and
\eqref{eq:point} now gives $C_w(x)=\1_R(x)$ there. Therefore the
ordinary supports, counted with multiplicity, partition $R$. They
are all small because $|R|\leqslant d-1$. Together with $b_R$ they give
value $p$.

Finally, suppose $d_{E_0}=1$ for an active block $E_0$. There are no
$p$ or $b_R$ factors. If a nonsmall factor belongs to another block
$F$, the equation $d_F=0$ confines its support to $D_0$, while
\eqref{eq:point} confines it to $E_0$. Its support would then have
size at most $|E_0\cap F|<d$, a contradiction. Thus all nonsmall
factors belong to $E_0$. Their supports cover each point of
$E_0\setminus D_0$ exactly once, since $f_{E_0}=1$ there.
Equation~\eqref{eq:point} says that all ordinary supports cover
precisely $E_0\cap D_0$ once inside $D_0$. Hence the supports partition
$E_0$, and $w$ again has value $p$.

All counts include repeated occurrences of variables. The argument
also applies when $D_0$ is empty: then the second family of equations
is empty, and the same three cases give the conclusion.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let $C$ be generated by a set of at most $n$ distinct elements, and
consider an identity of $S_7$ under an assignment into $C$. Express
each assigned element as a polynomial in distinct symbols naming
the generators. Substitution gives an identity $u\approx v$ of
$S_7$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:criterion} gives
\[
 \operatorname{c}(u)=\operatorname{c}(v),\qquad \delta(u)=\delta(v).
\]
If both evaluations are zero, they agree. Otherwise interchange the
sides if necessary, and let $u$ have nonzero value $r$. By flatness,
every monomial of $u$ has value $r$. Every generator occurring in $u$
is therefore nonzero; discard the other generators, which also do
not occur in $v$. Each monomial $w$ of $v$ uses only $\operatorname{c}(u)$ and
is met exactly once by every member of $\delta(u)$.

If $r=p$, Lemma~\ref{lem:topfibre} gives $w=p$.
If $r=a_W$, all occurring generators are ordinary, and every
monomial of $u$ partitions $W$ by their supports. For each $x\in W$,
the generator symbols whose supports contain $x$ form a transversal
of $u$. Therefore the supports in $w$ cover every point of $W$
exactly once. They are all contained in $W$, so they partition $W$
and $w=a_W$.

Suppose $r=b_R$, and put $s=|R|$. Every occurring generator divides
$r$, since it is a factor of a monomial with value $r$. Pass to the
Rees quotient of Lemma~\ref{lem:link}. These generators remain
distinct and nonzero, and $r$ becomes the common block product.
The new parameters satisfy
\begin{align*}
 (k-s)-2(d-s-1)&=k-2(d-1)+s>0,\\
 (k-s)-n(d-s-1)&=k-n(d-1)+(n-1)s>0.
\end{align*}
Lemma~\ref{lem:topfibre} applies there and gives $w=b_R$ in the
quotient. Since a retained nonzero element has a singleton preimage,
$w=b_R$ in $B(\mathcal H)$ as well.

In all cases every monomial of $v$ has value $r$, so $u=v$.
Consequently $C$ satisfies every identity of $S_7$ and belongs to
$\mathsf V(S_7)$ by Birkhoff's theorem. Every element of $B(\mathcal H)$ has
square $0$, and $0$ absorbs multiplication. Thus $C$ also satisfies
$x^2y\approx x^2$, and hence $C\in\mathcal N$. Notice that $0$
belongs to every nonempty subsemiring $C$, since it is the square of
any element of $C$. The generators considered throughout the proof
were arbitrary elements of $B(\mathcal H)$; no assumption that they are
vertex elements has been made.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:random}
Let $m,D$ be positive integers, let $\varepsilon>0$, and put $d=2D+4$.
For every fixed integer $k>d$ and every sufficiently large integer $N$,
there is a $k$-uniform hypergraph $\mathcal H=(V,\mathcal E)$ with $|V|=N$
having the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[$(1)$]
\item If $E,F\in\mathcal E$ are distinct, then $|E\cap F|<d$.
\item Let $\kappa:V\to\{1,\ldots,m\}$ be any colouring, let
      $R\subseteq V$ with $|R|\leqslant D$, and let $q\in\mathbb N_0^m$ satisfy
      $\lVert q\rVert_1=k-|R|$. If
      \[
       q_i>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
       |\kappa^{-1}(i)\setminus R|\geqslant\varepsilon N,
      \]
      then there is an $E\in\mathcal E$ containing $R$ such that
      \[
       |(E\setminus R)\cap\kappa^{-1}(i)|=q_i
       \qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant m).
      \]
\end{enumerate}
In particular, $N$ may be chosen sufficiently large in any prescribed
residue class modulo a fixed positive integer.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Fix a set $V$ of size $N$ and select each of its $k$-element subsets
independently with probability
\[
 \rho=N^{-k+D+3}.
\]
Since $k>2D+4$, we have $0<\rho\leqslant1$ for $N\geqslant1$.

Call a triple $(\kappa,R,q)$ \emph{eligible} if it satisfies the
hypotheses in (2), and put $r=|R|$. The number of $k$-element subsets
that would provide the required extension is
\[
 T(\kappa,R,q)=
 \prod_{i=1}^m\binom{|\kappa^{-1}(i)\setminus R|}{q_i}.
\]
There is a constant $c>0$, depending only on the fixed parameters
$m,D,k,\varepsilon$, such that
\[
 T(\kappa,R,q)\geqslant cN^{k-r}
\]
for every eligible triple and all sufficiently large $N$. Indeed, if
$q_i>0$ and $N\geqslant2k/\varepsilon$, then
\[
 \binom{|\kappa^{-1}(i)\setminus R|}{q_i}
 \geqslant \frac{(\varepsilon N/2)^{q_i}}{q_i!}.
\]
There are only finitely many possible vectors $q$ with $\lVert q\rVert_1\leqslant k$,
so the constants in the resulting product have a common positive lower
bound. The same $c$ therefore works for every eligible colouring,
every core $R$ of size at most $D$, and every permitted vector $q$.
All parameters $m,D,k,\varepsilon$ are fixed before $N$ tends to
infinity.

Let $X$ be the number of selected extensions of a fixed eligible triple.
It is binomial, with mean
\[
 \mu=T(\kappa,R,q)\rho
      \geqslant cN^{D+3-r}\geqslant cN^3.
\]
We use the following elementary Chernoff bound~\cite{Chernoff}:
\[
 \Pr(X\leqslant\mu/2)\leqslant e^{-\mu/8}.
\]
For completeness, it follows by applying Markov's inequality to
$2^{-X}$:
\[
 \Pr(X\leqslant\mu/2)
 \leqslant 2^{\mu/2}\mathbb E(2^{-X})
 =2^{\mu/2}(1-\rho/2)^{T(\kappa,R,q)}
 \leqslant \exp\bigl((\log 2-1)\mu/2\bigr)
 \leqslant e^{-\mu/8}.
\]
Consequently, the probability that this triple has fewer than
$(c/2)N^3$ selected extensions is at most $e^{-cN^3/8}$.
There are at most
\[
 m^N\left(\sum_{r=0}^D\binom Nr\right)(k+1)^m
\]
triples. Multiplying this bound by $e^{-cN^3/8}$ gives a quantity tending
to zero. Thus, with probability tending to one, every eligible triple
has at least $(c/2)N^3$ selected extensions.

It remains to control intersections. Let $Y$ count ordered pairs of
distinct selected blocks with intersection of size at least $d$. Then
\begin{align*}
 \mathbb EY
 &\leqslant\binom Nk\sum_{j=d}^{k-1}
       \binom kj\binom{N-k}{k-j}\rho^2\\
 &=O(N^{2k-d}\rho^2)
  =O(N^{2D+6-d})=O(N^2).
\end{align*}
The constants here depend only on $k$ and $D$. Hence
$\Pr(Y>N^{5/2})=O(N^{-1/2})$ by Markov's inequality.
For sufficiently large $N$, choose a realization in which every eligible
triple has at least $(c/2)N^3$ extensions and $Y\leqslant N^{5/2}$.
Delete every block belonging to a pair counted by $Y$. This removes at
most $2N^{5/2}$ blocks. Since
\[
 (c/2)N^3>2N^{5/2}
\]
for all sufficiently large $N$, at least one extension of every eligible
triple remains. The surviving blocks satisfy both (1) and (2).
The argument applies to every sufficiently large integer $N$, which also
proves the final assertion.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:semilinear}
The set of multiplicity vectors of a regular language is semilinear.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Semilinear sets are closed under finite unions and Minkowski sums, since
\[
 (b+\mathbb N_0 P)+(c+\mathbb N_0 Q)=(b+c)+\mathbb N_0(P\cup Q).
\]
They are also closed under forming the additive submonoid generated by
a set. To see this, write
$U=\bigcup_{i=1}^r(b_i+\mathbb N_0 P_i)$ and let $U^{\oplus *}$ be the set
of all finite sums of elements of $U$, including the empty sum. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:star}
 U^{\oplus *}=\{0\}\ \cup\!
 \bigcup_{\varnothing\ne I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}}
 \left(\sum_{i\in I}b_i+
 \mathbb N_0\left(\{b_i:i\in I\}\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}P_i\right)\right).
\end{equation}
For the forward inclusion, choose a linear component for each summand
and let $I$ consist of the components used. One copy of each $b_i$,
$i\in I$, contributes the displayed base; further copies of bases and
all periods contribute to the generated monoid. Conversely, an element
on the right is a sum of elements of $U$: use one summand from each
component in $I$, place all of that component's period contributions in
this summand, and add the required further copies of its base.

empty word, and the empty language have semilinear sets of multiplicity
vectors. It follows by induction that every regular expression does too.
For completeness, a finite automaton gives such an expression as follows.
Number its states $1,\ldots,t$, and let $R_{ij}^{(r)}$ describe paths
from $i$ to $j$ whose internal states lie in $\{1,\ldots,r\}$.

together with the empty word when $i=j$. Successively use

\[
 R_{ij}^{(r)}=
 R_{ij}^{(r-1)}\ \cup\
 R_{ir}^{(r-1)}\bigl(R_{rr}^{(r-1)}\bigr)^*
 R_{rj}^{(r-1)}.
\]
After the last state, take the union over the initial and accepting

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:core}
Let $M$ be a finite semigroup, regarded as an alphabet of size $m$.
For $s\in M$, let $L_s$ consist of all nonempty words whose ordered
product in $M$ is $s$. There are integers $D,L\geqslant1$ and a real number
$\varepsilon>0$, depending only on $M$, with the following property.
For every sufficiently long word $w\in L_s$, write $z$ for its
multiplicity vector and $N$ for its length. There are vectors
$c,h\in\mathbb N_0^m$, with $h\ne0$, such that
\begin{enumerate}[$(1)$]
\item $c\leqslant z$ coordinatewise, $\lVert c\rVert_1\leqslant D$, and
      $\lVert c\rVert_1\equiv N\pmod L$;
\item $\lVert h\rVert_1$ divides $L$, and $c+\mu h$ is the multiplicity vector
      of a word in $L_s$ for every $\mu\in\mathbb N_0$;
\item $h_i>0$ implies $z_i\geqslant2\varepsilon N$.
\end{enumerate}
The length threshold is independent of $s$. It may also be chosen so
that, whenever $h_i>0$, one has $z_i-c_i\geqslant\varepsilon N$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Each language $L_s$ is regular. An automaton recognizing it has the states
of $M$ and a new initial state. Reading a letter $a$ in the initial state
leads to $a$; reading $a$ in state $x\in M$ leads to $xa$. The only
accepting state is $s$, and the initial state is not accepting.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:semilinear}, fix a semilinear representation for the
multiplicity vectors of each $L_s$. There are finitely many linear
components in these representations altogether. Delete zero period
vectors. At least one nonzero period remains, since the union of the
languages $L_s$ is $M^+$ and has words of arbitrarily large length.
Let $L$ be the least common multiple of the lengths of all the nonzero
period vectors. Put
\[
 D=\max\left\{1,\
 \max_{(b,P)}\left(\lVert b\rVert_1+(L-1)\sum_{h\in P}\lVert h\rVert_1\right)\right\},
\]
where $(b,P)$ ranges over all the chosen components. Let $B_0$ be the
largest base length, let $J\geqslant1$ be the largest number of periods in a
component, and let $C\geqslant1$ be the largest period length. Set
\[
 \varepsilon=\frac1{4JC}.
\]

Take $w\in L_s$ of length $N>2B_0$ and choose a component containing
its multiplicity vector. Write
\[
 z=b+\sum_{i=1}^j\lambda_i v_i.
\]
Since $N>2B_0$, this component has at least one period. Moreover,
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^j\lambda_i\lVert v_i\rVert_1
 =N-\lVert b\rVert_1\geqslant N/2.
\]
As $j\leqslant J$ and $\lVert v_i\rVert_1\leqslant C$, some index $a$ satisfies
\[
 \lambda_a\geqslant\frac{N}{2JC}=2\varepsilon N.
\]
Set $h=v_a$ and
\[
 c=b+\sum_{i=1}^j(\lambda_i\bmod L)v_i,
\]
where each remainder belongs to $\{0,\ldots,L-1\}$.
Then $c\leqslant z$ and $\lVert c\rVert_1\leqslant D$. Every coordinate of $z-c$ is
divisible by $L$, so $\lVert c\rVert_1\equiv N\pmod L$. Also $\lVert h\rVert_1$ divides
$L$ by the choice of $L$. Adding any nonnegative multiple of $h$ to
$c$ keeps the vector in the same linear component, proving (2).
If $h_i>0$, its $i$th coordinate contributes at least $\lambda_a$ to
$z_i$, which proves (3). Finally, $c_i\leqslant D$ gives
\[
 z_i-c_i\geqslant2\varepsilon N-D\geqslant\varepsilon N
\]
when $N\geqslant D/\varepsilon$. All constants were chosen using all $s\in M$
at once, so a common length threshold suffices.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:separation}
Let $M$ be a finite semigroup and let $n\geqslant1$. There exist integers
$d,k,N$ and a hypergraph $\mathcal H$ satisfying all the hypotheses of
Theorem~\ref{thm:local}, with $|V|=N>k$, for which
\begin{equation}\label{eq:separator}
 \mathcal P^{+}(M)\models t_{\mathcal H}\approx t_{\mathcal H}+q_{\mathcal H},
 \qquad
 B(\mathcal H)\not\models t_{\mathcal H}\approx t_{\mathcal H}+q_{\mathcal H}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal P^{+}(M)$ is the ai-semiring of nonempty subsets of $M$, with
union as addition and setwise multiplication.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Choose $D,L,\varepsilon$ for $M$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:core} and put
$d=2D+4$. Choose an integer
\[
 k>\max\{d,\ 2(d-1),\ n(d-1),\ D\}.
\]
With these parameters fixed, choose $N>k$ sufficiently large for
Lemmas~\ref{lem:random} and~\ref{lem:core} to apply and such that
$N\equiv k\pmod L$. Lemma~\ref{lem:random}, with $m=|M|$, provides
a hypergraph $\mathcal H$ on $N$ vertices satisfying the stated intersection
and extension properties. It has no isolated vertices. Indeed, given
$v\in V$, take $R=\{v\}$, colour every vertex with one colour, and
request $k-1$ further vertices of that colour. This request is eligible
for large $N$, since $D\geqslant1$ and $\varepsilon\leqslant1/4$, and so a block
containing $v$ exists. The choices of $d,k$ also give
\[
 k>2(d-1),\qquad k>n(d-1),
\]
as required for Theorem~\ref{thm:local}.

We prove the first assertion of~\eqref{eq:separator}. Assign an arbitrary
nonempty subset $X_v\subseteq M$ to each variable $x_v$, and take
$s\in q_{\mathcal H}(X_v:v\in V)$. By the definition of setwise multiplication,
there are elements $\alpha_v\in X_v$ whose product in the chosen order
of $V$ is $s$. Regard these elements, in that order, as a word $w\in L_s$,
and let $z$ be its multiplicity vector. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:core} to
obtain $c,h$.

Colour vertex $v$ by the element $\alpha_v\in M$. Since $c\leqslant z$, we
can select a set $R\subseteq V$ containing exactly $c_i$ vertices of
each colour $i$. Thus $|R|=\lVert c\rVert_1\leqslant D$, and
\[
 |R|\equiv N\equiv k\pmod L.
\]
Because $\lVert h\rVert_1$ divides $L$ and $k>D\geqslant|R|$, the number
\[
 \mu=\frac{k-|R|}{\lVert h\rVert_1}
\]
is a positive integer. The vector $\mu h$ has coordinate sum
$k-|R|$. Every colour used by it still occurs at least $\varepsilon N$
times outside $R$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:core}. The extension property
therefore gives a block $E\supseteq R$ whose vertices outside $R$ have
multiplicity vector $\mu h$. The colours on the whole block have
multiplicity vector $c+\mu h$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:core}(2), there is a word in $L_s$ with precisely this
vector. For each letter $i$, the number of its occurrences in that
word equals the number of vertices of $E$ coloured $i$. Choose a
bijection between those occurrences and those vertices, independently
for each colour. Reading the matched vertices in the order of the
word lists each vertex of $E$ exactly once. This yields an ordering
$(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$ of $E$ for which
\[
 \alpha_{v_1}\cdots\alpha_{v_k}=s.
\]
The ordered product here is the product of the chosen word in $L_s$;
no commutation of factors in $M$ is involved. The corresponding
monomial is a summand of $t_{\mathcal H}$, and each
$\alpha_{v_i}$ belongs to $X_{v_i}$. Hence
$s\in t_{\mathcal H}(X_v:v\in V)$. This proves
\[
 q_{\mathcal H}(X_v:v\in V)\subseteq t_{\mathcal H}(X_v:v\in V)
\]
for every assignment, which is exactly the asserted identity in
$\mathcal P^{+}(M)$.

For the second assertion, evaluate $x_v$ as $a_{\{v\}}$ in $B(\mathcal H)$.
The product over each block, in any order, is $p$, so $t_{\mathcal H}$ has
value $p$. The word $q_{\mathcal H}$ is a product of $N>k$ degree-one elements
and therefore has value $0$. Its addition to $p$ gives $0$, whereas
$p\ne0$. Thus the identity fails in $B(\mathcal H)$.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:uniformsize}
For every pair of positive integers $n,r$ there exist positive
integers $d,L,k_0$ with the following property.
For every $k\geqslant k_0$ and every sufficiently large $N$ satisfying
$N\equiv k\pmod L$, there are a finite commutative flat semiring $B$
and an identity $\sigma$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[$(1)$]
\item every subsemiring of $B$ generated by at most $n$ elements
belongs to $\mathcal N$;
\item every element of $B$ has square zero, and its multiplicative
reduct has nilpotency index exactly $k+1$;
\item every ai-semiring of cardinality at most $r$ satisfies
$\sigma$, whereas $B$ does not;
\item the cardinality of $B$ satisfies
\[
 N+2\ \leqslant\ |B|\ \leqslant\
 2+(2^k-2)\frac{\binom Nd}{\binom kd}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
Here the threshold for $N$ may depend on $k$.
In particular, $B\notin\mathcal U_r$, and for each fixed $k\geqslant k_0$
such semirings have arbitrarily large cardinality.

Let $A_r$ be the direct product of the members of $\mathcal K_r$,

 \max\{d,\,2(d-1),\,n(d-1),\,D\}.

 \sigma:\quad t_{\mathcal H}\approx t_{\mathcal H}+q_{\mathcal H}.
\]

All nonzero elements of $B$ have positive degree, and degrees add in
a nonzero product. No degree exceeds $k$, so every product of $k+1$
elements is zero. The product of the $k$ vertex elements of any block
is $p\ne0$. Hence the nilpotency index is exactly $k+1$.

Given an ai-semiring $C$, adjoin a fresh element $\bot$ and prescribe
\[
 c+\bot=\bot+c=c,\qquad c\bot=\bot c=\bot
 \quad(c\in C\cup\{\bot\}).
\]
The enlarged addition is a semilattice operation with new identity,
and the enlarged multiplication is associative with new zero.
For distributivity, if the outside factor is $\bot$, both sides
are $\bot$; if a summand is $\bot$, its zero product disappears
from the sum on the other side. All other cases are the original
distributive laws in $C$. Thus the extension is an ai-semiring
of cardinality $|C|+1$ containing $C$ as a binary subsemiring.
Repeating the construction embeds every ai-semiring of cardinality
at most $r$ into one of cardinality exactly $r$.
Consequently $\mathcal U_r\subseteq\mathcal T_r$; the reverse
inclusion follows from the definitions.

axiomatic rank. For $r=1$, the variety is trivial and is defined by
$x\approx y$. For $r=2$, finite basability is a result of Shao and
Ren~\cite{SR}, who proved that every subvariety of the variety
generated by all two-element ai-semirings has a finite basis.

 B=B(\mathcal H),\qquad \sigma=(t_{\mathcal H}\approx t_{\mathcal H}+q_{\mathcal H}).
\]

First consider $\{+,\cdot,1\}$.
Equip $\mathcal P^{+}(M)$ with the named identity $\{1_A\}$.
The map $X\mapsto\sum X$ is an onto unital homomorphism to $A$,
so $A\models\sigma$. Use $B^1$ as the finite algebra violating
$\sigma$. The same assignment of vertex elements as in $B$ gives
this violation, because $B$ is a binary subsemiring of $B^1$ and
$\sigma$ has no constants. By Lemma~\ref{lem:witness-unit}, every
at most $n$-generated unital subalgebra belongs to $\mathsf V_1(S_7)$.

Next consider $\{+,\cdot,0\}$.
Use the full power semiring $\mathcal P(M)$ with named zero
$\varnothing$. The identity $\sigma$ holds there.
When all variable values are nonempty, this is
Proposition~\ref{prop:separation}; if a variable has value
$\varnothing$, then $q_{\mathcal H}$ has value $\varnothing$, since it
contains every variable, so the identity is automatic.
The map $X\mapsto\sum X$, with the empty sum interpreted as $0_A$,
is an onto homomorphism preserving the named zero.
Consequently $A\models\sigma$.

The finite algebra $B^\bot$ still violates $\sigma$ under the
vertex assignment. A subalgebra generated by at most $n$ elements
is either $\{\bot\}$ or $C^\bot$, where $C$ is the binary subsemiring
of $B$ generated by its non-$\bot$ generators. This description
follows from the neutral and absorbing laws for $\bot$.
Since $C\in\mathsf V(S_7)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-adjunction} gives
$C^\bot\in\mathsf V_0(S_7^\bot)$.

Finally, consider $\{+,\cdot,0,1\}$.
Name both $\varnothing$ and $\{1_A\}$ in $\mathcal P(M)$.
The same homomorphism preserves both constants, so
$A\models\sigma$. Use $(B^1)^\bot$ as the finite algebra violating
$\sigma$. An at most $n$-generated subalgebra is obtained by taking
the unital subalgebra of $B^1$ generated by the non-$\bot$ generators
and adjoining $\bot$. If there are no such generators, the unital
subalgebra is $\{1\}$. Lemmas~\ref{lem:witness-unit} and
\ref{lem:zero-adjunction} show that the resulting subalgebra belongs
to $\mathsf V_{0,1}(S_7^\bot)$.

In each case, let $K$ be the small algebra in the statement and let
$\mathsf V(K)\subseteq\mathcal W\subseteq\mathsf V(A)$ in the relevant
signature. The constructed finite algebra satisfies all identities
of $\mathcal W$ in at most $n$ variables: the values in any assignment
generate a subalgebra in $\mathsf V(K)\subseteq\mathcal W$.
It does not belong to $\mathcal W$, because it fails $\sigma$,
which holds in $A$. As $n$ is arbitrary, $\mathcal W$ has infinite
axiomatic rank. Taking $\mathcal W=\mathsf V(A)$ proves strong nonfinite
basability in each of the three signatures.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:rank}
Let $A$ be a finite ai-semiring. If all identities of $A$ follow from
its identities in at most $n$ variables, for some positive integer $n$,
then $A$ has a finite identity basis. Conversely, a finite identity
basis gives such a bound on the number of variables.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
Let $F_n$ be the set of $n$-ary term functions of $A$. This set is
finite, since its elements are maps $A^n\to A$. For each $f\in F_n$,
choose a representing term $t_f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$. Let $\pi_i$ denote
the $i$th projection. Consider the finite set $\Sigma_n$ consisting of
\[
 x_i\approx t_{\pi_i}\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant n)
\]
and
\[
 t_f\mathbin{\circ}t_g\approx t_{f\mathbin{\circ}g}
 \qquad(f,g\in F_n,\quad \circ\in\{+,\cdot\}),
\]
where $f\mathbin{\circ}g$ is the pointwise operation on term functions.
All these identities hold in $A$.

Induction on the formation of a term $t$ proves
$\Sigma_n\vdash t\approx t_{\widehat t}$, where $\widehat t$ is the
term function induced by $t$. Thus, if $u\approx v$ is an identity
of $A$ in the variables $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, then
$\widehat u=\widehat v$ and $\Sigma_n\vdash u\approx v$.
Renaming variables shows that $\Sigma_n$ implies every identity of
$A$ in at most $n$ variables. Under the hypothesis, it is therefore
a finite basis for $\operatorname{Id}(A)$.

Conversely, the maximum number of variables occurring in a finite
basis gives the required bound.
