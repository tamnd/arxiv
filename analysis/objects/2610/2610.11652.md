<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{thm: equation}
{Let $G$ be a finite abelian group of order $n$} and let $K$ be a field in which $n$ is invertible.
The TPE system consist of $d_\varrho,c\in K^\times$, a
nondegenerate symmetric bicharacter $\chi:G\times G\to K^\times$,
$a:G\to K^\times$, and $\beta:G\to K$, satisfying
\begin{align}
 d_\varrho^2&=n+nd_\varrho,\label{eq:dim_rho}\\
 a(-g)&=a(g),\qquad a(0)=1,\label{eq:apm}\\
 a(g+h)\chi(g,h)&=a(g)a(h),\label{eq:achi}\\
 c^3\sum_{g\in G}a(g)&=\frac1n,\label{eq:c_nonzero}\\
 \sum_{g\in G}a(g)^{-1}\chi(h,g)\beta(g)&=cn\beta(h),\label{eq:Fourier}\\
 \beta(g)\beta(-g)+\frac{\delta_{g,0}}{d_\varrho}&=\frac{a(g)}n,\label{eq:betapm}\\
 \frac{a(h-g)}n\sum_{k\in G}\chi(h,k)^{-1}
 \frac{\beta(g-k)\beta(k)}{a(k)}
 &=\beta(-h)\beta(g)\beta(h-g)+\frac{\delta_{h,0}\delta_{g,0}}{d_\varrho}.
 \label{eq:two_beta_identity}
\end{align}
A unitary TPE system over $ K = \mathbb{C} $ requires further
\begin{align}
  d &> 0, \\
  \overline{\beta(g)} &= \frac{\beta(-g)}{a(g)}.
\end{align}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The primary structural advantage of the TPE system lies in the form of its most complicated equation~\eqref{eq:two_beta_identity}: it equates a sum of quadratic terms to a single cubic term, whereas the corresponding equation in Izumi's system equates a sum of cubic terms to a single quadratic term. While such a shift might appear minor in a general polynomial system, it is crucial in the near-group setting, where this is the \emph{sole} non-trivial equation governing the system.

    This structural simplification provides substantial theoretical and computational leverage. For instance, in characteristic two, setting $g=2h$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:two_beta_identity} directly yields the Frobenius relation $\beta(2h)=\beta(h)^4$. Crucially, the fundamental role of the TPE framework is further validated by the work of Radchenko and Wheeler \cite{RadchenkoWheeler}, who constructed solutions to Izumi's subsystem by first establishing that they satisfy an equation analogous to TPE-equation~\eqref{eq:two_beta_identity}, rather than tackling the pentagon equations directly.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
Let $G$ be a finite abelian group of order $n$. The existence of a spherical (resp., unitary) near-group fusion category with fusion rules $G + n$ and trivial Frobenius--Schur indicator over a field $K$ (resp., $\mathbb{C}$) is equivalent to the solvability of the TPE system (resp., the unitary TPE system).

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:cubic-reduction}\label{prop:beta-normalization}
Let $G$ be a finite abelian group of order $n$, over a field $K$ in
which $n$ is invertible. Fix a nondegenerate symmetric bicharacter
$\chi$, an even quadratic refinement $a$ satisfying
\eqref{eq:apm}--\eqref{eq:achi}, and $d\ne0$ with $d^2=n+nd$; put $d_\varrho=d$.
Then the TPE system is equivalent to
\[
 \beta(0)=-d^{-1}
\]
and the cubic equation~\eqref{eq:two_beta_identity}. More precisely,
these conditions force $B:=\sum_{g\in G}\beta(g)\ne0$, and the
remaining equations hold with the unique choice
\[
 c=-\frac1{dB}.
\]
Every $\beta(g)$ is then nonzero, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta-zero-convolution}
 \beta(0)=-\frac1d,\qquad
 \sum_{k\in G}\frac{\beta(g-k)\beta(k)}{a(k)}
 =\delta_{g,0}-\frac1d.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Write $b=\beta(0)=-1/d$ and
$q(g)=\beta(g)\beta(-g)/a(g)$. Thus $q(-g)=q(g)$. For the
unnormalized Fourier transform
\[
 (\mathcal Ff)(h)=\sum_{k\in G}\chi(h,k)^{-1}f(k),
\]
character orthogonality gives $\mathcal F^2q=nq$,
$\mathcal F\delta_0=\mathbf1$, and $\mathcal F\mathbf1=n\delta_0$.
The case $g=0$ of the cubic identity, divided by $a(h)/n$, is
\[
 \mathcal Fq=nbq+\frac nd\delta_0.
\]
Applying $\mathcal F$ again gives
\[
 (1-nb^2)q=\frac{nb}{d}\delta_0+\frac1d\mathbf1.
\]
Since $1-nb^2=n/d$, this is precisely
\[
 q=\frac1n\mathbf1-\frac1d\delta_0,
\]
which proves~\eqref{eq:betapm} and nonvanishing of every $\beta(g)$.

Put
\[
 C_h=\sum_{g\in G}\frac{\beta(g)\beta(h-g)}{a(h-g)}.
\]
The case $h=0$ of the cubic equation, with its other argument renamed,
now gives
\[
 C_h=nbq(h)+\frac nd\delta_{h,0}=b+\delta_{h,0},
\]
using $n/d+n/d^2=1$. Divide the cubic equation by $a(h-g)$ and sum
over $g$ to obtain
\[
 \frac Bn\sum_{k\in G}\chi(h,k)^{-1}\frac{\beta(k)}{a(k)}
 =\beta(-h)C_h+\frac{\delta_{h,0}}d=b\beta(-h).
\]
At $h=0$ the right side is $b^2\ne0$, so $B\ne0$.
Replacing $h$ by $-h$ proves~\eqref{eq:Fourier} for $c=b/B$.

Finally, set $S_a=\sum_g a(g)$, $S_{a^{-1}}=\sum_g a(g)^{-1}$,
and
\[
 (Tf)(h)=\sum_g a(g)^{-1}\chi(h,g)f(g).
\]
The refinement identity and character orthogonality give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar-fourier-toolkit}
 S_aS_{a^{-1}}=n,\qquad
 (T^2)_{h,j}=S_{a^{-1}}a(h)\chi(h,j)^{-1},\qquad
 T^3=nS_{a^{-1}}I.
\end{equation}
Since $T\beta=cn\beta$ and $\beta\ne0$, we get
$c^3S_a=1/n$, proving~\eqref{eq:c_nonzero}.

Conversely, suppose the full scalar system holds. Its quadratic
identity gives $\beta(0)^2=1/n-1/d=1/d^2$. At $(g,h)=(0,0)$
the cubic identity then gives $\beta(0)^3=-1/d^3$, whence
$\beta(0)=-1/d$. The displayed summed cubic identity and the
Fourier equation force $c=b/B$, proving uniqueness.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:F-identities}\label{prop:sF-well-defined}
The function $F$ satisfies
\[
 F(z)=\gamma(\omega_1-z),\qquad F(z)F(n-z)=1,\qquad
 F(z+n)=\frac{\sin(\pi z/\omega_1)}{\sin(\pi z/\omega_2)}F(z).
\]
For every nonzero integer $m$,
\[
 F(m+n)=(-1)^mF(m),\qquad F(-m)=\frac{(-1)^m}{F(m)}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
 F(0)=\frac{\sqrt n}{\omega_2},\qquad
 F(0)^2-1=\sqrt n\,F(0),\qquad F(k)>0\quad(0\leq k<n).
\]
The product $s(x)F(x)$ is $n$-periodic on
$\mathbb Z\setminus n\mathbb Z$. The zero residue class is exceptional:
$F(n)=F(0)^{-1}\ne F(0)$.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Reflection gives the first two identities, and the two gamma shifts give
\[
 \frac{F(z+n)}{F(z)}
 =\frac{\gamma(\omega_2+z)}{\gamma(\omega_1+z)}
 =\frac{\sin(\pi z/\omega_1)}{\sin(\pi z/\omega_2)}.
\]
At a nonzero integer $m$, the sines do not vanish and their ratio is
$(-1)^m$, since $1/\omega_2-1/\omega_1=1$.
Reflection then gives the formula for $F(-m)$, while
$s(m+n)=(-1)^m s(m)$ proves periodicity on the nonzero classes.
Taking $z\to0$ in~\eqref{eq:gamma-shift1} and using the residue at zero
gives $F(0)=\gamma(\omega_1)=\sqrt n/\omega_2$.
The equation $\omega_2^2+n\omega_2=n$ proves its quadratic identity,
and~\eqref{eq:gamma-positivity} proves positivity.

\noindent\textbf{Step 1: the finite sum as a residue sum.}

Put
\[

\]
and choose \(\phi\in(-\pi,\pi]\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi-direct}
\phi\equiv\pi-A\pmod{2\pi}.
\end{equation}
Then
\[
(-1)^k e^{-i\phi k}=e^{iAk},
\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
\]

Define
\[
R_g(z):=\frac{F(z)}{F(z-g)},
\qquad
K_g(z):=
\frac{\pi e^{-i\phi z}}{\sin\pi z}R_g(z),
\qquad
J_g(z):=
e^{-i\phi z}\gamma(-z)\gamma(z-g).
\]

Let
\[
L:=\left\lfloor\frac{g}{\omega_2}\right\rfloor
\]
and choose
\[
0<\varepsilon<
\min\{1,(L+1)\omega_2-g\},
\qquad
\sigma:=-\varepsilon.
\]
In the strip
\[
\sigma<\mathrm{Re} (z)<\sigma+n,
\]
the poles of \(K_g\) are
\[
0,1,\ldots,n-1
\]
and
\[
p_\ell:=g-\ell\omega_2,
\qquad
1\leq\ell\leq L.
\]
The points \(p_\ell\) are nonintegral by the irrationality of \(\omega_2\).

At an integer \(k\),
\[
\operatorname{Res}_{z=k}K_g(z)
=
e^{iAk}R_g(k).
\]
For \(k\neq g\),
\[
R_g(k)
=
(-1)^{g-k}F(k)F(g-k),
\]
whereas
\[
R_g(g)=\frac{F(g)}{F(0)}.
\]
Therefore
\begin{align}
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{Res}_{z=k}K_g(z)
&=
(-1)^gS_{g,h}
+
(1-F(0)^2)e^{iAg}\frac{F(g)}{F(0)}.
\label{eq:integer-residues-direct}
\end{align}

The vertical asymptotics of the hyperbolic gamma function give, uniformly on the strip,
\[
R_g(x+iy)
=
O\left(e^{-\pi g|y|/n}\right),
\]
and hence
\[
K_g(x+iy)
=
O\left(
e^{-[\pi(1+g/n)-|\phi|]|y|}
\right).
\]
Thus the horizontal sides of the residue rectangle vanish as its height tends to infinity.

Using
\[
R_g(z)
=
-4
\sin\frac{\pi z}{\omega_2}
\sin\frac{\pi(z-g)}{\omega_1}
\gamma(-z)\gamma(z-g),
\]
together with
\[
\frac1{\omega_2}-\frac1{\omega_1}=1,
\]
one obtains
\[
K_g(z+n)-K_g(z)
=
-4\pi
\sin\frac{\pi g}{\omega_1}
J_g(z).

\begin{align}

+
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
\operatorname{Res}_{z=p_\ell}K_g(z)
=
-2\sin\frac{\pi g}{\omega_1}
\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}
J_g(z)\frac{dz}{i}.
\label{eq:rectangle-direct}
\end{align}

\operatorname{Res}_{z=p_\ell}J_g(z),
\qquad
0\leq\ell\leq L,
\]
where \(p_0=g\). A direct substitution of
\(p_\ell=g-\ell\omega_2\) gives

\sin\frac{\pi g}{\omega_1}\,r_\ell,
\qquad
1\leq\ell\leq L.
\]

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma-direct}

=
-2\sin\frac{\pi g}{\omega_1}I_\sigma
-
4\pi\sin\frac{\pi g}{\omega_1}
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}r_\ell,
\end{equation}

:=
\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}
J_g(z)\frac{dz}{i}.
\]

\[

\qquad\text{and}\qquad

The deformation crosses precisely
\[
p_0,p_1,\ldots,p_L,

and therefore
\[
I_{\rm sep}
=
I_\sigma
+
2\pi\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}r_\ell.

=

+

\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Step 2: meromorphic continuation of the separating-contour integral.}

For independent complex parameters \(g,\alpha\), consider
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BSS-family}
\mathcal I(g,\alpha)
:=
\int_{C_{\rm sep}(g)}
e^{\pi i z(2\alpha-g-\Omega)/n}
\gamma(z-g)\gamma(-z)\frac{dz}{i\sqrt n}.
\end{equation}
The contour $C_{\rm sep}(g)$ is precisely the separating contour used
in $I_{\rm sep}$.
For \(\mathrm{Re} (g)<0\), the two pole families
\[
g-j\omega_1-k\omega_2
\]
and
\[
j\omega_1+k\omega_2
\]
are separated by a vertical contour $ \mathrm{Re} (z) = \sigma $. The integral is therefore well defined in a nonempty open region of the \((g,\alpha)\)-plane where the convergence conditions of the BSS formula are satisfied.

On compact subsets avoiding pole collisions, the separating contour may be chosen continuously in the parameters, and the vertical asymptotics of the hyperbolic gamma function imply locally uniform convergence. Hence \(\mathcal I(g,\alpha)\) is holomorphic there. Continuing the contour across a pole changes the integral by the corresponding residue; consequently \(\mathcal I(g,\alpha)\) admits a meromorphic continuation in the parameters.

A collision between the two pole families can occur only when
\[
g\in\omega_1\mathbb Z+\omega_2\mathbb Z.
\]
By
\[
(\omega_1\mathbb Z+\omega_2\mathbb Z)\cap\mathbb Z=n\mathbb Z,
\]
none of \(1,\ldots,n-1\) is a collision point. Thus the separating-contour integral may be continued from \(\mathrm{Re} (g)<0\) to each
\[
g=1,\ldots,n-1.
\]

The BSS identity (Lemma~\ref{lem:BSS_identity}) gives
\begin{align}
\mathcal I(g,\alpha)
&=
e^{\pi i g(2\alpha-g-\Omega)/(2n)}
\gamma(\alpha)
\gamma(\Omega+g-\alpha)
\gamma(-g).
\label{eq:BSS-meromorphic}
\end{align}
Both sides are meromorphic in \(g\) and \(\alpha\), so the identity remains valid under the above continuation.

For our fixed integers \(g,h\), choose
\[
\alpha
=
\frac{\Omega+g}{2}
-\frac{n\phi}{2\pi}.
\]
Then
\[
\frac{\pi i}{n}(2\alpha-g-\Omega)z
=
-i\phi z,
\]
and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Isep-direct}
I_{\rm sep}
=
\sqrt n\,
e^{-ig\phi/2}
\gamma(-g)
\gamma(\alpha)
\gamma(\Omega+g-\alpha).
\end{equation}

Moreover,
\[
r_0
=
\frac{\sqrt n}{2\pi}
e^{-i\phi g}\gamma(-g).
\]
Using
\[
2\sin\frac{\pi g}{\omega_1}\gamma(-g)
=
(-1)^{g+1}F(g),
\]
equation \eqref{eq:direct-Isep} becomes
\begin{align}
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{Res}_{z=k}K_g(z)
&=
(-1)^g\sqrt n\,
e^{-ig\phi/2}
F(g)
\gamma(\alpha)
\gamma(\Omega+g-\alpha)
+
(-1)^{g+1}\sqrt n\,
e^{-i\phi g}F(g).
\label{eq:residues-BSS-direct}
\end{align}
Since
\[
e^{-i\phi g}
=
(-1)^ge^{iAg}
\]
and
\[
\frac{1-F(0)^2}{F(0)}
=
-\sqrt n,
\]
the second term in \eqref{eq:residues-BSS-direct} is exactly the correction term in \eqref{eq:integer-residues-direct}. Cancelling these correction terms gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S-BSS-direct}
S_{g,h}
=
\sqrt n\,
e^{-ig\phi/2}
F(g)
\gamma(\alpha)
\gamma(\Omega+g-\alpha).
\end{equation}

\qquad

=
\sqrt n\,F(g)
\frac{s(\overline{-h})}

as required.

\label{prop:abelian-refinements}
Let $G=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\mathbb Z/m_i\mathbb Z\,e_i$ and let
$\chi:G\times G\to\mathbb T$ be a symmetric bicharacter. Choose
$\xi_i$ with
\[
 \xi_i^2=\chi(e_i,e_i),\qquad \xi_i^{m_i^2}=1.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:abelian-quadratic-formula}
 a\!\left(\sum_i x_ie_i\right)
 =\prod_i\xi_i^{-x_i^2}
  \prod_{i<j}\chi(e_i,e_j)^{-x_ix_j}
\end{equation}
is a well-defined even quadratic refinement of $\chi$. Such refinements
form a torsor for $\operatorname{Hom}(G,\{\pm1\})$; in particular
there are exactly $|G[2]|$ of them. If $|G|$ is odd, the unique one is
$a(g)=\chi(g/2,g)^{-1}$.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
If $m_i$ is odd, there is a unique permissible square root,
$\xi_i=\chi(e_i,e_i)^{(m_i+1)/2}$. If $m_i$ is even, both square
roots satisfy the required power condition. Increasing $x_i$ by $m_i$
does not change the diagonal factor because
$\xi_i^{2m_ix_i+m_i^2}=1$, or a mixed factor because
$\chi(e_i,e_j)^{m_i}=1$. Thus the formula is well defined. Negation
preserves it, and expansion of $(x_i+y_i)^2$ gives
$a(x+y)\chi(x,y)=a(x)a(y)$, including the mixed terms.
The quotient of two refinements is a character. Evenness says that
this character has square $1$, and conversely multiplying by any
such character preserves all requirements. Finally,
$|\operatorname{Hom}(G,\{\pm1\})|=|G/2G|=|G[2]|$.
For odd-order $G$, division by $2$ is unique; the stated formula has
the required polarization, and uniqueness follows from $G[2]=0$.

\label{lem:mixed-gauge}
There are unitary morphisms
\[
 m_{g,h}:g\otimes h\longrightarrow g+h,\qquad
 L_g:g\otimes\varrho\longrightarrow\varrho,\qquad
 R_g:\varrho\otimes g\longrightarrow\varrho
\]
for which the associativity coefficients on $(g,h,k)$, $(g,h,\varrho)$,
and $(\varrho,g,h)$ are $1$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Choose the $L_g$ unitarily, with $L_0$ the unit constraint. Define
$m_{g,h}$ by requiring the two composites
$(g\otimes h)\otimes\varrho\to\varrho$ to agree after inserting the
associator. This determines a unitary $m_{g,h}$, since the relevant
morphism spaces are one-dimensional. The pentagon on $(g,h,k,\varrho)$
then makes the $m_{g,h}$ associative. Thus the pointed associator and
the left module associator are simultaneously trivialized.

The right maps must also respect the bends. Let
$t:\mathbf1\to\varrho\otimes\varrho$ be the standard coevaluation, so
$t^\dagger t=d$. Define $U_g:\varrho\to\varrho\otimes g$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-mate-normalization}
 (\operatorname{id}_\varrho\otimes L_g)
 (U_g\otimes\operatorname{id}_\varrho)t=t,
 \qquad R_g=U_g^\dagger.
\end{equation}
Rigidity makes the displayed assignment from $U_g$ to its left-hand
side an isomorphism of one-dimensional morphism spaces, so $U_g$
exists uniquely. If $U_g^\dagger U_g=s\operatorname{id}_\varrho$,
taking the squared norm in this equation gives $sd=d$; hence $U_g$
is unitary. Apply the same defining equation successively to $U_h$
and $U_g$, and use the left module relation. Uniqueness gives
\[
 (\operatorname{id}_\varrho\otimes m_{g,h})
 (U_g\otimes\operatorname{id}_h)U_h=U_{g+h}.
\]
Taking adjoints proves the right module relation. This is the
categorical normalization of \cite[Remark~3.2]{Izumi2017}; it fixes
both module associativity and the mixed bends, rather than choosing
their phases independently. Associators have been suppressed in
these formulas by coherence.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:unitarity-gap}
Let $G$ be a finite abelian group of order $n$, and let
$(d,\chi,a,c,\beta)$ satisfy the TPE system over
$\mathbb C$, with $d=(n+\sqrt{n^2+4n})/2$. Put
\[
 p_g=n|\beta(g)|^2,\qquad t=p_0=\frac1{d+1},\qquad
 q_g=\sum_{k\in G}p_kp_{g-k}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive-convolution}
 p_gp_{-g}=1,\qquad q_g=p_gq_{-g}\qquad(g\ne0).
\end{equation}
Let $\kappa>1$ be the unique root in $(1,\infty)$ of
$X^3-2X^2-X+1=0$, so $\kappa=2.24697960\ldots$.
Either the tuple satisfies Hermitian reflection, or
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unitarity-gap}
 \max_{g\ne0}\sqrt n\,|\beta(g)|>\sqrt\kappa.
\end{equation}
In particular, the coarse inequalities
$\sqrt n\,|\beta(g)|\le\sqrt2$ for all $g\ne0$
already imply Hermitian reflection and unitary reconstruction.
More precisely, every nonunitary tuple must have $n\ge5$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unitarity-gap-finite-n}
 M^{2/(n-2)}<(M-1)(1-M^{-2}),\qquad
 M=\max_{g\ne0}p_g.
\end{equation}
In particular, every positive-root algebraic scalar tuple with
$n\le4$ satisfies Hermitian reflection.

<!-- proof-30: body_md -->
Since $\chi$ and $a$ take values of modulus $1$, Equation~\eqref{eq:betapm}
gives
$p_gp_{-g}=1$ for $g\ne0$, and $\beta(0)=-1/d$ gives
$t=n/d^2=1/(d+1)\in(0,1)$.
For fixed $g\ne0$, taking absolute squares of the cubic TPE and summing
over $h$, character orthogonality gives
$$
\frac1n\sum_{k\in G}
|\beta(g-k)|^2|\beta(k)|^2
=
|\beta(g)|^2
\sum_{h\in G}
|\beta(-h)|^2|\beta(h-g)|^2.
$$
Multiplying by $n^3$ yields the second identity
in~\eqref{eq:positive-convolution}.

If every nonzero $p_g$ is one, the quadratic equation gives
$\overline{\beta(g)}=\beta(-g)/a(g)$, so Hermitian reflection holds. Otherwise set $M=\max_{g\ne0}p_g>1$ and choose
$g$ with $p_g=M$. Then $g\ne-g$, hence $n\ge3$.
Put $\delta=M-1$, $e_0=0$, and $e_k=p_k-1$ for $k\ne0$.
Every nonzero $p_k$ lies in $[M^{-1},M]$, and
\[
 |e_k|\le\delta,\qquad e_{-k}=-e_k/p_k.
\]
Expanding $p=\mathbf1+(t-1)\delta_0+e$ gives, for $g\ne0$,
\[
 q_g=n-2+2t+2\sum_k e_k+2(t-1)e_g+(e*e)_g.
\]
Since $q_g=Mq_{-g}$ and $e_g-e_{-g}=\delta(1+M^{-1})$,
\begin{align*}
 (e*e)_g-(e*e)_{-g}
 &=\delta q_{-g}+2(1-t)\delta(1+M^{-1})\\
 &>\delta(n-2)M^{2/(n-2)}.
\end{align*}
To obtain the last inequality, retain the $n-2$ terms of $q_{-g}$
with $k\ne0,-g$. Their product is $M^2$: indeed,
$\prod_{k\ne0}p_k=1$ and removing $p_{-g}=M^{-1}$ leaves
product $M$, in each of the two factors. The arithmetic--geometric
mean inequality now gives $q_{-g}>(n-2)M^{2/(n-2)}$.
On the other hand, reindexing the second convolution gives
\[
 (e*e)_g-(e*e)_{-g}
 =\sum_{k\ne0,g}e_ke_{g-k}
       \left(1-\frac1{p_kp_{g-k}}\right).
\]
For $x,y\in[M^{-1},M]$ one has
\[
 |(x-1)(y-1)(1-(xy)^{-1})|
 \le\delta^2(1-M^{-2}).
\]
Indeed, its left side equals
\[
 \left|\frac{x-1}{\sqrt x}\right|
 \left|\frac{y-1}{\sqrt y}\right|
 \left|\frac{xy-1}{\sqrt{xy}}\right|,
\]
whose first two factors are at most $\delta/\sqrt M$ and whose
third factor is at most $M-M^{-1}$. Consequently the convolution
difference is at most $(n-2)\delta^2(1-M^{-2})$. Combining this
with its strict lower bound proves~\eqref{eq:unitarity-gap-finite-n}.
Its left side is greater than one, so
\[
 1<(M-1)(1-M^{-2}),\qquad M^3-2M^2-M+1>0.
\]
The function $(M-1)(1-M^{-2})$ is strictly increasing for $M>1$,
which gives $M>\kappa$. Since $\kappa>2$, the simpler sufficient
bound follows. For $n=3,4$, the left side
of~\eqref{eq:unitarity-gap-finite-n} is at least $M$, whereas the
right side is less than $M$, a contradiction. For $n=1,2$ all
nonzero elements are involutions and already have $p_g=1$.

and therefore $s(k)F(k)=\mu_n\Phi_{S,0,0}
(z_{n,k};\varepsilon_n)$.

Write $T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
and $S=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
Then $A_n=T^{n+2}S$, and the eta transformation laws give
\[
 \mu_{A_n}=e^{\pi i(n+2)/12}e^{-\pi i/4}=\mu_n.
\]
On the upper half-plane the defining products
\cite[equation~(1)]{RadchenkoWheeler} give
$\Phi_{A_n,0,0}(z;\tau)=\Phi_{S,0,0}(z;\tau)$:
both have denominator parameter $e^{-2\pi i/\tau}$ and argument
$z/\tau$. Meromorphic continuation gives the same identity at
$\tau=\varepsilon_n$. For $\mathbf u=(0,-k)$ the fixed-point
argument is
\[
 \frac{u_1\varepsilon_n+u_2}{\varepsilon_n^{-1}-1}
 =z_{n,k},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:rm-value}. Finally the reciprocal conversion to
$\operatorname{shin}$ has correction factor $1$, because the second
coordinate of $(I-A_n)\mathbf r_{n,k}$ is zero
\cite[Definition~4.18 and Proposition~4.19]{Kopp}.

<!-- proposition-8: body_md -->
\label{prop:finite-coefficient-field}
The field
\[
 L_n=\mathbb Q\bigl(\zeta_{4n},E_n(k):k\in C_n\bigr)
\]
is a number field, stable under ordinary complex conjugation. It
contains all scalar coefficients of the TPE system
and $K_n=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{\Delta_n})$.
Exactly $[L_n:K_n]$ embeddings $\iota:L_n\hookrightarrow\mathbb C$
send $d_-$ to $d_+$.
<!-- end proposition-8 -->
<!-- proof-33: body_md -->
\cite[Theorem~1]{RadchenkoWheeler} give algebraicity of $E_n(k)$;
at zero this also follows from $E_n(0)^2=\varepsilon_n$.
Moreover
\[
 \sqrt{\Delta_n}=2E_n(0)^2-(n+2)\in L_n.
\]
The phases $s(k)$ lie in $\mathbb Q(\zeta_{4n})$, as does
$\sqrt n$ by
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{g\in C_n}^{} a(g) = \sqrt{n}e^{\pi i(n-1)/4}.
\end{align*} 
Its phase is a power of
$i$ for odd $n$, and $\zeta_8\in\mathbb Q(\zeta_{4n})$ for even $n$.
Hence $\beta(k)=E_n(k)/\sqrt n$, $a(k)$, $\chi(g,h)$, $d_-$,
and $c=\beta(0)/\sum_k\beta(k)$ all belong to $L_n$.
Positivity of $F(k)$ and $s(k)^2=a(k)$ give
$\overline{E_n(k)}=a(k)^{-1}E_n(k)$, proving conjugation stability.
Finally each embedding of $K_n$ has $[L_n:K_n]$ extensions to $L_n$,
because number fields are separable.

<!-- proposition-9: body_md -->
\label{prop:unitary-galois-criterion}
\label{cor:unit-circle-reformulation}
\label{cor:coarse-galois-unitarity}
Let $\iota:L_n\hookrightarrow\mathbb C$ satisfy
$\iota(d_-)=d_+$. The transported tuple satisfies the unitary scalar
condition of Corollary~\ref{thm: reconstruction} precisely when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unitary-galois-criterion}
 \overline{\iota(\beta(g))}
 =\frac{\iota(\beta(-g))}{\iota(a(g))}\qquad(g\in C_n),
\end{equation}
or, equivalently, when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unit-circle}
 |\iota(F(k))|=1\qquad(1\leq k<n).
\end{equation}
Either of the following weaker bounds already implies these conditions:
\[
 |\iota(F(k))|\leq\sqrt2\quad(1\leq k<n),
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad
 \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}|\iota(F(k))|^2\leq n-\frac12.
\]
In each case the specifically reconstructed positive-root category
admits a unitary structure.
<!-- end proposition-9 -->
<!-- proof-35: body_md -->
The scalar identities survive under $\iota$. The transported values
of $\chi,a$ are roots of unity, and character orthogonality and the
Gauss equation give $|\iota(c)|=n^{-1/2}$. Thus the algebraic
reflection agrees with ordinary complex conjugation on those
parameters. The remaining condition is
\eqref{eq:unitary-galois-criterion}.
For $1\leq k<n$, set $S_k=\iota(s(k))$, $X_k=\iota(F(k))$
and $r=\iota(\sqrt n)\in\{\sqrt n,-\sqrt n\}$.
Since $s(n-k)=s(k)$, $F(n-k)=F(k)^{-1}$ and $a(k)=s(k)^2$,
the two sides of \eqref{eq:unitary-galois-criterion} are
\[
 r^{-1}S_k^{-1}\overline{X_k}
 \quad\text{and}\quad r^{-1}S_k^{-1}X_k^{-1}.
\]
They agree exactly when $|X_k|=1$. At zero the equation is automatic,
because $\iota(\beta(0))=-d_+^{-1}$ is real.
The first sufficient bound follows from
Theorem~\ref{thm:unitarity-gap}, since
$\sqrt n\,|\iota(\beta(k))|=|\iota(F(k))|$ for $k\ne0$.
For the second, $d_+^{-2}=n^{-1}-d_+^{-1}$ turns the bound into
\eqref{eq:coarse-defect-bound}; apply
Corollary~\ref{cor:coarse-defect-bound}.
Finally use Corollary~\ref{thm: reconstruction}.

<!-- proposition-10: body_md -->
\label{prop:small-rank-unitarity}
The unit-circle conclusion of Conjecture~\ref{conj:unitary-galois}
holds for $1\leq n\leq6$. Consequently the positive-root categories
admit unitary structures for these orders.
<!-- end proposition-10 -->
<!-- proof-36: body_md -->
For $n\leq4$, Theorem~\ref{thm:unitarity-gap} applies to every
positive-root scalar solution.

For $n=5$, Proposition~\ref{prop:rm-normalization} identifies our
solution with the cycle-$(7)$ example of
\cite[\S4.3]{RadchenkoWheeler}. Its nonzero values are products of
roots of unity and roots of
\[
 P(Z)=Z^4-Z^3-\frac{3+3\sqrt5}{2}Z^2-Z+1.
\]
After any embedding reversing $\sqrt5$, divide the conjugate
polynomial by $Z^2$ and put $W=Z+Z^{-1}$. This gives
\[
 W^2-W-\frac{7-3\sqrt5}{2}=0,
 \qquad W=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{15-6\sqrt5}}2.
\]
Both traces lie in $(-2,2)$, so all roots have modulus one.

For $n=6$, put
\[
 r=F(0)=\sqrt{4+\sqrt{15}},\qquad
 X=F(1)+F(5),\qquad Y=F(2)+F(4).
\]
Reciprocity gives $F(5)=F(1)^{-1}$, $F(4)=F(2)^{-1}$ and
$F(3)=1$. Proposition~\ref{prop:cubic-reduction} gives directly
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^5s(k)F(k)=\sqrt6\,r\,e^{5\pi i/12}
 =r+e^{5\pi i/12}X+e^{2\pi i/3}Y+e^{3\pi i/4}.
\]
Equating real and imaginary parts after multiplication by
$e^{-5\pi i/12}$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:n6-exact-traces}
 X=\frac{2+\sqrt{15}+\sqrt3}{2},\qquad
 Y=\frac{(\sqrt3+1)r-\sqrt6}{2},
\end{equation}
using $\sqrt6\,r=3+\sqrt{15}$ in the original embedding.
If $\iota(\sqrt{15})=-\sqrt{15}$, then
\[
 \iota(X)=\frac{2-\sqrt{15}\pm\sqrt3}{2}\in(-2,2).
\]
Also $\iota(r)^2=4-\sqrt{15}>0$, so $\iota(r)$ is real and
every possible choice of the remaining signs satisfies
\[
 |\iota(Y)|\leq
 \frac{(\sqrt3+1)\sqrt{4-\sqrt{15}}+\sqrt6}{2}<2.
\]
Indeed $\sqrt{15}>15/4$, $\sqrt3<7/4$ and $\sqrt6<5/2$
bound the last expression by $31/16<2$.
The conjugate traces of both reciprocal pairs lie in $(-2,2)$,
and hence all four conjugated values lie on the unit circle;
the midpoint remains $\iota(F(3))=1$.

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
\label{rem:arithmetic-content}
\label{rem:galois}
The algebraic scalar equations survive under every field embedding;
ordinary complex conjugation need not. On $\iota(L_n)$ the
transported involution is
$\iota\circ\overline{(\,\cdot\,)}\circ\iota^{-1}$, which can
differ from ordinary conjugation. Likewise the gamma integral
evaluates the original real embedding, not $\iota(F(k))$:
formally conjugating its periods is analytic continuation, during
which the separating contour crosses poles, rather than Galois
transport. The scalar product identity gives only
$\iota(\beta(g))\iota(\beta(-g))=\iota(a(g))/n$ for $g\ne0$;
reflection requires $|\iota(\beta(g))|^2=1/n$.
This is the real/unitary distinction described in
\cite[\S6]{RadchenkoWheeler}.
<!-- end remark-5 -->
<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
\label{rem:hilbert-twelfth}
For $K_n=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{n(n+4)})$, Proposition~\ref{prop:rm-normalization}
identifies $E_n(k)$ for $1\leq k<n$, up to roots of unity and
inversion, with real-multiplication values of the Shintani--Faddeev
modular cocycle. Kopp's limit formula \cite[Theorem~1.1]{Kopp}
relates squares of these values, with explicit root-of-unity factors,
to exponentials of derivatives at $s=0$ of differences of ray class
partial zeta functions. The Stark conjectures predict their
class-field placement and Galois behavior, as explained after
Conjecture~\ref{conj:unitary-galois}; algebraicity alone is already
unconditional by Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-coefficient-field}.
This is the connection with Hilbert's twelfth problem: constructing
abelian extensions through explicit analytic special values
\cite{Stark1976,AFK}. The categorical application uses the predicted
Galois behavior to obtain unitarity; it does not assert that this cyclic
family generates all abelian extensions of $K_n$.
