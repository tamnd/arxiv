<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For every positive integer $n$, each of $\mathcal A_n$ and $\mathcal V_n$
is finitely based if and only if $n\leq2$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:padding}
Every ai-semiring of order at most $n$ belongs to $\mathcal A_n$. The
varieties $\mathcal A_n$ and $\mathcal V_n$ are locally finite.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Given a semiring $S$, adjoin a new element $\omega$ absorbing for
both operations. Each associativity or distributivity instance
involving $\omega$ has value $\omega$ on both sides; the remaining
instances are unchanged. If $S$ is an ai-semiring, so is the
extension. Iterating this construction embeds every ai-semiring
of order at most $n$ into one of order $n$.

For fixed $n$ there are finitely many operation tables on an
$n$-element set. Thus each of the two joint varieties is generated
by a finite direct product of finite algebras. If $D$ is a finite
algebra, its free algebra on $r$ generators in $\operatorname{Var}(D)$ is
represented by the $r$-ary term functions on $D$, so is finite.
Consequently both joint varieties are locally finite.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:deduction}
In the free commutative ai-semiring, an elementary application of
an inequality $r\preceq\mathbf{s}$ has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deduction}
\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{K}\varphi(\mathbf{s})
\ \longleftrightarrow\
\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{K}\varphi(\mathbf{s})+\mathbf{K}\varphi(r).
\end{equation}
The additive context $\mathbf{l}$ and the multiplicative context
$\mathbf{K}$ may be absent. Every equational deduction is a finite
sequence of such steps.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Equational completeness describes deductions as contextual
substitution instances of defining identities. Expand a context
with one distinguished occurrence of its argument by
distributivity. Addition contributes to $\mathbf{l}$, and multiplication
contributes to the factors on either side of the occurrence. In
the commutative setting these factors combine into $\mathbf{K}$.
Applying this to $\mathbf{s}\approx\mathbf{s}+r$ gives
\eqref{eq:deduction}. For explicit formulations in ai-semirings,
see~\cite[Lemma~1.1]{RLZC25} and~\cite[Lemma~2.1]{YS26}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:test-validity}
The variety $\mathcal T$ satisfies $\sigma_k$ for every $k\in\mathcal N$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
In $H^0$, if $z=0$, then $P_kz=0$. Otherwise $z\leq z^2$, so
$P_kz\leq P_kz^2$. This proves absorption in $H^0$.

Consider $G$. Unless $\mathbf{F}_k=\infty$, all its words have a
common group value $c=P_k$. Every variable then takes a group
value because it occurs in $\mathbf{F}_k$. Since $P_k$ is a product
of squares, $c^2=1$, and $R_k=P_k$ gives $z^2=1$.
Multiplying the $k$ equalities $B_i=c$ yields
\[
c^k=P_k\left(\prod_{i=1}^k y_i\right)^4z^k=P_kz.
\]
As $k$ is odd, $c^k=c=P_k$, whence cancellation gives $z=1$.
Therefore $Q_k=c=\mathbf{F}_k$. The case $\mathbf{F}_k=\infty$ is
automatic.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:affine}
Let $L,L_0,\ldots,L_t$ be linear forms on a finite-dimensional
vector space over a field. If
\[
L_0(\xi)=\cdots=L_t(\xi)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L(\xi)=L_0(\xi),
\]
then $L\in\operatorname{Aff}\{L_0,\ldots,L_t\}$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $M$ have rows the coefficient vectors of $L_i-L_0$ for
$1\leq i\leq t$. The hypothesis says that $L-L_0$ annihilates
$\ker M$. The annihilator of $\ker M$ is the row space of $M$.
Thus $L-L_0$ is a linear combination of the $L_i-L_0$, which is
equivalent to the asserted affine representation.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:absorbed}
For every $k\in\mathcal N$ and every nonempty commutative word $w$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:absorbed}
\mathcal T\models w\preceq\mathbf{F}_k
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
w\in\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The reverse implication follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:test-validity}.
Suppose that $\mathcal T\models w\preceq\mathbf{F}_k$.

Assign $a$ to one variable and $1$ to all the others in $H^0$.
For a variable in $\mathbf{F}_k$, the value of $\mathbf{F}_k$ is $a^2$;
for an outside variable, it is $1$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-bound}
\operatorname{c}(w)\subseteq\{x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_k,z\},
\qquad m(v,w)\leq2.
\end{equation}
Assign $g^2$ to one $x_i$ or one $y_j$ and $1$ to the other
variables in $G$. All words of $\mathbf{F}_k$ then have value $1$.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:even-bound}
m(x_i,w),m(y_j,w)\in\{0,2\}.
\end{equation}

Every pair of different $x$- or $y$-variables occurring in $w$
must occur together in some word of $\mathbf{F}_k$. Otherwise,
assigning $a$ to the two variables and $1$ to all the others
in $H^0$ gives $\mathbf{F}_k\leq a^2$ but $w=\infty$, by
\eqref{eq:even-bound}. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:incidence}
x_i\text{ can occur with }y_j\text{ only if }j\in\{i,i+1\},
\end{equation}
and two different $y$-variables in $w$ must be adjacent on the
cycle.

The subsemiring $D$ implies that at least one word of $\mathbf{F}_k$
has content contained in $\operatorname{c}(w)$. Indeed, assign $1$ to the
variables of $w$ and $0$ to all other variables. If no such word
existed, then $w=1$ and $\mathbf{F}_k=0$.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{Case 1: $w$ contains every $x_i$.}
Each $y_j$ occurs together with only two $x$-variables in
$\mathbf{F}_k$. Since $k\geq5$, condition~\eqref{eq:incidence}
excludes every $y_j$ from $w$. Equations
\eqref{eq:degree-bound} and~\eqref{eq:even-bound} now give
$w\in\{P_k,Q_k,R_k\}$.

\smallskip
In the remaining cases choose an $x_j$ absent from $w$. Set
$x_j=0$, $z=a$, and every other variable equal to $1$ in $H^0$.
Then $P_k=R_k=0$ and $\mathbf{F}_k=a$. Hence
$m(z,w)\leq1$. The content condition supplied by $D$
forces $w$ to contain $A_k$ or some $B_i$ in content, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:z-once}
m(z,w)=1.
\end{equation}

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{Case 2: $\operatorname{c}(B_i)\subseteq\operatorname{c}(w)$ for some $i$.}
The occurrence of $x_i$ excludes all $y$-variables except
$y_i,y_{i+1}$. An additional $x_j$ would have to occur together
with both $y_i$ and $y_{i+1}$, which forces $j=i$. Thus
$\operatorname{c}(w)=\operatorname{c}(B_i)$, and the degree restrictions give $w=B_i$.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{Case 3: $\operatorname{c}(A_k)\subseteq\operatorname{c}(w)$.}
Write
\[
m(x_i,w)=2\delta_i,\qquad
m(y_j,w)=2\eta_j,
\qquad \delta_i,\eta_j\in\{0,1\},\quad\delta_1=1.
\]
Condition~\eqref{eq:incidence} gives $\eta_j=0$ for
$3\leq j\leq k$. Consider the valuations in $G$ given by
\[
x_i=g^{u_i},\qquad y_j=g^{v_j},\qquad z=g^{2t},
\qquad u_i,v_j,t\in\mathbb F_2.
\]
The values of all relevant words lie in $\{1,g^2\}$. After
dividing their exponents by $2$, their values are represented by
the following linear forms over $\mathbb F_2$:
\begin{align*}
P_k,R_k&:\ \sum_i u_i, & A_k&:\ u_1+t,\\
B_i&:\ u_i+v_i+v_{i+1}+t,
&w&:\ \sum_i\delta_i u_i+\sum_j\eta_jv_j+t.
\end{align*}
When the forms for $\mathbf{F}_k$ have a common value, absorption
forces the form for $w$ to have that value. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:affine}, it is an affine combination of the former
forms. Write its coefficients as
$\alpha,\gamma,\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_k$, merging the equal forms
for $P_k$ and $R_k$ into the coefficient $\alpha$.
Comparing the coefficient sum and the $t$-coordinate gives
\[
1=\alpha+\gamma+\sum_i\beta_i,
\qquad 1=\gamma+\sum_i\beta_i,
\qquad\alpha=0.
\]
The other coordinates give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine-coordinates}
\delta_i=\gamma\mathbf1_{i=1}+\beta_i,
\qquad \eta_j=\beta_j+\beta_{j-1}.
\end{equation}
As $\eta_j=0$ for $j\geq3$, we obtain
$\beta_2=\cdots=\beta_k$, and hence
$\delta_2=\cdots=\delta_k$. If these entries are $1$, Case~1
applies. Otherwise they are all $0$, and
\eqref{eq:affine-coordinates} gives
$\eta_1=\eta_2=\beta_1$. Thus $w=A_k$ or $w=B_1$.
The cases exhaust the content condition, proving the lemma.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:class}
For every commutative term $\mathbf{u}$ and every $k\in\mathcal N$,
\[
\mathcal T\models\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{F}_k
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{F}_k\ \text{or}\ \mathbf{u}=\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k.
\]
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
If $\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{F}_k$ holds in $\mathcal T$, then each word of
$\mathbf{u}$ is absorbed by $\mathbf{F}_k$. Lemma~\ref{lem:absorbed}
gives $\mathbf{u}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k$. We show that every word
of $\mathbf{F}_k$ belongs to $\mathbf{u}$.

For $P_k$, evaluate in $D$ with all $x_i=1$ and all $y_i=z=0$.
Only $P_k$ has value $1$. For $A_k$, evaluate in $D$ with
$x_1=z=1$ and all other variables $0$. Again only the selected
word has value $1$.

For $R_k$, evaluate in $H^0$ with $z=a$ and all other variables
$1$. Then $R_k=a^2$, while every other word of $\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k$
has value at most $a$. For $B_i$, set $y_i=y_{i+1}=a$ and all
other variables equal to $1$ in $H^0$. Only $B_i$ contains both
selected $y$-variables, so $B_i=\infty$ and all other words
have value at most $a^2$.

Each valuation separates one word of $\mathbf{F}_k$ from the sum
of all the other words of $\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k$. Hence
$\mathbf{F}_k\subseteq\mathbf{u}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k$.
The converse follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:test-validity}.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:locals-required}
Let $\varnothing\ne\mathbf{E}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_k$. If
$G\models Q_k\preceq\mathbf{E}$, then every $B_i$ belongs to
$\mathbf{E}$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Suppose that $B_j$ is absent. Set $u_2=1$ and $u_i=0$ for
$i\ne2$, so $u_1=0$ and $\sum_i u_i=1$ in $\mathbb F_2$.
Choose $v_1,\ldots,v_k\in\mathbb F_2$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:path}
v_i+v_{i+1}=u_i\qquad(i\ne j).
\end{equation}
Deleting the $j$th edge of a cycle leaves a path, so these
equations are solvable by choosing one endpoint value and
proceeding along the path. Evaluate in $G$ by
\[
x_i=g^{u_i},\qquad y_i=g^{v_i},\qquad z=g^2.
\]
Then $P_k=R_k=A_k=g^2$. For $i\ne j$,
equation~\eqref{eq:path} gives $B_i=g^2$ as well. But
$Q_k=P_kz=1$. Distinct group elements are incomparable in
the flat additive order, so $Q_k\preceq\mathbf{E}$ fails.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:substitution}
Let $\mathcal T\models r\preceq\mathbf{s}$, where $r\in X_c^+$ and
$\mathbf{s}$ is a commutative term. Suppose that a substitution
$\varphi$ and a word $h\in X_c^*$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:instance}
h\varphi(\mathbf{s})\subseteq\mathbf{F}_k,
\qquad Q_k\in h\varphi(r).
\end{equation}
Then $|\operatorname{c}(\mathbf{s})|\geq k$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Every word of $h\varphi(r)$ is absorbed by $\mathbf{F}_k$ in
$\mathcal T$, because $r\preceq\mathbf{s}$ and
$h\varphi(\mathbf{s})\subseteq\mathbf{F}_k$. Therefore
Lemma~\ref{lem:absorbed} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:all-images}
h\varphi(r)\subseteq\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k.
\end{equation}
Every letter has degree at most $2$ in the words on the right.
For each variable $v\in\operatorname{c}(r)$, choosing the same
nonempty image word at all its occurrences shows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source-degree}
m(v,r)\leq2.
\end{equation}
Also, $\operatorname{c}(r)\subseteq\operatorname{c}(\mathbf{s})$. Otherwise a variable
occurring only in $r$ could be assigned $\infty$ in $G$, with
all other variables of $r$ and $\mathbf{s}$ assigned $1$, contradicting
$r\preceq\mathbf{s}$.

We claim that if $m(v,r)=2$, then $\varphi(v)$ consists
of a single commutative word. By~\eqref{eq:all-images}, every
word in $\varphi(v)$ is supported on the $x$-variables,
the $y$-variables, and $z$, and every letter has degree at most
$1$ in it: choosing that word at both occurrences of $v$ gives
the asserted bound. Choose an occurrence of $v$ in a word of
$\mathbf{s}$ and fix all other choices in an expansion. Replacing
the choice at this occurrence by $w,w'\in\varphi(v)$ gives
two words in $\mathbf{F}_k$ after multiplication by $h$.
Every $x$- and $y$-degree in $\mathbf{F}_k$ is even. Thus the
corresponding degrees of $w$ and $w'$ differ by an even number.
They are each $0$ or $1$, so they are equal.

If the $z$-degrees of $w$ and $w'$ differed, they would differ
by exactly $1$. The two resulting words of $\mathbf{F}_k$ would
then differ only by one occurrence of $z$. No such pair exists:
the only distinct words in $\mathbf{F}_k$ with the same
$x$- and $y$-degrees are $P_k$ and $R_k$, whose $z$-degrees
differ by $2$. Therefore the $z$-degrees also agree and
$w=w'$, proving the claim.

Take an expansion in~\eqref{eq:instance} producing $Q_k$.
Each variable occurring once in $r$ has one selected
image word, and the preceding claim ensures that a variable
occurring twice has the same word selected at both occurrences.
Choose those images and choose arbitrary image words for the
remaining variables. This defines a word substitution
$\psi$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coherent}
h\psi(r)=Q_k,
\qquad h\psi(\mathbf{s})\subseteq\mathbf{F}_k.
\end{equation}
The identity $r\preceq\mathbf{s}$ is valid in $G$, so
$G\models Q_k\preceq h\psi(\mathbf{s})$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:locals-required}, every $B_i$ occurs in
$h\psi(\mathbf{s})$. Hence $h$ divides all the $B_i$ and
contains no $x$-variable.

For each $i$, choose a word $s_i\in\mathbf{s}$ such that
$h\psi(s_i)=B_i$. Some variable $v_i$ of $s_i$ has an image
$\psi(v_i)$ containing $x_i$. This image divides $B_i$.
For $j\ne i$ the word $B_j$ contains no $x_i$, so the same
variable cannot be used as $v_j$. The variables
$v_1,\ldots,v_k$ are distinct members of $\operatorname{c}(\mathbf{s})$.
Thus $|\operatorname{c}(\mathbf{s})|\geq k$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:criterion}
Let $\mathcal W$ be an ai-semiring variety containing $H^0$
and $G$. If $\mathcal W$ satisfies $\sigma_k$ for infinitely
many $k\in\mathcal N$, then $\mathcal W$ has no equational
basis whose identities use a uniformly bounded number of
variables. In particular, $\mathcal W$ is nonfinitely based.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Suppose that $\Sigma$ is such a basis, with variable bound $m$.
Choose $k\in\mathcal N$ with $k>m$ and
$\mathcal W\models\sigma_k$. There is a deduction of
$\sigma_k$ from $\Sigma$.

Adjoin the identity $xy\approx yx$ to the deduction system
and work with commutative terms. A deduction remains
valid in this enlarged system, and all its identities hold in
$\mathcal T$, because $\mathcal T\subseteq\mathcal W$ and $\mathcal T\subseteq\mathsf{CAI}$.
Replace each basis identity by word absorption inequalities.
The resulting set still has variable bound $m$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:deduction}, a sequence of contextual
substitution instances takes $\mathbf{F}_k$ to $\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k$.
All intermediate terms are equivalent to $\mathbf{F}_k$ in
$\mathcal T$. Lemma~\ref{lem:class} therefore leaves only the two
possible terms $\mathbf{F}_k$ and $\mathbf{F}_k+Q_k$.

Consider the first step changing the former into the latter.
A contraction in~\eqref{eq:deduction} introduces no word,
so this step expands an inequality $r\preceq\mathbf{s}$:
\[
\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{K}\varphi(\mathbf{s})
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{K}\varphi(\mathbf{s})+\mathbf{K}\varphi(r).
\]
Choose a word $h$ of the multiplicative context that produces
$Q_k$; if the context is absent, take $h=\varepsilon$. The left side
gives $h\varphi(\mathbf{s})\subseteq\mathbf{F}_k$, while
$Q_k\in h\varphi(r)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:substitution} implies
$|\operatorname{c}(\mathbf{s})|\geq k>m$, a contradiction.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:cyclic-codimension}
If a semigroup has a cyclic subsemigroup with at most one element
outside it, then its set of squares is a subsemigroup.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Only $S=\langle a\rangle\cup\{x\}$ needs consideration.
Every square is either an even power of $a$ or $x^2$.
Products of two even powers are squares, as is $(x^2)^2$.
For a mixed product, if $xa=x$, then $x^2a^{2i}=x^2$.
Otherwise $xa=a^j$ for some $j\geq1$, and
\[
 x^2a^{2i}=x(xa^{2i})
 =xa^{j+2i-1}=a^{2j+2i-2}
 \qquad(i\geq1).
\]
This is again a square. The argument for $a^{2i}x^2$ is the
left--right dual.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:orthodox-small}
Let $S$ be a semigroup of order at most five. If $E(S)$ is a
subsemigroup, then $Q(S)$ is a subsemigroup.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
If a cyclic subsemigroup has at least four elements, apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:cyclic-codimension}. We may therefore suppose that
every cyclic subsemigroup has order at most three. If every square
is idempotent, there is nothing to prove. A nonidempotent square
must then come from one of the following two cyclic subsemigroups:
\[
 C_3,\qquad N_3=\{a,b,e\},\quad a^2=b,\quad a^3=a^4=e.
\]
This follows immediately by considering the index and period of
a cyclic semigroup of order at most three.

First suppose that $S$ contains a group $H\cong C_3$, with identity
$e$. All elements of $H$ are squares. Unless exactly one element
of $S\setminus H$ is a square, $Q(S)$ is either $H$ or $S$.
Write $S\setminus H=\{c,d\}$ in the remaining case, with
$c\in Q(S)$ and $d\notin Q(S)$; a missing second element would
already be covered by Lemma~\ref{lem:cyclic-codimension}.
If $c$ is idempotent, closure of $E(S)$ gives
$ec,ce\in\{e,c\}$. If $ec=e$, then $gc=g$ for every $g\in H$.
If $ec=c$, the group $H$ acts on $c$ by left multiplication.
Its orbit is disjoint from $H$ and has at most two elements, so
it has one element; thus $gc=c$. The same argument on the right
shows that all mixed products lie in $H\cup\{c\}$.
If $c$ is not idempotent, its square root must be $d$.
The cyclic subsemigroup of $d$ cannot be a group of order three,
since $d$ is not a square. Consequently
$d^2=c$ and $d^3=d^4=e$, giving $ec=ce=e$ and again
$gc=cg=g$. Thus $Q(S)$ is closed in this case.

It remains to consider $N_3\subseteq S$. The element $a$ is not
a square: if $a=x^2$, a cyclic subsemigroup of order at most
three containing $x$ would either make $a$ a group element or
make $a^2$ idempotent. Both are impossible in $N_3$.
If there is at most one element outside $N_3$, use
Lemma~\ref{lem:cyclic-codimension}. Otherwise write the two
remaining elements as $c,d$. If neither is a square, then
$Q(S)=\{b,e\}$ is closed.

Suppose that only $c$ is a square. If $c$ is nonidempotent, its
root is $d$, and the same cyclic-semigroup argument gives
$d^2=c$ and $d^3=d^4=e$. In this case $e$ is a zero of $S$,
and every element has a power equal to $e$.
The products $bc$ and $cb$ cannot equal $a$ or $d$.
For example, $bc=a$ gives $a=a(ac)$, and $bc=d$ gives
$d=(bd)d$; iterating the indicated multiplier gives $a=e$ or
$d=e$. The two reversed products are treated in the same way.
Hence $Q(S)=\{b,c,e\}$ is closed.

If instead $c$ is idempotent, then
$ce,ec\in E(S)=\{c,e\}$. We show that $cb\in Q(S)$.
If $cb=a$, idempotence of $c$ gives $ca=a$, whence
$cb=ca^2=(ca)a=b$, a contradiction.
If $cb=d$, put $v=ca$. Then $va=d$.
The possibilities $v=a,b,e$ give $va=b,e,e$, respectively.
The possibility $v=c$ would give $cb=c$.
Thus $v=d$ and $da=d$. It follows that
\[
 d=da^3=de=cbe=ce\in E(S),
\]
contrary to $d\notin Q(S)$. The proof for $bc$ is dual.

Finally, suppose that both $c$ and $d$ are squares. If either
is nonidempotent, its root must be the other, and these two
elements together with $e$ form a group of order three.
Indeed, a cyclic $N_3$ would make its generator nonsquare by
the argument already used for $a$.
Since $be=eb=e$, multiplication of $b$ by an element of this
group gives that group element. Closure follows.
If $c,d$ are both idempotent, closure of $E(S)$ handles all
products except those involving $b$. For an idempotent $h$,
$bh=a$ would imply $ah=a$ and hence
$bh=a^2h=a(ah)=b$. Similarly $hb\ne a$.
As $a$ is the only nonsquare, this completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:nonsquare-products}
Let $S$ be an ai-semiring of order at most five. If
$b\in K(S)\setminus Q(S)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:right-stable}
 bz=bz^2\qquad\text{for every }z\in S.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Since $Q(S)$ is not a subsemigroup, Lemma~\ref{lem:orthodox-small}
provides idempotents $e,f$ for which $v=ef$ is not idempotent.
The three elements $e,f,v$ are distinct. Put
\[
 w=fe,\qquad r=efe,\qquad s=fef.
\]
None of $w,r,s$ belongs to $\{e,f,v\}$. For $w$ this follows
because $fe=e,f$, or $ef$ would imply $(ef)^2=ef$.
For $r$, equality to $e$ or $v$ gives the same consequence,
whereas $r=f$ and $er=r$ would give $ef=f$.
The argument for $s$ is dual. There are at most two available
elements, so at least one of $w=r$, $w=s$, or $r=s$ holds.

Suppose first that $w=r$. Associativity gives
\[
 w^2=w,\quad s=wf,\quad s^2=s,\quad v^2=v^3=s.
\]
If $s\ne w$, these five distinct elements exhaust $S$, all
except $v$ are idempotent, and the displayed equations prove
\eqref{eq:right-stable} for its only nonsquare $v$.
The case $w=s\ne r$ is dual: again all elements except $v$
are idempotent and $v^2=v^3=r$, which is enough for
\eqref{eq:right-stable} on the right as well.

If $r=s=h\ne w$, the five elements are $e,f,v,w,h$.
Associativity gives
\[
 h^2=h,\qquad v^2=w^2=vw=wv=h,
\]
and $h$ absorbs multiplication by each of these five elements.
The only possible nonsquares are $v,w$. Multiplication by
an idempotent satisfies~\eqref{eq:right-stable} automatically;
multiplication by $v$ or $w$ gives $h$. This proves the claim.

The remaining case has $w=r=s=o$. Then
$T=\{e,f,v,o\}$ is a subsemigroup with table
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
 &e&f&v&o\\\hline
e&e&v&v&o\\
f&o&f&o&o\\
v&o&v&o&o\\
o&o&o&o&o
\end{array}
\]
and its only nonsquare is $v$. If there is an extra element
$z$, any nonsquare in $K(S)$ is still $v$: if $z$ is not a
square then all squares, and hence their products, lie in $T$;
if $z$ is a square it cannot be the element under consideration.
It remains to prove $vz=vz^2$.

The values $vz=v,o$ give the result immediately; for the latter,
$oz=v^2z=v(vz)=o$. The value $vz=f$ is impossible because
$e(vz)=vz$ but $ef=v\ne f$.
If $vz=e$, the equations
\[
 v(zf)=v,\qquad v(ze)=e
\]
and the displayed table force $zf=f$ and $ze=z$. Hence
$zv=zef=f$, and $(ez)v=e(zv)=v$ forces $ez=e$.
Therefore $vz^2=ez=e=vz$.

Finally suppose that $vz=z$. Then $oz=z$, and the fixed
elements of left multiplication by $v$ are exactly $o,z$.
Since $v z^2=z^2$, we have $z^2\in\{o,z\}$.
If $z^2=o$, the equations $oz=z$ and $z^2=o$ show that
$o,z$ are incomparable: multiplying either proposed comparison
on the right by $z$ gives its reverse.
But $o+z$ must also be fixed by left multiplication by $v$,
so it would equal $o$ or $z$, a contradiction.
Thus $z^2=z$, completing the proof.

<!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{lem:three-exceptions}
Let $S$ be an ai-semiring of order at most five, and let $b$
be a square. If, for some $z\in S$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-variable-failure}
 bz\not\leq b+bz^2+z,
\end{equation}
then $S$ is $E_1$, $E_2$, or $G$, with the following failing
elements: the displayed $b,z$ in $E_1,E_2$, or
$b=z=g^2$ in $G$. In particular, except in $G$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-square}
 ubz\leq ub+ubz^2+uz\qquad(u,z\in S,\ b\in Q(S)).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-12 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
Fix a failure, and put
\[
 q=bz,\quad s=bz^2,\quad f=b+s+z,\quad t=f+q.
\]
If $b\leq q$, right multiplication by $z$ gives $q\leq s$,
a contradiction. Thus $b\not\leq q$. Since $b\leq f$ and
$q\not\leq f$, the elements $q,f$ are incomparable and
$q,f,t$ are distinct. Also $b,q$ are incomparable.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{Idempotent $b$.}
Suppose $b^2=b$. Neither $f=b$ nor $z=b$ is possible.
If $f=z$, left multiplication by $b$ gives
$q=bf=b+s+q$, contradicting $b\not\leq q$.
Thus $b,z,f,q,t$ are five distinct elements and exhaust $S$.
Necessarily $b+q=t$. Left multiplication by $b$ fixes $b,q,t$,
sends $z$ to $q$, and sends $f$ to
$b+s+q=t$. It fixes $s$, and $s\leq f$, so $s=b$.
The equation $bz^2=b$ then forces $z^2=b$, because $b$ is
the only preimage of $b$ under that left multiplication.
Hence $z^3=q$, $z^4=b$, and $f=b+z$.
The join $z+q$ is $q$ or $t$. The latter possibility would
give $(z+q)z=b$ while $tz=(b+q)z=q+b=t$.
Thus $z<q$, and all of~\eqref{eq:exception-order} follows.
Distributing the products of $f=b+z$ and $t=b+q$ now gives
exactly $E_1$.

For the remaining cases suppose that $b=a^2$ is not idempotent.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{Case 1: $b,z,f$ are distinct.}
They exhaust $S$ together with $q,t$, and $b+q=t$.
The root $a$ cannot be $f$ or $t$: either choice dominates
both $b,z$, giving $q=bz\leq a^2=b$.
Nor is $a=b$. If $a=q$, then $q=q^2z$, so $q$ lies in a
cyclic group $H$ and $b=q^2\in H$.
Both $f,t$ are outside $H$, since they strictly dominate $b$.
If $z\notin H$, the size bound gives $|H|\leq2$, making
$b$ idempotent. If $z\in H$, then $z=q^{-1}$; group orders
two and three would give $z=q$ and $z=b$, respectively,
and larger orders require at least six elements including
$f,t$. All possibilities are excluded.
Consequently $a=z$, so $b=z^2$, $q=z^3$, and
$s=z^4\in\{z,b,f\}$.
The value $s=z$ gives a group $C_3$, where the join of $z,b$
contains $q$. The value $s=b$ makes $b$ idempotent.
Thus $s=f$.
Since $z,b\leq f$, multiplication on the right by $z$ gives
$z^5=fz\geq b,q$, whence $z^5=t$ and $z^6=t$.
Neither $z\leq b$ nor $b\leq z$ is possible, since right
multiplication by $z$ would give $b\leq q$ or $q\leq b$.
Thus $z+b=f$. If $z+q=t$, multiplication by $z$ would give
$t=tz=b+f=f$. Therefore $z+q=q$, yielding exactly $E_2$.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{Case 2: $f=z\ne b$.}
Here $b<z$, and $a\notin\{b,z,t\}$.
If $a=q$, then~\eqref{eq:group-element} applies.
The comparison $b\leq z$ gives $q^4=b^2\leq bz=q$.
Comparability within the cyclic group forces $q^4=q$.
Its order is three, and $s=qz=bq$ is its identity.
The $C_3$ join property yields $q\leq b+s\leq f$, a
contradiction.
Otherwise $S=\{a,b,z,q,t\}$ with five distinct elements.
Since $a^4=b^2\leq q$, its only possible values are $a,q$.
If $a^4=a$, the group $\{a,a^2,a^3\}\cong C_3$ has no
place for its third element: $z,t$ dominate $b$, while
$q$ dominates $a$.
If $a^4=q$, the value $a^3$ cannot be $a$ or $b$, and
cannot be $z$ or $t$ either: these dominate $b$, which would
give $q=a^4\geq a^3$. Thus $a^3=q$ and all later powers
equal $q$. But then $s=qz=b^2z=bq=q$, again a contradiction.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{Case 3: $z=b\ne f$.}
We have $q=b^2$ and $f=b+b^3$.
The root $a$ cannot dominate $b$, because this would give
$b=a^2\geq b^2=q$. Thus $a\notin\{b,f,t\}$.
If $a=q$, then $b^4=b$, yielding a group $C_3$ whose join
$b+b^3$ contains $q=b^2$, a contradiction.
Otherwise $S=\{a,b,q,f,t\}$. None of these five elements
can be $a^3$: the values $a,b$ contradict $a^4=q$; the
value $q$ would make all later powers equal $q$ and hence
give $b^3=q\leq f$; and the values $f,t$ dominate $b$,
forcing $q=a^4\geq a^3\geq b$.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{Case 4: $f=b$.}
Here $z,s\leq b$ and $q\leq b^2$.
The root $a$ is not $b$ or $t$, and cannot be $z$,
since $z\leq z^2$ would give $b=z^2\leq z^3=q$.
If $a=q$, then $q=q^2z$ gives a cyclic group, and
$q\leq b^2=q^4$ forces $q=q^4$. Its order is three;
then $s=qz=bq$ is its identity, contradicting $s\leq b$.
Thus $a\notin\{b,q,t,z\}$.

First suppose $b^2=q$. We obtain
$a^4=q$ and $a^6=b^3=qz=s\leq b$.
The element $a^3$ differs from $a,b,q,t$: the first two
values contradict $a^4=q$, the third would give $a^6=q$,
and the fourth would imply $q=a^4\geq a^3=t$.
Hence $S=\{a,b,a^3,q,t\}$.
The value $a^5=b$ would give $a^3=a^6\leq b$ and then
$q\leq a^3\leq b$.
The values $a^5=a^3,q$ give $a^6=q$.
The value $a^5=t$ gives
$a^6=at=a^3+a^5\geq q$.
All contradict $q\not\leq b$. Therefore $a^5=a$,
and the four powers of $a$ form $C_4$. Thus $S=G$;
its flat order forces $z=b=a^2$.

Finally suppose $b^2\ne q$. Then $z\ne b$, so
$S=\{a,z,b,q,t\}$. As $b^2\geq q$, its only possible
values are $a,t$. If $b^2=a$, then $a^4=a$ gives a group
$C_3$, whose third element cannot be $z,t,q$: they satisfy
$z\leq b$, $b\leq t$, and $q\leq a$, respectively.
Thus $b^2=t$.
The only possible values of $a^3$ are $q,t$:
$a,b$ would give $a^4=b$, and $z\leq b$ would give
$t=a^4\leq a^3=z\leq b$.
Put $v=az$, so $av=q$.
If $a^3=q$, then $v\leq q$, leaving only $v=a,q,z$;
these give $av=b,t,z$, respectively, not $q$.
If $a^3=t$, then $at=t$.
The equation $av=q$ excludes $v=a,b,t$; the value $v=z$
would also give $av=z$. Consequently $v=q$ and $aq=q$.
Now $as=a(qz)=s$, but the only fixed elements of left
multiplication by $a$ are $q,t$, neither of which lies
below $b$. This contradicts $s\leq b$ and finishes the
classification.

To obtain~\eqref{eq:local-square}, multiply the two-variable
inequality on the left by $u$ whenever it holds.
In its failing instances in $E_1,E_2$, both $uf$ and $ut$
equal $t$ for every $u$. Since $q\leq t$, left
multiplication still gives $uq\leq uf$.
Only $G$ remains exceptional.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:six}
For every $n\geq6$, the variety $\mathcal A_n$ contains an
inherently nonfinitely based $36$-element ai-semiring.
Consequently $\mathcal A_n$ and $\mathcal V_n$ are nonfinitely based;
neither is contained in a locally finite finitely based
variety of the same signature.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Put
\[
M=\overline{\mathcal A_2^1}\times\mathcal B_2^1.
\]
The two factors belong to $\mathcal A_6$, so $M\in\mathcal A_6$.
If $M\models Z_r\preceq u$, projection onto the first
factor and maximality force $u=Z_r$. If
$M\models u\preceq Z_r$, projection onto the second factor
and minimality give the same conclusion. Every Zimin word
is therefore isolated in $M$. As $M$ is finite, it generates
a locally finite variety; the cited criterion makes $M$
inherently nonfinitely based.

to every $\mathcal A_n$ with $n\geq6$, and hence so does $M$.
The inclusion $\mathcal A_n\subseteq\mathcal V_n$ places $M$ in $\mathcal V_n$

finitely based variety containing either one would contain
$M$, contrary to its inherent nonfinite basability.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:intersection}
For every positive integer $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intersection}
\mathcal V_n\cap\mathsf{AI}=\mathcal A_n.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
Let $N=n!$, and let $S$ be an $n$-element semiring with
commutative addition. Every cyclic subsemigroup of $(S,+)$
has index and period at most $n$. Since $N\geq n$ and $N$
is divisible by every possible period,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multiple}
2Ns=Ns=N^2s\qquad(s\in S).
\end{equation}
For $n=1$ these equalities hold trivially.

The map $e:S\to S$ given by $e(s)=Ns$ satisfies
\[
e(a+b)=e(a)+e(b),
\qquad e(a)e(b)=N^2(ab)=N(ab)=e(ab).
\]
Here additive commutativity and distributivity justify the
expansion of the products and sums, and~\eqref{eq:multiple}
gives the middle equality. Thus $e$ is an idempotent
endomorphism whose image is an ai-semiring of order at most
$n$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:padding}, $e(S)\in\mathcal A_n$.

If an identity $u\approx v$ holds in $\mathcal A_n$, then for
every valuation $\theta$ in $S$, the homomorphism property
of $e$ gives
\[
N u(\theta)=e(u(\theta))
=u(e\circ\theta)=v(e\circ\theta)
=e(v(\theta))=N v(\theta).
\]
Hence $Nu\approx Nv$ holds in every generator of $\mathcal V_n$,
and therefore in $\mathcal V_n$. In an ai-semiring this identity
reduces to $u\approx v$. Thus every member of
$\mathcal V_n\cap\mathsf{AI}$ satisfies all identities of $\mathcal A_n$, proving
one inclusion in~\eqref{eq:intersection}. The other is immediate.
