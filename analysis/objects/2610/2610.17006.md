The quantity $m(X,Y)$ is independent of the representative $x$ of $X$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
If $x'=R_kx$, then $R_k(x^{(i)})=(R_kx)^{(i+k)}$, and $i\mapsto i+k$ is a bijection.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
For every necklace $X$,

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Partition the sensitive coordinates according to the necklace reached by their flip.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
For $i,j\in\mathbb Z_n$, $[x^{(i)}]=[x^{(j)}]$ iff for some $k$, either (i) $R_kx=x$ and $j=i+k$, or (ii) $|D_k(x)|=2$ and $D_k(x)=\{j,i+k\}$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Suppose first that $[x^{(i)}]=[x^{(j)}]$.  By the definition of a necklace, there is some $k\in\mathbb Z_n$ such that
\[
R_k(x^{(i)})=x^{(j)}.
\]
Rotation carries the flip at coordinate $i$ to a flip at coordinate $i+k$; that is,
\[
R_k(x^{(i)})=(R_kx)^{(i+k)}.
\]
Hence
\[
(R_kx)^{(i+k)}=x^{(j)},
\]
and, after flipping coordinate $i+k$ on both sides,
\[
R_kx=(x^{(j)})^{(i+k)}.
\]
There are now two cases.

If $j=i+k$, the two flips on the right occur at the same coordinate and therefore cancel.  Thus
\[
R_kx=x,
\]
so $R_k$ stabilizes $x$, and $j=i+k$.  This is alternative~(i).

If $j\ne i+k$, then the two flips occur at distinct coordinates.  Consequently $(x^{(j)})^{(i+k)}$ differs from $x$ exactly at the two coordinates $j$ and $i+k$.  Therefore
\[
D_k(x)=\{r:x_r\ne(R_kx)_r\}=\{j,i+k\},
\]
so $|D_k(x)|=2$.  This is alternative~(ii).

Conversely, suppose that alternative~(i) holds.  Then $R_kx=x$ and $j=i+k$, so
\[
R_k(x^{(i)})=(R_kx)^{(i+k)}=x^{(j)}.
\]
Hence $[x^{(i)}]=[x^{(j)}]$.

Finally, suppose that alternative~(ii) holds.  Since
\[
D_k(x)=\{j,i+k\},
\]
the word $R_kx$ is obtained from $x$ by flipping exactly those two coordinates; equivalently,
\[
R_kx=(x^{(j)})^{(i+k)}.
\]
Flipping coordinate $i+k$ gives
\[
R_k(x^{(i)})=(R_kx)^{(i+k)}=x^{(j)},
\]
and again $[x^{(i)}]=[x^{(j)}]$.  Thus the two stated alternatives are both necessary and sufficient.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
Fix $k\ne0$, put $d=\gcd(n,k)$ and $L=n/d$. The permutation $r\mapsto r+k$ has $d$ cycles of length $L$. Then $|D_k(x)|=2$ iff $x$ is constant on $d-1$ cycles and, on the remaining cycle, has one nonempty proper consecutive arc of ones and one of zeros; equivalently its cyclic pattern is $1^a0^{L-a}$, $1\le a\le L-1$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
This is the prescribed-shift one-active-cycle structure appearing in~\cite{Makhynko}; we include the argument for completeness.

The permutation $r\mapsto r+k$ decomposes $\mathbb Z_n$ into $d=\gcd(n,k)$ disjoint cycles, each of length $L=n/d$.  Fix one such cycle and write it in cyclic order as
\[
C=(r,r+k,r+2k,\ldots,r+(L-1)k),
\]
where all indices are taken modulo $n$.  By definition,
\[
r+tk\in D_k(x)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x_{r+tk}\ne x_{r+(t+1)k},
\]
with $t$ interpreted modulo $L$.  Hence the number of elements of $D_k(x)$ lying on $C$ is exactly the number of transitions between $0$ and $1$ in the cyclic binary word
\[
x_r,x_{r+k},x_{r+2k},\ldots,x_{r+(L-1)k}.
\]

A cyclic binary word has an even number of transitions.  Indeed, whenever the value changes from $0$ to $1$, it must later change from $1$ back to $0$ before returning to the starting position.  Thus a cycle contributes either $0$ or at least $2$ elements to $D_k(x)$.  Moreover, it contributes $0$ precisely when $x$ is constant on that cycle.

Suppose now that $|D_k(x)|=2$.  Since the cycles are disjoint and every cycle contributes an even nonnegative number of disagreements, exactly one cycle contributes $2$, while each of the remaining $d-1$ cycles contributes $0$.  Therefore $x$ is constant on those $d-1$ cycles.  On the unique active cycle there are exactly two transitions.  Starting immediately after one of them, all the $1$'s occur consecutively until the second transition, and all the remaining entries are $0$'s.  Since both transitions occur, both symbols appear.  Consequently, after a cyclic choice of starting point, the pattern on this cycle is
\[
1^a0^{L-a}
\qquad\text{for some }1\le a\le L-1.
\]
Equivalently, the $1$'s form one nonempty proper consecutive arc of the cycle and the $0$'s form the complementary arc.

Conversely, suppose that $x$ is constant on $d-1$ cycles and that on the remaining cycle its cyclic pattern is $1^a0^{L-a}$ with $1\le a\le L-1$.  Each constant cycle contributes no element to $D_k(x)$, while the active cycle has exactly two transitions, one from $1$ to $0$ and one from $0$ to $1$.  Hence that cycle contributes exactly two elements to $D_k(x)$, and therefore $|D_k(x)|=2$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
If $X=[x]$ and $Y=[x^{(i)}]$, then
\[\boxed{m(X,Y)=\frac{h_x+q_i(x)}{h_{x^{(i)}}}.}\]
Hence $h_{x^{(i)}}\mid h_x+q_i(x)$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Set $y=x^{(i)}$, so that $Y=[y]$, and consider
\[
\mathcal P_i=\{(R_k,j)\in \langle R_1\rangle\times\mathbb Z_n:
R_k(x^{(i)})=x^{(j)}\}.
\]
We count $|\mathcal P_i|$ in two ways.

First fix $j$.  There exists a rotation $R_k$ with
$R_k(x^{(i)})=x^{(j)}$ if and only if $[x^{(j)}]=[x^{(i)}]=Y$.
By the definition of $m(X,Y)$, there are exactly $m(X,Y)$ such coordinates
$j$.  For each such $j$, choose one rotation $R_{k_0}$ satisfying
$R_{k_0}y=x^{(j)}$.  Then
\[
R_ky=x^{(j)}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
R_{k_0}^{-1}R_ky=y.
\]
Thus the rotations carrying $y$ to $x^{(j)}$ form a coset of
$H_y=\operatorname{Stab}(y)$ and hence there are exactly $h_y=h_{x^{(i)}}$ of them.
Consequently,
\[
|\mathcal P_i|=m(X,Y)h_{x^{(i)}}.
\]

We now count by fixing the rotation $R_k$.  Since rotation carries a flip
at $i$ to a flip at $i+k$,
\[
R_k(x^{(i)})=(R_kx)^{(i+k)}.
\]
Hence a coordinate $j$ with $R_k(x^{(i)})=x^{(j)}$ exists precisely when
\[
(R_kx)^{(i+k)}=x^{(j)}.
\]
If $R_kx=x$, then this equality holds with the unique choice $j=i+k$.
Therefore every stabilizing rotation contributes exactly one pair to
$\mathcal P_i$, for a total contribution of $h_x$.

Suppose next that $R_kx\ne x$.  The flip-equivalence criterion from
Theorem~3.1 shows that the above equality can hold only when
\[
|D_k(x)|=2
\quad\text{and}\quad
i+k\in D_k(x).
\]
Indeed, if $D_k(x)=\{i+k,j\}$, then flipping $i+k$ in $R_kx$ removes one
of the two disagreements with $x$, and flipping $j$ in $x$ removes the
other, giving
\[
(R_kx)^{(i+k)}=x^{(j)}.
\]
Conversely, if $(R_kx)^{(i+k)}=x^{(j)}$ and $R_kx\ne x$, then $R_kx$ and
$x$ can differ only at the two coordinates $i+k$ and $j$; hence
$D_k(x)=\{i+k,j\}$ and $|D_k(x)|=2$.  Thus the nonstabilizing rotations
that contribute to $\mathcal P_i$ are exactly those counted by $q_i(x)$,
and each contributes exactly one pair.  Therefore
\[
|\mathcal P_i|=h_x+q_i(x).
\]

Equating the two counts gives
\[
m(X,Y)h_{x^{(i)}}=h_x+q_i(x),
\]
and hence
\[
m(X,Y)=\frac{h_x+q_i(x)}{h_{x^{(i)}}}.
\]
Since $m(X,Y)$ is an integer, it follows immediately that
$h_{x^{(i)}}\mid h_x+q_i(x)$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
For adjacent necklaces $X$ and $Y$,
\[
|X|m(X,Y)=|Y|m(Y,X).
\]
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $E(X,Y)$ be the set of edges of the $n$-dimensional hypercube having one endpoint in the orbit $X$ and the other endpoint in the orbit $Y$.  We count $|E(X,Y)|$ in two ways.

First fix a word $x\in X$.  By definition, $m(X,Y)$ is the number of coordinates $i$ for which the one-coordinate flip $x^{(i)}$ belongs to $Y$.  This number is independent of the chosen representative of $X$.  Indeed, if $x'=R_kx$, then rotation commutes with a coordinate flip in the sense that
\[
(R_kx)^{(i+k)}=R_k(x^{(i)}),
\]
with indices taken modulo $n$.  Since $Y$ is itself a rotation orbit, $x^{(i)}\in Y$ if and only if $(R_kx)^{(i+k)}\in Y$.  Thus every vertex of $X$ is incident with exactly $m(X,Y)$ edges whose other endpoint lies in $Y$.

Consequently, counting the edges of $E(X,Y)$ from their endpoints in $X$ gives
\[
|E(X,Y)|=|X|\,m(X,Y).
\]

The same argument with the roles of $X$ and $Y$ reversed shows that every vertex of $Y$ is incident with exactly $m(Y,X)$ edges whose other endpoint lies in $X$.  Hence
\[
|E(X,Y)|=|Y|\,m(Y,X).
\]

Equating the two expressions for the same set of hypercube edges yields
\[
|X|m(X,Y)=|Y|m(Y,X),
\]
as claimed.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
Let $I=\{0,1,\ldots,w-1\}\subseteq\mathbb Z_p$, $2\le w\le p-2$. If $I=\{b,b+r,\ldots,b+(w-1)r\}$ with $r\ne0$, then $r=\pm1\pmod p$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Put
\[
J:=\{b,b+r,\ldots,b+(w-1)r\}\subseteq\mathbb Z_p.
\]
By hypothesis, $J=I$, where $I=\{0,1,\ldots,w-1\}$.  In the
$r$-arithmetic ordering of $J$, translation by $r$ sends
\[
b,b+r,\ldots,b+(w-2)r
\]
to the next $w-1$ elements
\[
b+r,b+2r,\ldots,b+(w-1)r.
\]
The only additional point of $J+r$ is $b+wr$, which does not lie in
$J$: otherwise $wr\equiv jr\pmod p$ for some $0\le j\le w-1$,
contradicting $r\ne0$ and $w<p$.
Hence
\[
|J\cap(J+r)|=w-1.
\]
Since $J=I$, this gives
\[
|I\cap(I+r)|=w-1.
\]
We now use the special form $I=\{0,1,\ldots,w-1\}$ to determine $r$.
Choose the integer representative $1\le r\le p-1$.

First suppose $1\le r\le p-w$.  Then the translate $I+r$ does not
wrap around modulo $p$, so
\[
I+r=\{r,r+1,\ldots,r+w-1\}.
\]
If $r\ge w$, then $I\cap(I+r)=\varnothing$, contradicting
$|I\cap(I+r)|=w-1$.  Thus $1\le r<w$, and in this case
\[
|I\cap(I+r)|=w-r.
\]
Therefore $w-r=w-1$, and hence $r=1$.

It remains to consider $p-w<r\le p-1$.  Set $s=p-r$, so
$1\le s<w$ and $r\equiv -s\pmod p$.  Thus $I+r=I-s$ in $\mathbb Z_p$.
The intersection may have two contributions: the unwrapped portion
contributes $w-s$ elements, and the wrapped portion contributes
$\max\{0,w+s-p\}$ elements.  Thus
\[
|I\cap(I+r)|=|I\cap(I-s)|
=(w-s)+\max\{0,w+s-p\}.
\]
If $w+s\le p$, the equality $|I\cap(I+r)|=w-1$ yields
$w-s=w-1$, hence $s=1$.  If $w+s>p$, the equality becomes
$2w-p=w-1$, which forces $w=p-1$, contrary to
$w\le p-2$.  Therefore only $s=1$ is possible, giving
$r=p-1\equiv-1\pmod p$.

Consequently $r\equiv\pm1\pmod p$, as claimed.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:prime-interval-uniqueness}
If $2\le|S|=w\le p-2$ and $S$ has arithmetic-progression representations with nonzero steps $k$ and $\ell$, then $\ell=\pm k$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Write the two representations as
\[
S=\{a,a+k,\ldots,a+(w-1)k\}
 =\{b,b+\ell,\ldots,b+(w-1)\ell\}
 \subseteq \mathbb Z_p.
\]
Since $k\ne0$ and $p$ is prime, $k$ is invertible modulo $p$.  Apply the
invertible affine map
\[
\phi(z)=k^{-1}(z-a).
\]
The first representation is carried to the standard interval
\[
I=\{0,1,\ldots,w-1\}.
\]
The second representation is carried to
\[
I=\{c,c+r,\ldots,c+(w-1)r\},
\qquad
c=k^{-1}(b-a),\quad r=k^{-1}\ell.
\]
Thus the same set $I$ is an arithmetic progression of length $w$ with
nonzero step $r$.  By the preceding lemma, $r\equiv\pm1\pmod p$.
Therefore
\[
k^{-1}\ell\equiv\pm1\pmod p,
\]
and multiplying by $k$ gives $\ell\equiv\pm k\pmod p$, as claimed.
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
If $2\le\operatorname{wt}(x)\le p-2$, then $q_i(x)\le1$ for every $i$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let $S=\operatorname{supp}(x)$ and put $w=|S|=\operatorname{wt}(x)$.  Recall that $q_i(x)$ counts the
nonzero rotations $R_k$ for which
\[
|D_k(x)|=2\qquad\text{and}\qquad i+k\in D_k(x).
\]
Suppose first that a nonzero step $k$ contributes to $q_i(x)$.  By the
distance-two rotation structure, the support of $x$ is a cyclic arithmetic
interval with step $k$.  Thus, for a suitable $a\in\mathbb Z_p$,
\[
S=\{a,a+k,\ldots,a+(w-1)k\}.
\]
Under translation by $k$, all interior points of this progression remain in
$S$; the only membership changes occur at its two ends.  Consequently
\[
D_k(x)=\{a,a+wk\}.
\]
Since $k$ contributes to $q_i(x)$ precisely when $i+k\in D_k(x)$, we must
have
\[
i+k=a\quad\text{or}\quad i+k=a+wk.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
i\in\{a-k,\,a+(w-1)k\}. \tag{5.1}
\]

Now suppose that a second nonzero step $\ell\ne k$ also contributes to
$q_i(x)$.  Then $\ell$ is another step giving an arithmetic-progression
representation of the same support $S$.  By Corollary~\ref{cor:prime-interval-uniqueness} (prime
arithmetic-interval uniqueness), $\ell=\pm k$.  Because $\ell\ne k$, the
only possibility is $\ell=-k$.

To determine when $-k$ can contribute, write the same progression in the
reverse direction:
\[
S=\{a+(w-1)k,\,a+(w-2)k,\ldots,a\}.
\]
For the step $-k$, the two disagreement positions are therefore
\[
D_{-k}(x)=\{a+(w-1)k,\,a-k\}.
\]
The condition $i-k\in D_{-k}(x)$ is equivalent to
\[
i\in\{a+wk,\,a\}. \tag{5.2}
\]

It remains to observe that the two possible sets of indices in (5.1) and
(5.2) are disjoint when $2\le w\le p-2$.  Indeed, an equality between an
element of
\[
\{a-k,a+(w-1)k\}
\quad\text{and an element of}\quad
\{a,a+wk\}
\]
would, after cancelling $a$ and using $k\ne0$ in $\mathbb Z_p$, force one
of
\[
-1\equiv0,\qquad -1\equiv w,\qquad w-1\equiv0,
\qquad w-1\equiv w \pmod p.
\]
The first and last are impossible, while the middle two give respectively
$w=p-1$ and $w=1$, both excluded by $2\le w\le p-2$.

Hence no fixed coordinate $i$ can satisfy the contribution condition for
both $k$ and $-k$.  Since every other possible near-period step is ruled out
by Corollary~\ref{cor:prime-interval-uniqueness}, at most one nonzero rotation contributes to $q_i(x)$.
Therefore
\[
q_i(x)\le1,
\]
as claimed.

If $2\le\operatorname{wt}(x)\le p-2$, then
\[m([x],[x^{(i)}])=1+q_i(x)\in\{1,2\}.\]
From the constant necklace to the unique adjacent weight-one (respectively weight-$(p-1)$) necklace, the directed multiplicity is $p$.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Assume first that $2\le \operatorname{wt}(x)\le p-2$.  Since $p$ is prime and $x$ is
nonconstant, the only rotation fixing $x$ is the identity; hence $h_x=1$.
Moreover, flipping one coordinate changes the weight by one, so
$1\le \operatorname{wt}(x^{(i)})\le p-1$.  Thus $x^{(i)}$ is also nonconstant, and therefore
$h_{x^{(i)}}=1$.

The multiplicity formula of Theorem~4.1 consequently reduces to
\[
m([x],[x^{(i)}])
=\frac{h_x+q_i(x)}{h_{x^{(i)}}}
=1+q_i(x).
\]
By the prime near-period lemma, $q_i(x)\le 1$ for every $i$.  Since $q_i(x)$
is a nonnegative integer, $q_i(x)\in\{0,1\}$, and hence
\[
m([x],[x^{(i)}])\in\{1,2\}.
\]
In particular, multiplicity two occurs exactly when $q_i(x)=1$, that is,
when there is a nontrivial rotation $R_k$ with $|D_k(x)|=2$ and
$i+k\in D_k(x)$.

It remains to consider the extreme layers.  The constant word $0^p$ has a
single necklace, while flipping any one of its $p$ coordinates produces a
weight-one word.  All weight-one words are rotations of one another, so all
$p$ coordinate flips land in the unique weight-one necklace.  Therefore the
directed multiplicity from $[0^p]$ to that necklace is $p$.  The same
argument, with zeros and ones interchanged, shows that the directed
multiplicity from $[1^p]$ to the unique weight-$(p-1)$ necklace is also $p$.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:prime-local}
If $2\le\operatorname{wt}(X)\le p-2$, then
\[\boxed{s(f,X)=B_f(X)+N_f(X).}\]
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Fix a representative $x\in X$.  By the necklace sensitivity formula from Section~2,
\[
s(f,X)=\sum_{\bar f(Y)\ne\bar f(X)}m(X,Y).
\]
Only neighboring necklaces $Y\sim X$ can have $m(X,Y)>0$, so the sum is over the bichromatic neighbors of $X$.  Because $2\le\operatorname{wt}(X)\le p-2$, both $X$ and every necklace obtained from $X$ by one coordinate flip are nonconstant.  The prime-length multiplicity result proved immediately above therefore gives
\[
m(X,Y)\in\{1,2\}
\]
for every neighbor $Y$ of $X$.

Now separate the bichromatic neighbors according to their multiplicity.  Each bichromatic neighbor contributes at least one to the sensitivity sum, and the number of such neighbors is exactly $B_f(X)$.  A bichromatic neighbor with multiplicity two contributes one further unit.  By definition, the number of these doubled bichromatic neighbors is $N_f(X)$.  Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
s(f,X)
&=\sum_{\substack{Y\sim X\\ \bar f(Y)\ne\bar f(X)}}m(X,Y)\\
&=\sum_{\substack{Y\sim X\\ \bar f(Y)\ne\bar f(X)}}1
 +\#\{Y:Y\sim X,\ \bar f(Y)\ne\bar f(X),\ m(X,Y)=2\}\\
&=B_f(X)+N_f(X).
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves the formula.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:explicit-prime}
Let $2\le\operatorname{wt}(X)\le p-2$.
If $\operatorname{supp}(x)$ is not a cyclic arithmetic interval, then
\[\boxed{s(f,X)=B_f(X).}\]
If $\operatorname{supp}(x)$ is a cyclic arithmetic interval as above, then
\[\boxed{s(f,X)=B_f(X)+\delta_f(X,Y^-)+\delta_f(X,Y^+).}\]
In particular,
\[0\le s(f,X)-B_f(X)\le2.\]
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
By Theorem~\ref{thm:prime-local},
\[
s(f,X)=B_f(X)+N_f(X),
\]
so it remains only to identify the neighboring necklaces that can have
multiplicity two.

Suppose first that $\operatorname{supp}(x)$ is not a cyclic arithmetic interval.  If some
neighbor $Y=[x^{(i)}]$ had $m(X,Y)=2$, then the prime multiplicity formula
would give $q_i(x)=1$.  Thus $x$ would admit a nontrivial rotation at Hamming
distance two.  By the distance-two structure from Section~3, its support
would then have to be a cyclic arithmetic interval, a contradiction.
Hence every neighboring necklace has multiplicity one, so $N_f(X)=0$ and
\[
s(f,X)=B_f(X).
\]

Now suppose
\[
\operatorname{supp}(x)=\{a,a+k,\ldots,a+(w-1)k\},\qquad 2\le w\le p-2.
\]
For the rotation with step $k$, the two endpoints of the resulting
distance-two disagreement give the flip-equivalence
\[
[x^{(a-k)}]=[x^{(a+wk)}]=Y^+.
\]
Similarly, using the reverse step $-k$ gives
\[
[x^{(a)}]=[x^{(a+(w-1)k)}]=Y^-.
\]
Thus $Y^+$ and $Y^-$ are precisely the two possible doubled neighboring
necklaces.  The prime arithmetic-interval uniqueness result shows that any
other nontrivial distance-two rotation has step $\pm k$, so no third doubled
neighbor can occur.  Consequently the doubled bichromatic neighbors are
exactly those among $Y^-$ and $Y^+$ for which the value of $\bar f$ differs
from $\bar f(X)$.  Therefore
\[
N_f(X)=\delta_f(X,Y^-)+\delta_f(X,Y^+),
\]
and substitution into Theorem~\ref{thm:prime-local} yields
\[
s(f,X)=B_f(X)+\delta_f(X,Y^-)+\delta_f(X,Y^+).
\]
Since each $\delta_f$ is either $0$ or $1$, the final bound
\[
0\le s(f,X)-B_f(X)\le2
\]
follows immediately.

\qquad\text{and}\qquad

s_1(F_{n,r})=r,\qquad
s_0(F_{n,r})=\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{r+1}\right\rfloor.

s(F_{n,r})

<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
First consider a $1$-input.  A sensitive coordinate must be a $1$ whose deletion destroys every cyclic run of length at least $r$.  Hence all sensitive coordinates lie in a single maximal run of ones.  If that run has length $L\ge r$, deleting a position is sensitive only when the remaining portions on both sides have lengths at most $r-1$.  The number of such positions is at most $r$.  Equality is attained by a word having exactly one run of $r$ ones and all other coordinates zero.  Thus $s_1(F_{n,r})=r$.

Now let $x$ be a $0$-input and suppose that it has $m$ sensitive zero coordinates.  For each such zero, let $a_i$ and $b_i$ be the lengths of the adjacent runs of ones on its two sides.  Flipping that zero creates a run of length $a_i+1+b_i$, so sensitivity implies
\[
a_i+b_i\ge r-1.
\]
Summing over the $m$ sensitive zeros, and observing that every $1$-coordinate can be counted at most twice, gives
\[
m(r-1)\le2\,\operatorname{wt}(x).
\]
Since $x$ contains at least these $m$ zero coordinates,
\[
\operatorname{wt}(x)\le n-m.
\]
Therefore
\[
m(r+1)\le2n,
\]
and hence
\[
s_0(F_{n,r})\le\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{r+1}\right\rfloor.

m=\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{r+1}\right\rfloor,\qquad q=r-1.

If $m=1$, then necessarily $r=n$; the word $1^{n-1}0$ is a $0$-input whose unique zero is sensitive, so the desired lower bound holds.

We enlarge the one-blocks, rather than the zero gaps, until their total length is exactly $n-m$.  This is possible while keeping every $a_i\le r-1$.  Indeed, the preceding inequality gives

Since $m\ge2$,
\[
2\bigl(m(r-1)-(n-m)\bigr)
=m(r-1)-t
\ge 2(r-1)-r
=r-2\ge0.
\]
Hence
\[
n-m\le m(r-1).
\]
Starting from the minimizing sequence, increase entries one unit at a time, never beyond $r-1$, until their sum is $n-m$.  The inequalities $a_i+a_{i+1}\ge r-1$ are preserved.

The resulting length-$n$ cyclic word
\[
1^{a_1}0\,1^{a_2}0\cdots1^{a_m}0
\]
has no run of $r$ ones, because every $a_i\le r-1$, and hence is a $0$-input.  Flipping any displayed zero joins its two adjacent one-blocks into a run of length
\[
a_i+1+a_{i+1}\ge r,
\]
so all $m$ displayed zeros are sensitive.  Therefore
\[
s_0(F_{n,r})\ge m.
\]
The upper and lower bounds agree.
<!-- end proof-23 -->
<!-- corollary-7: body_md -->
For $(n,r)=(11,4)$,
\[
s(F_{11,4})=4.
\]
Thus the cyclic run-threshold construction gives an explicit $11$-variable rotation symmetric Boolean function of sensitivity $4$.

The construction itself and its $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ sensitivity behavior are known from the work of Chaubal and G\'al~\cite{ChaubalGal}.  The contribution here is the exact two-parameter formula above.  In particular, choosing $r$ near the balance point $r(r+1)\approx2n$ recovers the familiar $O(\sqrt n)$ scale, but the asymptotic construction is not claimed as new.
