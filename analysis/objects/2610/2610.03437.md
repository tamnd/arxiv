<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:k-pairing-width}
The map \(\Phi_k:\mathcal P\longrightarrow\mathcal P\) is a weight-preserving bijection.
Let \(\mu=\Phi_k(\lambda)\), and let \(c_i^{(k)}(\mu)\) count its parts congruent to
\(i\pmod k\), with multiplicity. Then \(c_0^{(k)}(\mu)=r_k(\lambda)\),
\(c_i^{(k)}(\mu)=\Sigma_i^{(k)}(\delta)\) for \(1\leq i<k\), and
\(\mu_1=W_k(\lambda)\). Hence, on partitions of each fixed size,
\[
\begin{aligned}
 &(r_k(\lambda),\Sigma_1^{(k)}(\delta),\ldots,
       \Sigma_{k-1}^{(k)}(\delta),W_k(\lambda))\\
 &\qquad\longleftrightarrow
 (c_0^{(k)}(\mu),c_1^{(k)}(\mu),\ldots,c_{k-1}^{(k)}(\mu),\mu_1).
\end{aligned}
\]
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- head-1: body_md -->
We first prove bijectivity. Given \(\lambda\), the \(k\)-pairing decomposition
\(\lambda\longleftrightarrow(\pi,\delta)\) is unique by Euclidean division of each multiplicity.

Conversely, let \(\mu\) be any partition. Split it uniquely as \(\mu=\gamma\sqcup\eta\), where every
part of \(\gamma\) is divisible by \(k\), while \(\eta\in\mathcal O_k\). Then \(\pi=(\gamma/k)'\) is
uniquely determined, and since \(\Theta_k\) is a bijection, \(\delta=\Theta_k^{-1}(\eta)\) is
uniquely determined. The pair \((\pi,\delta)\) uniquely reconstructs \(\lambda\) by
\eqref{eq:sec2-24}. Hence \(\Phi_k\) is bijective.

For the weight,
\[
 |\Phi_k(\lambda)|=|k\pi'|+|\Theta_k(\delta)|=k|\pi|+|\delta|=|\lambda|.
\]
Thus \(\Phi_k\) is weight-preserving.

All parts of \(k\pi'\) are divisible by \(k\), whereas no part of \(\eta\) is divisible by \(k\). Hence the parts of \(\mu\) congruent to \(0\pmod k\) are precisely the parts of \(k\pi'\). By \eqref{eq:sec2-33}, \(c_0^{(k)}(\mu) = \ell(k\pi') = \pi_1 = r_k(\lambda)\). For \(1\leq i<k\), all parts of \(\mu\) congruent to \(i\pmod k\) come from \(\eta\). By \eqref{eq:sec2-14}, \(c_i^{(k)}(\mu) = c_i^{(k)}(\eta) = \Sigma_i^{(k)}(\delta)\). Finally,
\[
 \mu_1=\max\{(k\pi')_1,\eta_1\}=\max\{k\ell(\pi),R_k(\delta)\}=W_k(\lambda),
\]
by \eqref{eq:sec2-21} and the definition of \(W_k\). This proves the theorem. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}

<!-- thm-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:marked-gap-bijection}
The map \(\Psi_k\) is a weight-preserving bijection
\[
\mathcal S_k^-\longleftrightarrow
  \{(\nu,J):\nu\in\mathcal O_k\setminus\{\varnothing\},\ J\subseteq G_k(\nu)\}.
\]
If \(\Psi_k(\lambda)=(\nu,J)\), then
\[
s_k(\lambda)=\ell(\nu),\qquad b_k(\lambda)=|J|,\qquad r_k(\lambda)=\max J.
\]
<!-- end thm-3 -->
<!-- head-4: body_md -->
Let \(\lambda\longleftrightarrow(A,\delta)\) be a simply \(k\)-paired partition of negative
\(k\)-pairing rank. Put \(\eta=\Theta_k(\delta), \quad t=\ell(\eta)=s_k(\delta)\). By
\eqref{eq:sec3-10}, \(A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,t-1\}\). Define \(\nu=\eta+kA', \quad J=A\). For \(1\leq
i<t\), equation \eqref{eq:sec3-27} gives \(\nu_i-\nu_{i+1} = \eta_i-\eta_{i+1} + k\mathbf1_{\{i\in
A\}} \geq0\). Moreover, since \(A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,t-1\}\), \(A'_t=0\), and therefore
\(\nu_t=\eta_t>0\). Thus \(\nu\) has exactly \(t\) positive parts.

Each part of \(\nu\) differs from the corresponding part of \(\eta\) by a multiple of \(k\). Hence \(\nu_i\equiv\eta_i\pmod k\). Since \(\eta\in\mathcal O_k\), \(k\nmid\eta_i\), and therefore also \(k\nmid\nu_i\). Thus \(\nu\in\mathcal O_k\). If \(i\in A\), then \eqref{eq:sec3-27} yields \(\nu_i-\nu_{i+1}\geq k\), so \(J=A\subseteq G_k(\nu)\). The map is weight-preserving because
\[
|\nu|=|\eta|+k|A'|=|\delta|+k\sum_{a\in A}a=|\lambda|.
\]

Here we used \(|A'|=\sum_{a\in A}a\). It remains to construct the
inverse.

Let \((\nu,J)\) satisfy \(\nu\in\mathcal O_k\setminus\{\varnothing\}, \quad J\subseteq G_k(\nu)\). Write \(t=\ell(\nu)\) and define
\[
\eta=\nu-kJ'.
\]

For \(1\leq i<t\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec3-36}
\eta_i-\eta_{i+1}
=
\nu_i-\nu_{i+1}
-
k\mathbf1_{\{i\in J\}}.
\end{equation}

If \(i\notin J\), the right-hand side is nonnegative because \(\nu\) is a partition.

If \(i\in J\), then \(i\in G_k(\nu)\), so \(\nu_i-\nu_{i+1}\geq k\), and \eqref{eq:sec3-36} is again
nonnegative.

Furthermore, \(J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,t-1\}\), so \(J'_t=0\) and hence \(\eta_t=\nu_t>0\). Thus
\(\eta\) is a partition with exactly \(t\) positive parts.

Since only multiples of \(k\) have been subtracted, \(\eta_i\equiv\nu_i\pmod k\). Because
\(\nu\in\mathcal O_k\), we also have \(\eta\in\mathcal O_k\). The bijection \(\Theta_k\) therefore
uniquely recovers \(\delta=\Theta_k^{-1}(\eta)\). Set \(A=J\). Then \(\max A<t=s_k(\delta)\), so the
simply \(k\)-paired partition determined by \((A,\delta)\) has negative \(k\)-pairing rank.

The two constructions are plainly inverse to one another. Finally, \(s_k(\lambda) = s_k(\delta) =
\ell(\eta) = \ell(\nu)\), \(b_k(\lambda) = |A| = |J|\), and \(r_k(\lambda) = \max A = \max J\). This
proves the theorem. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}

\label{thm:unsigned-gap-enumeration}
For \(n\geq1\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
 E_k^{\mathrm{sp}}(n)-O_k^{\mathrm{sp}}(n)
 &=\#\{\nu\in\mathcal O_k(n):g_k(\nu)=0\},\\
 M_k(n)&=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal O_k(n)}2^{g_k(\nu)}.
\end{aligned}

 \sum_{n\geq1}M_k(n)q^n

\end{aligned}

\label{thm:residue-class-divisors}
For \(n\geq1\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec3-49}
 E_{k,a}^{\mathrm{sp}}(n)-O_{k,a}^{\mathrm{sp}}(n)
 =\#\{d\mid n:d\equiv a\pmod k\}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end thm-4 -->
<!-- head-7: body_md -->
By Corollary~\ref{thm:signed-gap-cancellation}, after signed cancellation only those \(k\)-regular
partitions \(\nu\) with \(g_k(\nu)=0\) remain.

Every part of \(\nu\) is congruent to \(a\pmod k\). Hence two distinct part sizes differ by a
positive multiple of \(k\), and therefore by at least \(k\).

But \(g_k(\nu)=0\) requires every internal gap to be strictly less than \(k\). It follows that no
two adjacent parts can be distinct. Hence \(\nu=(d^t)\) for some \(d\equiv a\pmod k\). Its weight is
\(n=dt\). Thus \(d\) is a divisor of \(n\) satisfying \(d\equiv a\pmod k\). Conversely, every such
divisor gives the unique rectangular partition \((d^{\,n/d})\). This proves \eqref{eq:sec3-49}. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}

\label{thm:residue-class-overpartitions}
For \(n\geq1\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec3-51}
 M_{k,a}(n)=\frac12\,\overline p_{a,k}(n),
\end{equation}
and
\[
\sum_{n\geq1}M_{k,a}(n)q^n
 =\frac12\left(\frac{(-q^a;q^k)_\infty}{(q^a;q^k)_\infty}-1\right).
\]
<!-- end thm-5 -->
<!-- head-8: body_md -->
Let \(\nu\) be a nonempty partition all of whose parts are congruent to \(a\pmod k\), and let
\(d(\nu)\) denote its number of distinct part sizes.

Whenever the part size changes, the difference is a positive multiple of \(k\), and hence is at
least \(k\). Therefore \(g_k(\nu) = d(\nu)-1\). By the bijection \(\Psi_k\), there are
\(2^{d(\nu)-1}\) partitions \(\lambda\in\mathcal S_{k,a}^-\) whose images have first component
\(\nu\).

On the other hand, an overpartition with underlying partition \(\nu\) has two choices for each of
its \(d(\nu)\) distinct part sizes, giving \(2^{d(\nu)}\) overpartitions.

More explicitly, a marked gap after a block of equal parts may be identified with the choice to
overline that part size. Every distinct part size except the smallest has such an internal gap below
its block. Thus the pairs \((\nu,J)\) with \(J\subseteq G_k(\nu)\) correspond exactly to
those overpartitions in which the smallest part is not overlined.

Consequently, \(2^{g_k(\nu)} = 2^{d(\nu)-1} = \frac12\,2^{d(\nu)}\). Summing over all nonempty
\(\nu\) proves \eqref{eq:sec3-51}. The product identity follows by assigning the factor
\((1+q^{a+kj})/(1-q^{a+kj})\) to each allowed part size, multiplying these factors, removing the empty object, and taking
one half. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}

<!-- thm-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:unique-representative}
For fixed \(d\) and \(\boldsymbol\alpha,\boldsymbol\beta\in\mathscr W_{k,d}\),
\[
\boldsymbol\alpha\sim\boldsymbol\beta
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h_{\boldsymbol\alpha}=h_{\boldsymbol\beta}.
\]
Every equivalence class contains exactly one representative whose component diagrams
satisfy \(\alpha^{(0)}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\alpha^{(k-1)}\), namely
\((d;U_1,\ldots,U_k)\).
<!-- end thm-7 -->
<!-- head-11: body_md -->
The function \(h_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\) is invariant under the transfers defining \(\sim\),
which proves the forward implication.

For the converse, we first show that every tuple in \(\mathscr W_{k,d}\) can be transformed
into one whose component diagrams are weakly decreasing under inclusion.
Consider two adjacent components \(\alpha^{(s)},\alpha^{(s+1)}\). By the lemma recalled above,
a sequence of transfers replaces them by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec4-adjacent-normalization}
\left(
\alpha^{(s)}\cup\alpha^{(s+1)},
\,
\alpha^{(s)}\cap\alpha^{(s+1)}
\right).
\end{equation}

Define \(I(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \sum_{s=0}^{k-1} s\,|\alpha^{(s)}|\). Under \eqref{eq:sec4-adjacent-normalization}, the contribution of the two components changes from \(s|\alpha^{(s)}| + (s+1)|\alpha^{(s+1)}|\) to \(s|\alpha^{(s)}\cup\alpha^{(s+1)}| + (s+1)|\alpha^{(s)}\cap\alpha^{(s+1)}|\). Using \(|A\cup B|+|A\cap B|=|A|+|B|\), the decrease in \(I\) is \(\left| \alpha^{(s+1)} \setminus \alpha^{(s)} \right|\). Thus \(I\) decreases strictly whenever \(\alpha^{(s+1)} \not\subseteq \alpha^{(s)}\). Apply \eqref{eq:sec4-adjacent-normalization} whenever
\(\alpha^{(s+1)}\not\subseteq\alpha^{(s)}\). Each step strictly decreases the nonnegative
integer \(I\), so the procedure terminates. At termination,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec4-9}
\alpha^{(0)}
\supseteq
\alpha^{(1)}
\supseteq
\cdots
\supseteq
\alpha^{(k-1)}.
\end{equation}

Now fix a cell \(x\) with \(h_{\boldsymbol\alpha}(x)=r\). In a tuple satisfying
\eqref{eq:sec4-9}, the component diagrams containing \(x\) are precisely the first \(r\)
components. Hence the component with index \(s\) is \(U_{s+1}\), and the terminal tuple is
\((d;U_1,\ldots,U_k)\). This also proves uniqueness.

If two tuples have the same multiplicity function \(h\), they have the same diagrams \(U_r\).
Both can therefore be transformed into the representative \((d;U_1,\ldots,U_k)\).
Since every transfer is reversible, the two tuples belong to the same equivalence class. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}

\label{thm:class-cardinality}
\label{prop:local-class-product}
Let \(\mathcal C_{d,h}\) be the equivalence class under \(\sim\) with multiplicity function
\(h\), set
\(b=(U_1)_1\), and write \(h_{i,j}=h(i,j)\). With the boundary values
\(h_{i,0}=k\) and \(h_{d+1,j}=0\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec4-20}
 |\mathcal C_{d,h}|=\Omega_k(U_1,\ldots,U_k)
 =\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{b}
 \binom{h_{i,j-1}-h_{i+1,j}}{h_{i,j}-h_{i+1,j}}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end thm-8 -->
<!-- head-12: body_md -->
Given a tuple \(\boldsymbol\alpha\in\mathscr W_{k,d}\) satisfying
\(h_{\boldsymbol\alpha}=h\), condition \eqref{eq:sec4-14} assigns a subset \(S(C)\) to each
component \(C\in\mathcal B\). These subsets satisfy \eqref{eq:sec4-15}.

Conversely, let \(S:\mathcal B\to2^{\{0,\ldots,k-1\}}\) satisfy \eqref{eq:sec4-15}.
Extend \(S\) to cells by putting \(S(x)=S(C)\) for \(x\in C\),
\(S(x)=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\) for \(x\in U_k\), and \(S(x)=\varnothing\) for \(x\notin U_1\).

For \(0\leq s<k\), define \(\alpha^{(s)} = \{x:s\in S(x)\}\). We claim that each \(\alpha^{(s)}\) is
a Young diagram. Suppose \(y\in\alpha^{(s)}\) and \(x\preceq y\). Then \(s\in S(y)\). By the
containment condition, \(S(y)\subseteq S(x)\), and therefore \(s\in S(x)\). Thus
\(x\in\alpha^{(s)}\). Hence \(\alpha^{(s)}\) is an order ideal.

Furthermore, every cell \(x\) belongs to exactly \(|S(x)|=h(x)\) component diagrams.
Thus the resulting tuple \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) satisfies \(h_{\boldsymbol\alpha}=h\).

The two constructions are inverse. By Theorem~\ref{thm:unique-representative}, the
tuples with multiplicity function \(h\) form one equivalence class. This proves the first
equality of \eqref{eq:sec4-20}.

For the product, we count the possible subsets
\(S_{i,j}=S(i,j)\subseteq\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\) directly.

Fill the cells row by row from bottom to top, and within each row from left to right.
Put \(S_{i,0}=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\) and \(S_{d+1,j}=\varnothing\).
When \(S_{i,j}\) is to be chosen, both \(S_{i,j-1}\) and \(S_{i+1,j}\) have already been
chosen. The inclusion \(S_{i+1,j}\subseteq S_{i,j-1}\) already holds: for \(j>1\),
\(S_{i+1,j}\subseteq S_{i+1,j-1}\subseteq S_{i,j-1}\); for \(j=1\), it follows from
\(S_{i,0}=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\). The Young-diagram conditions require
\(S_{i+1,j}\subseteq S_{i,j}\subseteq S_{i,j-1}\), with \(|S_{i,j}|=h_{i,j}\).
The subset \(S_{i+1,j}\) already provides \(h_{i+1,j}\) required elements. We therefore
choose \(h_{i,j}-h_{i+1,j}\) additional elements from \(S_{i,j-1}\setminus S_{i+1,j}\),
which has cardinality \(h_{i,j-1}-h_{i+1,j}\).

Hence the number of possible choices for \(S_{i,j}\) is
\[
\binom{
h_{i,j-1}-h_{i+1,j}
}{
h_{i,j}-h_{i+1,j}
}.
\]

Multiplying over all cells yields \eqref{eq:sec4-20}. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}

<!-- thm-9: body_md -->
\label{thm:tuple-class-generating-function}
We have
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec4-23}
 \sum_{d\geq0}\sum_{\substack{U_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq U_k\\\ell(U_1)\leq d}}
 \Omega_k(U_1,\ldots,U_k)q^{d^2+\sum_r|U_r|}
 =\sum_{d\geq0}\frac{q^{d^2}}{(q;q)_d^k}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end thm-9 -->
<!-- head-13: body_md -->
By Theorem~\ref{thm:unique-representative}, every tuple belongs to a unique
equivalence class represented by \((d;U_1,\ldots,U_k)\), where
\(U_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq U_k\). By Theorem~\ref{thm:class-cardinality}, the class contains
\(\Omega_k(U_1,\ldots,U_k)\) tuples, each of size \(d^2+\sum_{r=1}^{k}|U_r|\) by
\eqref{eq:sec4-22}. This gives the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:sec4-23}.

For the right-hand side, fix \(d\). Each of the \(k\) component partitions has at most \(d\)
parts and contributes \(1/(q;q)_d\). These partitions are chosen independently, and the
term \(d^2\) in the size contributes the factor \(q^{d^2}\). Thus the generating function is
\(q^{d^2}/(q;q)_d^k\). Summing over \(d\geq0\) proves the identity. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}

\label{thm:representatives-plane-partitions}

For fixed \(d\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec4-24}
\sum_{\substack{
U_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq U_k\\
\ell(U_1)\leq d
}}
q^{\sum_{r=1}^{k}|U_r|}
=
\prod_{i=1}^{d}
\prod_{r=1}^{k}
\frac1{1-q^{i+r-1}}.
\end{equation}

Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec4-25}
\sum_{d\geq0}
\sum_{\substack{
U_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq U_k\\
\ell(U_1)\leq d
}}
q^{d^2+\sum_{r=1}^{k}|U_r|}
=
\sum_{d\geq0}
q^{d^2}
\prod_{i=1}^{d}
\prod_{r=1}^{k}
\frac1{1-q^{i+r-1}}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end thm-10 -->
<!-- head-14: body_md -->
Pad each \(U_r\) with zeros to \(d\) parts and form the array
\[
\Pi
=
\begin{pmatrix}
(U_1)_1&(U_1)_2&\cdots&(U_1)_d\\
(U_2)_1&(U_2)_2&\cdots&(U_2)_d\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
(U_k)_1&(U_k)_2&\cdots&(U_k)_d
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Since each \(U_r\) is a partition, \((U_r)_1\geq (U_r)_2\geq\cdots\geq (U_r)_d\). Thus the rows of
\(\Pi\) are weakly decreasing.

The inclusion \(U_r\supseteq U_{r+1}\) means \((U_r)_i\geq(U_{r+1})_i\) for all \(i\), so the columns
are also weakly decreasing.

Hence \(\Pi\) is precisely a plane partition whose shape is contained in a \(k\times d\) rectangle.
Moreover, \(|\Pi| = \sum_{r=1}^{k}|U_r|\). Conversely, every plane partition whose shape
is contained in a \(k\times d\) rectangle determines a unique sequence
\(U_1\supseteq\cdots\supseteq U_k\) by reading its rows.

The classical MacMahon formula for plane partitions whose shape is contained in a
\(k\times d\) rectangle~\cite{mac16} gives
\[
\sum_{\Pi}q^{|\Pi|}
=
\prod_{r=1}^{k}
\prod_{i=1}^{d}
\frac1{1-q^{r+i-1}},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:sec4-24}. Multiplying by \(q^{d^2}\) and summing over \(d\) gives \eqref{eq:sec4-25}. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}

\label{thm:cyclic-root-of-unity}
Let \(\zeta\) be a primitive \(k\)-th root of unity, \(0\leq t<k\),
\(g=\gcd(k,t)\), and \(L=k/g\), with \(\gcd(k,0)=k\). Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec4-33}
 \sum_{\boldsymbol\alpha\in\mathscr W_k}
 \zeta^{t c_{\mathrm{col}}(\boldsymbol\alpha)}q^{N(\boldsymbol\alpha)}
 =\sum_{d\geq0}\frac{q^{d^2}}{(q^L;q^L)_d^g}
 =\sum_{\substack{\boldsymbol\alpha\in\mathscr W_k\\
 \sigma^t(\boldsymbol\alpha)=\boldsymbol\alpha}}q^{N(\boldsymbol\alpha)}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end thm-11 -->
<!-- head-15: body_md -->
Fix \(d\). By \eqref{eq:sec4-31}, the component with index \(s\) contributes
\(1/(\zeta^{ts}q;q)_d\). Thus the contribution for this value of \(d\) to the left-hand side
of \eqref{eq:sec4-33} equals
\[
q^{d^2}
\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}
\frac1{(\zeta^{ts}q;q)_d}.
\]

Expanding the finite \(q\)-Pochhammer symbols,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sec4-34}
q^{d^2}
\prod_{j=1}^{d}
\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}
\frac1{1-\zeta^{ts}q^j}.
\end{equation}

The root \(\zeta^t\) has order \(L\). As \(s\) runs through \(0,\ldots,k-1\), every \(L\)-th root of unity appears exactly \(g\) times. Hence
\[
\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}
(1-\zeta^{ts}x)
=
(1-x^L)^g.
\]

Substituting \(x=q^j\) into \eqref{eq:sec4-34} gives \(\frac{q^{d^2}} {(q^L;q^L)_d^g}\). This proves
the first equality.

Now consider the fixed points of \(\sigma^t\). Its action on the \(k\) component positions
has \(g\) cycles, each of length \(L\). A tuple is fixed precisely when its component
partitions are equal within each cycle. For fixed \(d\), it is therefore determined by \(g\)
arbitrary partitions \(I^{(1)},\ldots,I^{(g)}\), with \(\ell(I^{(j)})\leq d\). Each such partition is repeated \(L\) times, so the total size is \(d^2 +
L\sum_{j=1}^{g}|I^{(j)}|\). Its generating function is again \(\frac{q^{d^2}} {(q^L;q^L)_d^g}\).
Summing over \(d\) completes the proof. \nopagebreak\hfill{\rule{4pt}{7pt}}
