<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:ulc-order}
If $s\leq D\leq D'$ and $h(z)\in\operatorname{ULC}(D)$, then $h(z)\in\operatorname{ULC}(D')$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We may assume $s \geq 2$, as the log-concavity conditions are satisfied when $s \leq 1$. 
By the definition of $\operatorname{ULC}(D)$, we obtain the inequality
\[a_i^2 \geq a_{i-1}a_{i+1} \frac{\binom{D}{i}^2}{\binom{D}{i-1}\binom{D}{i+1}} 
= a_{i-1}a_{i+1} \left( \frac{i+1}{i} \right) \! \left( 1 + \frac{1}{D-i} \right)
\]
for each $1 \leq i < s$. Observe that the factor $1 + \frac{1}{D-i}$ is monotonically decreasing with respect to $D$. 
Thus, we have $h(z) \in \operatorname{ULC}(D')$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:hadamard}
Let $P\subset\mathbb R^p$ and $Q\subset\mathbb R^q$ be a lattice $p$-polytope and a lattice $q$-polytope, respectively. Then the following statement holds.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If both $h_P^*(z)$ and $h_Q^*(z)$ are real-rooted, then $h_{P\times Q}^*(z)$ is real-rooted \emph{\cite[Theorem~0.2]{Wagner}}.
\item If both are log-concave with no internal zeros, then so is $h_{P\times Q}^*(z)$ \emph{\cite[Corollary~5.1]{LM}}.
\item If $h_P^*\in\operatorname{ULC}(p)$ and $h_Q^*\in\operatorname{ULC}(q)$, then $h_{P\times Q}^*\in\operatorname{ULC}(p+q)$ \emph{\cite[Theorem~1.2]{BFJ}}.
\item If both are symmetric and $r_P=r_Q$, then $h_{P\times Q}^*(z)$ is symmetric. If both are also $\gamma$-positive, then so is $h_{P\times Q}^*(z)$ \emph{\cite[Theorem~1.3]{BFJ}}.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
Let $f(z)$ and $g(z)$ be two polynomials. Then the following result holds.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If both $f(z)$ and $g(z)$ are symmetric, then $f(z)g(z)$ is symmetric. 
\item If both $f(z)$ and $g(z)$ are symmetric unimodal, then $f(z)g(z)$ is symmetric unimodal \emph{\cite{StanleyLC}}.
\item If both $f(z)$ and $g(z)$ are nonnegative log-concave with no internal zeros, then $f(z)g(z)$ is log-concave \emph{\cite{StanleyLC}}.
\item If $f(z)\in\operatorname{ULC}(A)$ and $g(z)\in\operatorname{ULC}(B)$, then $f(z)g(z)\in\operatorname{ULC}(A+B)$ \emph{\cite{Liggett}}.
\item If both $f(z)$ and $g(z)$ are real-rooted, then $f(z)g(z)$ is real-rooted \emph{\cite{StanleyLC}}.
\item If both $f(z)$ and $g(z)$ are $\gamma$-positive, then $f(z)g(z)$ is $\gamma$-positive (by definition).
\end{enumerate}

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:join-shape}
Standard lattice joins preserve symmetry, log-concavity with no internal zeros, real-rootedness, and $\gamma$-positivity of $h^*$-polynomial. They preserve ultra log-concavity both in dimension order and in actual-degree order. They also
preserve the simultaneous conditions of symmetry and unimodality.

\label{Example-Veryample}
Let

This polytope appears in \cite[SEction 9]{Balletti2021}.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:free-formula}
Let $P\subset\mathbb R^p$ and $Q\subset\mathbb R^q$ be full-dimensional lattice polytopes containing the origin. Then
\[\widetilde h_{P\oplus Q}(z)=\widetilde h_P(z)\widetilde h_Q(z), \qquad h_P^*(z)=\Psi(\widetilde h_P(z)),
 \qquad h_{P\oplus Q}^*(z)=\Psi(\widetilde h_P(z)\widetilde h_Q(z)).
\]
Moreover, we have 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:free-product-criterion}
 h_{P\oplus Q}^*(z)=h_P^*(z)h_Q^*(z)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ell_P=1\quad \text{or}\quad \ell_Q=1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- example-8: body_md -->
{(\cite[Example~1]{Stapledon})}
The intervals $I=[-2,2]$ and $I'=[-1,3]$ are lattice polytopes, and both have $h^*(z)=1+3z$. Their gauges give
\[\widetilde h_I(z)=1+2z^{1/2}+z,\qquad \widetilde h_{I'}(z)=1+z^{1/3}+z^{2/3}+z;
\]
According to \cref{thm:free-formula}, we have 
\[h_{I\oplus I}^*(z)=1+10z+5z^2,\qquad h_{I'\oplus I'}^*(z)=1+9z+6z^2.
\]

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:free-join}
Let $P\subset\mathbb R^p$ and $Q\subset\mathbb R^q$ be full-dimensional lattice
polytopes containing $0$. Then
\[ h_{P\oplus Q}^*(z)=h_{P*Q}^*(z) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ell_P=1\ \text{or}\ \ell_Q=1.
\]
Under these equivalent conditions, for $h^*$-polynomial, free sums preserve symmetry, log-concavity with no internal zeros, real-rootedness, $\gamma$-positivity, and ultra log-concavity in either dimension order or actual-degree order. They also preserve the simultaneous conditions of symmetry and unimodality.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
The free sum of two lattice polytopes does not preserve the unimodality of the $h^*$-polynomial.
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $A$ be the five-dimensional polytope in \cref{ex:join-unimodal}, expressed in integer affine coordinates.
Set $\widehat A=\operatorname{conv}(0,(A,1))\subset\mathbb R^6$. It is a height-one lattice pyramid, so 
$$h_{\widehat A}^*(z)=1+6z+6z^2+113z^3.$$
Every lateral facet contains the apex $0$, and the only facet not containing $0$ is the base at height one. 
Its normal is the primitive last coordinate vector, so $\ell_{\widehat A}=1$.
The twelve-dimensional free sum therefore has numerator
\[ h_{\widehat A\oplus\widehat A}^*(z) =1+12z+48z^2+298z^3+1392z^4+1356z^5+12769z^6.
\]
$h_{\widehat A}^*(z)$ is unimodal, but $h_{\widehat A\oplus\widehat A}^*(z)$ is not unimodal since $1392>1356<12769$. 
Thus even the exact numerator product formula does not preserve unimodality without an additional hypothesis.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- example-9: body_md -->
Let $P=Q=[0,2]$. The point $(1,1)$ belongs to
$P\oplus Q=2\Delta_2$, but its only possible height in $P*Q$ is
$t=1/2$. Thus the projection on the ambient integer spaces is
surjective, whereas the projection on the lattice points of these
polytopes is not. In this case
\[
 h_P^*(z)=h_Q^*(z)=1+z,\qquad
 h_{P*Q}^*(z)=1+2z+z^2,\qquad

\[ |(k/2)T\cap\mathbb Z^2|= \sum_{s=\lceil-5k/2\rceil}^{2k} \max\left\{0,\ \left\lfloor\frac{4s+k}{9}\right\rfloor -\left\lceil\frac{5s-k}{9}\right\rceil+1\right\}.

\[ \begin{array}{c|rrrrrrr}
 k&0&1&2&3&4&5&6\\ \hline
 |(k/2)T\cap\mathbb Z^2|&1&3&7&13&22&32&46
 \end{array}.

 h_0^*=1,\quad

 h_2^*=70-4\cdot15+6=16,\quad
 h_3^*=202-4\cdot70+6\cdot15-4=8.
\end{align*}

\[ 1=30n-\lambda(u_n)=\sum_{i=1}^n(30-\lambda(a_i)),
\]

lattice points of $R$. In particular, $R$ is not very ample and is not IDP. Since a unimodular triangulation
implies IDP, it has none of the three triangulation properties considered here.

The origin is interior, and $R$ contains the three standard basis vectors, so it is spanning.

<!-- theorem-12: body_md -->
\label{prop:join-eventual-idp}
For nonempty lattice polytopes $P,Q$, the following conditions are equivalent: (1). $P*Q$ is very ample;
(2). $P*Q$ is IDP; (3). both $P$ and $Q$ are IDP.
<!-- end theorem-12 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
If both $P$ and $Q$ are IDP, then the join is IDP by \cite[Theorem~5.2]{LTX}.
We know that an IDP polytope is very ample.

For the remaining implication: $(1)\Rightarrow(3)$, suppose that $P$ is not IDP. Fix $c\geq2$ and $u\in cP\cap\mathbb Z^p$ that is
not a sum of $c$ lattice points of $P$, and fix a lattice point $q_0\in Q$.

\[(u,(n-c)q_0,n-c)\in n(P*Q)\cap\mathbb Z^{p+q+1}.
\]

Consequently, such undecomposable lattice points exist in every dilation $n(P*Q)$ for $n \ge c$, proving that the join $P*Q$ is not very ample. By symmetry, an analogous argument applies if $Q$ is not IDP.

<!-- theorem-13: body_md -->
\label{ex:minkowski-magic}
The Minkowski sum of two lattice polytopes does not preserve Ehrhart positivity or magic positivity.
<!-- end theorem-13 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Let $e_1,\ldots,e_4$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb Z^4$. For an integer $m\geq2$, set
\[ P=3\operatorname{conv}(0,e_1,e_2,e_3),\qquad Q_m=[0,(1,m-1,m-1,m)].
\]
The dimensions of $P$, $Q_m$, and $P+Q_m$ are three, one, and four, respectively. 
The last assertion follows because the direction of $Q_m$ has nonzero fourth coordinate. 
The segment is primitive, since the first coordinate of its direction is one. The Ehrhart polynomials are 
\[ L_P(n)=\binom{3n+3}{3} =(n+1)^3+\frac{5}{2}n(n+1)^2+n^2(n+1),\qquad 
 L_{Q_m}(n)=n+1.
\]
These are magic expansions with nonnegative coefficients.

We compute $L_{P+Q_m}(n)$ for $n\geq1$ by fixing the fourth coordinate $z\in\{0,1,\ldots,mn\}$. The corresponding slice, in its first three coordinates, is
\[ \left(\frac zm,\frac{(m-1)z}{m},\frac{(m-1)z}{m}\right) +3n\Delta_3,
 \qquad \Delta_3=\operatorname{conv}(0,e_1,e_2,e_3)\subset\mathbb R^3.
\]
For each of the $n+1$ heights where $z$ is a multiple of $m$, the translation vector is integral. 
Consequently, the slice contains exactly $\binom{3n+3}{3}$ lattice points.

Every remaining height is uniquely $z=m\ell+j$, with $0\leq\ell\leq n-1$ and $1\leq j\leq m-1$. The fractional parts of
the translation vector $\left(\frac zm,\frac{(m-1)z}{m},\frac{(m-1)z}{m}\right)$ are
$f_1=\frac jm$, $f_2=1-\frac jm$, and $f_3=1-\frac{j}{m}$, respectively.
Now we need to count the number of integer lattice points \((y_1,y_2,y_3)\) satisfying the following conditions:
\begin{align*}
y_1 - f_1 \ge 0,\quad
y_2 - f_2 \ge 0,\quad
y_3 - f_3 \ge 0,\quad
(y_1 - f_1) + (y_2 - f_2) + (y_3 - f_3) \le 3n.
\end{align*}
Consider the condition \(y_1 \ge f_1\). Since \(y_1\) must be an integer and \(0<f_1 = \frac{j}{m}<1\), the smallest possible value of \(y_1\) is in fact 1.
Similarly, \(y_2 \ge 1 - \frac{j}{m}\) implies that the minimum value of \(y_2\) is \(1\); \(y_3 \ge 1 - \frac{j}{m}\) implies that the minimum value of \(y_3\) is \(1\).

Set $k_i=y_i-1\geq 0$. Therefore, we have 
$$(k_1 + 1 - f_1) + (k_2 + 1 - f_2) + (k_3 + 1 - f_3) \le 3n.$$
Furthermore, we obtain 
$$k_1 + k_2 + k_3 \le 3n - \left(1 + \frac{j}{m}\right).$$
We have reduced the problem to finding three nonnegative integers \(k_1,k_2,k_3\) whose sum is at most $\lfloor 3n - 1 - \frac{j}{m} \rfloor = 3n - 2$.
The number of such triples is $\binom{3n-2+3}{3}$. There are $n(m-1)$ heights of this type. 

Thus we have 
\[\begin{aligned}
 L_{P+Q_m}(n)&=(n+1)\binom{3n+3}{3}+n(m-1)\binom{3n+1}{3}\\
 &=\frac{9m}{2}n^4+\frac{27}{2}n^3+\left(15-\frac m2\right)n^2+\frac{13}{2}n+1.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The formula also gives $L_{P+Q_m}(0)=1$. For $m=31$, the coefficient of $n^2$ is $-1/2$. 
Consequently, Minkowski sums preserve neither Ehrhart positivity nor magic positivity, even when polytopes $P$ and $Q_m$ are magic positive.

\label{ex:minkowski-shape}
The Minkowski sum of two lattice polytopes does not preserve the unimodality, log-concavity, ultra log-concavity, real-rootedness, or $\gamma$-positivity of the $h^*$-polynomial; nor does it preserve spanning, very ampleness, IDP, unimodular triangulations, regular unimodular triangulations, or quadratic triangulations.

In $\mathbb R^8$ with lattice $\mathbb Z^8$, set
\[ P=\operatorname{conv}(0,e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4),\qquad
 Q=\operatorname{conv}(0,e_5,e_6,e_7,2e_8-e_1-\cdots-e_7).
\]
We will show that both polytopes are unimodular four-simplices, while the Minkowski sum is an eight-dimensional nonspanning polytope with a nonunimodal $h^*$-polynomial.

Consider the invertible linear map
\[ T(x_1,\ldots,x_8) =\left(x_1+\frac{x_8}{2},\ldots,x_7+\frac{x_8}{2},\frac{x_8}{2}\right).
\]
Its image of $\mathbb Z^8$ is the lattice
\[ T(\mathbb Z^8)=\mathbb Z^8\ \cup\ \left(\mathbb Z+\frac12\right)^8.
\]
Indeed, the parity of $x_8$ determines the two cases. Conversely, a vector $y$ in either displayed set has the integer preimage
$x_i=y_i-y_8$ for $1\leq i\leq7$ and $x_8=2y_8$.
Thus $T$ must be used together with this image lattice when counting points.


The images $T(P)$ and $T(Q)$ are the standard four-simplices in the first and last four coordinate positions, respectively. 
The half-integer coset has no point on either coordinate subspace: all four coordinates in the other block would have to vanish. Consequently, the image lattice on each subspace is exactly its usual copy of $\mathbb Z^4$. This proves that both polytopes are unimodular in their integer affine coordinates.  Each has $h^*=1$, and its triangulation consisting of the simplex and all its faces is quadratic: the simplex is unimodular, constant lifting heights give regularity, and every subset of its vertices is a face, which gives the flag property.


As a real polytope, $T(P+Q)=\Delta_4\times\Delta_4$, where $\Delta_4=\operatorname{conv}(0,e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4)\subset\mathbb R^4$. 
The two independent four-dimensional direction spaces also show that $\dim(P+Q)=8$.
In the integer coset, the $n$-th dilate contains $\binom{n+4}{4}^2$ lattice points. 
In the half-integer coset write $y_i=k_i+1/2$ for $1\leq i\leq8$. 
Nonnegativity is equivalent to $k_i\geq0$, and each four-coordinate sum is at most $n$ precisely when
the corresponding sum of the $k_i$ is at most $n-2$. It follows that
\[L_{P+Q}(n)=\binom{n+4}{4}^2+\binom{n+2}{4}^2.
\]
The second binomial term is zero for $n=0,1$.

Therefore, we have 
\[h_{P+Q}^*(z)=1+16z+37z^2+32z^3+37z^4+16z^5+z^6.
\]
The inequalities $37>32<37$ show that it is not unimodal; in particular, $32^2<37^2$ also violates log-concavity. 
Furthermore, $h_{P+Q}^*(z)$ is neither ultra log-concave nor real-rooted.
The polynomial is symmetric, but it cannot be $\gamma$-positive because $\gamma$-positivity implies unimodality. Thus all five properties fail to be preserved, although $h_P^*(z)=h_Q^*(z)=1$.

At $n=1$ the half-integer coset contributes no point. Therefore every lattice point of $P+Q$ has even eighth coordinate in the original $x$-coordinates. On the other hand, the sum $P+Q$ contains
\[0,e_1,\ldots,e_7,2e_8-e_1-\cdots-e_7.
\]
These points generate $\mathbb Z^7\times2\mathbb Z$. The lattice-point difference
group of $P+Q$ is therefore exactly $\mathbb Z^7\times2\mathbb Z$, which has index
two in $\mathbb Z^8$. The sum is not spanning. Under the conventions of
\cref{sec:definitions}, it is consequently not very ample or IDP and
admits no unimodular, regular unimodular, or quadratic triangulation.
This completes the proof.

\label{ex:full-dimensional-holes}

For $D$, subtracting its edge column $e_4$ from the last two edge columns gives $v,e_4,e_1,e_3$.

At $z=1$, the two lower bounds force integer coordinates $x,y$ to be

The first summand belongs to $2A$, since its coefficients are nonnegative and sum to $4/3\leq2$; the second belongs to $D$.
Because $0\in A\cap D$, one has $2A\subset nA$ and $D\subset nD$, so the claimed follows.

The Cayley polytope has dimension $r+1$, and, for every integer
$n\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cayley}
 L_{\operatorname{Cayley}(P,Q)}(n)=
 \sum_{k=0}^{n}\bigl|((n-k)P+kQ)\cap\mathbb Z^d\bigr|.

The direction space of the Cayley polytope is generated by
$U\times\{0\}$, $V\times\{0\}$, and $(q_0-p_0,1)$.
The last vector is independent of the horizontal subspace, which has
dimension $r$. This proves the dimension formula.

Let $n\geq1$. By grouping together the coefficients of points in each layer in a convex combination, we show that the slice of

\[ \bigl((n-t)P+tQ\bigr)\times\{t\}.
\]
At $t=0$ or $t=n$, this means the corresponding single dilated layer, using $0P=0Q=\{0\}$. 
A lattice point must have an integer height $k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}$. 
By counting the disjoint slices, we obtain \eqref{eq:cayley}. At $n=0$, both sides equal one.

<!-- theorem-17: body_md -->
\label{prop:cayley-idp}
The polytope $\operatorname{Cayley}(P,Q)$ is IDP if and only if, for all nonnegative integers $a,b$, every lattice point of $aP+bQ$ is a sum of $a$ lattice points of $P$ and $b$ lattice points of $Q$. By convention, an empty sum evaluates to zero.
<!-- end theorem-17 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
The slice description at dilation one shows that the lattice points of $\operatorname{Cayley}(P,Q)$ are exactly
\[\bigl((P\cap\mathbb Z^d)\times\{0\}\bigr) \ \cup\ \bigl((Q\cap\mathbb Z^d)\times\{1\}\bigr).
\]
Suppose first that the Cayley polytope is IDP. Fix $a,b\geq0$ with $a+b>0$ and $u\in(aP+bQ)\cap\mathbb Z^d$. 
Assume $u=av_1+bv_2$ with $v_1\in P$ and $v_2\in Q$. Therefore, we have
$$(u,b)=(av_1,0)+(bv_2,b)=a(v_1,0)+b(v_2,1)= (a+b) \left( \frac{a}{a+b}(v_1, 0) + \frac{b}{a+b}(v_2, 1) \right).$$
The point $(u,b)$ belongs to $(a+b)\operatorname{Cayley}(P,Q)$. 
An IDP decomposition has $a+b$ summands, each at
height zero or one. Their heights sum to $b$, so exactly $b$ summands
come from the $Q$ layer and the remaining $a$ come from the $P$ layer.
Projecting to $\mathbb R^d$ gives the required mixed decomposition.
The case $a=b=0$ is the empty sum.

Conversely, fix $n\geq1$ and a lattice point $(u,k)$ of
$n\operatorname{Cayley}(P,Q)$. Its height satisfies $0\leq k\leq n$, and
$u\in(n-k)P+kQ$. Apply the assumed property with $a=n-k$ and $b=k$.
Lift the resulting $P$ summands to height zero and the $Q$ summands
to height one. Their sum is $(u,k)$, and there are exactly $n$
summands. This proves IDP.

<!-- theorem-18: body_md -->
\label{prop:cayley-spanning}
Suppose that $P$ and $Q$ are spanning in their integer affine coordinates. Then $\operatorname{Cayley}(P,Q)$ is spanning if and only if
$(\mathbb Z^d\cap U)+(\mathbb Z^d\cap V)=\mathbb Z^d\cap(U+V)$ holds. In particular, it is spanning when both inputs are full-dimensional and spanning in $\mathbb R^d$.
<!-- end theorem-18 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
To determine whether $\operatorname{Cayley}(P,Q)$ is spanning, we analyze the lattice generated by the differences of its integer points. Fix base points $p_0\in P\cap\mathbb Z^d$ and $q_0\in Q\cap\mathbb Z^d$. The integer points of the Cayley polytope reside entirely within the two layers $(P\cap\mathbb Z^d)\times\{0\}$ and $(Q\cap\mathbb Z^d)\times\{1\}$. Consequently, the lattice of differences is generated by the horizontal displacements within each individual layer, together with a single cross layer displacement. Specifically, this lattice is generated by the elements
\[ (u-p_0,0) \text{ for } u\in P\cap\mathbb Z^d, \qquad  (v-q_0,0) \text{ for } v\in Q\cap\mathbb Z^d, \qquad  \text{and } (q_0-p_0,1).
\]

Consider the affine map $\Phi \colon \mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R \to \mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R$ defined by 
\[\Phi(x,t) = \bigl(x-p_0-t(q_0-p_0), t\bigr).
\]
Because its inverse, given by $(x,t) \mapsto \bigl(x+p_0+t(q_0-p_0), t\bigr)$, is also an integral affine transformation, $\Phi$ restricts to an automorphism of the ambient lattice $\mathbb Z^{d+1}$. By construction, $\Phi$ translates our chosen base points $(p_0,0)$ and $(q_0,1)$ to the origin $(0,0)$ and the unit vector $(0,1)$, respectively. 

In this transformed coordinate system, the affine span of the Cayley polytope is mapped to $(U+V)\times\mathbb R$. The ambient lattice restricted to this affine span is exactly $\bigl(\mathbb Z^d\cap(U+V)\bigr)\times\mathbb Z$. 

We now determine the image of the difference lattice under the linear part of $\Phi$. By the hypothesis that $P$ and $Q$ are spanning, the horizontal displacements $u-p_0$ generate the sublattice $\mathbb Z^d\cap U$, and the displacements $v-q_0$ generate $\mathbb Z^d\cap V$. Coupled with the vertical generator which maps to $(0,1)$, the transformed difference lattice is precisely
\[
  \bigl((\mathbb Z^d\cap U)+(\mathbb Z^d\cap V)\bigr)\times\mathbb Z.
\]

By definition, the Cayley polytope is spanning if and only if this generated difference lattice coincides with the ambient lattice of its affine span. Comparing the two Cartesian products, this equality holds if and only if $(\mathbb Z^d\cap U)+(\mathbb Z^d\cap V) = \mathbb Z^d\cap(U+V)$. Finally, if both $P$ and $Q$ are full-dimensional in $\mathbb R^d$, then $U=V=\mathbb R^d$, rendering this geometric condition trivially satisfied.

\label{ex:polar-payne}
The reflexive polarity preserves neither unimodality nor log-concavity of the $h^*$-polynomial.

We consider the six-dimensional simplex
\[ R=\operatorname{conv}\bigl(e_1,\ldots,e_6,-(1,1,1,1,1,3)\bigr).
\]
This is the member with parameters $r=0$, $s=3$, and $k=2$ of Payne's family \cite{Payne}, in the coordinates recorded by

\[ h_R^*(z)=(1+z+z^2)(1+z^2+z^4)=1+z+2z^2+z^3+2z^4+z^5+z^6.
\]
It is neither unimodal nor log-concave. We will verify that $R^*$ has a log-concave $h^*$-polynomial and a quadratic
triangulation, while $R$ is not very ample.

First, the identity
\[
 0=\frac19\bigl(-(1,1,1,1,1,3)+e_1+\cdots+e_5+3e_6\bigr)
\]
expresses the origin as a convex combination of all seven vertices with positive coefficients. These vertices affinely span $\mathbb R^6$, so $0\in\operatorname{relint}(R)$. The polar inequalities are
\[ y_i\leq1\quad(1\leq i\leq6),\qquad y_1+\cdots+y_5+3y_6\geq-1.
\]
The integer affine change $u_i=1-y_i$ therefore gives $R^*=\mathbf 1-T$, where
$$T=\{u\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^6:u_1+\cdots+u_5+3u_6\leq9\}=\operatorname{conv}(0,9e_1,\ldots,9e_5,3e_6).$$
In particular, $R^*$ has integer vertices, which directly verifies reflexivity. For each integer $n\geq0$, the map
$u\longmapsto n\mathbf 1-u$ is a bijection from $nT\cap\mathbb Z^6$ to $nR^*\cap\mathbb Z^6$. Thus the reflection and translation preserve the
Ehrhart polynomial.


For a lattice point of $nT$, its sixth coordinate is an integer $j\in\{0,\ldots,3n\}$. Once this coordinate is fixed, the other
five are nonnegative integers with sum at most $9n-3j$. Adding a nonnegative slack coordinate identifies these points with
six-tuples of nonnegative integers having sum $9n-3j$. Their number is $\binom{9n-3j+5}{5}$. Consequently, we have 
\[ L_{R^*}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{3n}\binom{9n-3j+5}{5} \qquad(n\geq0).
\]

\[ h_{R^*}^*(z)=1+2514z+40236z^2+91645z^3+40236z^4+2514z^5+z^6.
\]
Therefore, the polynomial $h_{R^*}^*(z)$ is symmetric, unimodal, and log-concave.

\[\{(x,t):x\in Q,\ 0\leq t\leq f(x)\},

Every Nakajima polytope admits a regular unimodular flag triangulation \cite[Corollary~2.9]{HPPS}; this is a quadratic triangulation in our terminology. \[ 0\leq u_6\leq3,\qquad 0\leq t_5\leq9-3u_6,\qquad 0\leq t_i\leq t_{i+1}\quad(1\leq i\leq4).

Indeed, an integer affine lattice bijection preserves normalized volumes and face incidences, and transporting the lifting heights
preserves regularity. In particular, $R^*$ has a regular unimodular triangulation, a unimodular triangulation, IDP, very ampleness, and spanning.

\[ -e_6=\frac13\bigl(e_1+\cdots+e_5-(1,1,1,1,1,3)\bigr)
\]
expresses $-e_6$ as a nonnegative combination of vertices of $R$ whose coefficients sum to two. Thus $-e_6\in2R$.

\[
 (n-2)e_1-e_6\in nR\cap\mathbb Z^6.
\]
Suppose this point were a sum of $n$ lattice points of $R$, and let $a$ denote the number of summands equal to $-(1,1,1,1,1,3)$.
The sixth coordinate forces the number of $e_6$ summands to be $3a-1$, so $a\geq1$. The second through fifth coordinates force
$a$ copies of each of $e_2,\ldots,e_5$, and the first coordinate forces $n-2+a$ copies of $e_1$. The number of nonzero summands is
therefore
\[a+(3a-1)+4a+(n-2+a)=n-3+9a\geq n+6>n,
\]
a contradiction. There is consequently an undecomposable lattice point in every $nR$ with $n\geq2$. Hence $R$ is not very ample and is not IDP. Since a unimodular triangulation implies IDP, $R$ has none of the three unimodular triangulation properties in \cref{tab:coefficients}. It is nevertheless spanning, because $0,e_1,\ldots,e_6\in R$.
This proves the five geometric failures in the direction $R^*\mapsto R$.

 c(rP)=\left\lceil\frac{c(P)}r\right\rceil,
 \qquad
 \deg h_{rP}^*=d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1-s_P}r\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-24: body_md -->
For \eqref{eq:dilation-extraction}, this is the standard Veronese formula in \cite[Lemma~3.1]{Jochemko} and
\cite[Section~1]{Zhang}.
For the codegree assertion, set $m\in\operatorname{int}(c(P)P)\cap\mathbb Z^d$ and $v\in P\cap\mathbb Z^d$. 
If $k\geq c(P)$, then
\[
 m+(k-c(P))v\in\operatorname{int}(kP)\cap\mathbb Z^d.
\]
Indeed, since $m$ belongs to the interior of $c(P)P$, there exists an open ball centered at $m$ that is entirely contained within $c(P)P$. Translating this ball by the vector $(k-c(P))v \in (k-c(P))P$ yields an open neighborhood of $m+(k-c(P))v$ that lies within the Minkowski sum $c(P)P + (k-c(P))P = kP$.
Thus $kP$ has an interior lattice point for every integer $k\geq c(P)$, and none for $1\leq k<c(P)$ by the definition of codegree.
It follows that $n(rP)$ has an interior lattice point exactly when $rn\geq c(P)$. The smallest positive integer $n$ with this property is $\lceil c(P)/r\rceil$. The degree formula now follows from $\deg h_{rP}^*=d+1-c(rP)$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:uniform-unimodal}
If $h(z)$ is a polynomial with nonnegative and unimodal coefficients, then for any integer $r \ge 1$, the product $B_r(z)h(z)$ is unimodal.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-25: body_md -->
The assertion is trivial for $r=1$. For $r \ge 2$, let $h(z) = \sum a_i z^i$ by setting $a_i=0$ outside its support.  Suppose the coefficient sequence achieves a peak at $m$. The $i$-th coefficient of the product $B_r(z)h(z)$ is given by $b_i = \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} a_{i-j}$, which yields the difference
$$b_i - b_{i-1} = a_i - a_{i-r}.$$

We determine the behavior of $b_i - b_{i-1}$ by examining three ranges of indices. When $i \le m$, both $a_i$ and $a_{i-r}$ are situated on the nondecreasing segment of the sequence (or preceding the support), ensuring $b_i - b_{i-1} \ge 0$. Conversely, when $i \ge m+r$, both terms lie on the nonincreasing segment (or beyond the support), guaranteeing $b_i - b_{i-1} \le 0$. In the intervening range $m < i < m+r$, the term $a_i$ is nonincreasing as $i$ grows, while $a_{i-r}$ is nondecreasing. Hence, the difference $a_i - a_{i-r}$ is monotonically nonincreasing across this interval.

It follows that the sequence of first differences $(b_i - b_{i-1})$ undergoes at most one sign change, passing exclusively from nonnegative to nonpositive. This establishes the unimodality of the product.

<!-- theorem-26: body_md -->
\label{thm:dilation-unimodal-lc}
For every integer $r\geq1$, integral dilation preserves unimodality and log-concavity of $h^*$-polynomials, with the no-internal-zeros convention for log-concavity.
<!-- end theorem-26 -->
<!-- proof-26: body_md -->
Suppose first that $h_P^*(z)$ is unimodal. Repeated application of \cref{lem:uniform-unimodal} shows that $B_r^{d+1}h_P^*(z)$ is unimodal. It is clear that taking the coefficients at indices $0,r,2r,\ldots$ preserves unimodality.
According to \eqref{eq:dilation-extraction}, we prove the unimodality of $h_{rP}^*(z)$.

Now suppose that $h_P^*(z)$ is log-concave with no internal zeros. The convolution theorem~\cite{StanleyLC}, applied successively to the $d+1$ factors $B_r(z)$, shows that $B_r^{d+1}(z)h_P^*(z)$ has a log-concave coefficient sequence $b_0,\ldots,b_N$ with every coefficient positive. 
Log-concavity is equivalent to the ratios $b_i/b_{i-1}$ being nonincreasing. Whenever all three selected indices occur, we have
\[\frac{b_{ri}}{b_{r(i-1)}}=\prod_{j=1}^r\frac{b_{r(i-1)+j}}{b_{r(i-1)+j-1}}\geq\prod_{j=1}^r\frac{b_{ri+j}}{b_{ri+j-1}}
 =\frac{b_{r(i+1)}}{b_{ri}}.
\]
Therefore, we have  $b_{ri}^2\geq b_{r(i-1)}b_{r(i+1)}$.
By \eqref{eq:dilation-extraction}, we prove the log-concavity assertion.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:even-ulc}
Suppose $f(z) = \sum_{i=0}^s b_i z^i \in \operatorname{ULC}(N)$ with $b_0 > 0$, and adopt the convention that $b_i = 0$ for $i > s$. Then the extracted series $f^{\langle 2,0 \rangle}(z)$ belongs to $\operatorname{ULC}(\lfloor N/2 \rfloor)$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
Let $M = \lfloor N/2 \rfloor$. The case $M=0$ yields a constant polynomial, making the assertion trivial. For $M \ge 1$, we can decompose the normalized coefficients of $f^{\langle 2,0 \rangle}(z)$ into two factors:
\[\frac{b_{2i}}{\binom{M}{i}}
 = \frac{b_{2i}}{\binom{N}{2i}}\frac{\binom{N}{2i}}{\binom{M}{i}}.
\]
The first factor, viewed as a sequence in $i$, forms a subsequence of the log-concave sequence $b_j / \binom{N}{j}$. By the same ratio argument applied in the proof of \cref{thm:dilation-unimodal-lc}, this subsequence preserves log-concavity.

The second factor is positive for $0 \le i \le M$. For $1 \le i \le M$, the ratio of consecutive terms evaluates to
\[\frac{\binom{N}{2i}/\binom{M}{i}}{\binom{N}{2i-2}/\binom{M}{i-1}}
 = \frac{(N-2i+2)(N-2i+1)}{2(2i-1)(M-i+1)}
 = \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{2M-2i+1}{2i-1}, & \text{if } N=2M,\\[6pt]
    \dfrac{2M-2i+3}{2i-1}, & \text{if } N=2M+1.
   \end{cases}
\]
In either case, as $i$ increases, the numerator is decreasing while the positive denominator is increasing. Thus, the ratio is monotonically nonincreasing, establishing the log-concavity of the second factor.

Since the termwise product of two nonnegative log-concave sequences is inherently log-concave, the normalized coefficient sequence of $f^{\langle 2,0 \rangle}(z)$ is log-concave. Furthermore, because $f(z)$ possesses no internal zeros and $b_0 > 0$, 
we have $f^{\langle 2,0 \rangle}(z) \in \operatorname{ULC}(M)$.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:dilation-double-ulc}
The operation of doubling a polytope preserves ultra log-concavity with respect to both the dimension and the actual degree. Consequently, this preservation extends to any dilation factor of the form $r=2^k$, where $k\geq 0$.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-29: body_md -->
Suppose that $h_P^*(z) \in \operatorname{ULC}(d)$. By Liggett's convolution theorem, we have
\[(1+z)^{d+1}h_P^*(z)\in\operatorname{ULC}(2d+1).
\]
By \cref{lem:even-ulc} with $N=2d+1$ and \eqref{eq:dilation-extraction} with $r=2$, we obtian $h_{2P}^*(z) \in \operatorname{ULC}(d)$.

Now, assume the stronger condition that $h_P^*(z) \in \operatorname{ULC}(s_P)$. In this case, Liggett's theorem implies that the product resides in $\operatorname{ULC}(s_P+d+1)$. A subsequent application of the same \cref{lem:even-ulc} deduces that
\[ h_{2P}^*(z) \in \operatorname{ULC}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{s_P+d+1}{2} \right\rfloor\right).
\]
According to the degree formula \eqref{eq:dilation-degree}, we find
\[
 \deg h_{2P}^* = d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1-s_P}{2}\right\rceil = \left\lfloor\frac{s_P+d+1}{2}\right\rfloor.
\]
Observe that this derived order coincides precisely with the new actual degree of $h_{2P}^*(z)$.

To establish the assertion for the factor $r=2^k$, we iterate the doubling procedure successively across the sequence of polytopes $P, 2P, \ldots, 2^{k-1}P$. The dimension $d$ remains invariant throughout this process, while the actual-degree preservation applies dynamically to the updated degree at each iterative step. This completes the proof.

<!-- theorem-28: body_md -->
Integral dilations preserve spanning, IDP, and very ampleness.
<!-- end theorem-28 -->
<!-- proof-30: body_md -->
Given a $v\in P\cap\mathbb Z^d$. For any $x\in P$, we have 
\[(r-1)v+x=r\left(\frac{r-1}{r}v+\frac1r x\right)\in rP.
\]
Therefore, we have $(r-1)v+P\subseteq rP$.
Thus $rv$ and $(r-1)v+x$ are lattice points of $rP$ whenever $x\in P\cap\mathbb Z^d$. Their difference is $x-v$.
If these differences generate $\mathbb Z^d$ for $P$, they also do so for $rP$. Thus integral dilations preserve spanning.

Now suppose $P$ is IDP. Set $u\in n(rP)\cap\mathbb Z^d=(nr)P\cap\mathbb Z^d$.
Write $u=u_1+\cdots+u_{nr}$ with $u_i\in P\cap\mathbb Z^d$.
Partition the $nr$ summands into $n$ groups of $r$.
Each group sum is a lattice point of $rP$, because the
average of the $r$ points belongs to $P$ by convexity.
Their $n$ group sums decompose $u$ as required.

Finally, suppose $P$ is very ample. We choose an integer $N$ such that every lattice point of $mP$ decomposes into $m$
lattice points of $P$ whenever $m\geq N$. For every integer $n\geq\lceil N/r\rceil$, the preceding argument applies with $m=nr\geq N$. Hence every lattice point of $n(rP)$ decomposes into $n$ lattice points of $rP$, and $rP$ is very ample.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:fractional-ulc-convolution}
Let $0\leq\alpha\leq1$. If $f\in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha(A)$ and $g\in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha(B)$, then $fg\in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha(A+B)$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-31: body_md -->
Write $f(z)=\sum_{i=0}^A a_i z^i$ and $g(z)=\sum_{i=0}^B b_i z^i$, and homogenize them as
\[ F(w_1,w_2)=\sum_{i=0}^A a_i w_1^i w_2^{A-i},
 \qquad
 G(w_1,w_2)=\sum_{i=0}^B b_i w_1^i w_2^{B-i}.
\]
On a polynomial ring in any finite list of variables, define a linear operator by the monomial rule
\[ N_\alpha(w^\nu)=\frac{w^\nu}{(\nu!)^{1-\alpha}}.
\]
For $N_\alpha(F)$, the sequence in the two variable Lorentzian characterization is
\[\frac{a_i}{(i!(A-i)!)^{1-\alpha}\binom Ai}=\frac{a_i(i!(A-i)!)^\alpha}{A!}=\frac{a_i}{\binom Ai^\alpha}(A!)^{\alpha-1}.
\]
The last factor is a positive constant independent of $i$. Thus $N_\alpha(F)$ is Lorentzian by the hypothesis on $f$.
The same calculation shows that $N_\alpha(G)$ is Lorentzian. It remains to show that $N_\alpha(FG)$ is Lorentzian.

Set $K=\max\{A,B\}$. Use two independent input lists $x=(x_1,x_2)$ and $y=(y_1,y_2)$, and define a linear operator
on the full coordinate-degree space with bounds $(K,K,K,K)$ by
\[C_\alpha(x^\nu y^\mu)=\left(\frac{\nu!\mu!}{(\nu+\mu)!}\right)^{1-\alpha} w^{\nu+\mu},
 \qquad
 0\leq\nu_j,\mu_j\leq K\quad(j=1,2).
\]
Its output bounds are $(2K,2K)$, and it is homogeneous of degree zero on the entire input space. Its symbol, with auxiliary lists
$u=(u_1,u_2)$ and $v=(v_1,v_2)$, is
\[\prod_{j=1}^2\left(\sum_{a,b=0}^K\binom Ka\binom Kb\left(\frac{a!b!}{(a+b)!}\right)^{1-\alpha}w_j^{a+b}u_j^{K-a}v_j^{K-b}\right).
\]
This is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $4K$ in six variables, as required for an operator with four input variables, two output
variables, and degree shift zero.

Consider one factor, with variables denoted by $w,u,v$. The exponent triple $(a+b,K-a,K-b)$ determines $(a,b)$ uniquely.
Consequently, multiplying the coefficient of this monomial by its monomial factorial gives its normalized coefficient without merging any terms. 
That coefficient is
\[ \binom Ka\binom Kb \left(\frac{a!b!}{(a+b)!}\right)^{1-\alpha} (a+b)!(K-a)!(K-b)!
=(K!)^2\left(\frac{(a+b)!}{a!b!}\right)^\alpha =(K!)^2\binom{a+b}{a}^{\alpha}.
\]
On the other hand, the normalized coefficient of the same monomial in $(w+u)^K(w+v)^K$ is
$ (K!)^2\binom{a+b}{a}$. It follows, coefficient by coefficient, that the factor in the symbol equals
\[ (K!)^{2(1-\alpha)} R_\alpha\bigl((w+u)^K(w+v)^K\bigr).
\]
The linear forms $w+u$ and $w+v$ are Lorentzian. Their powers and their product are Lorentzian by the product theorem, including
$K=0$, when the product is the constant polynomial $1$.
The normalized coefficient power theorem shows that the displayed factor is Lorentzian. Multiplication by its positive constant
preserves its support and multiplies every relevant Hessian by that constant, so it preserves the Lorentzian property.
The product theorem then shows that the full symbol of $C_\alpha$ is Lorentzian. All the hypotheses of the operator theorem have now been verified, so $C_\alpha$ preserves the Lorentzian property on its input space.

The polynomials $N_\alpha(F)(x)$ and $N_\alpha(G)(y)$ are Lorentzian in the four input variables, and their product is
Lorentzian. This product belongs to the input space because each coordinate exponent is at most $K$. For every pair of monomials,
the factorial factors cancel as follows:
\[ C_\alpha\left( \frac{x^\nu y^\mu}{(\nu!\mu!)^{1-\alpha}} \right)
 =\frac{w^{\nu+\mu}}{((\nu+\mu)!)^{1-\alpha}}.
\]
By applying this identity to all monomial pairs and using linearity, we have
\[ C_\alpha\bigl(N_\alpha(F)(x)N_\alpha(G)(y)\bigr) =N_\alpha(FG)(w).
\]
Terms with the same exponent $\nu+\mu$ are summed on both sides, exactly as in the product $FG$. Therefore $N_\alpha(FG)$ is
Lorentzian by the operator theorem.

Finally, $FG$ is the homogenization of $fg$ in order $A+B$. For $0\leq i\leq A+B$, the same normalization calculation gives
\[\frac{[w_1^iw_2^{A+B-i}]N_\alpha(FG)}{\binom{A+B}i}=\frac{[z^i](fg)}{\binom{A+B}i^\alpha}((A+B)!)^{\alpha-1}.
\]
The sequences on the two sides differ by one positive constant, so they have the same support and satisfy the same log-concavity
inequalities. The Lorentzian property of $N_\alpha(FG)$ therefore gives exactly the defining condition for $\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha(A+B)$.
The product $fg$ is nonzero because both factors are nonzero with nonnegative coefficients. Hence $fg\in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha(A+B)$.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:logarithmic-comparison}
The function
\[f(x)=\log\left(1+\frac1x\right),\qquad x>0,
\]
is positive and decreasing, and $\log f$ is convex. For every fixed $y\geq0$, the quotient $f(x+y)/f(x)$ is nondecreasing in $x$.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-32: body_md -->
The positivity follows from $1+1/x>1$. We have
\[ f'(x)=-\frac1{x(x+1)},\qquad f''(x)=\frac{2x+1}{x^2(x+1)^2}.
\]
In particular, $f(x)$ is decreasing. The second derivative of its logarithm is
\[(\log f)''(x)=\frac{f(x)f''(x)-f'(x)^2}{f(x)^2}=\frac{(2x+1)f(x)-1}{x^2(x+1)^2f(x)^2}.
\]
The integral representation
\[
 f(x)=\int_x^{x+1}\frac{dt}{t}\geq\frac1{x+1}
\]
implies $(2x+1)f(x)-1\geq x/(x+1)>0$. Hence $\log f(x)$ is convex, and its first derivative is nondecreasing. 
For fixed $y\geq0$, it follows that
\[\frac{d}{dx}\log\frac{f(x+y)}{f(x)}= (\log f)'(x+y)-(\log f)'(x)\geq0.
\]
Since the quotient is positive, this proves the assertion.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:fractional-ulc-section}
Let $r\geq2$, $N\geq0$, and $0\leq e<r$ be integers, with $e\leq N$, and set $D=\lfloor(N-e)/r\rfloor$. For $1\leq k\leq D-1$, we have
\[\left(\frac{\binom{N}{rk+e}^2}{\binom{N}{r(k-1)+e}\binom{N}{r(k+1)+e}}\right)^{53/(40r)}
 \geq\frac{\binom{D}{k}^2}{\binom{D}{k-1}\binom{D}{k+1}}.
\]
Consequently, if $h(z)\in\operatorname{ULC}_{53/(40r)}(N)$, then $h^{\langle r,e\rangle}(z)$ is zero or belongs to $\operatorname{ULC}(D)$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-33: body_md -->
If $D\leq1$, the case is trivial. Suppose $D\geq2$ and fix $1\leq k\leq D-1$. Write $N-e=rD+b$, where $0\leq b<r$, and set $n=rk+e$. Then $n-r\geq0$ and $n+r\leq N$. By canceling the factorials, we obtain
\begin{align*}
 \frac{\binom{N}{n}^2}{\binom{N}{n-r}\binom{N}{n+r}}
 &=\frac{(n-r)!(n+r)!}{(n!)^2}\frac{(N-n+r)!(N-n-r)!}{((N-n)!)^2}\\
 &=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(1+\frac{r}{n-r+j}\right)\left(1+\frac{r}{N-n-r+j}\right).
\end{align*}
Because $e,b\leq r-1$, the positive denominators satisfy
\begin{align*}
 n-r+j&=r(k-1)+e+j\leq rk+j-1,\\
 N-n-r+j&=r(D-k-1)+b+j\leq r(D-k)+j-1.
\end{align*}
It follows that
\begin{align*}
 \log\left(1+\frac{r}{n-r+j}\right)&\geq f\left(k+\frac{j-1}{r}\right),\\
 \log\left(1+\frac{r}{N-n-r+j}\right)&\geq f\left(D-k+\frac{j-1}{r}\right),
\end{align*}
where $f$ is the function in \cref{lem:logarithmic-comparison}.

For $x>0$, set $I(x)=\int_0^1f(x+t)\,dt$. On each of the $r$ subintervals of length $1/r$, the decreasing function $t\mapsto f(x+t)$ does not exceed its value at the left endpoint. 
By integrating over these subintervals and summing the results, we have
\[\frac1r\sum_{j=1}^{r}f\left(x+\frac{j-1}{r}\right)\geq I(x).
\]
For each $t\in[0,1]$, \cref{lem:logarithmic-comparison} states that $f(x+t)/f(x)$ is nondecreasing in $x$. Thus, for $x\geq1$,
\[\frac{I(x)}{f(x)}=\int_0^1\frac{f(x+t)}{f(x)}\,dt\geq\int_0^1\frac{f(1+t)}{f(1)}\,dt
=\frac{3\log3-4\log2}{\log2}.
\]
For the final equality, $f(1)=\log2$, and integration of $\log(u+1)-\log u$ from $u=1$ to $u=2$ gives $I(1)=3\log3-4\log2$. 
The choice of the exponent is justified by
\[\frac{53}{40}(3\log3-4\log2)\geq\log2.
\]
Indeed, this is equivalent to $3^{53}\geq2^{84}$.
Consequently, we obtain $(53/40)I(x)\geq f(x)$ for every $x\geq1$.
By applying the preceding estimates at $x=k$ and $x=D-k$, we prove
\begin{align*}
\frac{53}{40r}\log\frac{\binom{N}{n}^2}{\binom{N}{n-r}\binom{N}{n+r}}
 &\geq\frac{53}{40}\bigl(I(k)+I(D-k)\bigr)\geq f(k)+f(D-k)\\
 &=\log\frac{(k+1)(D-k+1)}{k(D-k)}=\log\frac{\binom{D}{k}^2}{\binom{D}{k-1}\binom{D}{k+1}}.
\end{align*}
By exponentiating, we obtain the required binomial inequality.

Now write $h(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_nz^n$ and set $q_n=a_n/\binom{N}{n}^{53/(40r)}$. 
The hypothesis states that $(q_n)$ is log-concave and its positive entries form an interval. 
We claim that $q_n^2\geq q_{n-r}q_{n+r}$. 
If either term on the right is zero, the claim follows from nonnegativity. 
Otherwise every entry from $q_{n-r}$ to $q_{n+r}$ is positive. 
Ordinary log-concavity says that the ratios $q_j/q_{j-1}$ are nonincreasing on this interval, and therefore
\[\frac{q_n}{q_{n-r}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{q_{n-r+j}}{q_{n-r+j-1}}\geq\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{q_{n+j}}{q_{n+j-1}}
 =\frac{q_{n+r}}{q_n}.\]
This proves the claim. By combining it with the binomial inequality, we have
\[a_{rk+e}^2\geq\frac{\binom{D}{k}^2}{\binom{D}{k-1}\binom{D}{k+1}}a_{r(k-1)+e}a_{r(k+1)+e},\qquad 1\leq k\leq D-1.
\]
Therefore, the sequence of coefficients of the section, when divided by $\binom{D}{k}$, is log-concave. Furthermore, because the support of $(a_n)$ is contiguous, any intermediate coefficient between two positive entries of the section necessarily corresponds to an index within the support of $(a_n)$, ensuring its positivity. Thus a nonzero section has no internal zeros and belongs to $\operatorname{ULC}(D)$.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:uniform-third-fractional}
Let $R\geq2$ be an integer and set
\[ c_n=[z^n](1+z+\cdots+z^R)^3,\qquad 0\leq n\leq3R.
\]
Then the sequence 
\[ \left(\frac{c_n}{\binom{3R}{n}^{\,53/(40(R+1))}}\right)_{n=0}^{3R}
\]
is log-concave.

Set $\alpha=53/(40(R+1))$, so $0<\alpha<1$. Both $(c_n)$ and $(\binom{3R}{n})$ are positive and symmetric. It suffices to prove the
normalized log-concavity inequality for $1\leq n\leq\lfloor3R/2\rfloor$.

Suppose first that $1\leq n\leq R-1$. For $0\leq m\leq R$, every weak composition of $m$ into three parts has all entries at most $R$, so $c_m=\binom{m+2}{2}$. By applying this formula at $m=n-1,n,n+1$, we have
\[\frac{c_n^2}{c_{n-1}c_{n+1}}=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{n(n+3)}.
\]
According to
$$\log \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{n(n+3)} = \log \frac{n+1}{n} - \log \frac{n+3}{n+2}= \log\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right) - \log\left(1 + \frac{1}{n+2}\right)$$
and $f(x) = \log(1+1/x)$, the logarithm of this ratio simplifies to $f(n) - f(n+2)$.
For the binomial sequence $\binom{3R}{n}$, we have
$$\log\frac{\binom{3R}{n}^2}{\binom{3R}{n-1}\binom{3R}{n+1}}
=\log\frac{n+1}{n} \cdot \frac{3R-n+1}{3R-n} 
= \log\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)+\log \left(1 + \frac{1}{3R-n}\right)=f(n) + f(3R-n).$$
Their quotient is
\[
 \frac{f(n)-f(n+2)}{f(n)+f(3R-n)}
 =\frac{1-f(n+2)/f(n)}{1+f(3R-n)/f(n)}.
\]
\cref{lem:logarithmic-comparison} implies that $f(n+2)/f(n)$
is nondecreasing in $n$. Furthermore, $f(3R-n)$ is nondecreasing and
$f(n)$ is positive and nonincreasing, so $f(3R-n)/f(n)$ is nondecreasing.
The numerator of the last quotient is positive and nonincreasing,
and its denominator is positive and nondecreasing. The quotient is
therefore minimized at $n=R-1$. 
Therefore, we have
$$\frac{f(n)-f(n+2)}{f(n)+f(3R-n)}\geq \frac{f(R-1) - f(R+1)}{f(R-1) + f(2R+1)}= \frac{\log\frac{R(R+1)}{(R-1)(R+2)}}{\log\frac{2R(R+1)}{(R-1)(2R+1)}}.$$
It remains to prove
\[40(R+1)\log\frac{R(R+1)}{(R-1)(R+2)}\geq53\log\frac{2R(R+1)}{(R-1)(2R+1)}.
\]

We prove this inequality for real $R\geq2$. Define the difference
\[\Phi(R)=40(R+1)\log\frac{R(R+1)}{(R-1)(R+2)}-53\log\frac{2R(R+1)}{(R-1)(2R+1)}.
\]
For $x\geq1$, the inequality $\log x\leq(x-x^{-1})/2$ follows by subtracting the left side from the right side: the difference vanishes at $x=1$, and its derivative is $(x-1)^2/(2x^2)\geq0$. Hence
\[ \log\frac{R(R+1)}{(R-1)(R+2)} \leq\frac{2(R^2+R-1)}{R(R-1)(R+1)(R+2)}.
\]
Furthermore, we have
\begin{align*}
\Phi'(R)&=40\log\frac{R(R+1)}{(R-1)(R+2)}+40(R+1)\left(\frac1R+\frac1{R+1}-\frac1{R-1}-\frac1{R+2}\right)
\\&\qquad\qquad -53\left(\frac1R+\frac1{R+1}-\frac1{R-1}-\frac2{2R+1}\right).
\end{align*}
By replacing the logarithm by its upper bound and writing the rational terms over the common denominator, we obtain
\[\Phi'(R)\leq-\frac{R^3-24R^2+215R+54}{R(R-1)(R+1)(R+2)(2R+1)}<0.
\]
The denominator is positive. The numerator is positive because its value at $R=2$ is $396$, and its derivative is
$3(R-8)^2+23>0$. 
Finally, we prove that $\Phi(R)\to0$ as $R\to\infty$. For the first term, the estimate $\log x\leq x-1$ gives
\[0\leq(R+1)\log\frac{R(R+1)}{(R-1)(R+2)}\leq\frac{2(R+1)}{(R-1)(R+2)}\longrightarrow0;
\]
the second logarithm also tends to 0. 
Since $\Phi$ is decreasing with limit zero, we obtain $\Phi(R)\geq0$ for $R\geq2$. 

We have shown that the value of the quotient at the minimum point $n=R-1$ is at least $\alpha=\frac{53}{40(R+1)}$.
Therefore, we obtain 
\[ f(n)-f(n+2)\geq\alpha \bigl(f(n)+f(3R-n)\bigr),\qquad 1\leq n\leq R-1.
\]
By taking the exponential, we obtain:
$$\frac{c_n^2}{c_{n-1}c_{n+1}}=e^{f(n)-f(n+2)}\geq e^{\alpha(f(n)+f(3R-n))}=\left(\frac{\binom{3R}{n}^2}{\binom{3R}{n-1}\binom{3R}{n+1}}\right)^\alpha.$$
This proves the normalized log-concavity inequality on the first range $1\leq n\leq R-1$.

Now suppose $R\leq n\leq\lfloor3R/2\rfloor$. 
For each $m\in\{n-1,n,n+1\}$, an application of the inclusion-exclusion principle to the weak compositions of $m$ into three parts yields
\[c_m=\frac{(m+1)(m+2)}{2}-3\frac{(m-R)(m-R+1)}{2}.
\]
The subtracted term accounts for configurations where $m\geq R+1$ and a specified entry exceeds $R$. 
A bijection with the weak compositions of $m-R-1$ into three parts is established by deducting $R+1$ from this designated entry, which contributes $\binom{m-R+1}{2}$ possibilities for each of the three coordinate choices. The upper bound $m\leq\lfloor3R/2\rfloor+1<2(R+1)$ guarantees that no two coordinates can simultaneously exceed $R$, confirming that no composition is excluded twice. Furthermore, the subtracted polynomial evaluates to zero at the boundary indices $m=R-1$ and $m=R$, ensuring the formula remains valid there. An expansion of this identity ultimately produces
\[c_m=\frac{3}{4}R^2+\frac{3}{2}R+1-\left(m-\frac{3}{2}R\right)^2.
\]
Set
\[ H=\frac34R^2+\frac32R+1,\qquad w=\left(n-\frac32R\right)^2.
\]
Then $0\leq w\leq R^2/4$. Using the formula for the three coefficients, we obtain
\begin{align*}
 c_n&=H-w,\qquad c_{n-1}c_{n+1}=(H-1-w)^2-4w,\\
 c_n^2-c_{n-1}c_{n+1}&=2H-1+2w,\qquad n(3R-n)=\frac94R^2-w.
\end{align*}
All three coefficients are positive, so the denominator below is
positive. The identities yield
\[
 n(3R-n)\left(\frac{c_n^2}{c_{n-1}c_{n+1}}-1\right)
 =\frac{(9R^2/4-w)(2H-1+2w)}{(H-1-w)^2-4w}.
\]
This expression is at least its value at $w=0$. Indeed, after
multiplying by the positive denominators, the difference is
\begin{align*}
 &(9R^2/4-w)(2H-1+2w)(H-1)^2-(9R^2/4)(2H-1)\bigl((H-1-w)^2-4w\bigr)
\\=& \frac{9R^2w(R+1)}{16}\bigl(12R^3+33R^2+32R+12-(8R+12)w\bigr).
\end{align*}
The identity follows by expanding in $w$ and substituting the displayed
value of $H$. Its right side is nonnegative, since $w\leq R^2/4$ implies
\[12R^3+33R^2+32R+12-(8R+12)w>0.\]
Consequently, we obtain 
\begin{align*}
n(3R-n)\left(\frac{c_n^2}{c_{n-1}c_{n+1}}-1\right)\geq\frac{(9R^2/4)(2H-1)}{(H-1)^2}=\frac{2(3R^2+6R+2)}{(R+2)^2}.
\end{align*}
The lower bound exceeds $\alpha(3R+1)$, as the following identity shows
\begin{align*}
\frac{2(3R^2+6R+2)}{(R+2)^2}-\frac{53(3R+1)}{40(R+1)}=\frac{81(R-2)^3+517(R-2)^2+888(R-2)+304}{40(R+1)(R+2)^2}>0.
\end{align*}
We have therefore proved
\[\frac{c_n^2}{c_{n-1}c_{n+1}} \geq1+\alpha\frac{3R+1}{n(3R-n)}.
\]
For $0<\alpha<1$, concavity of $x\mapsto(1+x)^\alpha$ on $[0,\infty)$ bounds this function by its tangent at zero:
$(1+x)^\alpha\leq1+\alpha x$. By applying this inequality, we obtain
\[\frac{c_n^2}{c_{n-1}c_{n+1}}\geq\left(1+\frac{3R+1}{n(3R-n)}\right)^\alpha=\left(\frac{\binom{3R}{n}^2}
{\binom{3R}{n-1}\binom{3R}{n+1}}\right)^\alpha.
\]
This proves normalized log-concavity on the second range. The first range was proved above, and symmetry covers the remaining indices. This completes the proof.

\label{lem:uniform-fourth-fractional}
Let $R\geq2$ be an integer and set
\[c_n=[z^n](1+z+\cdots+z^R)^4,\qquad 0\leq n\leq4R.
\]
Then we have 
\[\frac{c_n^2}{c_{n-1}c_{n+1}}\geq1+\frac{6R}{(R+1)n(4R-n)},\qquad 1\leq n\leq4R-1.
\]
In particular, the sequence 
$$\left(\frac{c_n}{\binom{4R}{n}^{53/(40(R+1))}}\right)_{n=0}^{4R}$$ is log-concave.

We know that all coefficients are positive, and $c_n=c_{4R-n}$. It suffices to prove the rational inequality for $1\leq n\leq2R$.
For $1\leq n\leq R-1$, the upper bound on the entries excludes no weak composition at the three indices $n-1,n,n+1$. Thus
$c_m=\binom{m+3}{3}$ at these indices. Therefore, we have
\[ \frac{c_n^2}{c_{n-1}c_{n+1}} =\frac{(n+1)(n+3)}{n(n+4)} =1+\frac3{n(n+4)}.
\]
It remains to compare $3/(n(n+4))$ with $6R/((R+1)n(4R-n))$. Since the denominators are positive, the required comparison is equivalent to
\[3(R+1)(4R-n)-6R(n+4)\geq0.
\]
The left side is a decreasing function of $n$. Its value at $n=R-1$ is
\[3(R+1)(3R+1)-6R(R+3)=3(R-1)^2\geq0.
\]
This proves the assertion on the first range.

For $R\leq n\leq2R$ and $m\in\{n-1,n,n+1\}$, the counting formula is
\[ 6c_m=(m+1)(m+2)(m+3) -4(m-R)(m-R+1)(m-R+2).
\]
For $m\geq R+1$, subtracting $R+1$ from a specified entry larger than $R$ gives a weak composition of $m-R-1$ into four parts. The number of such compositions is $\binom{m-R+2}{3}$, and there are four choices of the specified entry. Two entries cannot both exceed $R$, because $m\leq2R+1<2(R+1)$. At $m=R-1,R$, the subtracted polynomial is zero. This proves the formula throughout the required range.

To display the sign of the resulting polynomial inequality, we set $a=2R-n$ and $b=n-R$. Then we have $a,b\geq0$, $R=a+b$, and $n=a+2b$. The three formulas are
\begin{align*}
 6c_{n-1}&=(a+2b)(a+2b+1)(a+2b+2)-4(b-1)b(b+1),\\
 6c_n&=(a+2b+1)(a+2b+2)(a+2b+3)-4b(b+1)(b+2),\\
 6c_{n+1}&=(a+2b+2)(a+2b+3)(a+2b+4)-4(b+1)(b+2)(b+3).
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}

where the complete list of coefficient polynomials is
\begin{align*}

\\ A_1(a)&=a(33a^5+256a^4+395a^3+248a^2+100a+96)

\\ A_4(a)&=8(55a^3+74a^2+52a+26)
\\ A_5(a)&=16(17a^2+24a+13)
\\ A_6(a)&=128(a+1)
\\ A_7(a)&=32
\end{align*}
Here every coefficient is nonnegative for $a\geq0$. As $a+b=R\geq2$, either $a>0$, in which
case $A_0(a)>0$, or $b>0$, in which case $A_7(a)b^7>0$. Thus the expression inside parentheses on the left is positive. After division by $(R+1)n(4R-n)c_{n-1}c_{n+1}>0$, this gives

This is the asserted log-concavity.

The degree formula in \cref{prop:dilation-formula} gives
\begin{align*}
s_{rP}=d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1-s_P}{r}\right\rceil=\left\lfloor d+1-\frac{d+1-s_P}{r}\right\rfloor
=\left\lfloor\frac{s_P+(r-1)(d+1)}r\right\rfloor.
\end{align*}
Here we used $\lfloor m-x\rfloor=m-\lceil x\rceil$ for an integer $m$.
If $d=0$, both numerators are $1$, and both assertions follow. We may therefore suppose $d\geq1$. The case $r=1$ is immediate, and
the case $r=2$ is \cref{prop:dilation-double-ulc}.

Suppose $1\leq d\leq4$ and $r\geq3$. Under either hypothesis, $h_P^*(z)$ is log-concave and has no internal zeros, because
$\operatorname{ULC}_1(A)\subseteq\operatorname{ULC}_0(A)$. Moreover, $s_P\leq d<2r$. Zhang's result \cite[Corollary~3.3]{Zhang}, as stated in \cref{thm:zhang-veronese}, therefore shows that $(B_r^{d+1}h_P^*)^{\langle r,0\rangle}=h_{rP}^*$ is real-rooted.
The coefficients of $h_{rP}^*$ are nonnegative and its constant coefficient is one. Thus its real zeros are negative, and its factorization into terms $1+\lambda z$, with $\lambda>0$, shows that every coefficient through its degree is positive; the constant case is the empty product. Newton's inequalities \cite[Theorem~2]{StanleyLC} then give $h_{rP}^*\in\operatorname{ULC}(s_{rP})$. Since $s_{rP}\leq d$, \cref{lem:ulc-order} also gives $h_{rP}^*\in\operatorname{ULC}(d)$.
This proves both assertions in these dimensions.

It remains to consider $d\geq5$ and $r\geq3$. Set $\alpha=53/(40r)$, and choose $A=d$ for the first assertion and
$A=s_P$ for the second. In either case the hypothesis is $h_P^*\in\operatorname{ULC}(A)$, so the inclusion established in the preceding subsection gives $h_P^*\in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha(A)$.

Every integer $L\geq6$ has the form $3a+4b$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$. Indeed, if $L$ is congruent to $0$, $1$, or $2$
modulo $3$, choose $b=0$, $1$, or $2$, respectively, and set $a=(L-4b)/3$. This integer is nonnegative: the smallest admissible
$L$ in the three cases is $6$, $7$, or $8$. Applying this fact to $L=d+1$ gives $d+1=3a+4b$.
The \cref{lem:uniform-third-fractional,lem:uniform-fourth-fractional}, with $R=r-1\geq2$, state that
\[ B_r^3\in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha(3(r-1)),\qquad B_r^4\in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha(4(r-1)).
\]
By repeated application of \cref{lem:fractional-ulc-convolution}, the normalization orders add when the factors are multiplied. Thus
\[ B_r^{d+1}=(B_r^3)^a(B_r^4)^b \in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha\bigl((r-1)(d+1)\bigr).
\]
If $a=0$ or $b=0$, only the factors with positive exponent are used.
Multiplication by $h_P^*$ and one further application of the same \cref{lem:fractional-ulc-convolution} yield
\[B_r^{d+1}h_P^*\in\operatorname{ULC}_\alpha\bigl(A+(r-1)(d+1)\bigr).
\]
Its constant coefficient is $h_0^*(P)=1$, so its zero-offset section is nonzero. 
\cref{lem:fractional-ulc-section}, with $e=0$, and \eqref{eq:dilation-extraction} now give
\[ h_{rP}^*=(B_r^{d+1}h_P^*)^{\langle r,0\rangle} \in\operatorname{ULC}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{A+(r-1)(d+1)}r\right\rfloor\right).
\]
For $A=d$, the numerator inside the floor is $rd+r-1$, and hence the order is $d$. For $A=s_P$, the order is $s_{rP}$ by the degree identity at the start of the proof. These are the two required conclusions.
