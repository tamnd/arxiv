\[
r_{in}=\frac{\sin \vartheta}{\beta(1-\sin\vartheta)}.
\]
Moreover, we can also express the area of $D_{\beta, \vartheta}$ in terms of $\beta$ and $\vartheta$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq: area dumbbell}
|D_{\beta,\vartheta}|=2\left(\frac{1}{\beta^2}(\pi-\vartheta)+\left(r_{in}+\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^2\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta-r_{in}^2\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\vartheta\right)\right).
\end{equation}
Now we can show that, for any given $\beta\in\left(\sqrt{2},3/2\right)$, there exists a value of $\vartheta$ such that $|D_{\beta, \vartheta}|=\pi$.
Indeed, plugging the expression for $r_{in}$ into~\eqref{eq: area dumbbell} we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
|D_{\beta,\vartheta}|=& 2\left(\frac{1}{\beta^2}(\pi-\vartheta)+\left(\frac{\sin \vartheta}{\beta(1-\sin\vartheta)}+\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^2\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta-\left(\frac{\sin \vartheta}{\beta(1-\sin\vartheta)}\right)^2\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\vartheta\right)\right)\\
=&\frac{2}{\beta^2}\left(\pi -\vartheta+\frac{\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta}{(1-\sin\vartheta)^2}-\frac{\sin^2\vartheta}{(1-\sin\vartheta)^2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\vartheta\right)\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
Now, computing this for $\vartheta=0$ and $\vartheta=\pi/5$, we find
\[
|D_{\beta,0}|=\frac{2\pi}{\beta^2},\qquad |D_{\beta,\frac{\pi}{5}}|=\frac{2}{\beta^2}\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}+\frac{\sin(\pi/5)}{\big(1-\sin(\pi/5)\big)^2}\left(\cos(\pi/5)-\frac{3\pi}{10}\sin(\pi/5)\right)\right),
\]
which, for $\beta\in(\sqrt{2}, 1.47)$, give us $|D_{\beta,0}|<\pi$ and $|D_{\beta,\pi/5}|>\pi$.
By continuity of $|D_{\beta, \vartheta}|$, we can conclude that for at least one value of $\vartheta$, that we call $\vartheta_0$, we have the desired area.
Let us denote by $D_{\beta}=D_{\beta, \vartheta_0}$ such set, that satisfies the area constraint.

Then, we can express and compute the functional ${\mathcal G}$ in terms of $\beta$ and $\vartheta_0$.
Let us start with the perimeter of $D_\beta$.
First, we introduce an auxiliary variable, that is, the angle $\varphi$ in Figure~\ref{fig:quarter of dumbbell}, which is given by
\[
\varphi=\arcsin\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\sin\vartheta_0\right).
\]
Now some easy trigonometrical computations lead us to the following formula for the perimeter:
\begin{equation*}
P(D_{\beta})=4\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\beta}(\pi -\vartheta_0)+r_{in}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\vartheta_0\right)+\cos\varphi-r_{in}\cos\vartheta_0 \right),
\end{equation*}
where, inside the parentheses, the first term is the length of the outer arc of circle, the second one is the length of the inner arc of circle and the rest is the length of the segment connecting the arc to the origin.
Hence, the isoperimetric deficit of $D_{\beta}$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq: deficit dumbbell}
\delta(D_{\beta})=\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}(\pi -\vartheta_0)+r_{in}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\vartheta_0\right)+\cos\varphi-r_{in}\cos\vartheta_0 \right)-1.
\end{equation}
Now we can compute the barycentric asymmetry.
It is easy to compute the area of $D_{\beta}\setminus B$ as 
\[
|D_{\beta}\setminus B|=4\cdot \left(\frac{1}{2\beta^2}(\pi-\vartheta_0)+\frac{1}{2\beta}\sin\vartheta_0\left(\cos\varphi+\frac{1}{\beta}\cos\vartheta_0\right)-\frac{\varphi}{2}\right).
\]
Hence, taking into account that $|D_{\beta}\Delta B|=2|D_{\beta}\setminus B|$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq: asimmetria dumbbell}
\lambda_0(D_{\beta})=\frac{8}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{2\beta^2}(\pi-\vartheta_0)+\frac{1}{2\beta}\sin\vartheta_0\left(\cos\varphi+\frac{1}{\beta}\cos\vartheta_0\right)-\frac{\varphi}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
Finally, we choose $\beta=1.43$; then, an admissible value of $\vartheta_0$ (that is, a value such that $|D_{\beta,\vartheta}|=\pi$) is $\vartheta_0\approx 21^\circ$. With this choice of $\beta$ and $\vartheta_0$ (which are the values used to plot Figure~\ref{fig:dumbbell}), \eqref{eq: deficit dumbbell} and~\eqref{eq: asimmetria dumbbell} give $\delta(D_{\beta})\approx 0.919$ and $\lambda_0(D_\beta)\approx 1.922$, thus finding ${\mathcal G}(D_\beta)\approx 0.2488<1/4$.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{formulaabc}
Let $E$ be a minimizer of ${\mathcal G}$ among connected sets of area $\pi$ and barycenter in the origin. Then, each path $\tau_i:\mathbb S^1\to\mathbb R^2$ is a ${\rm C}^{1,1}$ curve and there are three constants $\alpha,\, \beta,\,\gamma$ such that, up to a rotation, the curvature $\kappa(x,y)$ at any simple point $(x,y)\in\partial E\setminus \partial B$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{abc}
\kappa(x,y)=\alpha x + \beta - \gamma \hbox{{\large $\chi$}{\Large $_{_{B}}$}}\,,
\end{equation}
where the constant $\gamma$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{formulagamma}
\gamma= 8\, \frac{\delta (E)}{\lambda_0(E)}=8\,\lambda_0(E)\, {\mathcal G} (E)\,.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
It is not hard to show that a minimizer $E$ of ${\mathcal G}$ is a dumbbell (possibly without segment) if and only if the constant $\alpha$ in~\eqref{abc} vanishes.
Indeed, it is clear that a dumbbell satisfies the condition $\alpha=0$ because it must hold $\kappa(x,y)=\kappa(-x,y)$.
Reversely, by a standard use of the so-called double spherical cap symmetrization (see e.g.~\cite{G}), it can be easily proved that the condition $\alpha=0$ implies that the set $E$ is a dumbbell.

<!-- defin-2: body_md -->
A set $E\subseteq\mathbb R^N$ of finite perimeter is said to be a \emph{$\Lambda$-minimizer at a point $x\in\mathbb R^N$} for a constant $\Lambda>0$ if there exists $r_0=r_0(x)>0$ such that for every ball $B(x,r)$ with $r<r_0$ and for every set $F$ such that $F\Delta E \subset\subset B(x,r)$, one has $P(E)\leq P(F)+ \Lambda r^N$. The set $E$ is said to be a \emph{$\Lambda$-minimizer} if it is a $\Lambda$-minimizer at \emph{every} point $x\in\partial E$.

\label{Lambda-min lemma}
Let $E\subseteq\mathbb R^2$ be as in Theorem~\ref{formulaabc}. Then $E$ is a $\Lambda$-minimizer at all points of $\partial^* E$.

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $E$ be a minimizer of ${\mathcal G}$ among connected sets of area $\pi$ and barycenter in the origin, and let $\Lambda$ be a constant to be specified later. Assume that $E$ is not a $\Lambda$-minimizer at some point $x\in\partial^* E$. Then, there exist a sequence of radii $r_j\to 0$ and of sets $F_j$ such that for any $j$ the symmetric difference $E\Delta F_j$ is compactly contained in $B(x,r_j)$, and moreover
\begin{equation}\label{assumption}
P(E) > P(F_j) + \Lambda r_j^2\,.
\end{equation}
We claim that, at least for $j$ large enough, it is admissible to think that $F_j$ is connected. Indeed, since $x\in\partial^* E$, as soon as $j$ is large enough there exists a radius $r_j\leq r_j'\leq 2r_j$ such that the intersection of $E$ with the circle $\partial B(x,r_j')$ is exactly an arc of the circle (with length almost exactly half of the total length of the circle). As a consequence, since $E$ is connected then so is also $E\setminus B(x,r_j')=F_j\setminus B(x,r_j')$. Therefore, if we call $F_j'$ the connected component of $F_j$ containing $F_j\setminus B(x,r_j')$, then we have that $E\Delta F_j'$ is compactly contained in $B(x,r_j')$, and since $P(F_j')\leq P(F_j)$ then~(\ref{assumption}) holds also with $F_j'$ and $r_j'$ in place of $F_j$ and $r_j$, up to change $\Lambda$ with $\Lambda/4$. We have then proved our claim.\par

We want to build competitors for ${\mathcal G}$ starting from the sets $F_j$. Fix $j\in\mathbb N$ and denote, for the sake of readability, $F=F_j$ and $r=r_j$. Then, we define $\widetilde E$ the set obtained from $F$ with a translation and a rescaling so as to have area $\pi$ and barycenter in the origin. In other words,
\[
\widetilde E = \sigma \big( F - v\big)\,,
\]
where
\begin{align*}
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac \pi{|F|} }\,, && v = {\rm bar} F\,.
\end{align*}
In particular, since $F$ is connected than the set $\widetilde E$ is a competitor for ${\mathcal G}$, and by minimality of $E$ we have ${\mathcal G}(\widetilde E)\geq {\mathcal G}(E)$. Our goal is to find a contradiction with this inequality as soon as $r$ is small enough and $\Lambda$ is large enough. To estimate ${\mathcal G}(\widetilde E)$, we need an upper bound for $\delta(\widetilde E)$ and a lower bound for $\lambda_0(\widetilde E)$. First of all, we can easily give bounds to $\sigma$ and $v$; indeed, since $|E|=\pi$ and $E\Delta F$ is compactly contained in a ball of radius $r$, then $\pi(1-r^2)\leq |F|\leq \pi (1+r^2)$, which for $r$ small enough leads to
\begin{equation}\label{estisigma}
1 - \frac{r^2} 2 \leq \sigma \leq 1 + r^2\,.
\end{equation}
In an analogous way, to estimate $|v|$, we can let $\overline R$ be any number larger than the diameter of $E$, and notice that, as soon as $r$ is small enough (and then the ball $B(x,r)$ has distance less than $\overline R$ from the origin), since ${\rm bar} E=0$ we easily have
\[
|F| {\rm bar} F = \int_F z\, dz = \int_{F\setminus E} z\, dz - \int_{E\setminus F} z\,dz\,,
\]
and then
\[
|F| |v| \leq \pi r^2 \overline R\,,
\]
which, recalling again that $|F|\geq \pi (1-r^2)$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{estiv}
|v| \leq 2 r^2 \overline R\,.
\end{equation}
To estimate $\delta(\widetilde E)$, we just have to use that $P(\widetilde E) = \sigma P(F)$, so that by~(\ref{assumption}) and~(\ref{estisigma}) we get
				\begin{equation}\label{too}
					\delta(\widetilde E) <\frac{\sigma P(E)-\sigma \Lambda r^2-2\pi}{2\pi}=\sigma \delta(E)+\sigma-1-\frac{\sigma \Lambda}{2\pi}\, r^2
					\leq \delta(E)+ \bigg(\delta(E) +1-\frac \Lambda{4\pi}\bigg)\, r^2\,,
				\end{equation}
				where we also used~(\ref{estisigma}) and the fact that $r$ is small.\par
				
				To estimate $\lambda_0(\widetilde E)$, instead, we first notice that, since $F\Delta E$ is compactly contained in a ball of radius $r$, we have
				\[
				\big| F \Delta B\big| \geq \big| E\Delta B\big| - \big|E \Delta F\big| \geq \pi \lambda_0(E) - \pi r^2\,,
				\]
				then we notice that by the definition of $\sigma$ and~(\ref{estiv}) we have
				\[\begin{split}
					\big| (F-v) \Delta B_{1/\sigma}\big| &= \big| F \Delta (B_{1/\sigma}+v)\big| \geq \big| F\Delta B\big| - \big| B\Delta B_{1/\sigma}\big|- \big| B_{1/\sigma}\Delta (B_{1/\sigma}+v)\big|\\
					&\geq \pi \lambda_0(E) - \pi r^2-\pi \bigg|\frac 1{\sigma^2}-1\bigg|-\frac 4\sigma\, |v|
					\geq \pi \Big(\lambda_0(E) - (2+4\overline R)r^2\Big)
				\end{split}\]
				and then finally that
				\[\begin{split}
					\lambda_0(\widetilde E) &= \frac{\big|(\sigma(F-v))\Delta B\big|}\pi
					=\sigma^2\, \frac{\big|(F-v)\Delta B_{1/\sigma}\big|}\pi
					\geq (1-r^2) \Big(\lambda_0(E) - (2+4\overline R)r^2\Big)\\
					&\geq \lambda_0(E)- r^2 \big( \lambda_0(E) + 2+4\overline R\,\big)\,.
				\end{split}\]
				Since this implies
				\[
				\frac 1{\lambda_0^2(\widetilde E)} \leq \frac 1{\Big(\lambda_0(E)- r^2 \big( \lambda_0(E) + 2+4\overline R\,\big)\Big)^2}
				\leq \lambda_0^{-2}(E) + 3 r^2 \big( \lambda_0(E) + 2+4\overline R\,\big)\lambda_0(E)^{-3}
				\]
				as soon as $r$ is small enough, putting this last estimate together with~(\ref{too}) we get
				\[\begin{split}
					{\mathcal G}(\widetilde E) &= \frac{\delta(\widetilde E)}{\lambda_0^2(\widetilde E)}
					\leq \bigg(\delta(E)+ \bigg(\begin{aligned}\delta(E) +1-\frac \Lambda{4\pi}\end{aligned}\bigg)\, r^2\bigg) \, \Big(\lambda_0^{-2}(E) + 3 r^2 \big( \lambda_0(E) + 2+4\overline R\big)\lambda_0(E)^{-3}\Big)\\
					&\leq {\mathcal G}(E) + r^2 \bigg( \frac{\delta(E) +1-\frac \Lambda{4\pi}}{\lambda_0^2(E)}+ \frac{3\delta(E) \big(\lambda_0(E) + 2+4\overline R\big)}{\lambda_0^3(E)}\bigg)\,,
				\end{split}\]
				where the last inequality is true as soon as we have chosen $\Lambda> 4\pi(\delta(E)+1)$. Keeping in mind that ${\mathcal G}(E)\leq {\mathcal G}(\widetilde E)$ by minimality, we find the required contradiction as soon as we have chosen
				\[
				\Lambda> 4\pi \bigg(4 \delta(E) +1 + \frac{\delta(E)}{\lambda_0(E) } \big(6+12\overline R\big)\bigg)\,.
				\]
				This finally concludes the proof.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{smcoco}
There exist two constants $\bar\eta<2\pi$ and $N_{\rm max}\in\mathbb N$ with the following property. Given a set $E\in \mathcal R$, there exists another set $E'\in \mathcal R$ with ${\mathcal G}(E')\leq {\mathcal G}(E)$, such that $\partial E'$ consists of at most $N_{\rm max}$ connected components, and all of them except at most one have length at least $\bar\eta$. Moreover, the inequality is strict if $E'\neq E$ and ${\mathcal G}(E)<1$. Finally, if ${\mathcal G}(E)<1$, then $d_H^+(E',B_{E'})\geq d_H^+(E,B_E)$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
It is possible to fix a smooth, connected set $D\in\mathcal R$, with connected boundary and such that ${\mathcal G}(D)=1$. 
Indeed, a simple calculation ensures that the ratio ${\mathcal G}$ of a rectangle with side-lengths $2\pi$ and $1/2$ is around $0.62$, while the ratio ${\mathcal G}$ of a square of area $\pi$ amounts to approximately $3.9$. Let $\bar\eta<2\pi$ be a small constant, to be specified later, and let $E\in \mathcal R$ be any set. We want to define a set $E'\in\mathcal R$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{almostall}
\begin{array}{c}
{\mathcal G}(E')\leq {\mathcal G}(E),\, \qquad d_H^+(E',B_{E'})\geq d_H^+(E,B_E) \hbox{ if $ {\mathcal G}(E)< 1$}\,, \\
\hbox{$\partial E'$ has at most one connected component shorter than $\bar\eta$}\,.
\end{array}
\end{equation}
First of all, if ${\mathcal G}(E)\geq 1$, then we simply take $E'=D$. In this case, (\ref{almostall}) is clearly valid (keep in mind that the boundary of $D$ is made of a single connected component). Instead, if ${\mathcal G}(E)<1$ and $\partial E$ has at most one connected component shorter than $\bar\eta$, then we take $E'=E$. Also in this case, the validity of~(\ref{almostall}) is obvious.\par

We want now to define a set $E'$ satisfying~(\ref{almostall}) in the case when ${\mathcal G}(E)<1$ and $\partial E$ has at least two connected components shorter than $\bar\eta$. In this case, assuming just for simplicity of notation that ${\rm bar} (E)=0$, we let $P$ be a point in $\partial E$ such that $|P|-1$ is maximum, and then $d_H^+(E,B_E)=|P|-1$. Now, we let $\tau$ be a connected component of $\partial E$ with ${\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\tau)=\eta\leq \bar\eta$, and such that $P\notin \tau$; notice that this is possible since $P$ belongs only to one connected component of $\partial E$, and there are at least two ones which are shorter than $\bar\eta$. By regularity of $E$, we know that $\tau$ is a regular, closed curve in $\mathbb R^2$, hence $\tau=\partial G$ for some bounded set $G$. Since $E$ is connected, in particular $G$ is a ``hole'' in $E$, that is, $G\subseteq \mathbb R^2\setminus E$. By the isoperimetric inequality, we have $\eta = {\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\tau) \geq 2 \sqrt\pi \sqrt{|G|}$, so that
\begin{equation}\label{esti1}
|G| \leq \frac{\eta^2}{4\pi}\,.
\end{equation}
We write now $F_0=E\cup G$, and we define a slightly modified set $F$ as follows. We let $P'$ be the point in the line passing through $O$ and $P$, having distance $\eta/5$ from $P$, and such that $|P'|-1>|P|-1$. Then, we define $F\supseteq F_0$ modifying in a smooth way $\partial F_0$ near the point $P$ so to pass through $P'$. The situation is depicted in Figure~\ref{DefF}, where $F$ has an ``outer cusp'' near $P$. Notice that this construction makes sense thanks to the definition of $P$, and since by construction $P\in \partial F_0$. By taking a sufficiently small ``cusp'', so that $\partial F\setminus \partial F_0$ is very close to two copies of the segment $PP'$, we can have
\begin{align}\label{estiinter}
\big| F \setminus F_0 \big| \leq \frac{\eta^2}{12\pi}\,, && \big|{\rm bar}(F)-{\rm bar}(F_0)\big|\leq \frac{\eta^2}{24\pi^2}\, P(E)\,, && {\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\partial F) \leq {\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\partial F_0) + \frac \eta 2\,.
\end{align}
As a consequence, by construction and~(\ref{esti1}) we have
\begin{align}\label{estipervol}
\pi \leq |F| \leq \pi + \frac{\eta^2}{3\pi}\,, && P(F) \leq P(E) -\frac \eta 2\,.
\end{align}

Keeping in mind that the diameter of $E$ is at most $P(E)/2$, by~(\ref{esti1}) we have
\[
\pi \big|{\rm bar}(F_0)\big| \leq |F_0|\, \big|{\rm bar}(F_0)\big|=\bigg| \int_{F_0} z\,dz\bigg|=\bigg| \int_G z\,dz\bigg|\leq |G| \, \frac{P(E)}2 \leq \frac{\eta^2}{8\pi}\, P(E)\,,
\]
which by~(\ref{estiinter}) implies
\begin{equation}\label{movbar}
|{\rm bar}(F)| \leq |{\rm bar}(F_0)| + \frac{\eta^2}{24\pi^2}\, P(E)\leq \frac{\eta^2}{6\pi^2}\, P(E)\,
\end{equation}
As a consequence, recalling~(\ref{estipervol}) we have
\[
\big| B_E \Delta B_F\big| \leq 4\big|{\rm bar}(E)-{\rm bar}(F)\big| + |F|-\pi \leq \bigg(\frac 2{3\pi^2}\, P(E) + \frac 1{3\pi}\bigg)\, \eta^2\,.
\]
We deduce
\[
\lambda_0(F) |F| = \big| F \Delta B_F\big| \geq |E\Delta B_E| - |F\Delta E| - |B_E\Delta B_F| \geq \pi \lambda_0(E) -\bigg(\frac 2{3\pi^2}\, P(E) + \frac 2{3\pi}\bigg)\, \eta^2\,.
\]
An immediate check, using again~(\ref{estipervol}),
\[
\lambda_0(F) \geq \lambda_0(E) - \bigg( \frac{\lambda_0(E)}{3\pi^2}+\frac {2P(E)}{3\pi^3} + \frac 2{3\pi^2}\bigg)\, \eta^2\,.
\]
Since by definition $P(E)=2\pi (\delta(E)+1)$, while $\lambda_0\leq 2$ for any set, the above estimate implies
\begin{equation}\label{estilambda}
\lambda_0(F) \geq \lambda_0(E) - \bigg(\frac {4\delta(E)+8}{3\pi^2} \bigg)\, \eta^2\,.
\end{equation}
Moreover, (\ref{estipervol}) implies
\[
2\pi \big(\delta(F) +1\big) \leq 2 \sqrt{\pi|F|}\, \big(\delta(F) +1\big) =P(F) \leq P(E)-\frac \eta 2 =2\pi\big(\delta(E)+1\big)-\frac\eta 2\,,
\]
which implies
\begin{equation}\label{estidelta}
\delta(F)\leq \delta(E)-\frac\eta{4\pi}\,.
\end{equation}
Let us now check the validity of the inequality~(\ref{claimend}), namely, ${\mathcal G}(F)\leq {\mathcal G}(E)$. Using~(\ref{estilambda}) and~(\ref{estidelta}), the inequality is proved if
\[
\frac{\begin{aligned}\delta(E)-\frac \eta{4\pi}\end{aligned}}{\begin{aligned}\bigg(\lambda_0(E)- \bigg(\frac {4\delta(E)+8}{3\pi^2} \bigg)\, \eta^2\bigg)^2\end{aligned}} \leq \frac{\delta(E)}{\lambda_0(E)^2}\,,
\]
which in turn is true if
\begin{equation}\label{lastone}
\lambda_0(E) \geq 8 \delta(E)\, \frac {4\delta(E)+8}{3\pi}\,\eta\,.
\end{equation}
Keep in mind that we are assuming that ${\mathcal G}(E)<1$, which means $\delta(E)< \lambda_0(E)^2$; since $\lambda_0(E)\leq 2$, this gives both $\delta(E)\leq 2\lambda_0(E)$ and $\delta(E)\leq 4$. Therefore,
\[
8\,\delta(E)\, \frac {4\delta(E)+8}{3\pi}\,\eta \,\leq\, 16\,\lambda_0(E)\, \frac{24}{3\pi}\,\eta =\frac{128}{\pi}\,\lambda_0(E)\,\eta\,,
\]
so the validity of~(\ref{lastone}) is surely obtained if
\[
1 \geq \frac {128}{\pi}\,\eta\,.
\]
Thus, we have proved that~(\ref{claimend}) holds true as soon as $\bar\eta$ has been chosen small enough. Finally, writing for brevity $F'=\sqrt{\pi/|F|} F$, we claim that
\begin{equation}\label{claimHaus}
d_H^+(F',B_{F'}) \geq d_H^+(E,B_E)\,.
\end{equation}
To show this inequality, we first consider $d_H^+(F,B_F)$. By construction, (\ref{movbar}) and~(\ref{estipervol}), we have
\[\begin{split}
d_H^+(F,B_F)&\geq d_H^+(E,B_E) + \frac\eta 5-\big| {\rm bar}(E) - {\rm bar}(F)\big|- \bigg|\sqrt{\frac{|F|}\pi} -1\bigg|\\
&\geq d_H^+(E,B_E) + \frac\eta 5 - \bigg(\frac{P(E)}{6\pi^2}+\frac 1{6\pi}\bigg)\, \eta^2\,.
\end{split}\]
Then, again by~(\ref{estipervol}), keeping in mind that $d_H^+(E,B_E)\leq P(E) = 2\pi (\delta(E)+1)\leq 10\pi$, and up to further decreasing $\bar\eta$ if necessary, we have
\[\begin{split}
d_H^+(F',B_{F'}) &= \sqrt{\frac \pi{|F|}}\, d_H^+(F,B_F) \geq \bigg(1-\frac{\eta^2}{6\pi^2}\bigg) \cdot \bigg(d_H^+(E,B_E) + \frac\eta 5 - \bigg(\frac{P(E)}{6\pi^2}+\frac 1{6\pi}\bigg)\, \eta^2\bigg)\\
&\geq d_H^+(E,B_E)\,,
\end{split}\]
so that~(\ref{claimHaus}) is established. Summarizing, starting from a set $E\in\mathcal R$ with ${\mathcal G}(E)<1$ and such that $\partial E$ has at least two connected components shorter than $\bar\eta$, we have built another set $F'\in\mathcal R$ such that ${\mathcal G}(F')={\mathcal G}(F)\leq {\mathcal G}(E)$ by~(\ref{claimend}), and $d_H^+(F',B_{F'}) \geq d_H^+(E,B_E)$ by~(\ref{claimHaus}). Moreover, $\partial F'$ has one connected component less than $\partial E$. Repeating this construction finitely many times, we end up with a set $E'$ satisfying~(\ref{almostall}). Thus, we have been able to find a set $E'$ satisfying~(\ref{almostall}) starting from any set $E\in\mathcal R$.\par

In order to conclude the proof, we only have to provide a bound on the number of connected components of $\partial E'$. However, this is very simple; indeed, by construction we have ${\mathcal G}(E')\leq 1$, and then $P(E')=2\pi(\delta(E')+1)\leq 2\pi (4{\mathcal G}(E')+1)\leq 10\pi$, and since all the connected components of $\partial E'$ except at most one have length at least $\bar\eta$, then their number is at most
\[
1+\frac{10\pi}{\bar\eta}\,,
\]
so the thesis is concluded by taking any natural number $N_{\rm max}$ larger than this last quantity.

\label{exminAd}
For any $d\leq 2$, there exists a set $\overline E$ which minimizes ${\mathcal G}$ in the class ${\mathcal A}_d$. More in general, there exists a minimizer for ${\mathcal G}$ in the class ${\mathcal A}_d$ as soon as $\inf\{{\mathcal G}(E),\, E\in {\mathcal A}_d\} < 1$.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let us take any sequence of ${\rm C}^1$ sets $E_j\in {\mathcal A}_d$ such that ${\mathcal G}(E_j)$ converges to $\inf\{{\mathcal G}(E),\, E\in{\mathcal A}_d\}$. Without loss of generality, we can assume that ${\mathcal G}(E_j)<1$ for every $j$; indeed, the ratio ${\mathcal G}$ for a rectangle of sidelengths $2\pi$ and $1/2$ is around $0.62$, and such a rectangle belongs to ${\mathcal A}_2$, so to any ${\mathcal A}_d$ with $d\leq 2$; of course, even if $d>2$ we can assume that ${\mathcal G}(E_j)<1$ if by assumption $\inf\{{\mathcal G}(E),\, E\in {\mathcal A}_d\} < 1$. Thanks to Lemma~\ref{smcoco}, we can assume without loss of generality that the boundary of each set $E_j$ consists of at most $N_{\rm max}$ connected components. As a consequence, up to passing to a subsequence, we can assume that all the boundaries have exactly $N$ connected components, for some $1\leq N\leq N_{\rm max}$. Let us then call $\tau_1^j,\, \tau_2^j,\, \dots\,,\, \tau_N^j$ the $N$ connected components of $\partial E_j$, which are regular, closed paths, that we parametrize at constant speed on $\mathbb S^1$; in particular, we call $\tau_1^j$ the external part of the boundary, that is, $E_j$ coincides with the internal part of the curve $\tau_1^j$ to which we remove the internal parts of the other curves $\tau_i^j$ with $i>1$. Up to a translation, all the sets $E_j$ have barycenter in the origin; moreover, since ${\mathcal G}(E_j)<1$, in particular $\delta(E_j)<4$ and so $P(E_j)<10\pi$. As a consequence, all the paths $\tau_i^j$ have length at most $10\pi$ and they are all contained in a ball centered in the origin and with radius $10\pi$. Thus, up to a further subsequence, we can assume that for every $1\leq i \leq N$ the paths $\tau_i^j :\mathbb S^1\to\mathbb R^2$ are uniformly converging to limiting paths $\tau_i$. Notice that all the paths $\tau_i$ are non-crossing curves by definition, and by construction each curve $\tau_i$ with $i>1$ is contained in the internal part of $\tau_1$. As a consequence, the curves $\tau_i$ determine a connected set $\overline E$, and we claim that this set minimizes ${\mathcal G}$ within ${\mathcal A}_d$.

First of all, we can notice that $\overline E$ has area $\pi$, since so does every set $E_j$. Moreover, by lower-semicontinuity, for each $1\leq i\leq N$ we have
\[
{\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\tau_i)\leq \liminf_{j\to\infty} {\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\tau_i^j)\,,
\]
and then $P(\overline E)\leq \liminf P(E_j)$, thus $\delta(\overline E)\leq \liminf \delta(E_j)$. Since the sets $E_j$ converge to $\overline E$ in the $L^1$ sense, we also have $\lambda_0(\overline E)=\lim \lambda_0(E_j)$. Therefore
\[
{\mathcal G}(\overline E)\leq \liminf {\mathcal G}(E_j) = \inf\{{\mathcal G}(E),\, E\in {\mathcal A}_d\}\,.
\]
Then, to conclude the proof, we only have to check that $\overline E$ belongs to ${\mathcal A}_d$. Since the sets $E_j$ are converging in the $L^1$ sense to $\overline E$ and they are equibounded, we have ${\rm bar}(\overline E)=0$ since all the barycenters are in the origin. Thus, we need to find a point $Q\in \partial \overline E$ with $|Q| \geq 1+d$; however, since each $E_j$ belongs to ${\mathcal A}_d$, there exist points $Q_j \in \partial E_j$ with $|Q_j|\geq 1+d$. Again possibly up to a subsequence, the points $Q_j$ converge to some point $Q$, which by construction belongs to $\partial\overline E$ and is as desired. The proof is then concluded.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{formulainAd}
Let $E$ be a minimizer of ${\mathcal G}$ in ${\mathcal A}_d$, with barycenter in the origin. Then, the curves forming $\partial E$ are of class ${\rm C}^{1,1}$ and, up to a rotation, there are three constants $\alpha,\, \beta,\,\gamma\in\mathbb R$ such that at every simple point $P=(x,y)\in\partial E\setminus \partial B$, except at most one, the curvature of $\partial E$ at $P$ is well defined, and it verifies
\begin{equation}\tag{\ref{abc}}
\kappa(x,y)=\alpha x + \beta - \gamma \hbox{{\large $\chi$}{\Large $_{_{B}}$}}\,.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The proof is exactly the same as the one of Theorem~\ref{formulaabc}. In particular, the regularity is standard, and the formula for the curvature is obtained for all the points where a variation can be performed. As in Theorem~\ref{formulaabc}, this is not possible at a point $P\in \partial E\cap \partial B$, since we need that all the points around $P$ are outside of $B$, or all inside $B$, and this is also not possible for a point which is not simple.
		
Let then $P$ be a simple point in $\partial E\setminus \partial B$; of course it is possible to perform a variation if $|P| \neq 1+ d$, since a small variation does not modify the value of $d$. Let, instead, assume that $|P|= 1+d$. Then, it is still possible to perform a variation ``towards the outside'', since sets in ${\mathcal A}_d$ have Hausdorff distance with $B$ larger or equal than $d$, and so increasing this distance leaves a set in the class ${\mathcal A}_d$. Instead, it is in principle not possible to perform a variation ``towards the inside'', because this might decrease the Hausdorff distance with $B$ and then rule the set out of the class ${\mathcal A}_d$. Nevertheless, the problem occurs only if there is \emph{exactly one point} of $\partial E$ with distance $1+d$ from the origin, because, if $P$ is not the unique such point, then a variation towards the inside near a point does not change the Hausdorff distance. In other words, the validity of the formula is also true for points of $\partial E$ having distance $1+d$ from the origin, unless there is only a single such point.

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We prove now that there exists a connected set which minimizes the functional ${\mathcal G}$. The fact that any minimizer is simply connected will be proved in Section~\ref{minsimcon}. For simplicity, we subdivide the proof in a few steps.
		
\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~I.}}\emph{ The minimizing sequence $\{E_j\}$ converging to $B$.}\\
Let us take any sequence $\{E_j\}$ of connected, planar sets, such that ${\mathcal G}(E_j) \to \inf \{ {\mathcal G} \}$. Up to a translation, we can assume that all the sets $E_j$ have barycenter in the origin. Since the ratio ${\mathcal G}$ is defined for all the planar, connected sets except the ball, then each set $E_j$ is surely not the ball, thus $d_j = d_H^+(E_j,B)>0$. Since we have noticed in Proposition~\ref{prop:estim_mod_dumb} that the value of ${\mathcal G}$ for the modified dumbbell is around $1/4$, it is admissible to assume that ${\mathcal G}(E_j)<1$ for every $j$. 
By Proposition~\ref{exminAd}, then, we can assume without loss of generality that each $E_j$ minimizes ${\mathcal G}$ within the corresponding class ${\mathcal A}_{d_j}$. As a consequence, and up to rotations, Lemma~\ref{formulainAd} provides us with three sequences $\{\alpha_j\},\, \{\beta_j\},\,\{\gamma_j\}$ with the property that the curvature at each simple point $P\equiv (x,y) \in \partial E_j\setminus \partial B$, except at most one, is given by
		\begin{equation}\label{formj}
			\kappa(x,y) = \alpha_j x +\beta_j- \gamma_j \hbox{{\large $\chi$}{\Large $_{_{B}}$}}\,.
		\end{equation}
		By Lemma~\ref{smcoco}, up to a subsequence we can assume that the boundaries of each $E_j$ consist of exactly $N$ connected components, for some $1\leq N \leq N_{\rm max}$, and we call them $\tau^1_j,\, \tau^2_j,\, \dots\,,\, \tau^N_j$, being $\tau^1_j$ the external one. Since all the sets $E_j$ have barycenter in the origin, and since $P(E_j)<10\pi$ for each $j$ by the fact that ${\mathcal G}(E_j)<1$, then all the sets are contained in the ball centered in the origin and with radius $10\pi$. Therefore, up to a further subsequence we can assume that each sequence $\{\tau^i_j\}_{j\in\mathbb N}$ (parametrized at constant speed) is converging to some path $\tau^i$. In particular, the sets $E_j$ are converging to some set $E$ in the $L^1$ sense; moreover, the set $E$ is connected and its boundary consists of the paths $\tau^i$. By the lower semicontinuity of the length of the paths, we know that $P(E) \leq \liminf P(E_j)$, thus $\delta(E)\leq \liminf \delta(E_j)$, and by the $L^1$ convergence of $E_j$ to $E$ we also know that $\lambda_0(E) =\lim \lambda_0(E_j)$, since the barycenter of $E$ is also in the origin. As a consequence, we immediately have that
		\[
		{\mathcal G}(E) = \frac{\delta(E)}{\lambda_0(E)^2} \leq \liminf \frac{\delta(E_j)}{\lambda_0(E_j)^2} = \liminf {\mathcal G}(E_j) = \inf {\mathcal G}\,,
		\]
		and then the set $E$ is a minimizer of ${\mathcal G}$, unless $E=B$, in which case ${\mathcal G}(E)$ is not well defined. In other words, the proof is concluded as soon as we can exclude that $E=B$. We assume then that $E=B$ and we look for a contradiction.
		
		\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~II.}}\emph{ The boundary of each $E_j$ is a single curve and~(\ref{formj}) holds.}\\
		The goal of this step is to reduce ourselves to the case when the number $N$ of connected components of each $\partial E_j$ is just $1$, so we assume by contradiction that $N>1$. Let us call $E_j^+$ the set whose boundary is $\tau^1_j$, then $E_j^+$ consists of $E_j$ plus all the ``holes'', i.e., the sets whose boundary is $\tau^i_j$ for $i>1$. Since $E_j$ is converging to $B$ in the $L^1$ sense, then $\lambda_0(E_j)\to 0$, and since ${\mathcal G}(E_j)<1$ then also $\delta(E_j)\to 0$; this means that the perimeter of $E_j$ is converging to $2\pi$. However, since the area of $E_j^+$ is more than $\pi$, we have
		\[
		P(E_j) = {\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\tau^1_j) + \sum_{i=2}^N {\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\tau^i_j)\geq 2\pi + \sum_{i=2}^N {\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\tau^i_j)\,,
		\]
		which since $P(E_j)\to 2\pi$ implies that the latter sum is going to $0$. Up to a subsequence, we can then assume the sum to be smaller than $\bar\eta$ for any $j$, where $\bar\eta$ is given by Lemma~\ref{smcoco}. According to Lemma~\ref{smcoco}, we deduce that the only possible values of $N$ are $1$ and $2$, and since we are assuming $N>1$ we have then $N=2$. In other words, each set $E_j$ has exactly a hole, whose perimeter is going to $0$ (and then, of course also the area of the holes is going to $0$).
		
		We can easily exclude that $E_j^+$ is a ball. Indeed, if $E_j^+$ is a ball, since for all simple points of $\partial E_j^+\setminus \partial B$ the curvature is given by~(\ref{formj}), then we deduce that $\alpha_j$ must be $0$, and that $\beta_j$ must converge to $1$ for $j\to +\infty$. In addition, keeping in mind that
		\[
		\gamma_j = \frac{8\delta_j}{\lambda_j} = 8 \lambda_j {\mathcal G}(E_j)\,,
		\]
		we have also that $\gamma_j$ is converging to $0$ for $j\to +\infty$. But then, formula~(\ref{formj}) only attains values very close to $1$, in particular positive values if $j$ is large enough. And this is in contradiction with the existence of a hole, because the boundary of a hole must have some simple point where the curvature is negative, against the validity of~(\ref{formj}).
		
		Having shown that $E_j^+$ is not a ball, we can define $F_j$ as the translation of $\sqrt{\pi/|E_j^+|} E_j^+$ which has barycenter in the origin. The new sequence $F_j$ is then another sequence of sets with area $\pi$, barycenter in the origin, and which are converging to the ball; since no $F_j$ is a ball, the ratio ${\mathcal G}(F_j)$ is defined. Since the boundary of each $F_j$ is a single curve (a slight translation of a slight rescaling of $\tau^1_j$), we will conclude this step once we show that also $\{F_j\}$ is a minimizing sequence. This can be easily obtained with an estimate very close to the one already done in Lemma~\ref{smcoco}. Indeed, let us call $v_j = |E^+_j|-\pi$ the area of the hole of $E_j$, which has boundary $\tau^2_j$. The perimeter of $F_j$ satisfies
		\[
		P(F_j) = \sqrt{\frac \pi {\pi + v_j}} \, P(E^+_j) \leq P(E^+_j) \leq P(E_j) - {\mbox{\script H}\,\,}^1(\tau^2_j) \leq P(E_j) - 2 \sqrt \pi \sqrt{v_j}\,,
		\]
		and then
		\[
		2\pi \delta(F_j) = P(F_j) - 2\pi \leq P(E_j)- 2 \sqrt \pi \sqrt{v_j} - 2\pi =2\pi \delta(E_j) - 2 \sqrt \pi \sqrt{v_j}\,,
		\]
		which gives
		\begin{equation}\label{poch}
			\delta(F_j) \leq \delta(E_j) - \sqrt{\frac{v_j}\pi}\,.
		\end{equation}
		On the other hand, since the whole $E_j$ is contained in the ball of radius $(1+d_j)$, then the distance between the barycenter of $E_j^+$ and the origin is at most $(1+d_j)v_j/\pi$. Calling $\widetilde B$ the ball with volume $\pi+v_j$ and centered in the barycenter of $E_j^+$, we have then
		\[
		\big| E_j^+ \Delta \widetilde B\big| \geq\big| E_j \Delta B\big|- \big| E_j^+ \Delta E_j\big|-\big| B\Delta \widetilde B\big|
		\geq \pi \lambda_0(E_j) - v_j - \frac{4(1+d_j)+\pi}\pi\, v_j\,.
		\]
		This implies that
		\[\begin{split}
			\pi \lambda_0(F_j) &= \frac \pi{|E_j^+|} \, \big| E_j^+ \Delta \widetilde B\big| \geq \frac{\pi}{\pi+v_j}\pi\lambda_0(E_j)- \frac{2\pi }{\pi+v_j}v_j-\frac{4(1+d_j)}{\pi(\pi+v_j)}v_j\\
&\geq
\left(1-\frac{v_j}{\pi}\right) \, \pi\lambda_0(E_j) - \frac{2 \pi }{\pi+v_j}v_j- \frac{4(1+d_j)}{\pi(\pi+v_j)}\, v_j,
		\end{split}\]
		thus
		\[
		\lambda_0(F_j) \geq \left(1-\frac{v_j}{\pi}\right)\lambda_0(E_j) - \frac{2 v_j}{\pi+v_j}-\frac{4(1+d_j)}{\pi^2(\pi+v_j)}\, v_j\,.
		\]
		Putting this estimate together with~(\ref{poch}), and keeping in mind that $\lambda_0(E_j)/\delta(E_j)\to +\infty$ since $\lambda_0(E_j)/\delta(E_j)> 1/\lambda_0(E_j)\to +\infty$, we deduce that ${\mathcal G}(F_j)<{\mathcal G}(E_j)$ for $j$ large. In other words, also $\{F_j\}$ is a minimizing sequence for ${\mathcal G}$. To conclude the step, then, we only have to show that formula~(\ref{formj}) is true also for $F_j$, which is not obvious since in principle $F_j$ might not be a minimizer of ${\mathcal G}$ in its class ${\mathcal A}_d$. However, the formula is true for points in $\partial E_j$, so in particular for points in $\partial E^+_j$, and since $F_j$ is a translation and rescaling of $E^+_j$ then the formula remains true, possibly with different values of $\alpha_j,\, \beta_j,\, \gamma_j$. Alternatively, we can observe the validity of the formula also noticing that we can assume $F_j$ to minimize ${\mathcal G}$ within the subclass of ${\mathcal A}_d$ (being $d=d_H^+(F_j,B)$) made by sets whose boundary is made of a single curve. Since of course the property of having the boundary made by a single curve remains true after a small variation, the proof of Lemma~\ref{formulainAd} works also for this subclass and then the formula for $F_j$ is established.
		
		\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~III.}}\emph{ The boundary of $E_j$ is an injective curve and~(\ref{formj}) holds.}\\
		In this step, we reduce ourselves to the case when $\partial E_j$ is not only a single curve, but also a simple one, i.e., there are no self-intersections. This can be obtained almost exactly as in Step~II. Indeed, assuming that $\partial E_j$ has self-intersections, we get that $\tau^1_j$ is the union of more than one simple curve. We can then call $E_j^-$ the part of $E_j$ whose boundary is the longest of the simple curves forming $\tau^1_j$. The isoperimetric inequality implies that the area of $E_j^-$ converges to $\pi$; moreover, $E_j^-$ is surely not a ball, because at any non-simple point of $\tau^1_j$ the two tangents have to coincide; as a consequence, we can again call $F_j$ the set $E_j^-$, rescaled so to have area $\pi$ and translated so to have barycenter in the origin. Calling this time $v_j=\pi - |E_j^-|$, exactly as in the previous step we have that
		\begin{align*}
			\delta(F_j) -\delta(E_j) \lesssim - \sqrt{v_j}\,, && \lambda_0(F_j) - \lambda_0(E_j) \gtrsim - v_j\,,
		\end{align*}
		and then we find again that ${\mathcal G}(F_j)<{\mathcal G}(E_j)$ for $j$ large, so that also $\{F_j\}$ is a minimizing sequence for ${\mathcal G}$. Again, the step is concluded up to notice that~(\ref{formj}) is valid also for $F_j$. And again, this is true because $\partial F_j$ is a rescaling and translation of a portion of $\partial E_j$; alternatively, also this time we can observe that the proof of Lemma~\ref{formulainAd} works also in the subclass of sets for which the boundary is a single, injective curve, because small variations do not rule sets out of this subclass.
		
		
		\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~IV.}}\emph{ Conclusion.}\\
		Summarizing, we have a sequence $\{E_j\}$ of sets with area $\pi$, barycenter in the origin, which are converging to the ball $B$ and for which ${\mathcal G}(E_j)\to \inf\{{\mathcal G}\}$; in addition, we know that the boundary of each $E_j$ is a single, injective curve, and that for all points of $\partial E_j\setminus \partial B$ the formula~(\ref{formj}) for the curvature holds. In addition, for almost all the points of $\partial E_j\cap\partial B$ the curvature is well-defined and equal to $1$. Since the Hausdorff distance between $E_j$ and $B$ is going to $0$, we deduce that necessarily $\alpha_j$ and $\gamma_j$ are converging to $0$, while $\beta_j$ is converging to $1$.
		
		But then, for $j$ sufficiently large the sets $E_j$ are nearly spherical, and, more than that, convex. However, the estimates of~\cite{CL,CL2,AFN} (see also the argument in~\cite{BCH}) ensure that, for such sets, one has
		\begin{equation}\label{reality}
			\lim_{j\to +\infty} {\mathcal G}(E_j) > {\mathcal G}(C)\,,
		\end{equation}
		where $C$ is a particular stadium, called ``optimal biscuit''. Since $C$ is a connected set with area $\pi$ and barycenter in the origin, this is in contradiction with the fact that $\{E_j\}$ is a sequence minimizing ${\mathcal G}$, and the contradiction concludes the thesis.
		It is important to remark that, in the above-cited papers, the authors did not claim the inequality~(\ref{reality}), but something different; however, what they proved was actually~(\ref{reality}). Let us be more precise. In those papers, the problem under consideration was to minimize not the ratio ${\mathcal G}(E)=\delta(E)/\lambda_0(E)^2$, but the ``classical'' ratio ${\mathcal F}(E)=\delta(E)/\lambda(E)^2$, where $\lambda(E)\leq \lambda_0(E)$ is the standard Fraenkel asymmetry; and in fact, what they claimed was not~(\ref{reality}) but
		\begin{equation}\label{reality2}
			\lim_{j\to +\infty} {\mathcal F}(E_j) > {\mathcal F}(C)\,.
		\end{equation}
		However, in their calculations, they were not estimating the Fraenkel asymmetry $\lambda(E)$, but the (much simpler to evaluate) barycentric asymmetry $\lambda_0(E)$, as it is explicitly written in~\cite[(74) of Lemma~4.2]{CL}. Thus, relying on the fact that $\lambda\leq \lambda_0$, and thus ${\mathcal G}\leq {\mathcal F}$, they obtained~(\ref{reality2}) by showing that (rewriting their estimate with our notation)
		\[
		\lim_{j\to +\infty} {\mathcal F}(E_j) \geq\lim_{j\to +\infty} {\mathcal G}(E_j) > {\mathcal F}(C)\,.
		\]
		Moreover, as observed in the same papers, for the set $C$ the ``Fraenkel ball'' is the barycentric one, which in our language means that ${\mathcal F}(C)={\mathcal G}(C)$. As a consequence, their results actually give exactly~(\ref{reality}).

Let $P=(x_P,y_P)$ be a point of $\tau$ with maximal distance from the origin, so in particular $P\notin \overline B$; the maximality implies that the (distributional) curvature $\kappa(P)$ must be smaller than $-|P|^{-1}$, hence strictly negative (and in principle, it might be $-\infty$). Let now $Q=(x_Q,y_Q)$ be a point of $\partial E$ with minimal coordinate $x_Q$. Again by minimality, the curvature $\kappa(Q)$ must be positive. Notice that if $P$ is a simple point, then the formula~(\ref{abc}) for the curvature at $P$ holds; hence, by the same formula, the curvature at any simple point not belonging to $\partial B$ and having $x$-coordinate smaller than $x_P$ is strictly negative. Since this is false at the point $Q$, this implies that $Q$ is either not simple or it belongs to $\partial B$. This is why the point $Q$ in Figure~\ref{fig:no hole outside} has been depicted on $\partial B$: however, this is just to fix the ideas, what we are going to use later is only that $\kappa_P<0$ and $\kappa_Q\geq 0$, and as noticed above this is true regardless of the fact that $P$ and/or $Q$ are simple or on $\partial B$.\par

Now, choose two simple points $R,L$ on $\tau_1\setminus \overline{B}$, that is, two points on the external boundary which lie outside of $\overline{B}$, with $x_L<x_R$; then, add a mass $\sigma\varepsilon$ around $L$ and remove it from $R$, choosing the constant $\sigma>0$ in such a way that the barycenter remains in the origin, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:no hole outside}.

In this way, it is sufficient to choose $\sigma$ such that the barycenter has null horizontal coordinate.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem: no hole inside B}
The presence of a hole contained in the barycentric ball is impossible.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $H$ be the set bounded by $\tau$ and set $|H|=a\pi$, with $a\in (0,1]$.
We now divide this proof into steps, for the sake of readability.
\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~I.}}\emph{ An upper bound for the area $|H|$.}\\
We can easily estimate the deficit of the set $E$ by 
\[
\delta(E)\geq \frac{2\pi(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{1+a})}{2\pi}-1=\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{1+a}-1.
\]
But, by Proposition~\ref{prop:estim_mod_dumb} and since $\lambda_0(E)\leq 2$ by definition, we can deduce that it must be $\delta(E)<1$ and hence $\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{1+a}-1<1$.
Thus, solving this inequality, we have that $a<9/16$.
\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~II.}}\emph{ A lower bound for the area $|H|$.}\\
Let us consider the set $F$ obtained by filling the hole $H$; namely, set $F=E\,\cup\, H$ and consider it as a competitor.
Let us start by estimating its deficit.
Since we filled the hole, we can write the perimeter of $F$ as follows
\begin{equation*}
P(F)=P(E)-P(H)=2\pi(\delta(E)+1)-P(H)\leq 2\pi(\delta(E)+1)-2\pi\sqrt{a}.
\end{equation*}
Now, using the fact that $|F|=|E|+|H|=(1+a)\pi$, we can bound $\delta(F)$ by above by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta of F}
\delta(F)=\frac{P(F)}{2\pi\sqrt{1+a}}-1\leq \frac{\delta(E)+1-\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{1+a}}-1=:\tilde{\delta}.
\end{equation}
Now we need an estimate for $\lambda_0(F)$.
First, notice that the barycenter of $F$ is given by
\[
{\rm bar}{F}=\frac{\int_Fz dz}{(1+a)\pi}=\frac{\int_Ez dz+\int_H zdz}{(1+a)\pi}=\frac{\int_Hz dz}{(1+a)\pi},
\]
and hence, since $H\subseteq B$, we have the following bound
\[
|{\rm bar}{F}|\leq \frac{|H|}{(1+a)\pi}=\frac{a}{1+a}.
\]
Now, we denote by $B'$ the barycentric ball for $F$ (i.e. the ball centered at ${\rm bar}{F}$ of radius $\sqrt{1+a}$) and we bound $|B'\setminus F|$ as follows
\[
|B'\setminus F|\geq|B'\setminus E|-a\pi\geq |B\setminus E|- |B\setminus B'|-a\pi. 
\]
However, an easy computation shows that 
\begin{equation}\label{firstesti2}
|B\setminus B'|\leq 2\left(1+\frac{a}{1+a}-\sqrt{1+a}\right),
\end{equation}
which plugged into the previous equality gives
\[
|B'\setminus F|\geq |B\setminus E|-a\pi-2\left(1+\frac{a}{1+a}-\sqrt{1+a}\right).
\]
Therefore, since $\lambda_0(F)=2|B'\setminus F|/|F|$, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:estim lambdaF}
\begin{split}
\lambda_0(F)&\geq \frac{2|B\setminus E|}{(1+a)\pi}-\frac{2a\pi}{(1+a)\pi}-\frac{4}{(1+a)\pi}\left(1+\frac{a}{1+a}-\sqrt{1+a}\right)\\
&=\frac{\lambda_0(E)}{1+a}-\frac{2a}{1+a}-\frac{4}{(1+a)\pi}\left(1+\frac{a}{1+a}-\sqrt{1+a}\right)=:\tilde\lambda.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
It is not hard to show that $\tilde\lambda\geq 0$. 
Indeed, since ${\mathcal G}(E)< 1/4$, we have 
\[
\lambda_0(E)^2>4\delta(E)\geq 4\big(\sqrt{1+a}+\sqrt{a}-1\big),
\]
so it follows 
\[
\tilde{\lambda}>\frac{2\sqrt{\sqrt{1+a}+\sqrt{a}-1}}{1+a}-\frac{2a}{1+a}-\frac{4}{(1+a)\pi}\left(1+\frac{a}{1+a}-\sqrt{1+a}\right),
\]
which is positive for all $a\in [0,9/16]$, as we check in Lemma~\ref{lem: lambda tilde positive} in the Appendix.

Now, since $E$ is a minimizer, we have a contradiction as soon as ${\mathcal G}(F)<{\mathcal G}(E)$ and hence as soon as 
\[ 
\frac{\tilde{\delta}}{\tilde{\lambda}^2}<\frac{\delta(E)}{\lambda_0(E)^2},
\]
thanks to~\eqref{eq:delta of F} and~\eqref{eq:estim lambdaF}.
Hence, we have a contradiction whenever
\begin{equation*}
\left(\frac{\delta(E)+1-\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{1+a}}-1\right)\lambda_0(E)^2<\delta(E) \cdot\tilde\lambda^2,
\end{equation*}
that is, whenever
\begin{equation}\label{eq: contradiction ineq}
\delta(E)\left(\frac{\lambda_0(E)^2}{\sqrt{1+a}}-\tilde\lambda^2\right)< \lambda_0(E)^2\left(\sqrt{\frac{a}{1+a}}+1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a}}\right),
\end{equation}
which, using the fact that $\tilde{\lambda}\leq \lambda_0(E)/(1+a)\leq \lambda_0(E)/\sqrt[4]{1+a}$, is equivalent to
\[
{\mathcal G}(E) = \frac{\delta(E)}{\lambda_0(E)^2}< \frac{\sqrt{\frac{a}{1+a}}+1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a}}}{\frac{\lambda_0(E)^2}{\sqrt{1+a}}-\tilde\lambda^2}.
\]
Consider now the denominator of the right-hand side. For any value of $a\in [0,9/16]$, and recalling the definition~(\ref{eq:estim lambdaF}) of $\tilde\lambda$, we can regard it as a function of $\lambda_0(E)$, and it is immediate to observe that it is an increasing function. As a consequence, since by definition $\lambda_0(E)\leq 2$, and keeping in mind that ${\mathcal G}(E)<1/4$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:estim_mod_dumb}, to get a contradiction it is enough to show that
\begin{equation}\label{thistimecontrad}
\frac 14 \leq \frac{\sqrt{\frac{a}{1+a}}+1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a}}}{\frac{4}{\sqrt{1+a}}-\left(\frac{2}{1+a}-\frac{2a}{1+a}-\frac{4}{(1+a)\pi}\left(1+\frac{a}{1+a}-\sqrt{1+a}\right)\right)^2}\,.
\end{equation}
And in turn, as we show in Lemma~\ref{lem: 2 vs 0.62}, this is true for any $a\leq 0.14$, so we deduce that $a>0.14$.

\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~III.}}\emph{ An improved estimate of ${\rm bar}{F}$ and $|B\setminus B'|$.}\\
Now that we have a lower bound for $|H|$, we can improve that bound~(\ref{eq:estim lambdaF}) on $\lambda_0(E)$ by improving the estimate of $|{\rm bar}{F}|$ and hence of $|B\setminus B'|$. In fact, if we consider the circular segment $H_{x_0} :=B\cap \{(x,y): x\geq x_0\}$, where $x_0$ is chosen so that $|H|=|H_{x_0}|$, then we have
\begin{equation*}
\bigg|\int_H z\,dz\bigg| \leq  \bigg|\int_{ H_{x_0}}z\,dz\bigg|=\frac 23\,(1-x_0^2)^{3/2}.
\end{equation*}
Observing that the area of $H_{x_0}$ is given by
\begin{equation} \label{formula}
| H_{x_0}|=\arccos x_0-x_0\sqrt{1-x_0^2}\,,
\end{equation}
we claim that the ratio
\begin{equation}\label{ratio}
\frac{\big|\int_{ H_{x_0}}zdz\big|}{|H_{x_0}|}=\frac{\frac 23\,(1-x_0^2)^{3/2}}{\arccos x_0-x_0\sqrt{1-x_0^2}}
\end{equation}
is an increasing function of $x_0$ (thus a decreasing function of $a$, since $| H_{x_0}|=|H|=a\pi$). To show the claim, it is enough to compute the derivative, so we are reduced to check that
\[
0<-2x_0\sqrt{1-x_0^2}\,\arccos x_0+2x_0^2(1-x_0^2)+\frac43\big(1-x_0^2\big)^2\,,
\]
which can be rewritten as
\[\begin{split}
0&<2\sqrt{1-x_0^2}\left(x_0^2\sqrt{1-x_0^2}-x_0\arccos x_0+\frac{2}{3}\left(1-x_0^2\right)^{3/2}\right)\\
&=2\sqrt{1-x_0^2}\cdot\bigg(\bigg|\int_{H_{x_0}}zdz\bigg|-x_0\big|H_{x_0}\big|\bigg)\,.
\end{split}\]
Thus, the validity of the claim follows since an obvious geometric argument implies that
\[
\bigg| \int_{H_{x_0}} z\, dz\bigg| \geq x_0  \, \big|H_{x_0}\big|\,.
\]
We can immediately take advantage of the claim. Indeed, since by Step~II we know that $|H_{x_0}|=|H|>0.14\pi$, and then by~(\ref{formula}) we deduce $x_0<0.61$, then we can bound the ratio in~(\ref{ratio}) by the value corresponding to $x_0=0.61$, so by substituting we get
\[
\frac{\left|\int_{H_{x_0}}zdz\right|}{|H_{x_0}|} < 0.77 \leq \frac 45 \,.
\]
And finally, this implies
\[
|{\rm bar}{F}|=\frac{\left|\int_Hzdz\right|}{(1+a)\pi}\leq\frac45 \cdot\frac{ a\pi}{(1+a)\pi}=\frac{4a}{5(1+a)}\,.
\]
Having improved the bound for ${\rm bar}{F}$, we can now improve also the bound for $|B\setminus B'|$. Keep in mind that $B$ is the unit ball centered at the origin, while $B'$ is a ball of radius $\sqrt{1+a}$ centered at distance ${\rm bar}{F}$ from the origin. Hence, $|B\setminus B'| \leq |B\setminus B''|$, where $B''$ is the ball with radius $\sqrt{1+a}$ centered at the point $(-4a/5(1+a),0)$.

We can now observe that both sides of this inequality are decreasing for $a\in [0.14,\,9/16]$. Indeed, a direct calculation of the derivatives ensures that the terms in parentheses in~(\ref{eq: angles theta and phi}) are both increasing for $a\geq 0$, and then $\vartheta$ and $\varphi$ are decreasing, thus so is also $\sin\vartheta$ since $\vartheta \in [0,\pi/2]$ by construction. Observing also that $\frac 85\, \sin\vartheta-0.62>0$ by monotonicity and since at $a=9/16$ we have $\frac 85\, \sin\vartheta\approx 0.55$, we get that also $\Psi$ is decreasing as a sum of positive decreasing terms. To conclude the validity of~(\ref{ineq: phi geq Psi}) and thus the proof, we can then just evaluate $\varphi(a)$ and $\Psi(a)$ for a few values of $a$.\par

More precisely, we define $a_1=0.14,\, a_2=0.19,\, a_3=0.27,\, a_4=0.38,\, a_5=0.50$ and $a_6=9/16$ and we calculate $\varphi(a)$ and $\Psi(a)$ at these values. Then, for every $a_i \leq a\leq a_{i+1}$ by monotonicity we have $\varphi(a)\geq \varphi(a_{i+1})$ and $\Psi(a)\leq \Psi(a_i)$, so we only have to check that $\varphi(a_{i+1})\geq \Psi(a_i)$ for every $i \in \{1,\,2,\,3,\,4,\,5\}$. Fix $s\in\{0.62,\,2\}$ and set $\psi(a)=1+\dfrac{a}{1+a}-\sqrt{1+a}$.

\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~0.}}\emph{ Reduction.}\\
With $r_s(a)=1-a-\tfrac{s}{\pi}\psi(a)$, the squared term in the denominator equals
\[
\Big(\tfrac{2}{1+a}(1-a)-\tfrac{2s}{(1+a)\pi}\psi\Big)^2=\tfrac{4}{(1+a)^2}r_s(a)^2\,,
\]
so the denominator is
\[
D := \frac{4}{\sqrt{1+a}}-\frac{4}{(1+a)^2}r_s(a)^2=\frac{4}{(1+a)^2}\big((1+a)^{3/2}-r_s(a)^2\big)\,.
\]
Since $\psi\ge0$ on $[0,9/16]$ and $r_s>0$ (as we are going to see in Step~I), we have $r_s\le1$, hence $r_s^2\le1<(1+a)^{3/2}$ for $a>0$ and then the denominator is positive on $(0,9/16]$. 

Thus, the asserted inequality is equivalent to $4N\ge D$, where $N=\sqrt{\tfrac{a}{1+a}}+1-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+a}}$ is the numerator. Since
\[
4N-D=4\sqrt{\tfrac{a}{1+a}}+4-\frac{8}{\sqrt{1+a}}+\frac{4}{(1+a)^2}r_s^2\,,
\]
$4N\ge D$ is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{defg}
f_s(a) := r_s^2\ \geq\ (1+a)^2\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+a}}-1-\sqrt{\tfrac{a}{1+a}}\right)= (1+a)^{3/2}\big(2-\sqrt{1+a}-\sqrt a\big) =: g(a)\,.
\end{equation}
Note that $f_s(0)=g(0)=1$, and since $\sqrt{1+a}+\sqrt a$ increases from $1$ to $2$ on $[0,9/16]$, one has $g\geq 0$ with $g(9/16)=0$. We are going to prove that $f_s\geq g$ on $(0,0.14)$ for $s=2$, and on $(0,9/16)$ for $s=0.62$.

\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~I.}}\emph{ $f_s$ is positive, decreasing and convex, with concave derivative.}\\
Each derivative of $\psi$ is a difference of two powers of $1+a$, in particular
\begin{align*}
\psi'&=(1+a)^{-2}-\tfrac12(1+a)^{-1/2}\,, \\
\psi''&=-2(1+a)^{-3}+\tfrac14(1+a)^{-3/2}\,, \\
\psi'''&=6(1+a)^{-4}-\tfrac38(1+a)^{-5/2}\,.
\end{align*}
On $[0,9/16]$, we have $(1+a)^{3/2}\le(25/16)^{3/2}=125/64<2$, hence $\psi'>0$, $\psi''<0$, $\psi'''>0$.
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
r_s'=-1-\tfrac{s}{\pi}\psi'<0\,, && r_s''=-\tfrac{s}{\pi}\psi''>0\,, &&
r_s'''=-\tfrac{s}{\pi}\psi'''<0\,.
\end{align*}
In particular, $r_s$ is a decreasing function of $a\in [0,9/16]$, and then for every $s\leq 2$ we have
\[
1 = r_s(0) \geq r_s(a)\geq r_s(9/16) = \frac{7}{16}-\frac{11s}{100\pi} \geq \frac{7}{16}-\frac{11}{50\pi} >0\,.
\]
Therefore, keeping in mind that $f_s=r_s^2$,
\begin{align*}
f_s'=2r_sr_s'<0\,, && f_s''=2(r_s')^2+2r_sr_s''>0\,, && f_s'''=6r_s'r_s''+2r_sr_s'''<0\,.
\end{align*}
That is, $f_s$ is positive, decreasing and convex, with $f_s'$ concave; in particular $f_s''$ is decreasing.

\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~II.}}\emph{ $g'(0^+)=-\infty$, $g''$ is decreasing on $[0,9/16]$, and $g''$ vanishes at a unique $a_0\in(0,0.16)$.}\\
The definition~(\ref{defg}) of $g$ gives
\[\begin{split}
g'(a) &=\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{1+a}\,\big(2-\sqrt{1+a}-\sqrt a\big)
-\frac{(1+a)^{3/2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt a}\right)\\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1+a}}{2}\left(6-4\sqrt{1+a}-4\sqrt a-\frac{1}{\sqrt a}\right)\,,
\end{split}\]
so $g'(a)\to-\infty$ as $a\to0^+$. Differentiating once more gives
\[
g''(a)=\frac{3}{2\sqrt{1+a}}-\sqrt{\frac{a}{1+a}}-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{a(1+a)}}
-\sqrt{\frac{1+a}{a}}+\frac{1}{4a}\sqrt{\frac{1+a}{a}}-2 .
\]

Now, we study the sign of $g''$ and $g'''$. Let $t := t(a) = \sqrt{\tfrac{a}{1+a}}$, which is strictly increasing and maps $[0,9/16]$ onto $[0,3/5]$. Let $\tilde g$ be defined as
\[
\tilde g(t(a)) := g''(a) = \frac{3}{2}\sqrt{1-t^2}-\frac{3}{4}t-\frac{3}{2t}+\frac{1}{4t^3}-2\,.
\]
Notice that this expression diverges to $+\infty$ for $t\to 0^+$ (so for $a\to 0^+$), thus it is positive in a right neighborhood of $0$.
By computing the first derivative in $t$ we obtain
\begin{equation}
\tilde g'(t)=-\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-t^2}}-\frac{3}{4t^4}+\frac{3}{2t^2}-\frac{3}{4}\leq -\frac{3}{4t^4}+\frac{3}{2t^2}
\end{equation}
which is negative for all $t \leq 3/5$. This implies that $\tilde{g}$ is decreasing in $t$ and has at most one zero;
moreover, we have
\[
\tilde{g}(0.37)\approx -0.002<0,
\]
thus there exists a unique $t_0\in (0,0.37)$ such that $\tilde{g}(t_0)=0$. Since $t(0.16)\approx 0.371>0.37$, this implies that there exists a unique $a_0\in (0,0.16)$ such that $g''(a_0)=0$. Lastly, since $t(a)$ is strictly increasing, $g'''(a)$ has the same sign as $\tilde{g}'(t)$, thus proving that $g''(a)$ is decreasing for $a\in [0,9/16]$, as we wanted to show.

\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~III.}}\emph{ Conclusion.}\\
Keep in mind that our goal is to show that $f_s\geq g$ for $a \in (0,0.14)$ if $s=2$, and for $a\in (0,9/16)$ if $s=0.62$. We set $h=f_s-g$, so that we have to establish $h\geq 0$. Notice that $h(0)=0$ since $f_s(0)=g(0)=1$. Since $g'(a)\to-\infty$ when $a\to 0^+$, while $f_s'(0)=-2-\tfrac{s}{\pi}$, we have $h'(0^+)=+\infty$, so $h$ is increasing in a right neighbourhood of $0$, hence the desired inequality $h\geq 0$ is true at least in a right neighborhood of $0$. We are now going to consider separately the case $s=2$ and the case $s=0.62$; in both cases, we will extensively use the fact that $f_s''$ and $g''$ are decreasing, as established in Steps~I and~II.

\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~IIIa.}}\emph{ The case $s=2$ on $(0,0.14)$.}\\
On $(0,0.08)$, monotonicity of $f_2''$ and $g''$ gives
\[
h''=f_2''-g''\le f_2''(0)-g''(0.08)\le-0.4<0,
\]
so $h$ is concave on $(0,0.08)$. Since $h(0)=0$ and $h(0.08)\geq 0.045$, we deduce that $h>0$ on the whole interval $(0,0.08]$.\par

On the interval $(0.08,0.14)$, the concavity of $f_2'$ and of $g'$ give
\[
f_2'(a)\leq f_2'(0.28) + f_2''(0.28)(a-0.28) \approx 3.11a-2.34
\]
and
\[
g'(a)\geq \frac{g'(0.28)-g'(0.08)}{0.2}(a-0.08)+g'(0.08)\approx 0.17a-1.45\,.
\]
Since $3.11a-2.34<0.17a-1.48$ for all $a \in (0.08, 0.14)$, we deduce that $h$ is strictly decreasing in that interval, and so for every $a$ in the interval we have
\[
h(a)\geq h(0.14)\geq 0.001>0\,,
\]
thus concluding this case.

\par\noindent{\underline{\it Step~IIIb.}}\emph{ The case $s=0.62$ on $(0,9/16)$.}\\
On $[0,9/16]$ one has $\psi\ge0$ and $r_s\in(0,1]$, so $s\mapsto f_s(a)=r_s(a)^2$ is decreasing; thus $f_{0.62}\ge f_2$, and then we already have $h>0$ on $(0,0.14)$ by Step~IIIa.\par

Let us then now consider the interval $[0.14,9/16)$; first of all, we can show that $h$ is convex there. This is clear in $[a_0,9/16)$, being $a_0$ given in Step~II; in fact, in $[a_0,9/16)$ we have that $h''=f_{0.62}''-g''>0$. Instead, in $[0.14,a_0)\subseteq [0.14,0.16)$ we can use that $f_{0.62}''$ and $g''$ are decreasing, and then
\[
h''(a)=f_{0.62}''(a)-g''(a) \geq f_{0.62}''(0.16)-g''(0.14)\ \geq\ 1.9>0\,.
\]
The convexity ensures that, in the interval $[0.14,9/16)$, $h$ lies above its tangent at any point, in particular at the point $a=0.285$. This gives us that, for every $a\in[0.14,9/16]$, one has
\[
h(a)\geq  h(0.285)+h'(0.285)(a-0.285) \geq 0.0021-0.0009|a-0.285| > 0.0021 - 0.0009 >0\,,
\]
which finally concludes the proof.
