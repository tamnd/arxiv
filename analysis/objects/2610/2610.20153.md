\label{lem:grading}
Choose a basis $u_1,\ldots,u_{2n}$ of $\mathfrak L$ and the basis
$w^1,\ldots,w^{2n}$ of $\overline\mathfrak L$ determined by
$2\langle u_a,w^b\rangle=\delta_a^b$.
For $0\ne\rho\in K$, the vectors
\[
s_I=c(w^{i_1})\cdots c(w^{i_r})\rho,
\qquad I=\{i_1<\cdots<i_r\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,2n\},
\]
with $s_\varnothing=\rho$, form a basis of $S$, and $K=\mathbb C\rho$.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grading}
\mathfrak U^{n-r}=\bigwedge^r\overline\mathfrak L\cdot K
\qquad(0\le r\le2n).
\end{equation}
Then
\[
S=\bigoplus_{j=-n}^n\mathfrak U^j,\qquad
\dim\mathfrak U^j=\binom{2n}{n-j},\qquad
\overline{\mathfrak U^j}=\mathfrak U^{-j},
\]
and Clifford multiplication satisfies
\[
c(\mathfrak L)\mathfrak U^j\subseteq\mathfrak U^{j+1},\qquad
c(\overline\mathfrak L)\mathfrak U^j\subseteq\mathfrak U^{j-1}.
\]
Moreover, there are natural vector-space isomorphisms
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spinor-cochain-map}
\begin{aligned}
F_r:\mathfrak U^{n-r}&\longrightarrow C^r(\mathfrak L,K)
=\bigwedge^r\mathfrak L^*\otimes K,\\
F_r(\alpha)(v_1,\ldots,v_r)&=c(v_r)\cdots c(v_1)\alpha.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We set $\mathfrak U^j=0$ outside $-n\le j\le n$ and extend $F_r$ by zero
outside $0\le r\le2n$.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Put $a_a=c(u_a)$ and $b_a=c(w^a)$. Isotropy and the Clifford
relation imply
\[
a_aa_b+a_ba_a=b_ab_b+b_bb_a=0,
\qquad a_ab_b+b_ba_a=\delta_a^b\operatorname{id}_S.
\]
Moving $a_a$ through a monomial and using $a_a\rho=0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contraction}
a_as_I=
\begin{cases}
0,&a\notin I,\\
(-1)^{t-1}s_{I\setminus\{a\}},&a=i_t.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Suppose a linear relation among the $s_I$ has a nonzero coefficient
indexed by a set $I=\{i_1<\cdots<i_r\}$ of maximal size.
Applying $a_{i_r}\cdots a_{i_1}$ kills every other term with a
nonzero coefficient and sends $s_I$ to $\rho$, a contradiction.
The $2^{2n}$ monomials therefore form a basis of $S$.
For fixed $a$, distinct monomials containing $a$ are sent by $a_a$
to distinct basis vectors, up to sign. A vector annihilated by all
$a_a$ can consequently have only its $s_\varnothing$ coefficient
nonzero. This proves $K=\mathbb C\rho$.
Grouping monomials by their length proves the direct decomposition,
the dimensions, and the two grading shifts.

Conjugation can also be read directly from this basis. The nonzero
top monomial $s_{\rm top}=b_1\cdots b_{2n}\rho$ is annihilated by
all $b_a$. Conjugating the definition of $K$ identifies this common
annihilator with $\overline K=\mathbb C\bar\rho$, so $s_{\rm top}$ spans it.
Repeated use of~\eqref{eq:contraction} shows that
$\bigwedge^r\mathfrak L\cdot s_{\rm top}$ is spanned by all $s_I$ of
length $2n-r$. Hence
\[
\overline{\mathfrak U^{n-r}}
=\bigwedge^r\mathfrak L\cdot\overline K
=\mathfrak U^{r-n}.
\]

Finally, the values of $F_r$ lie in $\mathfrak U^n=K$ by the grading shifts,
and the map is alternating because Clifford operators from $\mathfrak L$
anticommute. For $w_1,\ldots,w_r\in\overline\mathfrak L$, the relations give
\[
F_r\bigl(c(w_1)\cdots c(w_r)\rho\bigr)(v_1,\ldots,v_r)
=\det\bigl(2\langle v_i,w_j\rangle\bigr)\rho.
\]
This identifies the monomial basis with the usual alternating
cochain basis and proves the last assertion.

\label{prop:integrable-differentials}
If $\mathcal J$ is integrable, then $\delta_+=\delta_-=0$. Consequently,
\[
\mathrm d=\partial+\overline\partial,\qquad
\partial^2=\overline\partial^2=0,\qquad
\partial\overline\partial+\overline\partial\partial=0.
\]
Moreover, a non-degenerate pure spinor $\rho$ defines an integrable
structure if and only if $\mathrm d\rho=c(z)\rho$ for some $z\in \overline{\mathfrak{L}}$.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:canonical-complex}
With the action $v\cdot s=D_vs$ on $K$, the maps
$F_p$ of~\eqref{eq:spinor-cochain-map} make the following diagram commute
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[row sep=large, column sep=large]
\cdots \arrow[r] & \mathfrak{U}^{n-p+1} \arrow[r, "\overline{\partial}"] \arrow[d, "F_{p-1}"] & \mathfrak{U}^{n-p} \arrow[r, "\overline{\partial}"] \arrow[d, "F_{p}"] & \mathfrak{U}^{n-p-1} \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "F_{p+1}"] & \cdots \\
\cdots \arrow[r] & C^{p-1}(\mathfrak L,K)  \arrow[r, "\mathrm d_{\mathfrak L,K}"] & C^{p}(\mathfrak L,K) \arrow[r, "\mathrm d_{\mathfrak L,K}"] & C^{p+1}(\mathfrak L,K) \arrow[r] & \cdots
\end{tikzcd}
\]
in other words,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain-identification}
F_{p+1}\overline\partial=\mathrm d_{\mathfrak L,K}F_p.
\end{equation}
After choosing $\rho$ to identify $K$ with $\mathbb C_\chi$, the differential
on the right is $\mathrm d_{\mathfrak L,\chi}=\mathrm d_\mathfrak L+\chi\wedge$.

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Take $\alpha\in\mathfrak U^{n-p}$ and $v_0,\ldots,v_p\in\mathfrak L$, and
write $c_i=c(v_i)$ and $D_i=D_{v_i}$.
The grading gives
$c_p\cdots c_0\alpha=c_p\cdots c_0\partial\alpha=0$.
Therefore
\[
F_{p+1}(\overline\partial\alpha)(v_0,\ldots,v_p)
=c_p\cdots c_0\mathrm d\alpha.
\]
Successively substitute $c_i\mathrm d=D_i-\mathrm d c_i$ to obtain
\[
c_p\cdots c_0\mathrm d\alpha
=\sum_{i=0}^p(-1)^i
c_p\cdots c_{i+1}D_i c_{i-1}\cdots c_0\alpha;
\]
the last term, $(-1)^{p+1}\mathrm d(c_p\cdots c_0\alpha)$, is zero.
Move $D_i$ to the far left using
$c_jD_i=D_ic_j-c([\![ v_i,v_j]\!])$ for $j>i$.
The term in which $D_i$ reaches the left acts on a value of $F_p$
in $K$, so it gives the $i$th coefficient-action term of
\eqref{eq:CE}. A bracket contribution for $i<j$ initially has
sign $(-1)^{i+1}$. To place that bracket in the first cochain
argument, move its Clifford factor to the far right, across
$j-1$ other factors. Because all these factors belong to the
isotropic space $\mathfrak L$, each interchange contributes only a minus
sign. The resulting sign is $(-1)^{i+j}$.
Writing $\widetilde\alpha=F_p(\alpha)$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
F_{p+1}(\overline\partial\alpha)(v_0,\ldots,v_p)
&=\sum_{i=0}^p(-1)^i v_i\cdot
\widetilde\alpha(v_0,\ldots,\widehat v_i,\ldots,v_p)\\
&\quad+\sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i+j}
\widetilde\alpha([\![ v_i,v_j]\!],v_0,\ldots,
\widehat v_i,\ldots,\widehat v_j,\ldots,v_p).
\end{align*}
This is the coefficient differential~\eqref{eq:CE}, proving
\eqref{eq:chain-identification}.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:L-almost-abelian}
Let $\mathcal J$ be an invariant generalized complex structure on
$\mathfrak g_A$, with eigenspace $\mathfrak L$ and canonical character $\chi$. Then
the map
\[
\phi:\mathfrak L\longrightarrow\mathbb C,\qquad \phi(X+\xi)=e^0(X),
\]
is a surjective Lie algebra homomorphism with abelian kernel
$\mathfrak n$. Hence, for any $Z\in\mathfrak L$ with $\phi(Z)=1$,
\[
\mathfrak L=\mathbb C Z\ltimes_C\mathfrak n,\qquad
C=\operatorname{ad}_Z|_{\mathfrak n}:\mathfrak n\to\mathfrak n,\qquad
\dim_\mathbb C\mathfrak n=2n-1.
\]
The endomorphism $C$ does not depend on the choice of $Z$.
Moreover, $\chi|_{\mathfrak n}=0$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chi-phi}
\chi=\tau\phi,\qquad \tau=\chi(Z),
\end{equation}
and $\tau$ does not depend on the choice of $Z$.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The vector part of $[\![ u,v]\!]$ lies in $[\mathfrak g_A,\mathfrak g_A]\subseteq\mathfrak h_\mathbb C$
by~\eqref{eq:cotangent-bracket}. Hence $\phi([\![ u,v]\!])=0$, and $\phi$ is a
homomorphism to the abelian Lie algebra $\mathbb C$. If $\phi=0$, the vector
projections of both $\mathfrak L$ and $\overline\mathfrak L$ would lie in $\mathfrak h_\mathbb C$. This
contradicts $E=\mathfrak L\oplus\overline\mathfrak L$. Thus $\phi$ is surjective, and
$\mathfrak n=\ker\phi$ is an ideal of dimension $2n-1$.

Take $X+\xi,\,Y+\eta\in\mathfrak n$, so that $X,Y\in\mathfrak h_\mathbb C$. Then
$[X,Y]=0$. For $W\in\mathfrak h$ the convention
$(\operatorname{ad}_X^*\eta)(W)=-\eta([X,W])$ gives zero, while for $W=e_0$ it gives
$\eta(AX)$. Therefore
\[
[\![ X+\xi,Y+\eta]\!]=\bigl(\eta(AX)-\xi(AY)\bigr)e^0 .
\]
By integrability, this element lies in $\mathfrak L$. Its pairing with $Z$ is
$\tfrac12\bigl(\eta(AX)-\xi(AY)\bigr)\phi(Z)$. Isotropy of $\mathfrak L$ forces
this pairing, and hence the bracket, to vanish. Thus $\mathfrak n$ is
abelian, and the semidirect-product decomposition follows. If
$\phi(Z')=1$, then $Z'-Z\in\mathfrak n$. Hence
$\operatorname{ad}_{Z'}|_{\mathfrak n}=\operatorname{ad}_Z|_{\mathfrak n}$.

For the character, fix $0\ne\rho\in K$. By~\eqref{eq:dgA} we can write
$\mathrm d\rho=e^0\wedge\beta$ for some $\beta\in S$. Let
$v=X+\xi\in\mathfrak n$, so that $e^0(X)=0$. Then
\[
c(v)(e^0\wedge\beta)
=\iota_X(e^0\wedge\beta)+\xi\wedge e^0\wedge\beta
=-e^0\wedge c(v)\beta .
\]
By~\eqref{eq:character}, $\chi(v)\rho=c(v)\mathrm d\rho\in e^0\wedge S$.
Suppose $\chi(v)\ne0$. Then $\rho\in e^0\wedge S$, so
$c(e^0)\rho=e^0\wedge\rho=0$, and $e^0\in\operatorname{Ann}(\rho)=\mathfrak L$. Since $e^0$ is
real, it would also lie in $\overline\mathfrak L$. This is impossible because
$\mathfrak L\cap\overline\mathfrak L=0$. Hence $\chi|_{\mathfrak n}=0$. The linear forms
$\chi$ and $\chi(Z)\phi$ agree on $Z$ and on $\mathfrak n$, which
gives~\eqref{eq:chi-phi}. Finally, $\chi(Z')=\chi(Z)$ whenever
$Z'-Z\in\mathfrak n$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:wang}
Let $\mathcal J$ be an invariant generalized complex structure on
$\mathfrak g_A$, and let $\tau$ be as in~\eqref{eq:chi-phi}. Put
\[
\Psi_p=\Psi_C|_{\bigwedge^p\mathfrak n^*}-\tau\operatorname{id}
\qquad(0\le p\le 2n-1),
\]
and let $\Psi_p$ be the zero map of the zero space otherwise.
Then, for $0\le p\le 2n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wang}
GH_{\overline\partial}^{n-p}(\mathfrak g_A)\cong H^p(\mathfrak L,\mathbb C_\chi)
\cong\ker\Psi_p\oplus\operatorname{coker}\Psi_{p-1}.
\end{equation}
Let $c_1,\ldots,c_{2n-1}$ be the eigenvalues of $C$, with algebraic
multiplicity. The eigenvalues of $\Psi_p$ are then
$\sum_{i\in I}c_i-\tau$, where $I$ runs over index subsets of size $p$.
If $C$ is diagonalizable, so is $\Psi_p$, and $\dim\ker\Psi_p$ is the
number of these sums that vanish.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The first isomorphism is~\eqref{eq:ACK}. By~\eqref{eq:cochain-splitting},
a $p$-cochain can be written uniquely as $\alpha+\phi\wedge\beta$, with
$\alpha\in\bigwedge^p\mathfrak n^*$ and
$\beta\in\bigwedge^{p-1}\mathfrak n^*$. We use~\eqref{eq:twist},
\eqref{eq:chi-phi} and~\eqref{eq:almost-d}, together with $\mathrm d_\mathfrak L\phi=0$
and $\phi\wedge\phi=0$. They give
$\mathrm d_\mathfrak L(\phi\wedge\beta)=-\phi\wedge\mathrm d_\mathfrak L\beta=0$, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-term}
\mathrm d_{\mathfrak L,\chi}(\alpha+\phi\wedge\beta)
=-\phi\wedge\Psi_C\alpha+\tau\phi\wedge\alpha
=-\phi\wedge\Psi_p\alpha.
\end{equation}
The complex is therefore the direct sum, over $p$, of the two-term
complexes
\[
\bigwedge^p\mathfrak n^*
\xrightarrow{\;-\phi\wedge\Psi_p\;}
\phi\wedge\bigwedge^p\mathfrak n^*,
\]

$\bigwedge^\bullet\mathfrak n^*$. Hence the summand indexed by $p$
contributes $\ker\Psi_p$ in degree $p$, and the summand indexed by $p-1$
contributes $\operatorname{coker}\Psi_{p-1}$ in degree $p$. This proves~\eqref{eq:wang}. The isomorphism sends $(\alpha,[\beta])$ to the class of
$F_p^{-1}\bigl((\alpha+\phi\wedge\beta)\otimes\rho\bigr)$.

diagonalizable, an eigenbasis of $C^T$ gives a wedge eigenbasis of
$\Psi_p$, which proves the kernel count.

\label{thm:master}

coefficient extraction. If $\operatorname{tr} A=0$, then

    C=g(z+\mathbf i y_0)=e^{\mathbf i\kappa y_0}g(z)=e^{\mathbf i\kappa y_0}C\implies C(1-e^{\mathbf i \kappa y_0})=0.
\end{align*}
Thus $\mathcal E_\kappa$ is
holomorphically trivial if $\kappa y_0\in2\pi\mathbb Z$, with frame
$e^{-\mathbf i\kappa y}$. Otherwise, it is a nontrivial line bundle of degree
zero on an elliptic curve, and all its cohomology vanishes.

If $\zeta y_0\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$, Lemma~\ref{lem:characters} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:invariance} give $GH_{\overline\partial}(X)\cong GH_{\overline\partial}(\mathfrak g_A)$.
If $\zeta y_0\in2\pi\mathbb Z$, then~\eqref{eq:character-hypothesis} fails
for $\kappa=\pm\zeta$. In~\eqref{eq:SS-splitting}, $E_2(Q)$ vanishes in
the rows $q=0,2$ and equals the row $q=1$ of $E_2$. By
\eqref{eq:second-pages}, this row has dimensions $2,4,2$ for
$p=0,1,2$. A spectral sequence with a single nonzero row degenerates, so
\[
\bigl(\dim GH^2_{\overline\partial},\ldots,\dim GH^{-2}_{\overline\partial}\bigr)(X)
=(1,4,6,4,1).
\]
Thus, for the same $\mathfrak g_A$ and $\mathcal J$, invariant forms compute
$GH_{\overline\partial}(X)$ for some lattices but not for others. Notice that the dependence on the lattice is not a surprise; the Dolbeault cohomology of complex solvmanifolds is also known to vary with the choice of $\Gamma$, as in \cite{Kasuya13}*{Example 1}.
