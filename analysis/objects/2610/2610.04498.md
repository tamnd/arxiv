<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:count}
Let $P$ be a $d$-dimensional polytope with a fixed size-$r$ real PSD
slack factorization $\ell_i(v)=\operatorname{tr}(A_iB_v)$. For $1\le k\le r$, put
\[
 N_{\le k}=\bigl|\{v\in\operatorname{vert}(P):\operatorname{rank} B_v\le k\}\bigr|.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:low-rank-count}
 N_{\le k}\le\prod_{i=r-k+1}^{r}\frac{3^i+1}{2}-1
 \le\frac{3^{rk-\binom{k}{2}}-1}{2}.
\end{equation}
No symmetry, inscribability, or genericity assumption is imposed on $P$
or its factorization.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:basic}
For a $d$-dimensional polytope $P$,
\[
\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(P)\ge d+1=\operatorname{rank} S_P.
\]
If $M$ is nonnegative and $R\circ R=M$, then $\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}(M)\le\operatorname{rank} R$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The first statement is \cite[Proposition 3.2]{GRT2013}. For the second,
write $R_{ij}=a_i^{\mathsf T}b_j$ with $a_i,b_j\in\mathbb R^r$ and observe that
$M_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(a_ia_i^{\mathsf T}b_jb_j^{\mathsf T})$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:faces}
If $F$ is a nonempty face of $P$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:face-bound}
\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(P)\ge\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(F)+\dim P-\dim F.
\end{equation}
Affine images and nonempty affine sections cannot increase PSD extension
complexity. If $0\in\operatorname{int}P$, then $\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(P^\circ)=\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(P)$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For a facet $H$, take a size-$r$ PSD factorization of $S_P$, with facet factor
$A_H\ne0$. If $v\in H$, positivity and $\operatorname{tr}(A_HB_v)=0$ imply
$\operatorname{im}B_v\subseteq\ker A_H$. Compress the factors to this kernel.
The facet inequalities of $H$ are restrictions of suitable facet inequalities
of $P$, so this gives a factorization of $S_H$ of size at most $r-1$.
Iteration along a face flag proves \eqref{eq:face-bound}; this is the argument
of \cite[Proposition 3.8]{GRT2013}. An affine section is imposed by extra
affine equations on the lift, and an image by composing the projection.
Polarity transposes a slack matrix up to positive scaling.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:rank-budget}
In a size-$r$ real PSD factorization of a $d$-polytope, every facet factor
and every vertex factor has rank at most $r-d$. If $d\ge2$ and $v$ lies
on a facet $F$, then
\[
\operatorname{rank} A_F+\operatorname{rank} B_v\le r-d+1.
\]
In particular, when $r=d+2$, every factor has rank one or two, and an
incident facet and vertex cannot both have rank-two factors.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Write $a=\operatorname{rank} A_F$. Compression to $\ker A_F$, as in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:faces}, factors the slack matrix of $F$ in size $r-a$.
For $d\ge2$, the dimension bound gives $r-a\ge d$. For a segment
($d=1$), the same inequality follows because the factor of its endpoint
is nonzero and supported on this kernel. Transposing a slack matrix of
the polar gives the identical rank bound for vertex factors.
Now let $d\ge2$ and $v\in F$. In the compressed factorization of $F$,
the rank of $B_v$ is unchanged. Applying the vertex-factor bound just
proved in dimension $d-1$ gives
$\operatorname{rank} B_v\le(r-a)-(d-1)$, as required. Nonzero slack rows and columns
ensure that all factors are nonzero. This refines the usual kernel
argument for the dimension bound \cite{GRT2013}.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:gram}
Every $B\in\mathbb S^{r}_+$ of rank at most $k\le r$ can be written
$B=YY^{\mathsf T}$, where $Y\in\mathbb R^{r\times k}$ and $Y_{ij}=0$ for
$i<j$. Such matrices $Y$ have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gram-dimension}
 M(r,k)=\sum_{j=1}^k(r-j+1)=rk-\binom{k}{2}
\end{equation}
free entries, and $\operatorname{tr} B=\|Y\|_F^2$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Start with any Gram factor $Y_0$, padding with zero columns, and take
an orthogonal QR decomposition $Y_0^{\mathsf T}=QR$. Then
$Y=R^{\mathsf T}$ has the prescribed zeros and
$YY^{\mathsf T}=Y_0Y_0^{\mathsf T}$. This works at every rank and
requires no common choice of nonzero pivots.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:sos-rank}
The quantity $\operatorname{sosdim}(P)$ equals the minimum factorization size
when every vertex factor has rank one. Moreover, if $d=\dim P$ and
$r=\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(P)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sos-gap}
 r\le\operatorname{sosdim}(P)\le
 \min\left\{\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{LP}}(P),\binom{r+1}{2}-\binom{d+1}{2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
If $f_1,\ldots,f_s$ is a basis of an admissible function space, set
$f(v)=(f_1(v),\ldots,f_s(v))$. The SOS identities have the form
$\ell_i(v)=f(v)^{\mathsf T}A_if(v)$ with $A_i\succeq0$, giving
$B_v=f(v)f(v)^{\mathsf T}$. These vectors are nonzero, since no vertex
lies on every facet. Conversely, the coordinates of $b_v$ in a
factorization with $B_v=b_vb_v^{\mathsf T}$ span an admissible space.
This proves the equivalence and the lower bound.

For the LP upper bound, a nonnegative factorization $S=TU$ of inner
dimension $q$ gives functions $f_a(v)=\sqrt{U_{av}}$, since
$S_{iv}=\sum_aT_{ia}f_a(v)^2$. For the other upper bound, apply
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-budget} and Lemma~\ref{lem:gram} with
$k=r-d$. The $M(r,k)$ free entries of one chosen Gram factor $Y_v$,
viewed as functions of $v$, certify each facet slack because
$\ell_i(v)=\|A_i^{1/2}Y_v\|_F^2$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:linear-branches}
Let $X$ be a nonempty compact subset of a polytope $P\subseteq\mathbb R^d$,
and put $h_X(c)=\max_{x\in X}c^{\mathsf T}x$.
Suppose real functions $h_\varepsilon(c)$ converge to $h_X(c)$ for
every fixed $c$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. If a nonzero real polynomial
$F(\varepsilon,c,z)$ satisfies
\[
 F(\varepsilon,c,h_\varepsilon(c))=0
 \qquad(\varepsilon>0,\ c\in\mathbb R^d),
\]
then at most $\deg_z F$ vertices of $P$ belong to $X$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Cancel the largest power of $\varepsilon$ dividing $F$. The relation
is preserved for $\varepsilon>0$, and now
\[
 F_0(c,z):=F(0,c,z)\ne0,\qquad \deg_z F_0\le\deg_z F.
\]
Passing to the limit gives $F_0(c,h_X(c))=0$ for every $c$.

Fix a vertex $v\in X$. On the open interior of its normal cone in $P$,
$h_X(c)=c^{\mathsf T}v$. Thus the polynomial
$F_0(c,c^{\mathsf T}v)$ vanishes on a real open set and is identically
zero. Hence $z-c^{\mathsf T}v$ divides $F_0$. Distinct vertices give
distinct monic linear factors in $z$, proving the bound.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:corners}
Partition $y\in\mathbb R^M$ into blocks $y_j\in\mathbb R^{m_j}$, where $m_j\ge1$ and $\sum_{j=1}^k m_j=M$. Let $q$ have degree at most four, be nonnegative on the unit sphere, and be invariant under changing the sign of any entire block independently. Suppose
\[
 T=\{y:\|y\|^2=1,\ q(y)=0\}
\]
is nonempty. Let $\phi:\mathbb R^M\to\mathbb R^d$ have coordinate polynomials of degree at most two with the same invariance, and suppose $\phi(T)\subseteq P$ for a polytope $P$. The number of vertices of $P$ belonging to $\phi(T)$ is at most
\[
 C(m_1,\ldots,m_k):=\prod_{j=1}^k\frac{3^{m_j}+1}{2}-1.
\]
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
\emph{Approximating the support function.}
We will apply Lemma~\ref{lem:linear-branches} to $X=\phi(T)$.
Put $\rho(y)=y^{\mathsf T}y$. Block-sign invariance implies that every
homogeneous term has even degree. Replacing $q_4+q_2+q_0$ by
$q_4+\rho q_2+\rho^2q_0$, and each coordinate $\phi_2+\phi_0$ by
$\phi_2+\rho\phi_0$, changes neither polynomial on the unit sphere.
We may therefore assume that $q$ is homogeneous quartic and $\phi$
homogeneous quadratic.

Use the fixed quartic $R(y)=\sum_{i=1}^M y_i^4$, which preserves all
block signs. For $\varepsilon>0$, set
\[
 p_{\varepsilon,c}(y)=-q(y)+\varepsilon\rho(y)c^{\mathsf T}\phi(y)
 +\varepsilon^2R(y),\qquad
 f_{\varepsilon,c}=p_{\varepsilon,c}/\varepsilon.
\]
On the unit sphere, $f=c^{\mathsf T}\phi-q/\varepsilon+\varepsilon R$.
The penalty forces maximizers toward $T$, while the smaller perturbation
will make the critical-point equations finite.
Its maximum $h_\varepsilon(c)$ is continuous in $(\varepsilon,c)$ for
$\varepsilon>0$, since the sphere is compact. For each fixed $c$,
$h_\varepsilon(c)\to\max_T c^{\mathsf T}\phi$. Indeed, evaluating at a
maximizer on $T$ gives the lower limit inequality. Boundedness on the
sphere and comparison with a point of $T$ give
$q(y_\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon)$ at any maximizer $y_\varepsilon$.
Every accumulation point therefore lies in $T$, and $-q/\varepsilon\le0$
gives the upper limit inequality.

\emph{Counting critical points.}
A maximum on the sphere is a critical value. Homogeneity turns the
critical-point equations into a cubic system with no multiplier.
For generic parameters with $\varepsilon\ne0$, the homogeneous quartic
$p=p_{\varepsilon,c}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smooth-quartic}
 \nabla p(x)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=0
 \qquad (x\in\mathbb C^M).
\end{equation}
This condition is the nonvanishing of the resultant of the partial
derivatives. For each fixed $c$, that resultant is a nonzero polynomial
in $\varepsilon$: as $\varepsilon\to\infty$, the coefficients of
$p_{\varepsilon,c}/\varepsilon^2$ tend to those of $R$, whose gradient
vanishes only at the origin. Thus only finitely many $\varepsilon$ are
excluded for each $c$.

Consider the cubic system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kkt}
 \nabla p(x)=x.
\end{equation}
Its homogenization is $\partial_i p(x)=t^2x_i$ in $\mathbb P^M$.
Condition \eqref{eq:smooth-quartic} excludes solutions at $t=0$.
The projective zero set is consequently finite: a positive-dimensional
projective variety meets every hyperplane. B\'ezout's theorem bounds its
length by $3^M$. The origin is a simple affine solution, since the
Jacobian of $\nabla p(x)-x$ there is $-I$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree}
 \bigl|\{x\ne0:\nabla p(x)=x\}\bigr|\le3^M-1.
\end{equation}

Let $K$ be the subset of these solutions with $\rho(x)\ne0$. Every
critical value of $f$ on the complex sphere is represented on $K$.
Indeed, at a sphere critical point $y$ we have $\nabla p(y)=\lambda y$.
Condition \eqref{eq:smooth-quartic} gives $\lambda\ne0$, and
$x=y/\sqrt\lambda$ satisfies \eqref{eq:kkt}, with
$\rho(x)=1/\lambda$. Euler's identity gives $4p(y)=\lambda$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-value}
 f(y)=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon\rho(x)}.
\end{equation}
Thus isotropic solutions need not be counted, and no zero-eigenvalue
exception occurs.

\emph{Identifying equal values by symmetry.}
Changing block signs preserves the value in \eqref{eq:cubic-value}.
We therefore count the orbits of $G=(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^k$ on $K$.
For $S\subseteq[k]$, write
$M_S=\sum_{j\in S}m_j$. The fixed points of the element that leaves
precisely the blocks in $S$ unflipped solve the restricted cubic system
on those blocks. The omitted gradient coordinates vanish by block-sign
invariance. For the same reason, the restricted quartic satisfies
\eqref{eq:smooth-quartic}; otherwise its nonzero gradient zero would
also be a gradient zero of $p$. The preceding B\'ezout argument gives
$|\operatorname{Fix}_K(g)|\le3^{M_S}-1$, including zero for
$S=\varnothing$. Burnside's lemma now yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sign-orbits}
 |K/G|=2^{-k}\sum_{g\in G}|\operatorname{Fix}_K(g)|
 \le2^{-k}\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}(3^{M_S}-1)
 =C(m_1,\ldots,m_k).
\end{equation}
This includes the stabilizers at zero blocks, which prevent simply
dividing the full count by $2^k$.

\emph{Passing from critical values to the limit.}
Form the homogeneous resultant $\mathcal R(\varepsilon,c,z)$ of
\[
 \partial_i p(x)-t^2x_i=0\quad(1\le i\le M),\qquad
 4\varepsilon z\rho(x)-t^2=0.
\]
It is a polynomial in the parameters, vanishing exactly when these
equations have a common zero in $\mathbb P^M$
\cite[Chapter 3]{CoxLittleOShea2005}.
Under \eqref{eq:smooth-quartic}, no such zero has $t=0$. For
$\varepsilon\ne0$, its roots in $z$ are therefore precisely the values
in \eqref{eq:cubic-value}. In particular, $\mathcal R$ is nonzero:
$z=0$ would force $t=0$.

Factor in $\mathbb R[\varepsilon,c,z]$ as
\[
 \mathcal R=A(\varepsilon,c)\prod_i F_i(\varepsilon,c,z)^{e_i},
 \qquad F=\prod_i F_i,
\]
where the $F_i$ are distinct irreducible factors of positive degree in
$z$. For generic parameters, $F$ retains its degree in $z$ and has
distinct roots, since the coefficient field has characteristic zero.
Each root is a critical value, so \eqref{eq:sign-orbits} gives
$\deg_z F\le C(m_1,\ldots,m_k)$.

For generic real parameters with $\varepsilon>0$, the maximum
$h_\varepsilon(c)$ is one of these values, and hence
$F(\varepsilon,c,h_\varepsilon(c))=0$. Both the maximum and the
polynomial are continuous. The same identity therefore holds for
\emph{every} $\varepsilon>0$ and $c\in\mathbb R^d$, including exceptional
parameters. The support-function convergence proved above and
Lemma~\ref{lem:linear-branches} finish the proof.

Put $M=rk-\binom{k}{2}$. If $N_{\le k}=0$, there is nothing to prove. Work in affine coordinates in which $P$ is full-dimensional. Boundedness implies that the facet normals positively span $\mathbb R^d$, so a strictly positive linear combination of the facet slacks is constant. That constant is positive by evaluation at an interior point. Rescaling gives positive weights $\lambda_i$ such that
\[
 \sum_i\lambda_i\ell_i(x)=1\quad\text{identically on }\mathbb R^d.
\]
Put $H=\sum_i\lambda_iA_i$. Compress to the orthogonal complement of its kernel if necessary. Each $A_i$ vanishes on that kernel, so all slack entries are preserved under this compression, and vertex-factor ranks cannot increase. A congruence transformation then makes $H=I$. The effective matrix order may have decreased to $r'\le r$ and the effective rank cap to $k'=\min\{k,r'\}$; the product $\prod_{i=r'-k'+1}^{r'}\frac{3^i+1}{2}$ is at most
$\prod_{i=r-k+1}^{r}\frac{3^i+1}{2}$, since its factors are increasing with
their indices and exceed one. It therefore suffices to prove the bound after compression. Reset $r,k$ to the effective values and $M=rk-\binom{k}{2}$. We now have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal}
 \sum_i\lambda_i A_i=I,\qquad \operatorname{tr} B_v=1.
\end{equation}

The slack map $x\mapsto(\ell_i(x))_i$ is an affine injection. Let $L$ be its affine image and choose affine equations for $L$, together with an affine map recovering $x$ from a slack vector in $L$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}, every rank-at-most-$k$ PSD matrix can be written as $YY^{\mathsf T}$ with $Y\in\mathbb R^{r\times k}$ and $Y_{ij}=0$ for $i<j$. The $M$ unrestricted entries form a vector $y\in\mathbb R^M$, partitioned
into column blocks of dimensions $m_j=r-j+1$. Substituting
\[
 z_i(y)=\operatorname{tr}(A_iYY^{\mathsf T})
\]
into the affine equations for $L$ gives polynomials $g_\alpha(y)$ of degree at most two. Substituting into the affine recovery map gives a quadratic map $\phi(y)$. Both are invariant under independent column signs, since these leave $YY^{\mathsf T}$ unchanged. Define
\[
 q(y)=\sum_\alpha g_\alpha(y)^2,\qquad
 T=\{y:\|y\|^2=1,\ q(y)=0\}.
\]
This is a nonnegative quartic of the type required by Lemma~\ref{lem:corners}.

If $y\in T$, then $(z_i(y))_i\in L$, and each $z_i(y)\ge0$. Thus $\phi(y)\in P$. Conversely, any chosen vertex factor of rank at most $k$ has a triangular Gram factor satisfying all $g_\alpha=0$ and $\|y\|^2=\operatorname{tr} B_v=1$, so that vertex belongs to $\phi(T)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:corners} proves the first inequality in \eqref{eq:low-rank-count}. For the second, repeatedly use
$(a+1)(b+1)\le2(ab+1)$ for $a,b\ge1$ to obtain
\[
 \prod_{j=1}^k\frac{3^{m_j}+1}{2}-1
 \le\frac{3^{\sum_jm_j}-1}{2}.\qedhere
\]

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:universal}
Let $P$ have $N$ vertices, dimension $d\ge1$, and $r=\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(P)$. Then
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{sosdim}(P)&\ge\lceil\log_3(2N+1)\rceil,\label{eq:universal-sos}\\
 N&\le\prod_{i=d+1}^{r}\frac{3^i+1}{2}-1,\label{eq:universal-count}\\
 r&\ge\left\lceil
 \frac{\sqrt{(2d+1)^2+8\log_3(2N+1)}-1}{2}
 \right\rceil.\label{eq:universal-rank}
\end{align}
Writing $r=d+t$, bound \eqref{eq:universal-count} is equivalently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:excess-count}
 \log_3(N+1)\le dt+\frac{t(t+1)}2-t\log_3 2
 +\sum_{i=d+1}^{d+t}\log_3(1+3^{-i}).
\end{equation}
The same bound \eqref{eq:universal-count} holds for the number of facets.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->

Use $k=1$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:count} for an SOS factorization, and

The second inequality in \eqref{eq:low-rank-count} gives

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:perm}
For $n\ge3$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:perm-explicit}
 \operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(\Pi_n)\ge\mathcal L_n:=
 \min\left\{r\in\mathbb Z:r\ge n+1,\quad
 n!+1\le\prod_{i=n}^{r}\frac{3^i+1}{2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\[
 \operatorname{sosdim}(\Pi_n)=\Theta(n\log n),\qquad
 \operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(\Pi_n)\ge n+\log_3n-O(1).
\]
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Corollary~\ref{cor:universal} gives
$\operatorname{sosdim}(\Pi_n)\ge\lceil\log_3(2n!+1)\rceil$ and, with $d=n-1$,
the product condition in \eqref{eq:perm-explicit}. The hexagonal-face
bound gives $r\ge n+1$. Proposition~\ref{prop:sos-rank} and Goemans' LP upper
bound prove the matching SOS upper bound.

Put $b_n=\log_3(2n!+1)$ and write $r=n-1+t$. The second inequality
in \eqref{eq:low-rank-count} gives
\[
 (n-1)t+\frac{t(t+1)}2\ge b_n.
\]
Its positive root in $t$ differs from $b_n/(n-1)$ by
$O((\log n)^2/n)$. Stirling's formula gives
$b_n/(n-1)=\log_3 n-1/\ln3+o(1)$, proving the claimed additive bound.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:thresholds}
The first five improvements over the face bound are
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
\text{lower bound}&n+2&n+3&n+4&n+5&n+6\\ \hline
\text{first }n&22&72&221&664&1990
\end{array}
\]
These thresholds require only integer arithmetic. For every integer $j\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold-integer}
 \mathcal L_n\ge n+j
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 2^j(n!+1)>\prod_{i=n}^{n+j-1}(3^i+1).
\end{equation}
Indeed, exclude the integer matrix order $r=n+j-1$ in
\eqref{eq:universal-count}. For fixed $j$, let $\eta_j(n)$ be the ratio
of the left side of the strict inequality to its right side. Then
\[
 \frac{\eta_j(n+1)}{\eta_j(n)}
 =\frac{(n+1)!+1}{n!+1}\frac{3^n+1}{3^{n+j}+1}
 >\frac{n}{3^j}\qquad(n\ge2).
\]
Exact comparisons for $3\le n\le\max\{3^j,n_j\}$, where $n_j$ is
the stated threshold, certify the first occurrences and persistence
through this range. The ratio is increasing thereafter. Each extra unit of excess asymptotically requires
multiplying $n$ by three.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:bounded-vertex-ranks}
For every fixed $k\ge1$,
\[
 r_k(\Pi_n)=\Theta(n\log n),\qquad r_k(R_N)=\Theta(\log N).
\]
The constants may depend on $k$.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
For a polytope $P$ with $N$ vertices, put $r=r_k(P)$ and
$\kappa=\min\{k,r\}$. The second inequality in
Theorem~\ref{thm:count} gives
\[
 r_k(P)\ge\frac{\log_3(2N+1)+\binom{\kappa}{2}}{\kappa}.
\]
In particular, when $k\le r_k(P)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bounded-rank-lower}
 r_k(P)\ge\frac{\log_3(2N+1)+\binom{k}{2}}{k}.
\end{equation}
For fixed $k$, this condition holds for all sufficiently large $N$,
since Corollary~\ref{cor:universal} forces $r_k(P)\to\infty$ as
$N\to\infty$. Apply \eqref{eq:bounded-rank-lower} with $N=n!$ for
the permutahedron and with $N$ for the regular polygon. The LP
upper bounds yield rank-one vertex factors by
Proposition~\ref{prop:sos-rank}, and hence apply for every $k\ge1$.

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:rank-distribution}
For every fixed factorization, the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},leftmargin=2em]
\item Fix $C>0$. In a size-$r$ factorization of $\Pi_n$ with $r\le Cn$,
put $k_n=\lfloor\log_3 n/(2C)\rfloor$. For sufficiently large $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:perm-rank-tail}
 \frac{\bigl|\{\sigma:\operatorname{rank} B_\sigma\le k_n\}\bigr|}{n!}
 \le3^{-\frac12n\log_3 n+O(n)}.
\end{equation}
Thus all but a vanishing fraction of the vertex factors have rank
$\Omega(\log n)$.
\item In a size-$r$ factorization of any $N$-vertex polytope, if
$1\le k\le r$ and $rk\le\tfrac12\log_3N$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polygon-rank-tail}
 \bigl|\{v:\operatorname{rank} B_v\le k\}\bigr|\le\frac{\sqrt N-1}{2}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $r=o(\log N)$ along a sequence of such factorizations,
the fraction of vertex factors of rank at most any fixed $K$ tends to zero.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end corollary-3 -->

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:p3-spans}
Suppose a polygon $P$ with $N\ge5$ vertices has a size-four PSD slack
factorization $S_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(A_iB_j)$. Set
$U=\operatorname{span}\{A_i\}$ and $W=\operatorname{span}\{B_j\}$ in
$\mathbb S^4$. Put $u=\dim U$ and $w=\dim W$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factor-spans}
 u+w\le13.
\end{equation}
Consequently, either $P$ or its polar has a compact size-four
spectrahedral lift of affine dimension at most five.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
The trace pairing restricted to $U\times W$ has rank
$\operatorname{rank} S=3$, since the factors span these spaces. Hence
$\dim(W\cap U^\perp)=\dim W-3$. As $\dim\mathbb S^4=10$,
\[
 \dim W-3\le\dim U^\perp=10-\dim U,
\]
which proves the inequality. Both factor families have positive definite
sums: otherwise compression would give a size-three factorization,
impossible for $N\ge5$ by \cite[Theorem 4.7]{GRT2013}.
Write the facet slacks as $\ell_i(x)$ and choose positive weights
$\lambda_i$ such that $\sum_i\lambda_i\ell_i(x)=1$ identically.
Such weights exist because $P$ is bounded and full-dimensional in its
affine hull. Put $H=\sum_i\lambda_i A_i\succ0$; positivity follows
from the positive definite sum and the strictly positive weights.

Consider
\[
 Q=\{B\in W:B\succeq0,\ \operatorname{tr}(HB)=1\}.
\]
It is compact because $H\succ0$. The average of the normalized vertex
factors is positive definite, so its affine dimension is $w-1$.
If $B=\sum_j t_jB_j$ lies in $Q$, then
$\sum_jt_j=1$, and
\[
 \operatorname{tr}(A_iB)=\ell_i\Bigl(\sum_jt_jv_j\Bigr).
\]
The resulting point is independent of the chosen coefficients, since
the facet normals span the dual of the affine hull. This defines an
affine projection of $Q$ into $P$, by nonnegativity of every trace.
Every $v_j$ lifts to $B_j$, so convexity gives equality of the image
with $P$. Applying the same construction to the polar gives a normalized
PSD slice in $U$ mapping onto $P^\circ$. Since $\min(u,w)\le6$,
one of these lifts has affine dimension at most five.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:corner-linear}
If $\mathbb S(E)\subseteq L$, where $E\subseteq\mathbb R^r$ has dimension $h$,
at most $h$ vertices of $P$ are attained by rank-one matrices supported on $E$.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
Normalize $H|_E$ to the identity by congruence. The two coordinates of
\eqref{eq:corner-map} on $\mathbb P(E)$ are Rayleigh quotients of symmetric matrices
$D_1,D_2$. If a unit vector $z$ maps to a vertex $v$, it maximizes
$z^{\mathsf T}(a_1D_1+a_2D_2)z$ for every $a$ in the interior of the normal
cone of $v$. Hence $z$ is an eigenvector for two linearly independent such
combinations, and thus a common eigenvector of $D_1,D_2$.
Different vertices give different pairs of eigenvalues. Their chosen common
eigenvectors are orthogonal, because at least one of the two symmetric
matrices has different eigenvalues on them. There are at most $h$.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:corner-curve}
Let $C\subseteq X_L$ be a geometrically integral real projective curve of
degree $\delta$, and let its normalization have genus $g$.
If the complex image of \eqref{eq:corner-map} on $C$ is not a line or a point, the
number of vertices of $P$ attained at real points of the normalization is at
most
\[
 3\delta+3g-3.
\]
If the image is a line or a point, the number is at most two.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
Pull the three quadratic sections back to the normalization and remove
their common base divisor. They give a basepoint-free linear series of
projective dimension two and degree $e\le2\delta$. There are no real base
points, since the denominator is positive. At a real normalization point
mapping to a vertex $v$, a local real parameter takes both signs. The two
opposite first derivatives would both have to belong to the pointed tangent
cone of $P$ at $v$. Therefore the derivative is zero; Figure~\ref{fig:corner-stationarity}
shows this local obstruction.

The vanishing sequence of the three sections is consequently at least
$(0,2,3)$, with ramification weight at least two. The Wronskian is a nonzero
section of $\mathcal L^{\otimes3}\otimes K^{\otimes3}$, so its zero divisor
has degree $3e+6g-6$. Equivalently this is the Pl\"ucker formula for a
$g^2_e$; see \cite[Section 4.2]{Esteves2006}. Counting one real preimage of each
distinct vertex gives the bound. If the complex image is a line, that line
contains at most two vertices of a polygon. A constant image contributes
at most one.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:corner-independent}
Any collection of at most seven rank-one matrices in $K_L$ mapping to
distinct vertices of $P$ is linearly independent. Consequently,
$c(L)\le\dim L$ if $\dim L\le6$.

If eight such matrices are dependent, they span a seven-dimensional
space. Their vectors have a common four-dimensional span, and after a
congruence on that span their rays are the projectors onto two
orthonormal bases.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
Choose a minimally dependent subcollection $z_i z_i^{\mathsf T}$ of size
$m\le7$, and split its dependence into two positive sums. Their common
range $E$ has dimension $h$, where
\[
 2h\le m,\qquad m-1\le\dim\mathbb S(E)=\binom{h+1}{2}.
\]
Thus $h\le3$. If $h=1$, all the normalized matrices have the same image.
If $h=2$, the inequalities force $m=4$, and the matrices span
$\mathbb S(E)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:corner-linear} allows only two corner
images on this space.

If $h=3$, then $m=6$ or $7$. For $m=7$ the matrices again span
$\mathbb S(E)$, which allows only three corner images. For $m=6$ their
span is a hyperplane in $\mathbb S(E)$, so its rank-one locus in
$\mathbb P(E)$ is a conic. A smooth conic contributes at most three
corners by Lemma~\ref{lem:corner-curve}; two real lines contribute at
most four by Lemma~\ref{lem:corner-linear}. A double line contributes
at most two, and two conjugate nonreal lines have at most one real point.
None can supply six distinct corners. This proves independence.

Now suppose eight matrices are dependent. Any seven are independent,
so their dependence is unique up to scale and has full support. Splitting
signs gives a positive matrix $M$ on a common range $E$. Since their span
has dimension seven, $\dim E\ge4$. Each side of the dependence has at
least $\dim E$ summands, so there are exactly four on each side and
$\dim E=4$. Congruence by $M^{-1/2}$ on $E$, with the positive
coefficients absorbed into the vectors, turns each sum into a
four-vector decomposition of the identity. Each is an orthonormal basis.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:five-factors}
In a real PSD slack factorization of a polygon, of any size, suppose a
facet factor $A$ of rank $a$ is a linear combination of other rank-one
facet factors. Every such expression has at least $a+2$ positive
coefficients and at least two negative coefficients.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
Write $A=\sum_i t_i a_i a_i^{\mathsf T}$, discarding zero coefficients,
and put $E=\operatorname{span}\{a_i\}$, $h=\dim E$.
There must be at least three terms. Indeed, two distinct facet vectors
cannot be proportional, and a PSD combination of at most two independent
rank-one matrices has only positive coefficients. Such a combination
cannot represent another facet: on the relative interior of that facet,
all the selected slacks are strictly positive.

Let $B,B'$ be the factors of the two endpoints of the facet of $A$.
Their compressions to $E$ are PSD and supported on $\ker(A|_E)$.
Three selected facet slacks are linearly independent: after placing the
origin inside the polygon and normalizing them to $1-c_i\cdot x$, the
$c_i$ are three distinct vertices of the polar polygon and are not
collinear. Neither compressed endpoint factor is zero, since three
distinct facets cannot meet at a vertex. If $\dim\ker(A|_E)\le1$, the
two compressions are positive multiples of each other. The three slacks,
and hence the homogeneous endpoint coordinates, would then be
proportional. The endpoints would coincide. Thus
\[
 h-a\ge2.
\]
Split the expression as $A=M_+-M_-$ according to the signs of $t_i$.
Since $M_+=A+M_-$, its range contains that of $M_-$ and therefore equals
$E$. The number of positive coefficients is at least $h\ge a+2$.
Also $h=\operatorname{rank} M_+\le a+\operatorname{rank} M_-$, so the number of negative
coefficients is at least $h-a\ge2$.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:corner-loci}
For $L\subseteq\mathbb S^4$ one has
\[
 c(L)\le8\quad(\dim L\le7),\qquad
 c(L)\le12\quad(\dim L\le8).
\]
No finiteness assumption on $X_L$ is needed.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Put $D=L^\perp$, the space of quadrics defining $X_L$, so $\dim D\ge2$.
The argument has one degree budget: apart from a persistent integral
quartic, each component contributes at most twice its degree, and the
total degree is four.

First suppose all quadrics in $D$ share a factor. Their common factor
is a real linear form $\ell$: a quadratic common factor would force
$\dim D=1$, and a nonreal linear factor would bring its conjugate.
Write $D=\ell E$, where $E$ contains at least two independent linear
forms. Then $X_L$ is a real plane together with a possibly empty real
linear space of dimension at most one. Lemma~\ref{lem:corner-linear}
gives at most $3+2=5$ corner images.

Otherwise choose real $q_1,q_2\in D$ with no common factor. Their
intersection $Y\subset\mathbb P^3_{\mathbb C}$ is a pure one-dimensional
complete intersection of degree four. Its reduced irreducible components
have total degree at most four. Call a component persistent if it is
contained in $X_L$.

For the nonpersistent components, choose $q\in D$ that vanishes
identically on none of them. On each such component of degree $\delta$,
B\'ezout bounds the points of $X_L$ by $2\delta$.
A real geometrically integral persistent component of degree at most
three is a line, a smooth conic, or a twisted cubic. A planar cubic
cannot occur, since both $q_1,q_2$ would contain its plane and share a
factor. The preceding lemmas bound the corner contributions of these
three curves by $2,3,6$, respectively, each at most twice its degree.
Nonreal persistent components occur in conjugate pairs. Only pairs of
lines or conics fit within degree four; their real points lie in the
intersection of the pair. Two distinct lines have at most one common
point, and two distinct conics have at most four. These contributions
also fit the same degree budget. Summing gives $c(L)\le8$ unless a
persistent integral quartic occurs.

Such a quartic exhausts the degree of $Y$, with multiplicity one. A
complete intersection has no embedded components, so $Y$ is reduced
and equals that quartic. Its saturated ideal is $(q_1,q_2)$, with
degree-two part $\operatorname{span}(q_1,q_2)$. Since every member of
$D$ vanishes on the quartic, necessarily $\dim D=2$ and $X_L=Y$.
Thus this exception occurs only when $\dim L=8$.

The quartic has arithmetic genus one by the complete-intersection
Hilbert polynomial. If its normalization has genus one, it is smooth,
and Lemma~\ref{lem:corner-curve} gives twelve corner images. Otherwise
the normalization has genus zero and the total delta invariant is one.
There is at most one singular point, hence at most one real point
without a real preimage on the normalization. The same lemma, together
with that possible extra point, gives at most $9+1=10$. This proves
both bounds, including the singular and nonreduced cases.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:elliptic-corners}\label{lem:quartic-refinement}
Suppose $X_W$ is a reduced geometrically integral complete intersection of two quadrics. If $T$ consists of three rank-one facets, then
\[
 \nu_1\le e(T)+4.
\]
If $X_W$ is smooth and $T$ consists of two rank-one facets, then $\nu_1\le e(T)+8$.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
For three facets write their factors as $a_i a_i^{\mathsf T}$, $1\le i\le3$. The vectors $a_i$ are independent: otherwise their three distinct projectors would span the symmetric matrices on a two-dimensional space, contrary to Lemma~\ref{lem:corner-linear}. The three facet slacks are also independent, since three distinct vertices of the polar polygon are not collinear. In these coordinates the corner map is the linear projection
\[
 \rho:X_W\longrightarrow\mathbb P^2,\qquad [z]\longmapsto[a_1^{\mathsf T}z:a_2^{\mathsf T}z:a_3^{\mathsf T}z],
\]
followed by coordinatewise squaring. Its center is a real projective point outside $X_W$: otherwise a real rank-one matrix would map to a point on three distinct polygon facets.

On the normalization, $\rho$ defines a basepoint-free series of degree four. Its image is not a line, because $X_W$ is nondegenerate. Thus it is birational onto a plane quartic or a double cover of a conic. Write $g\in\{0,1\}$ for the normalization genus. There are at most $1-g$ real points of $X_W$ without real preimages on the normalization, since its arithmetic genus is one.

In the birational case the plane quartic has total delta invariant $3-g$. Points with zero differential map to singularities, so there are at most $3-g$ such images. Adding the at most $1-g$ exceptional real points gives at most $4-2g\le4$ possible corner images away from $T$. In the double-cover case, Riemann--Hurwitz gives $2g+2$ ramification points, and adding the exceptional real points gives at most $g+3\le4$. Coordinatewise squaring is unramified off the three coordinate lines, while the derivative of the corner map vanishes at every real normalization point mapping to a corner, by Lemma~\ref{lem:corner-curve}. This proves the first assertion.

For two facets, with vectors $a,b$, consider the pencil $[a^{\mathsf T}z:b^{\mathsf T}z]$ on the smooth genus-one curve. Removing its base divisor gives a nonconstant map to $\mathbb P^1$ of degree at most four, hence with at most eight ramification points. At a corner off these facets, the derivative of the ratio of their slacks is zero. That ratio is $(a^{\mathsf T}z/b^{\mathsf T}z)^2$, whose square root is finite and nonzero there, so the pencil is ramified. This bounds the corners off $T$ by eight.

<!-- proof-22: body_md -->
Let $P$ be an $N$-gon with a size-four PSD factorization. We may assume
$N\ge6$ and, by polarity, $u\le w$. Equation~\eqref{eq:factor-spans}
then gives $u\le6$.
If $u\le4$, the compact strictly feasible slice $K_U$ has affine dimension
$u-1\le3$. For a three-dimensional slice, a generic planar shadow has
type $(4,2,1)$: matrix order four, image dimension two, and one eliminated
variable. In \cite[Theorem 1.1 and Table 1]{SinnSturmfels2015}, the entry
$p=1$, $n=4$ has only the rank-three branch, of degree
$\delta(2,4,3)=12$. Thus its algebraic boundary has degree twelve.
For a two-dimensional slice there are no eliminated variables, and
the determinant bound is four.
The degree bound persists under degeneration. To see this, write the
slice in parameter form as
\[
 K_0=\{t:B_0+\mathcal A_0(t)\succeq0\},
 \qquad B_0\succ0,
\]
with $\mathcal A_0$ injective. Take generic perturbations
$B_\epsilon\to B_0$, $\mathcal A_\epsilon\to\mathcal A_0$ and put
$K_\epsilon=\{t:B_\epsilon+\mathcal A_\epsilon(t)\succeq0\}$.
Compactness of $K_0$ gives a uniform $\eta>0$ such that
$\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal A_0(t))\le-\eta$ on the unit sphere.
For small $\epsilon$ this remains at most $-\eta/2$, so every
$t\in K_\epsilon$ satisfies $\|t\|\le2\|B_\epsilon\|/\eta$.
Thus the perturbed sets are uniformly bounded, and strict feasibility
persists.

For $t\in K_0$ and fixed $0<\delta<1$, one has
\[
 B_0+\mathcal A_0((1-\delta)t)
 =(1-\delta)(B_0+\mathcal A_0(t))+\delta B_0
 \succeq\delta\lambda_{\min}(B_0)I.
\]
The positive margin is uniform on $K_0$, so
$(1-\delta)K_0\subseteq K_\epsilon$ for sufficiently small $\epsilon$.
Conversely, every limit of feasible parameters from $K_\epsilon$ lies
in $K_0$, by continuity of the pencil and closedness of the PSD cone.
Uniform boundedness and then $\delta\downarrow0$ give Hausdorff
convergence. Choose generic projections converging to the original
one; their planar images converge as well.

Normalize the resulting boundary polynomials to coefficient norm one
and take a convergent subsequence. The limit is a nonzero polynomial
of degree at most twelve. Boundaries of full-dimensional compact convex
bodies converge under this Hausdorff limit, so the polynomial vanishes
on every polygon edge. Its restriction to each edge line vanishes on
a nonempty interval, hence identically. The distinct edge lines
therefore divide it, giving $N\le12$.

For the remaining dimensions, Corollary~\ref{cor:five-dim-facets}, Lemma~\ref{lem:corner-loci}, and the run bound \eqref{eq:factor-runs} give the following preliminary bounds. The case $(u,w)=(5,8)$ uses Corollary~\ref{cor:five-eight}.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:exclude-thirteen}
A thirteen-gon has no size-four PSD slack factorization with $(u,w)=(6,7)$.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
Suppose such a factorization exists, with $A_i$ indexed by the edge $[v_i,v_{i+1}]$ and $B_i$ by $v_i$, cyclically modulo thirteen. The rank-one counts are at most six and eight. Both sides contain rank-two factors, and equality is forced throughout
\[
 13\le f_1+\nu_1-\max(\rho_F,\rho_V)\le6+8-1=13.
\]
After cyclic relabelling, $A_0,\ldots,A_6$ have rank two, $A_7,\ldots,A_{12}$ have rank one, and $B_0,\ldots,B_7$ have rank one. Write
\[
 A_{6+i}=a_i a_i^{\mathsf T}\quad(1\le i\le6),\qquad
 B_j=q_jq_j^{\mathsf T}\quad(0\le j\le7).
\]
By Theorem~\ref{lem:corner-independent}, the six facet projectors form a basis of $U$, and any seven of the eight vertex projectors form a basis of $W$. The rank-three trace pairing and $u+w=13$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thirteen-complements}
 U^\perp\subset W.
\end{equation}
Lemma~\ref{lem:five-factors} shows that every rank-two facet has a full-support expansion in the six rank-one facet factors, with four positive and two negative coefficients.

The parameter line comes from the kernel of a rank-two facet. Fix such a factor $A=FDF^{\mathsf T}$, where $F=[a_1\ \cdots\ a_6]$ and $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_6)$ is invertible. The matrix $F$ has row rank four because $U$ contains a positive definite matrix. The six forms $a_i^{\mathsf T}|_{\ker A}$ are nonzero and pairwise nonproportional. A zero restriction would make its facet contain both endpoints of the different facet of $A$. Two proportional restrictions would make the corresponding slacks proportional at both endpoints, giving a linear dependence with the slack of $A$. Both conclusions contradict polygon incidence: three distinct facet slacks are independent.

Choose a basis matrix $K$ of $\ker A$ so that
\[
 d_i a_i^{\mathsf T}K=c_i(1,t_i),\qquad c_i\ne0,
\]
with the six real $t_i$ distinct. Since $F$ has row rank four and $AK=0$, the two columns of $DF^{\mathsf T}K$ form a basis of $\ker F$. Thus this kernel is spanned by $(c_i)_{i=1}^6$ and $(c_it_i)_{i=1}^6$. Put
\[
 v(t)=(1,t,t^2,t^3)^{\mathsf T},\qquad p(t)=\prod_{i=1}^6(t-t_i).
\]
Lagrange interpolation gives $\sum_i t_i^j/p'(t_i)=0$ for $0\le j\le4$. Hence, for $\mathsf V=[v(t_1)\ \cdots\ v(t_6)]$, the kernel of $\mathsf V$ is spanned by $(1/p'(t_i))_i$ and $(t_i/p'(t_i))_i$. Comparing the two kernels gives
\[
 F=T \mathsf V\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac1{c_i p'(t_i)}\right)
\]
for an invertible real $T$. Apply congruence by $T^{-1}$ to the facet factors and the inverse congruence to the vertex factors. Thus $U$ lies in the seven-dimensional Hankel space $\mathcal H$, spanned by $v(t)v(t)^{\mathsf T}$, with $v(\infty)=(0,0,0,1)^{\mathsf T}$.

In these coordinates, positive semidefiniteness gives a real moment representation. Write a Hankel matrix as $A_{ij}=m_{i+j}$ and associate to it the functional $L_A(t^k)=m_k$ on binary sextics, written in affine coordinates. For a cubic $h$ with coefficient vector $c$, one has $L_A(h^2)=c^{\mathsf T}Ac$. Every nonnegative real binary sextic is a sum of two squares of real binary cubics: factoring over $\mathbb C$ writes it as $g\overline g=(\Re g)^2+(\Im g)^2$. Thus $A\succeq0$ makes $L_A$ nonnegative on every nonnegative sextic. The evaluation matrices $v(t)v(t)^{\mathsf T}$, normalized to trace one and including $t=\infty$, form a compact set. Their convex hull is a compact base in the trace-one hyperplane, so the convex cone they generate is closed. Duality therefore expresses $A$ as a positive combination of these evaluation matrices.

Consequently each rank-two facet has a representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-moment-atoms}
 A_i=\alpha_i v(s_i)v(s_i)^{\mathsf T}+\beta_i v(r_i)v(r_i)^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 \alpha_i,\beta_i>0,\quad s_i\ne r_i,
 \quad 0\le i\le6.
\end{equation}
All atoms lie in the two-dimensional range of $A_i$, and any three distinct points of the cubic moment curve are independent, so precisely two distinct atoms occur. Neither is one of the $t_k$, since that rank-one facet would then contain both endpoints of the facet of $A_i$. The seven unordered pairs are distinct: equal pairs would give equal kernels, making two distinct facets contain the same two endpoints.

Define the real binary cubics $h_j(t)=q_j^{\mathsf T}v(t)$, $0\le j\le7$. Orthogonality at the endpoints of $A_i$ shows that $h_i$ and $h_{i+1}$ both vanish at $s_i,r_i$. For $1\le j\le6$, the two distinct pairs coming from $A_{j-1},A_j$ lie among the roots of $h_j$; they overlap in exactly one point and give three distinct real roots. The endpoint cubics $h_0,h_7$ have, respectively, the additional roots $t_6,t_1$ from their incident rank-one facets. Thus all eight cubics have three distinct real projective roots, and no two are proportional.

Writing $v(s,t)=(s^3,s^2t,st^2,t^3)^{\mathsf T}$ for the homogeneous version of $v(t)=v(1,t)$, consider multiplication
\[
 \mu:\mathbb S^4\longrightarrow\mathbb R[s,t]_6,\qquad B\longmapsto v(s,t)^{\mathsf T}Bv(s,t).
\]
Its kernel is $\mathcal H^\perp$, of dimension three. By \eqref{eq:thirteen-complements}, $\mathcal H^\perp\subset U^\perp\subset W$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four-sextics}
 \mathcal L=\mu(W)=\operatorname{span}\{h_0^2,\ldots,h_7^2\},\qquad \dim\mathcal L=4.
\end{equation}
This space has no base point over $\mathbb C$: the $q_j$ span $\mathbb R^4$, since their projectors span $W$ and $W$ contains a positive definite matrix. Any three distinct squares are independent. A dependence would express one square as a nonnegative combination of at most two others; evaluation at its three distinct real roots would make the other cubics proportional to it.

At most four cubics can vanish at a real projective point $a$. Indeed, cubics vanishing at $a$ form a three-dimensional space $E_a$. Multiplication from $\operatorname{Sym}^2E_a$ to sextics has a one-dimensional kernel, as is seen by removing the common squared linear factor and multiplying binary quadratics. Five vanishing cubics would give five independent projectors, hence at least four independent squares. But the sections of $\mathcal L$ vanishing at $a$ form a space of dimension three, a contradiction.

Let $a$ be the common root of $h_0,h_1,h_2$ coming from the node pairs of $A_0,A_1$, and choose an affine parameter in which $a$ is finite. Since $\mathcal L$ has dimension four and no base point, its subspace of elements vanishing at $a$ has dimension three. The independent squares $h_0^2,h_1^2,h_2^2$ form a basis of that subspace. Each has a double zero at $a$, so every element of $\mathcal L$ that vanishes there has a double zero.

At most four of the eight cubics vanish at $a$, so at least four among $h_3,\ldots,h_7$ do not. Two of these are consecutive, say $h_j,h_{j+1}$. Write $h_j=Q\ell$ and $h_{j+1}=Qm$, with $Q$ quadratic and $\ell,m$ nonproportional linear forms. All three are nonzero at $a$. Now
\[
 m(a)^2h_j^2-\ell(a)^2h_{j+1}^2
   =Q^2\bigl(m(a)\ell-\ell(a)m\bigr)
        \bigl(m(a)\ell+\ell(a)m\bigr)
\]
belongs to $\mathcal L$ and vanishes at $a$. On the right, the first linear factor is nonzero as a polynomial and vanishes at $a$, whereas the other factors do not vanish there. This gives a simple zero at $a$, contradicting the double zero forced by the preceding paragraph.

\label{thm:octagons}
Let $h>1$ and $\varrho=h/(h+1)$. The octagon
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oct-family}
P_h=\operatorname{conv}\{(\pm \varrho,0),(0,\pm \varrho),(\pm\tfrac12,\pm\tfrac12)\}
\end{equation}
has real PSD rank four. All four sign choices are included in the last pair.
The regular case $h=1+\sqrt2$ recovers the rank-four result of
\cite[Example 5.2]{VGG2018}.

<!-- proof-24: body_md -->
Consider
\[
Q_h=\operatorname{conv}\{u_+,u_-,v_+,v_-,w_+,w_-\},\quad
u_\pm=(\pm1,0,1),\quad v_\pm=(0,\pm1,-1),\quad w_\pm=(0,0,\pm h).
\]
For $s,t,\varepsilon\in\{\pm1\}$ the eight facet inequalities are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oct-facets}
s(1-\varepsilon/h)x+t(1+\varepsilon/h)y+(\varepsilon/h)z\le1.
\end{equation}
Each is tight at exactly $u_s,v_t,w_\varepsilon$ and strict at the other
three vertices. These eight distinct supporting triangles are all the
facets: a three-dimensional polytope with six vertices has at most eight
facets, by Euler's formula and the fact that every facet has at least three
edges. In particular, $Q_h$ is a combinatorial octahedron.

Set
\[
a_\varepsilon=\sqrt{2(1-\varepsilon/h)},\qquad
b_\varepsilon=\sqrt{2(1+\varepsilon/h)}.
\]
In the column order $u_+,u_-,v_+,v_-,w_+,w_-$, the nonnegative square root of
the slack matrix has row
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root-row}
R_{s,t,\varepsilon}=\left(
\frac{1-s}{2}a_\varepsilon,\frac{1+s}{2}a_\varepsilon,
\frac{1-t}{2}b_\varepsilon,\frac{1+t}{2}b_\varepsilon,
\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\sqrt2},\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\sqrt2}\right).
\end{equation}
Both $a_\varepsilon$ and $b_\varepsilon$ are linear combinations of $1$ and
$\varepsilon$, since $\varepsilon$ takes two values. The six columns
therefore lie in the span of the four functions
\[
1,\quad\varepsilon,\quad s a_\varepsilon,\quad t b_\varepsilon.
\]
Thus $\operatorname{rank} R\le4$, and $\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(Q_h)=4$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}.

The plane $z=0$ meets the edges $u_sv_t$, $u_sw_-$ and $v_tw_+$ in,
respectively,
\[
(s/2,t/2,0),\qquad(s\varrho,0,0),\qquad(0,t\varrho,0).
\]
These are all the edge intersections with that plane, so
$Q_h\cap\{z=0\}$ is $P_h$. Since $1/2<\varrho<1$, all eight displayed points are
vertices. Lemma~\ref{lem:faces} gives $\operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(P_h)\le4$; the classification
of polygons of PSD rank three gives the reverse inequality
\cite[Theorem 4.7]{GRT2013}.

For $h=1+\sqrt2$ one has $\varrho=1/\sqrt2$. Multiplying $P_h$ by $\sqrt2$ gives
the octagon with vertices $(\pm1,0)$, $(0,\pm1)$ and
$(\pm1/\sqrt2,\pm1/\sqrt2)$, which is regular.

\label{rem:explicit-lift}
Define $a=\sqrt{2(1-1/h)}$, $b=\sqrt{2(1+1/h)}$,

The six coefficient vectors in the factorization

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:rank-three-lines}
Let
\[
 X(x,y,\tau)=C+xD_x+yD_y+\tau_1K_1+\tau_2K_2+\tau_3K_3,
 \qquad D_x,D_y,K_j,C\in\mathbb S^4,
\]
be a strictly feasible pencil with compact spectrahedron
$Q\subseteq\mathbb R^5$ and planar shadow $P=\pi(Q)$, where
$\pi(x,y,\tau)=(x,y)$. Define
\[
 Z=\{[z]\in\mathbb P^3_{\mathbb C}:z^{\mathsf T}K_jz=0, j=1,2,3\},
 \qquad
 Z_{\mathrm{act}}=\{[z]\in Z:
 (z^{\mathsf T}D_xz,z^{\mathsf T}D_yz)\ne(0,0)\}.
\]
If $Z$ is finite, then $|Z|\le8$. The critical values of $\pi$ on the
smooth rank-three locus of $\{\det X=0\}\subseteq\mathbb C^5$ lie on
the at most eight lines
\begin{equation}\label{eq:branch-line}
 L_z:\quad z^{\mathsf T}Cz+xz^{\mathsf T}D_xz+yz^{\mathsf T}D_yz=0,
 \qquad [z]\in Z_{\mathrm{act}}.
\end{equation}
If, in addition, $P$ is a polygon and the relative interior of every edge contains
an open segment of points admitting rank-three PSD lifts, then $P$
has at most eight edges.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-25: body_md -->
Put $F=\det X$. At a rank-three point with kernel $\mathbb C z$,
$\operatorname{adj}(X)=\gamma zz^{\mathsf T}$ for some $\gamma\ne0$, so
\[
 dF=\gamma\bigl(z^{\mathsf T}D_xz,z^{\mathsf T}D_yz,
 z^{\mathsf T}K_1z,z^{\mathsf T}K_2z,z^{\mathsf T}K_3z\bigr).
\]
At a smooth point, the projection is critical precisely when this
tangent hyperplane contains the three vertical coordinate directions.
Thus $[z]\in Z$, and smoothness forces $[z]\in Z_{\mathrm{act}}$.
The identity $Xz=0$ then gives \eqref{eq:branch-line}, which is a genuine
line because its two variable coefficients do not both vanish.
Three quadrics in $\mathbb P^3$ with finite common zero locus have
at most eight distinct common zeros by B\'ezout's theorem.

For the polygon assertion, any rank-three PSD lift of a boundary point
is smooth in $\{F=0\}$: the direction toward a strictly feasible matrix
has nonzero determinant differential, since its quadratic form is
positive on a real kernel vector. Locally the smooth hypersurface is
PSD, as its other three eigenvalues remain positive. Its projection
cannot be a submersion at a boundary point of $P$, since that would
put an open planar neighborhood in $P$. Such a lift is therefore
critical. Every edge line contains infinitely many critical values
and hence coincides with one of the finitely many lines $L_z$.
Distinct edges have distinct supporting lines, proving the bound.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:branch-vs-loci}
The restriction to $Z_{\mathrm{act}}$ excludes identically zero equations
that cannot arise from smooth critical points. Strict feasibility alone
does not exclude them over $\mathbb C$: a nonzero complex vector can
satisfy $z^{\mathsf T}Hz=0$ even for $H\succ0$.
For generic data of type $(4,2,3)$, the degree-eight rank-three branch
splits into eight lines over $\mathbb C$. Irreducibility over the field
generated by the pencil coefficients is compatible with this splitting;
see \cite[Theorem 1.1 and Remark 2.2]{SinnSturmfels2015}.

Choose the polarity in Proposition~\ref{prop:p3-spans} so that $\dim W\le6$.
For the resulting span-reduced lift, the vertical matrix space is
\[
 \mathcal V=W\cap U^\perp,\qquad \dim \mathcal V=\dim W-3\le3.
\]
Its kernel locus satisfies
\[
 Z_{\mathcal V}=\{[z]\in\mathbb P^3_{\mathbb C}:zz^{\mathsf T}\perp \mathcal V_{\mathbb C}\}
 \supseteq X_U,
 \qquad X_U=\{[z]\in\mathbb P^3_{\mathbb C}:zz^{\mathsf T}\in U_{\mathbb C}\}.
\]
The inclusion follows from $\mathcal V\subseteq U^\perp$. Equality holds when

when $\dim W=6$. If $\dim W<6$, the locus $Z_{\mathcal V}$ is necessarily
positive-dimensional. Thus the full pencil and the span-reduced lift
must be distinguished when applying Lemma~\ref{lem:rank-three-lines}.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:octagon-branch}
For every $h>1$, the lift in \eqref{eq:explicit-lift} has type $(4,2,3)$.
Each point in the relative interior of an edge has a unique lift,
of rank three; each vertex has a unique lift, of rank two.
The reduced Zariski closure of the critical values of the projection
on the smooth rank-three determinantal locus is the union of the
eight edge lines, with defining polynomial
\[
 b_h(x,y)=\prod_{s,t,\varepsilon\in\{\pm1\}}
 \bigl(1-s(1-\varepsilon/h)x-t(1+\varepsilon/h)y\bigr).
\]
Its degree is eight. These eight rank-three branch lines persist
under sufficiently small generic perturbations and specialize to $b_h$.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-26: body_md -->
With $\alpha,\beta$ as in Remark~\ref{rem:explicit-lift}, the equations
of that lift are equivalent to $X\succeq0$ for the pencil
\[
 X(x,y,\tau)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 1-\tau_1-2\tau_2-2\tau_3&(\tau_2-\tau_3)/h&-\alpha x/4&-\alpha y/4\\
 (\tau_2-\tau_3)/h&\tau_1&-\beta x/4&\beta y/4\\
 -\alpha x/4&-\beta x/4&\tau_2&0\\
 -\alpha y/4&\beta y/4&0&\tau_3
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Indeed, expansion of $q_{s,t,\varepsilon}^{\mathsf T}Xq_{s,t,\varepsilon}$
gives the required slack. Conversely, comparing the seven independent
sign functions $1,\varepsilon,sa_\varepsilon,\varepsilon sa_\varepsilon,
tb_\varepsilon,\varepsilon tb_\varepsilon,st$ gives exactly these equations.
The five pencil directions are independent. The average of the six
matrices $c_jc_j^{\mathsf T}$ is a positive-definite feasible point
at $(x,y)=(0,0)$, and
$\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{diag}(1,1,2,2)X)=1$ proves compactness.
Thus the affine dimension is five and the projection eliminates three variables.

Write $K_i=\partial X/\partial\tau_i$. The kernel locus $Z$ of
Lemma~\ref{lem:rank-three-lines} is defined here by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:octagon-kernel-quadrics}
 z_2^2-z_1^2=0,\qquad
 z_3^2-2z_1^2+(2/h)z_1z_2=0,\qquad
 z_4^2-2z_1^2-(2/h)z_1z_2=0.
\end{equation}
There are exactly eight projective solutions, namely
$[q_{s,t,\varepsilon}]$. They are simple: $z_1\ne0$, and in the
chart $z_1=1$ the Jacobian determinant is $8z_2z_3z_4\ne0$.
For each solution, \eqref{eq:branch-line} is exactly the corresponding
edge line, so $Z_{\mathrm{act}}=Z$. Lemma~\ref{lem:rank-three-lines}
places all smooth rank-three critical values on these eight lines.

Conversely, the edge indexed by $(s,t,\varepsilon)$ is the section
of the triangular facet $\operatorname{conv}\{u_s,v_t,w_\varepsilon\}$ of $Q_h$.
An interior point of the section has strictly positive barycentric
weights on these three vertices. Its lift is therefore
\[
 X=\lambda_u c_{u_s}c_{u_s}^{\mathsf T}
   +\lambda_v c_{v_t}c_{v_t}^{\mathsf T}
   +\lambda_w c_{w_\varepsilon}c_{w_\varepsilon}^{\mathsf T},
 \qquad \lambda_u,\lambda_v,\lambda_w>0.
\]
The three vectors are independent: the minor on rows $1,3,4$ of
$[c_{u_s}\ c_{v_t}\ c_{w_\varepsilon}]$ equals $st/(4\sqrt2)$.
Thus $X$ has rank three and kernel $q_{s,t,\varepsilon}$.
The differential in $(x,y)$ is a nonzero multiple of the edge normal,
so this is a smooth critical point. At an endpoint exactly two
weights are positive, giving rank two.

Every PSD lift over this edge satisfies $Xq=0$, since $q^{\mathsf T}Xq=0$.
The vectors $K_1q,K_2q,K_3q$ are independent by the same Jacobian
calculation, so $Xq=0$ determines the three fiber coordinates uniquely.
This proves both uniqueness assertions. Each edge supplies an open
segment of critical values, so the reduced branch polynomial is exactly
$b_h$ and the eight-line bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:rank-three-lines} is attained.

Finally, the eight simple projective kernel solutions continue under
small perturbations of the three vertical matrices. Each continued
kernel gives a line $q^{\mathsf T}X(x,y,\tau)q=0$, independent of $\tau$;
the equations $Xq=0$ still determine $\tau$ uniquely along that line.
Near an interior edge point, positivity on $q^\perp$ persists.
The eight lines therefore remain rank-three boundary branches, and
their product specializes, up to a nonzero scalar, to $b_h$.

<!-- corollary-6: body_md -->
\label{cor:square-symmetry}
Every convex octagon invariant under $(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x)$ and
$(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$ has real PSD rank four.
<!-- end corollary-6 -->
<!-- proof-27: body_md -->
The center is the origin. A nonzero orbit under this group has four points
if it lies on the axes or the diagonals, and eight points otherwise.
There are two possibilities for an eight-vertex invariant polygon.

If the vertex set consists of two four-point orbits, one must lie on the
axes and the other on the diagonals: two distinct orbits on the same four
rays cannot both consist of extreme points. Write these vertices as
$(\pm A,0),(0,\pm A),(\pm B,\pm B)$ with $A,B>0$.
All eight are extreme precisely when $B<A<2B$. Scaling by $1/(2B)$ gives
\eqref{eq:oct-family}, with $\varrho=A/(2B)$ and $h=\varrho/(1-\varrho)>1$.

Otherwise the vertices form the orbit of $(a,b)$ with $a>b>0$.
The eight edge normals of this polygon lie alternately on the axes and
the diagonals. Its polar is therefore an octagon of the first type.
Explicitly, its polar has axis vertices $(\pm1/a,0),(0,\pm1/a)$ and diagonal
vertices $(\pm1/(a+b),\pm1/(a+b))$; here $1<(a+b)/a<2$.
Polarity preserves PSD rank, proving the claim.

<!-- corollary-7: body_md -->
\label{cor:equivariant-octagon}
The regular octagon satisfies
\[
 \operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(R_8)=4,\qquad \operatorname{xc}_{\mathrm{PSD}}^{C_8}(R_8)=5.
\]

<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
Theorem~\ref{thm:octagons} gives the unrestricted value. The equivariant
construction for regular $2^k$-gons in \cite{FSP2017polygons} has size
$2k-1$, giving the upper bound five. We prove the matching lower bound
using a small instance of the Fourier separation method from that paper.

Suppose a size-four rotation-equivariant lift exists. The equivariant
factorization theorem of \cite{FSP2015equivariant} gives covariant
vertex factors. Averaging an inner product makes the representation
orthogonal; write $R$ for its generator. Since rotations act freely
and transitively on facets, one facet factor can also be rotated to
give covariant facet factors. The ranks are therefore constant on each
side, and Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-budget} forces one side to have
rank one. If the vertex factors are $R^jbb^{\mathsf T}R^{-j}$, a fixed
facet slack is a sum of squares of coordinate functions of $R^jb$.
If the facet factors are $R^iaa^{\mathsf T}R^{-i}$, the same slack is
$\|B_0^{1/2}R^{-j}a\|^2$. In either case it has an SOS certificate in
a rotation-invariant real function space of dimension at most four.

Index the vertices by $j\in\mathbb Z_8$, put $\delta=\pi/8$, and use
the facet slack
\[
 \ell(j)=\cos\delta-\cos(\pi j/4+\delta).
\]
Write $\chi_k(j)=e^{\pi i k j/4}$ and
$H_k=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{\Re\chi_k,\Im\chi_k\}$.
The real function space decomposes into $H_0,H_1,H_2,H_3,H_4$;
the first and last have dimension one and the others dimension two.
A certifying invariant space of dimension at most three would give a
size-three lift by Proposition~\ref{prop:sos-rank}, which is impossible.
Thus its Fourier support is either
\[
 K=\{\pm a,\pm b\}\quad(1\le a<b\le3),
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad K=\{0,4,\pm a\}\quad(1\le a\le3).
\]
If all frequencies have the same parity, their differences are even,
so sums of squared moduli cannot have the nonzero frequency-one
coefficient of $\ell$. Multiplying functions by $\chi_4(j)=(-1)^j$
preserves their squares and shifts $K$ by four. The remaining supports
therefore reduce to $\{\pm1,\pm2\}$ and $\{0,4,\pm1\}$.

For $\gamma=1,2$, define a complex-linear functional by
\[
 L_\gamma(\chi_k)=
 \begin{cases}
 e^{-i\widetilde k\delta},&
 k\equiv\widetilde k\pmod8,\quad |\widetilde k|\le\gamma,\\
 0,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\]
For $K=\{\pm1,\pm2\}$ take $\gamma=1$ and the two clusters
$\{-2,-1\}$, $\{1,2\}$. For $K=\{0,4,\pm1\}$ take $\gamma=2$
and the clusters $\{-1,0,1\}$, $\{4\}$. In each case the matrix
$(L_\gamma(\chi_{k-l}))_{k,l\in K}$ is zero between clusters and has
rank-one Hermitian PSD blocks within them. Hence
$L_\gamma(|f|^2)\ge0$ for every function supported on $K$, whereas
$L_\gamma(\ell)=\cos\delta-1<0$. This contradiction excludes size four.
