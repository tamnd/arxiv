<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:full-dimension}
Let \(A,B\subset\mathbb R^2\) be nonempty Borel sets. Then for almost all
\(g\in\mathrm O(2)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-dimension}
 \dim_{\mathrm H}(gA-B)\ge\min\{\dim_{\mathrm H} A+\dim_{\mathrm H} B,2\}.
\end{equation}
Moreover, let \(\mu,\nu\) be compactly supported probability measures
with Frostman exponents \(\alpha,\beta\in(0,2]\), respectively. There
is a Borel set \(\mathcal G\subset\mathrm O(2)\) with \(m(\mathcal G)=1\) such that, for every
\(g\in\mathcal G\) and every Borel set \(S\subset\mathbb R^2\),
\[
 \dim_{\mathrm H} S<\min\{\alpha+\beta,2\}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 ((g_\#\mu)*\widetilde\nu)(S)=0.
\]
Here \(\widetilde\nu(E)=\nu(-E)\).
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:breaking-four-thirds}
Let \(t>s_0\), and let \(\mu,\nu\) be compactly supported
\(t\)-Frostman probability measures on \(\mathbb R^2\). Then for almost all
\(g\in\mathrm O(2)\),
\[
 (g_\#\mu)*\widetilde\nu\ll\mathcal L^2.
\]
In particular, if \(A,B\subset\mathbb R^2\) are Borel sets with
\(\min\{\dim_{\mathrm H} A,\dim_{\mathrm H} B\}>s_0\), then
\(\mathcal L^2(gA-B)>0\) for almost all \(g\in\mathrm O(2)\).

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{fd:lem:cover}
Let \(d>0\), let \(K\subset\mathbb R^2\) be bounded and let \(S\subset K\) with
\(\dim_{\mathrm H} S<d\). For every integer \(j_{0}\ge1\), there is a countable
family $\mathcal Q$ of dyadic squares meeting $K$ such that
\[
 S\subset\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal Q}Q,\qquad
 \operatorname{side}(Q)\le2^{-j_{0}},\qquad
 \sum_{Q\in\mathcal Q}\operatorname{side}(Q)^d\le1.
\]
At each scale \(2^{-j}\), the family therefore contains at most
$\lfloor2^{jd}\rfloor$ squares.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Since $\mathcal H^d(S)=0$, we may cover $S$ by balls of radii
$r_i\le2^{-j_0-1}$ with
\[
 \sum_i r_i^d\le(16\cdot2^d)^{-1}.
\]
Each ball is covered by at most $16$ dyadic squares whose side length is
between $r_i$ and $2r_i$. We discard squares that do not meet $S$ and
remove repetitions. The resulting family $\mathcal Q$ satisfies
\[
 \operatorname{side}(Q)\le2^{-j_0},\qquad
 \sum_{Q\in\mathcal Q}\operatorname{side}(Q)^d
 \le16\cdot2^d\sum_i r_i^d\le1.
\]
Thus,
\[
 |\{Q\in\mathcal Q:\operatorname{side}(Q)=2^{-j}\}|\,2^{-jd}
 \le\sum_{Q\in\mathcal Q}\operatorname{side}(Q)^d\le1.
\]
This completes the proof.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{fd:lem:cayley}
For $u\in\mathbb R$ and $\theta=2\arctan u$,
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:cayley}
 (\operatorname{Id}-u\mathsf J)(R_\theta a-b)=p+uv.
\end{equation}
On every bounded interval of $u$, the change of coordinates
\[
 (\theta,z)\longmapsto\bigl(u,(\operatorname{Id}-u\mathsf J)z\bigr)
\]
is smooth and invertible. Both $d\theta/du$ and its spatial Jacobian
are bounded above and below by positive constants.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The Cayley formula reads
\[
 R_{2\arctan u}
 =\frac{(1-u^2)\operatorname{Id}+2u\mathsf J}{1+u^2}.
\]
Since $\mathsf J^2=-\operatorname{Id}$, multiplication gives
$(\operatorname{Id}-u\mathsf J)R_{2\arctan u}=\operatorname{Id}+u\mathsf J$. Thus,
\[
 (\operatorname{Id}-u\mathsf J)(R_\theta a-b)
 =a-b+u\mathsf J(a+b)=p+uv.
\]
The remaining bounds follow from
\[
 \det(\operatorname{Id}-u\mathsf J)=1+u^2,
 \qquad \frac{d\theta}{du}=\frac{2}{1+u^2}.
\]
This completes the proof.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{fd:lem:localization}
Suppose \(W\) has dimensions \(r_1\times r_2\times h\), where
\(0<r_1\le r_2\le8\) and \(h\le8\). Then
\(\mathcal A(W)\cap\mathcal P\) has diameter at most \(Cr_2\). In
particular, its preimage under \(\mathscr L\)
is contained in a product of two planar balls of radius $Cr_2$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
If $r_2$ is bounded below, the diameter bound follows from the boundedness
of $\mathcal {P}$. We may therefore assume that $r_2$ is small and
$\mathcal A(W)\cap\mathcal P$ is nonempty. Let $(p,v)$ belong to this
intersection. The points of its segment with $u=-1/3$ and $u=1/3$ are
at distance at least $2/3$ from each other. Thus,
\[
 h^2+r_1^2+r_2^2\ge4/9.
\]
Since $r_2$ is small, $h$ is bounded below and is the longest side of $W$.
We call the line through the centre of $W$ parallel to that side its
longitudinal axis. The components of the difference of the two segment
points perpendicular to this axis have magnitude at most $Cr_2$. Hence, the axis makes an angle $O(r_2)$ with the segment.
Since $|v|$ is small, the $u$-component of the axis is bounded away from zero, so we can write the axis as $(u,p_0+uv_0)$.

Every point $(u,x)\in W$ is within $Cr_2$ of an axis point
$(u',p_0+u'v_0)$. Since $|v_0|$ is bounded,
\[
 |x-p_0-uv_0|
 \le |x-p_0-u'v_0|+|u'-u|\,|v_0|\le Cr_2.
\]
Applying this at $u=\pm1/3$ on each contained segment gives
\[
 |p-p_0+(v-v_0)/3|+|p-p_0-(v-v_0)/3|\le Cr_2.
\]
Adding and subtracting gives $|p-p_0|+|v-v_0|\le Cr_2$.

We shall evaluate the constraint from the narrowest slab of the prism
at two points of each segment. This gives two independent constraints
on each point of the pair. After fixing one point, these
constraints place the other in a ball of radius $Cr_1$.

By Lemma~\ref{fd:lem:localization}, there are planar balls $B_1,B_2$ of

 \mathscr L^{-1}(\mathcal A(W)\cap\mathcal P)\subset B_{1}\times B_{2}.

Let $(n_0,n)\in\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^2$ be a unit normal to the pair of faces

\[
 |n_0u+n\cdot x-c|\le r_1/2.
\]
Substituting $x=p+uv$ at $u=\pm1/3$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:slab-constraints}
 |n\cdot p-c|\le Cr_1,
 \qquad |n_0+n\cdot v|\le Cr_1.
\end{equation}
For sufficiently small $r_1$, the second inequality and the small bound
on $|v|$ imply \(|n|\ge c_0>0\). Otherwise \(|n_0|\) would be close

For a fixed $a$, inserting $p=a-b$ and $v=\mathsf J(a+b)$ into

 |n\cdot a-n\cdot b-c|\le Cr_1,\qquad

\]
The coefficient matrix is
\[
 M_n=\begin{pmatrix}-n^{\mathsf T}\\ n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf J\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its two rows are perpendicular and have norm $|n|$, so
$|\det M_n|=|n|^2\ge c_0^2$ and its inverse has uniformly bounded norm.

the \(a\)-coordinates, of radius $Cr_2$, gives
\[
 \eta(\mathcal A(W))\le Cr_1^\beta r_2^\alpha.
\]
Fixing $b$ instead and using the two independent constraints on $a$
gives $Cr_1^\alpha r_2^\beta$. When $r_1$ is bounded below, both estimates
follow from total mass one after changing the constant. This proves
\eqref{fd:eq:anisotropic}.

To prove \eqref{fd:eq:prism-bound}, we may assume that $\alpha\ge\beta$,
since \eqref{fd:eq:anisotropic} is symmetric in the exponents.
Suppose first that $\alpha+\beta\le2$, so $s=\alpha+\beta$.
Let $x_1=r_1/\rho$ and $x_2=r_2/\rho$. Then $1\le x_1\le x_2$ and
\[
 \rho^{-s}r_1^\alpha r_2^\beta=x_1^\alpha x_2^\beta.
\]
If $\alpha\le1$, then $\beta\le1$ and this is at most $x_1x_2$.
If $\alpha>1$, then
\[
 \frac{x_1^\alpha x_2^\beta}{x_1x_2}
 =x_1^{\alpha-1}x_2^{\beta-1}
 \le x_2^{\alpha+\beta-2}\le1.
\]
Thus, \eqref{fd:eq:anisotropic} gives \eqref{fd:eq:prism-bound}.

If $\alpha+\beta\ge2$, then $s=2$ and $\alpha\ge1$. For

\]
For $r_2\le8$, the same expression is bounded by a fixed constant.
This proves \eqref{fd:eq:prism-bound} also in this case.

product of two balls of radius $Cr$. Hence, its product mass is at most
$Cr^{\alpha+\beta}\le Cr^s$ for $r\le1$, proving
\eqref{fd:eq:parameter-ball}. Every positive measure $\eta_0\le\eta$
inherits the same upper bounds. This completes the proof.

We first sample the tubes independently according to their weights.
The prism estimate controls the number selected in each sufficiently
large prism. We then remove tubes with nearby parameters. This gives
the required bound for smaller prisms and makes the remaining tubes
essentially distinct.

Let $L_{\rho}=\log(1/\rho)$. We select the indices in $G$ independently with
probabilities
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:probability}
 \mathbb P(i\text{ is selected})
 =\frac{w_i}{C_2\rho^sL_{\rho}^4}.
\end{equation}
The ball bound gives $w_i\le C\delta^s$, so these are valid probabilities
for $C_2$ fixed sufficiently large and $\delta$ sufficiently small.
If $X$ is the number selected, then
\[
 M_0:=\mathbb EX=\frac{a_G}{C_2\rho^sL_{\rho}^4},\qquad
 \operatorname{Var}X\le M_0.
\]
Since $a_G\ge\delta^{s/2}$ and $\rho=C_{\rm fd}\delta$,
\[
 M_0\ge c_s\delta^{-s/2}(\log(1/\delta))^{-4}\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
Here $s>0$ is fixed, and the convergence is uniform in $G$ and
$\eta_0$ under the stated bounds. By Chebyshev's inequality,
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:sample-lower}
 \mathbb P(X<M_0/2)\le4/M_0=o(1).
\end{equation}

We first choose a collection of at most $\rho^{-N_0}$ test prisms, for a fixed
absolute $N_0$, with the following property. Every
$r_1\times r_2\times2$ prism $W$ containing a $\rho$-tube in the unit
ball has an associated test prism $W'$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:net}
 W\cap B(0,1)\subset W',\qquad
 r'_1\le4r_1,\quad r'_2\le4r_2,
\end{equation}
where the transverse widths are ordered so that
$\rho\le r'_1\le r'_2\le4$, and the length of $W'$ is at most six.
To construct this collection, we first truncate widths exceeding two when
covering $W\cap B(0,1)$, and we replace the longitudinal constraint by an
interval of length two containing the projection of the unit ball. The
resulting centres lie in a bounded set. We then round the transverse widths
upward to dyadic values and approximate the centres and orthonormal frames
by finite nets of accuracy $\rho^3$. Finally, we double the rounded widths
and enlarge the length to six. Because the approximation error is
$O(\rho^3)$ and the relevant widths are at least $2\rho$ whenever $W$
contains a $\rho$-tube, this construction gives \eqref{fd:eq:net}. The
centre and frame nets have polynomial cardinality in $\rho^{-1}$, while the
two dyadic width choices contribute only a logarithmic factor. Thus, after
increasing $N_0$, the total cardinality is at most $\rho^{-N_0}$.

For each test prism $W'$, denote by $X_{W'}$ the number of selected tubes
contained in it. The sum of the weights $w_i$ over all indices
$i\in G$ with $T_i\subset W'$ is at most
$C\rho^{s-2}r'_1r'_2$. To see this, notice that the segments depend Lipschitz
continuously on $(p,v)$ in the bounded parameter region. Hence, all
parameters in the cubes of those representatives have their segments in
an $O(\delta)$ enlargement of $W'$. For sufficiently small $\delta$, this enlargement has transverse
widths comparable to $r'_1,r'_2$, and all its dimensions are at most eight.
The prism estimate then applies. With
$k_{W'}=r'_1r'_2\rho^{-2}$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:prism-expectation}
 \mathbb EX_{W'}\le\frac{Ck_{W'}}{C_2L_{\rho}^4}.
\end{equation}
For a sum $X_{\rm B}$ of independent Bernoulli variables with mean
$M_{\rm B}>0$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:bernoulli}
 \mathbb P(X_{\rm B}\ge k)\le(eM_{\rm B}/k)^k
\end{equation}
for $k\ge M_{\rm B}$. Independence gives
\[
 \mathbb E e^{s_{\rm B}X_{\rm B}}
 \le\exp\bigl((e^{s_{\rm B}}-1)M_{\rm B}\bigr)
 \quad\text{for }s_{\rm B}\ge0.
\]
The exponential Markov inequality with $e^{s_{\rm B}}=k/M_{\rm B}$ gives
\[
 \mathbb P(X_{\rm B}\ge k)
 \le\exp\bigl(-s_{\rm B}k+(e^{s_{\rm B}}-1)M_{\rm B}\bigr)
 \le(eM_{\rm B}/k)^{k}.
\]
The claim is immediate when $M_{\rm B}=0$.

We choose $C_{5}>N_0+10$. If $k_{W'}\ge C_{5}L_{\rho}$, then
\eqref{fd:eq:bernoulli}, applied with $k=\lceil k_{W'}\rceil$, together
with \eqref{fd:eq:prism-expectation}, shows that the failure probability
is at most $e^{-k_{W'}}$ for sufficiently small $\rho$. A union bound
over the test prisms has total failure probability at most
\[
 \rho^{-N_{0}}e^{-C_{5}L_{\rho}}=\rho^{C_{5}-N_{0}}=o(1).
\]
Thus, with probability $1-o(1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:large-counts}
 X_{W'}\le2k_{W'}
\end{equation}
for $k_{W'}\ge C_{5}L_{\rho}$.

This leaves the prisms with $k_{W'}<C_{5}L_{\rho}$. We treat them by
separating nearby parameters. Let
\[
 r_0=C_3\rho L_{\rho},
\]
where $C_3$ is large relative to $C_{5}$ and the constant in
Lemma~\ref{fd:lem:localization}. Let $N_{\rm pair}$ be the number of
unordered pairs of distinct selected indices with $|q_i-q_j|\le r_0$.
If $|q_i-q_j|\le r_0$, then $Q_j\cap\mathcal P\subset B(q_i,r_0+C\delta)$.
Thus, the parameter ball estimate gives
\begin{align*}
 \mathbb E N_{\rm pair}
 &\le\frac{C}{C_2^2\rho^{2s}L_{\rho}^8}
       \sum_{i\in G}w_i
       \sum_{j:\,|q_i-q_j|\le r_0}w_j\\
 &\le\frac{C a_G r_0^s}{C_2^2\rho^{2s}L_{\rho}^8}
 \le C_s M_0L_{\rho}^{s-4}=o(M_0).
\end{align*}
Since $s\le2$, the last logarithmic power tends to zero.
By Markov's inequality, \(N_{\rm pair}\le M_0/8\) with probability \(1-o(1)\).
The preceding probability estimates allow us to choose an outcome for
which \(N_{\rm pair}\le M_0/8\), \(X\ge M_0/2\), and
\eqref{fd:eq:large-counts} all hold. From this outcome, we remove every selected index belonging to a pair at distance at most $r_0$. At most $2N_{\rm pair}\le M_0/4$ indices are removed, so at least $M_0/4$ remain. Their parameters are $r_0$-separated. If a test prism has $k_{W'}<C_{5}L_{\rho}$, then $r'_1\ge\rho$ implies $r'_2<C_{5}\rho L_{\rho}$.
Lemma~\ref{fd:lem:localization} shows that the parameters of its contained segments have diameter at most $CC_{5}\rho L_{\rho}<r_0$. Such a prism contains
at most one remaining tube.

For an arbitrary $r_1\times r_2\times2$ prism, we compare it with its
associated test prism. In the large case, the number of remaining tubes is
at most
\[
 2r'_1r'_2\rho^{-2}\le32r_1r_2\rho^{-2}
 \le100r_1r_2\rho^{-2}.
\]
In the small case it is at most one, and the same final bound holds:
a prism containing a $\rho$-tube has both transverse widths at least
$2\rho$.

It remains to prove essential distinctness. For parameters in \(\mathcal P\),
the intersection of any tube with
a time slice has area at most $C\rho^2$. If two tubes intersected in
more than half their common volume, the set of times at which their
slices meet would have length bounded below by an absolute constant.
At each such time,
\[
 |p_i-p_j+u(v_i-v_j)|\le C\rho.
\]
Here the same estimate also holds near the rounded tube ends, since a
point in a tube is within $\rho$ of an axis point, and the slopes are
bounded. We may choose two such times $u_1,u_2$ with $|u_{1}-u_{2}|\ge c>0$. Then
\[
 |u_{1}-u_{2}|\,|v_{i}-v_{j}|\le C\rho,
 \qquad |p_i-p_j|\le C\rho+|u_{1}|\,|v_i-v_j|\le C\rho.
\]
This contradicts $r_0$-separation for small
$\rho$. Thus, the remaining tubes are essentially distinct.
Their containment in the unit ball follows from \eqref{fd:eq:normalization}
and \eqref{fd:eq:segment}. Their number is at least $M_0/4$, so
\eqref{fd:eq:selection-size} follows.
This completes the proof.

\label{fd:prop:testing}
Let $\mu,\nu$ be compactly supported Frostman probabilities with exponents
$\alpha,\beta\in(0,2]$, and suppose that \eqref{fd:eq:normalization} holds.
Set $s=\min\{\alpha+\beta,2\}$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there are

$\Omega\subset I_0\times\mathbb R^2$ and $0<\delta<\delta_\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:testing}
 \int_\Omega F_\delta(u,x)\,d u\,d x
 \le C_\varepsilon\left(
        \delta^{s/2}+\bigl(\delta^{-(2-s)-\varepsilon}|\Omega|\bigr)^{1/Q_{\rm test}}\right).
\end{equation}
The exponent $Q_{\rm test}$ depends only on $\varepsilon$. The constants
and $\delta_\varepsilon$ may also depend on the fixed Frostman exponents
and constants, but are independent of $\Omega$ and $\delta$.

For each $d<s$, we control simultaneously all unions of at most
$2^{jd}$ dyadic squares at scale $2^{-j}$. The testing estimate and
Borel--Cantelli give a null set outside which the mass of every such
union tends to zero at a summable rate. The covering lemma then gives
the conclusion for every Borel set of dimension less than $d$.

We first prove the claim in the line coordinates, assuming
\eqref{fd:eq:normalization}. All the probabilities $\lambda'_u$,
$u\in I_0$, are supported in a fixed closed bounded square $K_0\subset\mathbb R^2$.
We fix a rational number $d\in(0,s)$ and set $\delta_j=2^{-j}$. We denote
by $\mathcal D_j$ the finite family of dyadic squares of side $\delta_j$ meeting
$K_0$. We define
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:Hj}
 H_j(u)=\max\left\{
 \lambda'_u\left(\bigcup\mathcal Q\right):
 \mathcal Q\subset\mathcal D_j,\quad
 |\mathcal Q|\le\lfloor2^{jd}\rfloor\right\},
\end{equation}
and
\[
 E_j=\{u\in I_0:H_j(u)>j^{-2}\}.
\]
For each fixed Borel square $Q$, the map
\[
 u\longmapsto\lambda'_u(Q)
 =\int\mathbf 1_Q(p+uv)\,d\eta(p,v)
\]
is Borel. The same holds for a finite union of squares. Since the
maximum in \eqref{fd:eq:Hj} is over finitely many families, $H_j$ and $E_j$
are Borel.

We enumerate those finite families and, for each $u\in E_j$, denote by
$\mathcal Q_j(u)$ the first family attaining the maximum in
\eqref{fd:eq:Hj}. The parameter set on which any particular family is
chosen is Borel. We define $\Omega_j\subset I_0\times\mathbb R^2$ by its sections:
\[
 (\Omega_j)_u=
 \begin{cases}
 N_{\delta_j}\!\left(\displaystyle\bigcup\mathcal Q_j(u)\right),
       &u\in E_j,\\[2mm]
 \varnothing,&u\notin E_j.
 \end{cases}
\]
Here $N_r$ denotes the open $r$-neighbourhood in $\mathbb R^2$.
$\Omega_j$ is Borel because it is a finite union of products of Borel
parameter sets with fixed open subsets of $\mathbb R^2$. Each section is a union
of at most $2^{jd}$ enlarged squares of area at most $C\delta_j^2$. Therefore,
\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:Omega-size}
 |\Omega_j|\le C\delta_j^{2-d}.
\end{equation}

For \(u\in E_j\), the nonnegativity of \(\varphi_{\delta_j}\), its support
in \(B(0,\delta_j)\), and the identity

 \int_{(\Omega_j)_u}F_{\delta_j}(u,x)\,d x
 \ge \lambda'_u\left(\bigcup\mathcal Q_j(u)\right)=H_j(u).

\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:Omega-mass}
 \int_{\Omega_j}F_{\delta_j}\,d u\,d x
 \ge\int_{E_j}H_j(u)\,d u
 \ge j^{-2}\mathcal L^1(E_j).
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:exceptional-sum}
 \mathcal L^1(E_j)
 \le C_{s,d}j^2\left(2^{-js/2}+2^{-j(s-d)/(2Q_{\rm test})}\right).

\end{equation}

$\lfloor2^{jd}\rfloor$ squares at each scale. By \eqref{fd:eq:Hj} and

\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{fd:eq:pushforward-chart}
 \lambda'_u=(\operatorname{Id}-u\mathsf J)_\#\lambda_{R_\theta}.
\end{equation}
The map $\operatorname{Id}-u\mathsf J$ is an invertible linear map of the plane.

\end{equation}
The implicit constant may depend on \(\varepsilon,\alpha_{\rm te},\beta_{\rm te}\), the

\label{rem:WW-conventions}
The two-ends condition in \cite[Definition~1.2]{WW} is stated for
$w\times w^{\alpha_{\rm te}}$ tubes along a line. Our ball condition implies that
condition, since such a tube is covered by a fixed number of balls of
radius $w^{\alpha_{\rm te}}$. Conversely, the part of a ball of that radius within
a $w$-neighbourhood of a line is covered by a fixed number of those tubes.
Thus, the two formulations differ only in their constants.

We shall also use \eqref{eq:WW} for unions of $Cw$-balls, where $C\ge1$
is fixed and the other hypotheses are imposed on these shadings.
Here is the reduction. The Katz--Tao bound gives a fixed upper bound for
the number of line parameters within distance $2Cw$ of any one parameter.
A finite colouring therefore partitions the lines into $Cw$-separated
families. One family retains a fixed proportion of $\sum\limits_\ell|Y(\ell)|$.

A ball of radius $\widetilde w^{\alpha_{\rm te}}$ is covered by a fixed number of balls of radius $w^{\alpha_{\rm te}}$, so the two-ends condition persists. Moreover,

 \qquad \Gamma_{Y,2-t}^{(\widetilde w)}
 \lesssim_C\Gamma_{Y,2-t}^{(w)},

\eqref{eq:WW} proves the claimed version. A fixed dilation places the
ambient region in the unit ball used in \cite{WW}. It changes areas,
line-parameter distances and all radii by fixed factors; radii between
one and the original ambient diameter are handled by a fixed covering.
The same argument therefore proves the stated bounded-region version.

to restrict to \(|n|\le R^2\), with error \(O(R^{-J})\) in supremum norm
for every prescribed \(J\).
In each error estimate below, we first fix the required power $J$
and then choose the integration-by-parts order sufficiently large.
This order may depend on $J/\delta_{\rm freq}$, but not on $R$.
There are at most \(CR^{5/2}\) remaining packets before
accounting for the bounded number of transverse cutoffs.

We also discard axes missing $B(0,3/4)$. On the support of
$\vartheta$, such an axis has a fixed positive separation, so
\eqref{eq:packet-point}, Cauchy--Schwarz in the coefficients, and summation
of the transverse tails give $O(R^{-J})$ for every prescribed $J$.
All remaining axes therefore meet the fixed ambient ball; their unit
segments and required dilations can be placed in $B(0,3)$.
Hence, the geometric containment hypothesis in Theorem~\ref{thm:RD} holds.

We associate each remaining product

\(\ell_{\theta,n}\) of length one and half-width \(C_{\rm pkt}w\),
centred at the point of the axis nearest the origin. The fine and coarse axes differ
by \(O(w)\) in the fixed ambient ball.

 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 Q+B(0,cw)\subset C_*G_{j,k},

where \(c\) is the fixed enlargement needed for smoothing at scale
\(R^{-1}\). These choices are independent of the measure and the scale.
Every tube comes from a chosen index \((j,k)\), so the strip on the
right satisfies \eqref{eq:light-test}.

split their indices modulo a sufficiently large fixed multiple of

each residue class. A further bounded colouring separates the $O(1)$
coarse angular cells and strip cutoffs. All resulting families meet the directional
and overlap conditions in \eqref{eq:RD}.

Theorem~\ref{thm:RD}. Before inserting
the spatial cutoffs, each packet is supported on its fine circle cap in

along $e_j^\perp$. We truncate these frequencies at $R^{\delta_{\rm freq}/4}/w$.
Their component normal to the fine cap is
$O(w)R^{\delta_{\rm freq}/4}/w=O(R^{\delta_{\rm freq}/4})$, and their tangential component is
$O(R^{1/2-3\delta_{\rm freq}/4})$. We likewise truncate the Fourier transform of
$\vartheta$ at $R^{\delta_{\rm freq}/4}$. The discarded parts, and their needed
derivatives, are $O(R^{-J})|c_{\theta,n}|$ for every prescribed $J$, since
the original cutoffs are smooth.

If \(L_\zeta=R^{\delta_{\rm freq}/4}/w\), the curvature of the circle contributes the
additional normal error
\[

 =O(R^{-3\delta_{\rm freq}/4}+R^{-3\delta_{\rm freq}/2}),
\]
which is smaller than the allowed normal thickness.


For each tube \(T\), let \(P_T\) be a smooth Fourier multiplier equal to
one on the truncated support. Its support is a rectangle of tangential
length $O(R^{1/2})$ and normal thickness at most $r^{3\delta_{\rm freq}/4}/10$.
After rescaling the two coordinates, its kernel has uniformly bounded
$L^1$ norm. We define
\[
 F_T=P_T\bigl(\vartheta\zeta_{j,k}\psi_{\theta,n}\bigr).

The function inside the multiplier is smooth and compactly supported,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:packet-six-sum}
 \sum_T\|F_T\|_6^2\lesssim R^{-2/3}.
\end{equation}
After summing all $O(R^{5/2})$ projected packets, the projection and
truncation errors on \(\operatorname{supp}\omega\) are still $O(R^{-J})$ in supremum
norm, upon increasing the integration-by-parts orders. The projection kernel has
rapid decay outside transverse distance $O(\rho)$ and longitudinal distance
$O(R^{-3\delta_{\rm freq}/4})$. Together with \eqref{eq:packet-point}, this gives an
absolute $L^6$ tail $O(R^{-J})|c_{\theta,n}|$ outside $2.1T$. There is a

transverse dilation of \(T\), while \eqref{eq:packet-point} controls the
spatial tails of the original wave packet. In the longitudinal direction,
$\operatorname{supp}\vartheta\subset B(0,1/2)$ lies a fixed positive distance from

\begin{equation}\label{eq:packet-decomposition-error}
 \vartheta\sum_j \mathrm l_jE_rf_j=\sum_TF_T+\mathcal E_J,

provided the integration-by-parts orders are sufficiently large.

tail errors above to be \(O(R^{-400})|c_{\theta,n}|\).

 \lesssim R^{-300}\le r^{-100}

for sufficiently large $R$. This is the relative decay required in

\(\delta_{\rm freq}\). By \eqref{eq:packet-six-sum},
\(\|F_T\|_6\lesssim1\). We divide the remaining packets into

satisfies the Fourier support and decay hypotheses of

 \int_{Y_M}v_R\lesssim\frac{|\mathcal W|q}{M}

 \lesssim R^{-t/3+\varepsilon_{\rm aux}+C\delta_{\rm freq}}(\log R)^Cq^{2/3}+O(R^{-50}).

absorbs the fixed logarithmic factors. Since $q\ge w$ and $1<t<2$,
the error $O(R^{-50})$ is absorbed as well. This proves
\eqref{eq:light-operator}. The proof is unchanged for a subfamily of
coarse angular cells. This completes the proof.

We first verify termination and identify the final bin lists.
We then count the recorded intervals and check the two-ends condition.
There are finitely many entries $(n,j,k)$ in the lists. Each round
either ends the construction or removes an entry. Thus, the construction
terminates. The operation at each point is finite and
measurable as explained above, and every residual mass is an integral
of a measurable function.

The masses $a_{jk}$ decrease throughout. If a bin is absent from a list
at any stage, its mass at that stage is at most the corresponding
threshold. Its final mass is no larger. Conversely, the final test
removes every bin whose mass is at most its threshold. This proves the
stated equivalence.

For a fixed $x$, chart, and threshold $n$, the set $D_{n,x}$ only
shrinks during the entire construction. Each angular interval
recorded because of that threshold deletes more than a
$C_{\rm 2e}^{-1}w^{\beta_c}$ fraction of its current length.

The initial length is bounded, and any nonempty set of cells has
length at least a fixed multiple of $w$. Thus, the number of such

\[
 1+\frac{\log(C/w)}{-\log(1-C_{\rm 2e}^{-1}w^{\beta_c})}
 \lesssim w^{-\beta_c}L_w.
\]
There are $O(L_w)$ thresholds and a fixed number of charts,
giving the asserted bound on $K$.

At the final round every set defined by the lists passes the angular
test, and the lists do not change thereafter. Thus, all the final sets
satisfy the two-ends condition.

$O(w)$ enlargement of that interval. Within the chart, this enlargement

cell contributes only a bounded number of neighboring cells. Thus,

 \|G_R\|_1\le\|P_R\lambda'\|_1+\|B_R\|_1
 \lesssim m_\mu m_\nu,
 \qquad
 \|B_R\|_2^2\lesssim R^{2-t}m_\mu m_\nu.
\]

Set $\eta_{\rm int}=\alpha_c/64$. For $0<\eta_{\rm int}<1$,
H\"older's inequality gives
\[
 \|F\|_{1+\eta_{\rm int}}^{1+\eta_{\rm int}}
 \le\|F\|_1^{1-\eta_{\rm int}}\|F\|_2^{2\eta_{\rm int}}.
\]
Apply this separately to $G_R$ and $B_R$. From
\eqref{int:tracked-decay} and \eqref{int:trivial-bounds},
\begin{align*}
 \|G_R\|_{1+\eta_{\rm int}}^{1+\eta_{\rm int}}
 &\lesssim R^{-\Delta\eta_{\rm int}/2}m_\mu m_\nu,\\
 \|B_R\|_{1+\eta_{\rm int}}^{1+\eta_{\rm int}}
 &\lesssim
 R^{-(1-\eta_{\rm int})\alpha_c/16+(2-t)\eta_{\rm int}}
 m_\mu m_\nu
 \lesssim R^{-\alpha_c/64}m_\mu m_\nu.
\end{align*}
The last inequality uses $2-t<1$ and $\eta_{\rm int}<1/2$.
Both powers decay. For the finitely many scales $2\le R<R_0$,
\eqref{int:trivial-bounds} gives directly
\[
 \|P_R\lambda'\|_{1+\eta_{\rm int}}^{1+\eta_{\rm int}}
 \le\|P_R\lambda'\|_\infty^{\eta_{\rm int}}
       \|P_R\lambda'\|_1
 \lesssim R^{(2-t)\eta_{\rm int}}m_\mu m_\nu.
\]
The low-frequency convolution $P_0\lambda'$ satisfies the same bound
without the power of $R$, by its fixed Schwartz kernel.

Minkowski's inequality now shows that the resolution
\eqref{eq:dyadic-resolution} converges in
$L^{1+\eta_{\rm int}}(\,d m(g)\,d z)$, with the
$(1+\eta_{\rm int})$-th power of its norm bounded by
$Cm_\mu m_\nu$. Each summand is jointly measurable. Moreover, the
sum of their $L^{1+\eta_{\rm int}}(\mathbb R^2)$ norms is finite for almost
every $g$, by Fubini's theorem and the finiteness of Haar measure.
For these $g$, the resolution identifies the sum with $\lambda'_g$
as a distribution. It is therefore its density. Restoring the fixed
normalizations proves \eqref{int:mass-integrability-bound} and the
joint measurability assertion. This completes the proof.

Choose
\[
 s_0<t<\min\{\alpha,\beta,4/3\},
 \qquad q=1+\eta_{\rm int}>1,
\]
where $\eta_{\rm int}$ is supplied by
Proposition~\ref{int:mass-integrability} for the uniform Frostman constant
specified below. Write $\Pi=\mu\otimes\nu$. Enlarge the Frostman
constants to at least one, so that their bounds hold at all radii.
Since $\mu$ and $\nu$ are probability measures, these bounds also
imply $t$-Frostman bounds.
Thus Theorem~\ref{thm:breaking-four-thirds} gives
$\lambda_g\ll\mathcal L^2$ for almost all $g$.

Now define a measure on
$\mathrm O(2)\times\mathbb R^2$ by
\[
 \Lambda(E)=\int_{\mathrm O(2)}\int_{\mathbb R^4}
  \mathbf 1_E(g,T_gp)\,d\Pi(p)\,d m(g).
\]
The absolute continuity just established and Fubini's theorem give
$\Lambda\ll m\times\mathcal L^2$. For every dyadic product cube $D=Q\times Q'$,
define $\Lambda_D$ in the same way with $\Pi|_D$ in place of $\Pi$.
Since $\Lambda_D\le\Lambda$, choose nonnegative jointly Borel densities
$f(g,z)$ and $f^D(g,z)$ for these measures. We now explain why these jointly measurable densities give the
desired densities for almost every fixed rotation.
Fix a dyadic product cube $D$ and a dyadic square $Q\subset\mathbb R^2$.
By the definitions of $\Lambda$ and $\Lambda_D$, for every Borel
set $E\subset O(2)$,
\[
 \int_E\int_Q f(g,z)\,dz\,dm(g)
 =\int_E\lambda_g(Q)\,dm(g),
\]
and
\[
 \int_E\int_Q f^D(g,z)\,dz\,dm(g)
 =\int_E (T_g)_\#(\Pi|_D)(Q)\,dm(g).
\]
Since these identities hold for every Borel set $E$, the corresponding
integrals over $Q$ agree for almost every $g$.
There are only countably many dyadic squares $Q$, so we may choose
one full-measure set of rotations on which both identities hold
for every such $Q$.

Dyadic squares determine finite Borel measures on $\mathbb R^2$.
Consequently, for every rotation in this full-measure set,
$f(g,\cdot)$ and $f^D(g,\cdot)$ are densities of
$\lambda_g$ and $(T_g)_\#(\Pi|_D)$, respectively.
Finally, there are only countably many dyadic product cubes $D$.
Taking the intersection of the corresponding full-measure sets,
we obtain one full-measure set of rotations for which these
conclusions hold simultaneously for every $D$.
We write the resulting densities as $f_g$ and $f_g^D$.

Fix $k\ge1$, set $r=2^{-k}$, and let $\mathcal D_k$ denote the half-open dyadic
squares of side $r$ in $\mathbb R^2$. For $Q\in\mathcal D_k$ with lower-left corner
$x_Q$, set
\[
 \mu^Q(E)=r^{-\alpha}\mu\bigl(Q\cap(x_Q+rE)\bigr).
\]
Then $\operatorname{supp}\mu^Q\subset[0,1]^2$ and
\[
 \mu^Q(B(u,\rho))\le C_\mu\rho^\alpha
 \qquad(\rho>0).
\]
For $\rho\le1$ this is bounded by $C_\mu\rho^t$, and for $\rho>1$
the uniform bound on $\mu^Q(\mathbb R^2)$ gives the same conclusion with a
larger constant. Define $\nu^{Q'}$ similarly, using $\beta$ and the
lower-left corner $y_{Q'}$. After translating $[0,1]^2$ by
$(-1/2,-1/2)$, all these measures are supported in $B(0,1)$ and satisfy
a common $t$-Frostman bound. Its constant $C_0$ depends only on the
original Frostman constants and exponents, not on $k,Q,Q'$. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{int:rescaled-piece-masses}
 \sum_{Q\in\mathcal D_k}\mu^Q(\mathbb R^2)=r^{-\alpha},
 \qquad
 \sum_{Q'\in\mathcal D_k}\nu^{Q'}(\mathbb R^2)=r^{-\beta}.
\end{equation}

Let $F_g^{QQ'}$ be the density of
$(g_\#\mu^Q)*\widetilde{\nu^{Q'}}$. Translating either measure only
translates this density, so Proposition~\ref{int:mass-integrability}
applies without changing its $L^q$ norm. Since
\[
 f_g^{Q\times Q'}(z)
 =r^{\alpha+\beta-2}
 F_g^{QQ'}\bigl((z-gx_Q+y_{Q'})/r\bigr)
\]
almost everywhere, that proposition and
\eqref{int:rescaled-piece-masses} give
\begin{align}
 \sum_{Q,Q'\in\mathcal D_k}\int_{\mathrm O(2)}\int_{\mathbb R^2}
       \bigl(f_g^{Q\times Q'}(z)\bigr)^q\,d z\,d m(g)
 &\le C r^{(\alpha+\beta-2)q+2}
       \sum_{Q,Q'}\mu^Q(\mathbb R^2)\nu^{Q'}(\mathbb R^2)\notag\\
 &\le C\,2^{-k(\alpha+\beta-2)\eta_{\rm int}}.
 \label{int:dyadic-density-sum}
\end{align}

For each $g$ in the common full-measure set, let
$\{\sigma_{g,z}\}_z$ be the probability measures obtained by disintegrating
$\Pi$ along $T_g$. Thus, for every Borel set $E\subset\mathbb R^4$,
\[
 \Pi(E)=\int\sigma_{g,z}(E)\,d\lambda_g(z),
 \qquad \sigma_{g,z}(T_g^{-1}(z))=1
 \quad\text{for $\lambda_g$-almost all }z.
\]
Uniqueness of Radon--Nikodym derivatives implies
\[
 \sigma_{g,z}(Q\times Q')
 =\frac{f_g^{Q\times Q'}(z)}{f_g(z)}
\]
for $\lambda_g$-almost all $z$, simultaneously for all $k,Q,Q'$.
Define the measurable function
\[
 S_k(g,z)=\sum_{Q,Q'\in\mathcal D_k}
       \left(\frac{f_g^{Q\times Q'}(z)}{f_g(z)}\right)^q
\]
on $\{0<f_g<\infty\}$, and set it equal to zero elsewhere. It agrees
almost everywhere with
$\sum\limits_{Q,Q'}\sigma_{g,z}(Q\times Q')^q$.
For every $0<\gamma<\alpha+\beta-2$, Tonelli's theorem and
\eqref{int:dyadic-density-sum} yield
\[
 \int_{\mathrm O(2)}\int_{\mathbb R^2}
   \left(\sum_{k\ge1}2^{k\gamma\eta_{\rm int}}S_k(g,z)\right)
   f_g(z)^q\,d z\,d m(g)
 \le C\sum_{k\ge1}
       2^{-k(\alpha+\beta-2-\gamma)\eta_{\rm int}}<\infty.
\]
Consequently, for almost all $g$ and $\lambda_g$-almost all $z$,
\begin{equation}\label{int:fibre-summability}
 \sum_{k\ge1}2^{k\gamma\eta_{\rm int}}S_k(g,z)<\infty.
\end{equation}
Here we used that $f_g^q\,d z$ and $\lambda_g=f_g\,d z$ have the same
null sets on $\{0<f_g<\infty\}$, which has full $\lambda_g$-measure.
Take common full-measure sets for a fixed countable sequence
$0<\gamma_j\uparrow\alpha+\beta-2$.

Fix such $g,z$, and write $\tau=\sigma_{g,z}$. Let $D_k(p)$ denote the
half-open dyadic product cube of side $2^{-k}$ containing $p$. For one
of these exponents $\gamma$, let $\mathrm{Bad}_k$ be the union of the product
cubes $D=Q\times Q'$ at scale $2^{-k}$ for which
$\tau(D)>2^{-k\gamma}$. Since these half-open cubes are disjoint,
\[
 \tau(\mathrm{Bad}_k)
 =\sum_{D\subset\mathrm{Bad}_k}\tau(D)^q\tau(D)^{-\eta_{\rm int}}
 \le 2^{k\gamma\eta_{\rm int}}S_k(g,z).
\]
By \eqref{int:fibre-summability} and Borel--Cantelli, for
$\tau$-almost every $p$,
$\tau(D_k(p))\le2^{-k\gamma}$ for all sufficiently large $k$.
Intersecting these full-measure sets over $\gamma_j$ gives
\[
 \liminf_{k\to\infty}
 \frac{\log\tau(D_k(p))}{\log 2^{-k}}
 \ge\alpha+\beta-2
 \qquad\text{for $\tau$-almost every }p.
\]

For completeness, this dyadic estimate also gives the required
Hausdorff dimension statement without any regularity assumption on the
conditional measures. Let $F$ be Borel with $\tau(F)>0$, and fix one
of the positive exponents $\gamma_j$. There are a Borel set
$F_0\subset F$ with $\tau(F_0)>0$ and an integer $k_0$ such that
\[
 \tau(D_k(p))\le2^{-k\gamma_j}
 \qquad(p\in F_0,\ k\ge k_0).
\]
A set $U$ with diameter $d\in(2^{-k-1},2^{-k}]$ meets at most $16$
dyadic cubes of side $2^{-k}$ in $\mathbb R^4$. Each of those cubes that meets
$U\cap F_0$ has $\tau$-mass at most $2^{-k\gamma_j}$.
Thus every countable cover $\{U_i\}$ of $F_0$ by sets with
$0<d_i=\operatorname{diam}U_i<2^{-k_0}$ satisfies
\[
 \tau(F_0)\le16\sum_i(2d_i)^{\gamma_j}.
\]
Sets of diameter zero meeting $F_0$ have zero $\tau$-mass by the same bound,
so allowing them in a cover does not change the conclusion.
It follows that $\mathcal H^{\gamma_j}(F_0)>0$, and hence
$\dim_{\mathrm H} F\ge\alpha+\beta-2$ by letting $j\to\infty$.

Finally, fix Borel sets $A,B$ with $\mu(A)=\nu(B)=1$.
Since $\Pi(A\times B)=1$, disintegration gives
$\sigma_{g,z}(A\times B)=1$ for $\lambda_g$-almost all $z$.
The conditional measure is also carried by $T_g^{-1}(z)$, so the result
just proved applies to $(A\times B)\cap T_g^{-1}(z)$. The map
\[
 u\longmapsto(g^{-1}u,u-z)
\]
is a similarity of ratio $\sqrt2$ from $gA\cap(B+z)$ onto this fibre
intersection. Therefore
$\dim_{\mathrm H}(gA\cap(B+z))\ge\alpha+\beta-2$ for $\lambda_g$-almost all $z$.
The admissible translations contain a measurable set of full
$\lambda_g$-measure, which has positive Lebesgue measure because
$\lambda_g\ll\mathcal L^2$. This proves all the assertions of the theorem.

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{int:set-intersections}
Let $A,B\subset\mathbb R^2$ be Borel sets.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item Suppose that $\min\{\dim_{\mathrm H} A,\dim_{\mathrm H} B\}>s_0$. There is a Borel
set $\mathcal G\subset\mathrm O(2)$ with $m(\mathcal G)=1$ such that, for every
$g\in\mathcal G$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, there is a Lebesgue measurable
set $Z_{g,\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb R^2$ of positive measure for which
\[
 \dim_{\mathrm H}\bigl(gA\cap(B+z)\bigr)
 \ge\dim_{\mathrm H} A+\dim_{\mathrm H} B-2-\varepsilon
 \qquad\text{for every }z\in Z_{g,\varepsilon}.
\]
\item Suppose that $\alpha,\beta\in(s_0,2]$ and
$\mathcal H^\alpha(A)>0$, $\mathcal H^\beta(B)>0$. For almost all $g\in\mathrm O(2)$, there
is a Lebesgue measurable set $Z_g\subset\mathbb R^2$ of positive measure such that
\[
 \dim_{\mathrm H}\bigl(gA\cap(B+z)\bigr)\ge\alpha+\beta-2
 \qquad\text{for every }z\in Z_g.
\]
\end{enumerate}

For \textup{(i)}, write $a=\dim_{\mathrm H} A$ and $b=\dim_{\mathrm H} B$. Choose increasing
sequences $\alpha_j\uparrow a$ and $\beta_j\uparrow b$ with
$s_0<\alpha_j<a$ and $s_0<\beta_j<b$.
By Frostman's lemma, compact subsets $A_j\subset A$ and $B_j\subset B$
support probability measures with exponents $\alpha_j$ and $\beta_j$,
respectively. Apply Theorem~\ref{int:intersection-theorem} to each pair
of measures, and intersect the resulting countably many sets of
orthogonal transformations of full measure. Replacing this intersection
by a Borel subset of full measure gives $\mathcal G$.
For $g\in\mathcal G$ and $\varepsilon>0$, choose $j$ such that
$\alpha_j+\beta_j>a+b-\varepsilon$. The theorem gives a Lebesgue
measurable set of translations of positive measure on which
\[
 \dim_{\mathrm H}\bigl(gA\cap(B+z)\bigr)
 \ge\dim_{\mathrm H}\bigl(gA_j\cap(B_j+z)\bigr)
 \ge\alpha_j+\beta_j-2>a+b-2-\varepsilon.
\]
This proves \textup{(i)} with the same set $\mathcal G$ for every
$\varepsilon>0$.

For \textup{(ii)}, Frostman's lemma and compact approximation
\cite[Theorems~8.8 and~8.13]{Mattila} give compact subsets $A_0\subset A$ and
$B_0\subset B$ supporting probability measures with exponents
$\alpha$ and $\beta$. Apply Theorem~\ref{int:intersection-theorem} to
these measures and use
$gA_0\cap(B_0+z)\subset gA\cap(B+z)$. This proves \textup{(ii)} and
completes the proof.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{int:intersection-equality}
Under the hypotheses of Corollary~\ref{int:set-intersections}\textup{(ii)},
suppose in addition that $\dim_{\mathrm H}(A\times B)=\alpha+\beta$.
Then the conclusion holds with equality:
\[
 \dim_{\mathrm H}\bigl(gA\cap(B+z)\bigr)=\alpha+\beta-2
\]
for almost all $g$ and a set of translations of positive Lebesgue measure.
Indeed, for every fixed $g$, the standard upper bound for fibres of the
Lipschitz map $(a,b)\mapsto ga-b$ gives
\[
 \dim_{\mathrm H}\bigl(gA\cap(B+z)\bigr)
 \le\dim_{\mathrm H}(A\times B)-2
 \qquad\text{for Lebesgue almost all }z;
\]
see \cite[Theorem~7.7]{Mattila}. Removing this null set of translations from $Z_g$
proves the assertion. The product-dimension hypothesis holds, in
particular, when
$\dim_{\mathrm H} A=\dim_{\mathrm P} A=\alpha$ and $\dim_{\mathrm H} B=\dim_{\mathrm P} B=\beta$, by the standard
product inequalities.
