<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
There exist infinitely many lens spaces with integral surgery number~$s_{\mathbb Z}=3$. More precisely, for each positive integer $a \equiv 7 \pmod 8$, we have
$$s_{\mathbb Z}\big(L(3a^2+5a-5,3a+8)\big) =3.$$
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The definite filling obstruction extends immediately to higher surgery numbers~\cite{AMP}. However, the linking-form obstruction is essentially restricted to rank 2. Indeed, it follows from work of Edmonds~\cite{Edmonds} that every cyclic linking form has an indefinite integral presentation by a $(3\times 3)$-matrix. We refer to Section~\ref{sec:Edmonds} for details.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
We also note that the recent instanton bound $q_3$ for the surgery number from~\cite{DLME} does not detect the examples considered here. In fact, their invariant $q_3$ vanishes on every lens space $L(p,q)$ with $p$ odd.

Indeed, any lens space can be obtained from the unknot by positive or negative surgery coefficients, and since $p$ is odd, $L(p,q)$ is a $(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)$-homology sphere, so Corollary~2 of~\cite{DLME} applies and gives $q_3(L(p,q))=0$.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- question-1: body_md -->
Is the integral surgery number of lens spaces universally bounded? For instance, is $s_{\mathbb Z}(L(p,q)) \le 3$ for every $p,q$?
<!-- end question-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
Let $p$ be a positive odd integer and $\lambda = \langle \frac qp\rangle$ be a linking form on $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$.
The Jacobi symbol $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$ is independent of the choice of $q$.
The linking form is non-degenerate if and only if $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) \neq 0$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
First off, $\lambda$ is non-degenerate if and only if $\gcd(q,p) = 1$, which in turn is equivalent to $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) \neq 0$, which proves the last statement.

Now, let $q'$ be another integer such that $\langle \frac{q'}p\rangle \cong \langle \frac qp\rangle$.
This means that there exist two generators $x, x' \in \mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ such that $\lambda(x,x) = \frac qp$ and $\lambda(x',x') = \frac{q'}p$.
Since $x$ and $x'$ both generate, there exists $a \in \mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, coprime with $p$, such that $x' = ax$.
But then
\[
\frac{q'}{p} = \lambda(x',x') = \lambda(ax,ax) = a^2\lambda(x,x) = \frac{a^2q}{p},
\]
and since $\gcd(a,p) = 1$ we have
\[
\left(\frac{q'}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{a^2q}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{a^2}{p}\right) \left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right),
\]
which proves independence of the presentation.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Let $\lambda \cong \langle \frac qp \rangle$ be a $\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z$-valued bilinear form on $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, with $p$ odd.
The \emph{Jacobi symbol} of $\lambda$ is $J(\lambda) = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$.

Let $Y$ be a rational homology 3-sphere such that $H_1(Y) \cong \mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ for some odd $p$.
The \emph{Jacobi symbol} of $Y$ is $J(Y) = J(\lambda_Y)$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
Since $\lambda_{L(p,q)}\cong \langle \frac qp \rangle$, we have $J(L(p,q)) = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$.
<!-- end example-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:indefinite}
Let $p\equiv1\pmod8$, and $Q$ be a symmetric $(2\times2)$-matrix with integral coefficients such that $\det Q=-p$ and $\operatorname{coker}(Q)\cong\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$. Let $\lambda$ be the bilinear form on $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ presented by $Q$. Then $J(\lambda)=+1$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:primitive-diagonal}
Let
\[
 Q=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}
\]
be an integral symmetric matrix with $\operatorname{coker}(Q)\cong\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, where $p$ is odd.  After an integral congruence $Q\mapsto P^TQP$ with $P\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb Z)$, one may assume that $\gcd(a,p)=1$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
It suffices to find a vector $v \in \mathbb Z^2$ of the form $v = e_1+te_2$ such that $\gcd(v^t Qv, p) = 1$. If we find such a $v$, then $(v,e_2)$ is a basis of $\mathbb Z^2$, and the upper left entry of the intersection matrix in this basis is $v^t Qv= a + 2bt + ct^2$.

Let $r$ be a prime divisor of $p$. We first observe that $a,b,c$ cannot all be divisible by $r$, so that $a + 2bt + ct^2$ is not the zero polynomial modulo $r$.

Indeed, if $r$ divided $a$, $b$, and $c$, then $Q(\mathbb{Z}^2)\subset r\mathbb{Z}^2$. Consequently, reduction modulo $r$ induces a surjection
\begin{equation*}
\operatorname{coker}(Q) = \mathbb{Z}^2/Q(\mathbb{Z}^2) \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}^2/r\mathbb{Z}^2 \cong (\mathbb Z/r\mathbb Z)^2,
\end{equation*}
which is impossible, since $\operatorname{coker}(Q)\cong \mathbb{Z}_p$ is cyclic, but $(\mathbb Z/r\mathbb Z)^2$ is not.

Since $r$ is an odd prime and $a + 2bt + ct^2$ is a non-zero polynomial, there is an integer $t_r$ such that 
\begin{equation*}
a+2bt_r+ct_r^2\not\equiv 0\pmod r. 
\end{equation*}

Doing this for every prime divisor $r$ of $p$, the Chinese remainder theorem provides an integer $t$ satisfying $t\equiv t_r\pmod r$ for every prime $r\mid p$. Hence 
\begin{equation*}
a+2bt+ct^2\not\equiv 0\pmod r 
\end{equation*}
for every prime divisor $r$ of $p$, and therefore 
\begin{equation*}
\gcd(a+2bt+ct^2,p)=1.
\qedhere
\end{equation*}
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:primitive-diagonal}, after an integral change of basis, we may write
\[
 Q=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}
 \qquad\text{with}\qquad \gcd(a,p)=1.
\]

First we compute 
\begin{equation*}
    \lambda(e_2,e_2)=(0, 1) \cdot \big({-Q^{-1}}\big) \cdot \begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
1
\end{pmatrix}=\frac{a}{p}
\end{equation*}
and since $\gcd(a,p)=1$, this element has order $p$. Thus the basic vector $e_2$ represents a generator of $\operatorname{coker}(Q)\cong\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ and therefore
\begin{equation*}
    J(\lambda)=\left(\frac{a}{p}\right),
\end{equation*}
which we will now compute to be $+1$. The determinant condition of $Q$ gives us $-p=\det(Q)=ac-b^2$, so $b^2\equiv p\pmod a$.
Thus for every odd prime factor $r$ of $a$, we see that $p$ is a non-zero square modulo $r$, i.e.\ 
\begin{equation*}
    \left(\frac{p}{r}\right)=1.
\end{equation*}
Because $p\equiv1\pmod4$, quadratic reciprocity implies
\begin{equation*}
\left(\frac{r}{p}\right)=1.
\end{equation*}
In addition, the assumption $p\equiv1\pmod8$ implies that
\[
 \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)=1,\qquad \left(\frac{2}{p}\right)=1.
\]
In summary, we can factor $a=\pm1 \cdot 2^k \cdot r_1\cdots r_n$ into its sign, its powers of $2$, and its odd prime factors $r_i$ and get
\begin{equation*}
 \left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{\pm1}{p}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)^k \cdot \left(\frac{r_1}{p}\right)\cdots\left(\frac{r_n}{p}\right)=1,
\end{equation*}
which proves the statement.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:criterion}
Let $Y$ be a homology lens space with $H_1(Y) \cong \mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ for some $p\equiv1\pmod8$, and
\begin{equation*}
J(Y)=-1.
\end{equation*}
Then $Y$ has no indefinite surgery presentation along a $2$-component link.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Now suppose that $Y$ is obtained by an indefinite integral surgery on a $2$-component link, and let $Q$ be its linking matrix. Since $H_1(Y)\cong\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ and the trace of the surgery is indefinite, we have ${\det Q} = -p$.

By Proposition~\ref{prop:indefinite}, since $\lambda_Y$ is presented by $Q$, $J(Y) = +1$, but by assumption $J(Y)=-1$, which gives a contradiction.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
We note that the obstruction is slightly stronger than stated.
    In fact, it obstructs the existence of indefinite fillings of rank 2 with trivial first homology in the \emph{topological} category: the key point is that the intersection form of a filling with trivial first homology still presents the linking form of the 3-manifold.
    A different way of looking at it is the fact that such a $Y$ cannot be a surgery along a 2-component link in any integer homology 3-sphere.

    Also, the statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion} is independent of the chosen sign convention for the linking form, thanks to the assumption $p \equiv 1 \pmod 8$.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:criteria2}
Let $Y$ be a homology lens space that admits an indefinite surgery presentation along a $2$-component link. Then
\begin{itemize}
\item[(0)] $\lambda_Y$ admits a presentation by an indefinite binary quadratic form.
\end{itemize}
If $\lambda_Y$ is only presented by \emph{even} indefinite binary quadratic forms and $|H_1(Y)|$ is odd, then we have that
\begin{itemize}
\item[(1)] if $Y = \partial P$, for a spin $4$-manifold $P$, we get
    \begin{itemize}
        \item [(a)] $\sigma(P) \equiv 0 \pmod{16}$,
        \item [(b)] either $\sigma(P) \ge 0$ or $b_2^+(P) \ge 2$, and
        \item [(c)] $b_2^+(P), b_2^-(P) \ge \frac18|\sigma(P)|$;
    \end{itemize}
\item[(2)] if $Y$ is an L-space, $d_{\textup{spin}}(Y)=0$.
\end{itemize}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Point (0) is obvious, and has in fact already been used in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}.

Suppose now that $Y$ is presented as an indefinite surgery along a 2-component link, and let $S$ be the trace of the surgery.
Since $\lambda_Y$ can only be presented by even quadratic forms, the intersection form of $S$ is even and hence $S$ is spin. Since it is the trace of an indefinite 2-component link, the signature of $S$ vanishes.

For point (1), we consider the auxiliary closed smooth 4-manifold $X = P \cup {-S}$.
Since $Y$ has a unique spin structure, $X$ is spin.
By additivity of the signature,
\[
\sigma(X) = \sigma(P) + \sigma(-S) = \sigma(P).
\]
Since $Y$ is a rational homology sphere, moreover,
\[
b_2^{\pm}(X) = b_2^\pm(P) + b_2^\pm(S) = b_2^\pm(P) + 1.
\]
To prove point (1a), we apply Rokhlin's signature theorem to $X$ to obtain
\[
\sigma(P) = \sigma(X) \equiv 0 \pmod{16}.
\]
For point (1b), suppose that $\sigma(P)<0$ and that $b_2^+(P)\le 1$. This implies
\[
\sigma(X) = \sigma(P)<0 \, \textrm{ and }\, 1\le b_2^+(X)=b_2^+(P)+1\le2.
\]
Since $X$ is spin, we can apply Donaldson's Theorem B~\cite{Donaldson-diagonalisation} and get that the intersection form of $X$ is either $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ or $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \oplus \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. In particular, the signature $\sigma(X)$ of $X$ is vanishing, a contradiction.

To prove point (1c), we apply Furuta's 10/8-theorem~\cite{Furuta-10over8} to $X$ and get
\[
b_2^+(P) + 1 = b_2^+(X) \ge \frac18|\sigma(X)|+1 = \frac18|\sigma(P)|+1.
\]
Finally, to prove point (2), we use Lin's inequalities from Pin(2)-Floer homology~\cite[Theorem~5]{Lin}.
Since $Y$ is an L-space, $\alpha(Y) = \beta(Y) = 2d_{\text{spin}}(Y)$: this follows from~\cite[Proposition~20]{Lin}. Since $p$ is odd, there is a unique spin structure on $Y$, and therefore $\alpha(Y)$ and $\beta(Y)$ are well-defined.
Note that, in particular, for $S^3$ all three invariants vanish~\cite[Section~3.8]{Manolescu}.
Since $Y = \partial S$ is the boundary of a spin 4-manifold with $b_2^+ = b_2^- = 1$, there is a spin cobordism from $S^3$ to $Y$ with $b_2^+ = b_2^- = 1$, and applying Lin's inequalities one gets
\[
2d_{\text{spin}}(Y) = \alpha(Y) \ge \beta(S^3) = 0.
\]
Since $-Y = \partial(-S)$ also has the same properties, we get
\[
-2d_{\text{spin}}(Y) = -\alpha(Y) \ge \beta(S^3) = 0.
\]
Combining the two inequalities, the result follows.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:representability}
    Suppose that $p$ is odd.
    The form $\lambda$ is represented by both a definite and an indefinite binary quadratic form, unless $J(\lambda) = -1$ and $p \equiv 1 \pmod 8$.
    In this case, $\lambda$ is represented by a definite form, but not by an indefinite one.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $\lambda = \langle \frac qp\rangle$.
    Following the proof of~\cite[Theorem~3.2]{Edmonds}, the $(2\times 2)$-matrix
    \[
    Q =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        -cp & bp \\
        bp & -a
    \end{pmatrix},
    \]
    whose inverse is
    \[
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \frac a p & b \\
        b & c
    \end{pmatrix},
    \]
    presents the linking form $\langle \frac qp\rangle$ if
    \[
    a \equiv q \pmod p \quad \text{and} \quad |{\det Q}| = p.
    \]
    Moreover, $Q$ is definite if $\det Q = p$ and indefinite if $\det Q = -p$.

    Note that the statement is symmetric if we change sign to $q$: if $Q$ represents $\langle \frac qp \rangle$, then $-Q$ represents $\langle -\frac qp \rangle$, and $Q$ is definite (respectively, indefinite) if and only if $-Q$ is.

    To prove the statement, for each $\varepsilon = \pm 1$ we need to find $a$, $b$, and $c$ satisfying
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:edmonds}
    \left\{
    \begin{array}{l}
    a \equiv q \pmod p, \\
    ac-pb^2 = \varepsilon.
    \end{array}
    \right.
    \end{equation}

    When $a$ is fixed, the second equation in~\eqref{eq:edmonds} has a solution in $b$ and $c$ if and only if $-pb^2 \equiv \varepsilon \pmod a$, or equivalently if $-\varepsilon p$ is a square residue modulo $a$.

    Fix $\sigma \in \{-1,1\}$. Since $p$ is odd, by Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, there exists a prime $a$ satisfying the congruence in~\eqref{eq:edmonds} and such that $a \equiv \sigma \pmod 4$.

    Let also $\eta \in \{-1,1\}$ be defined by $p \equiv \eta \pmod 4$.
    Then $-\varepsilon p$ is a square modulo $a$ if and only if $\left(\frac{-\varepsilon p}{a}\right) = 1$. By multiplicativity of the Jacobi symbol and quadratic reciprocity, we get
    \[
    \left(\frac{-\varepsilon p}{a}\right) = \left(\frac{-\varepsilon}{a}\right) \left(\frac{p}{a}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}2\cdot \frac{a-1}2}\left(\frac{-\varepsilon}{a}\right) \left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = \eta^{\frac{\sigma-1}2} \left(\frac{-\varepsilon}{a}\right) \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)
    \]
    so that
    \[
    \left(\frac{-\varepsilon p}{a}\right) = 1
    \quad \text{if and only if}\quad 
    \eta^{\frac{\sigma-1}2}\left(\frac{-\varepsilon}{a}\right) = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right) .
    \]

    The values of $\eta^{\frac{\sigma-1}2}\left(\frac{-\varepsilon}{a}\right)$ are given in the following table:
    \[
    \begin{array}{c|c|c}
                &   \varepsilon = 1    & \varepsilon = -1\\
    \hline
    \eta = 1  &   -\sigma           & 1\\
    \hline
    \eta = -1 &   1                 & \sigma
    \end{array}
    \]
    This means that we have a solution of~\eqref{eq:edmonds} with $a$ prime and odd unless $(J(\lambda), \varepsilon, \eta) = (-1,-1,1)$ or $(J(\lambda),\varepsilon,\eta) = (-1,1,-1)$.

    In the latter case, it suffices to change the sign of $\lambda$, which flips the sign of $J(\lambda)$ since $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

    Let us look at the former case: $(J(\lambda), \varepsilon, \eta) = (-1,-1,1)$.
    By Proposition~\ref{prop:indefinite}, we know that if $p \equiv 1 \pmod 8$ then there are indeed no indefinite forms representing $\lambda$.
    This leaves out the case $p \equiv 5 \pmod 8$, for which we \emph{do} find solutions of~\eqref{eq:edmonds}.
    Instead of looking for solutions with $a$ prime, we now look for solutions with $a = 2r$, and $r$ a prime. (Primes $r$ such that $2r \equiv q \pmod p$ exist by Dirichlet's theorem, again.)
    The strategy is the same: a solution to~\eqref{eq:edmonds} is now equivalent to having a solution to the congruences
    \[
    \begin{cases}
        -pb^2 &\equiv -1 \pmod r,\\
        pb^2 &\equiv -1 \pmod 2.
    \end{cases}
    \]
    The second congruence is equivalent to asking that $b$ be odd.
    The first congruence is equivalent to $\left(\frac{p}{r}\right) = 1$.
    But now:
    \[
    \left(\frac{p}{r}\right) = \left(\frac{r}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{p}\right)\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) = -J(\lambda) = 1,
    \]
    since $p \equiv 5 \pmod 8$.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:even}
    When $p$ is even or $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, there is no even indefinite binary quadratic form representing $\lambda$.

    When $p \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, there is an even indefinite binary quadratic form representing $\lambda$ if and only if $J(\lambda) = 1$.

    When $p \equiv 5 \pmod 8$, there is an even indefinite binary quadratic form representing $\lambda$.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
If $Q = \begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}$ represents an even, indefinite quadratic form on $\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$.
    Then $a$ and $c$ are even, and $b$ cannot be even, for otherwise $\operatorname{coker} Q$ would not be cyclic. Then
    \[
    -p = \det Q = ac-b^2 \equiv -b^2 \equiv -1 \pmod 4.
    \]
    This proves the first statement.

    Now, let us suppose that either $p \equiv 5 \pmod 8$ or $J(\lambda) = 1$ and $p \equiv 1 \pmod 8$.
    We argue as in the last part of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:representability}: we look for a solution of~\eqref{eq:edmonds} with $a = 2r$ and $r$ prime.

We define $L_a := L(3a^2+5a-5,3a+8)$. There are three steps to the proof: first, we will prove that $s_{\mathbb Z}(L_a) \le 3$; then we will prove that $L_a$ has no indefinite surgery presentation along a $2$-component link, and finally that it has no definite surgery presentation along a $2$-component link.

First, let us write the Hirzebruch--Jung continued fraction expansions of $\frac{3a^2+5a-5}{3a+8}$ and $\frac{3a^2+5a-5}{3a^2+2a-13}$:
\begin{align*}
\frac{3a^2+5a-5}{3a+8} &= [a, 2^{[a+1]}, 3, 2]^-,\\
\frac{3a^2+5a-5}{3a^2+2a-13} &= [2^{[a-2]}, a+4, 3]^-.
\end{align*}

\vskip 0,2cm

We can re-write the first as
\[
\frac{3a^2+5a-5}{3a+8} = a-1 + \frac{3}{3a+8} = a-1 - \frac1{-a-3-\frac1{-3}} = [a-1,-a-3,-3]^-,
\]
which gives a surgery presentation along a 3-component link, coming from the plumbing corresponding to the continued fraction expansion.
This shows that the integral surgery number $s_{\mathbb Z}(L_a) \le 3$.

\vskip 0,2cm

To show that $L_a$ is not an indefinite surgery along a 2-component link, we use Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}.
Note that, when $a \equiv 7 \pmod 8$, we have $3a^2+5a-5 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion} is indeed satisfied, and $3a+8 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
Using quadratic reciprocity and the other properties of the Jacobi symbols, stated in Section~\ref{ss:jacobi}, we have
\begin{align*}
J(L_a) &= \left(\frac{3a+8}{3a^2+5a-5}\right) = \left(\frac{3a^2+5a-5}{3a+8}\right) = \left(\frac{3}{3a+8}\right)\\
&= \left(\frac{3a+8}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{-1}{3}\right) = -1.
\end{align*}

By Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}, $L_a$ is not an indefinite surgery along a 2-component link.

\vskip 0,2cm

Finally, we prove that $L_a$ does not bound a definite 4-manifold whose second homology has rank 2. If $L(p,q)$ bounds a positive-definite $4$-manifold $W$ with
$b_2(W)=2$, Proposition~\ref{p:donaldson} implies that
\[
Q_{P(p,q)} \hookrightarrow (\mathbb Z^{b_2(P(p,q))+2},-I).
\]
On the other hand, if $L(p,q)$ bounds a negative-definite $4$-manifold
$W$ with $b_2(W)=2$, then $-W$ is a positive-definite filling of $-L(p,q)\cong L(p,p-q)$,
and Proposition~3.1 implies that
\[
Q_{P(p,p-q)} \hookrightarrow
(\mathbb Z^{b_2(P(p,p-q))+2},-I).
\]
Consequently, in order to rule out definite surgery presentations on
two-component links, it suffices to prove that neither 
\begin{align*}
    Q_1 := Q_{P({3a^2+5a-5},{3a+8})} \textrm{ nor } Q_2 := Q_{P({3a^2+5a-5},{3a^2+2a-13})}
\end{align*}
embeds in codimension 2 in a negative definite diagonal lattice.

We start with the former.
Call $v_1, \dots, v_{a+4}$ the basis vectors of $Q_1$ coming from the plumbing, so that $v_1\cdot v_1 = -a, v_{a+3}\cdot v_{a+3} = -3$, $v_j \cdot v_j = -2$ otherwise, and $v_j\cdot v_{j+1} = 1$.
Suppose that $Q_1$ embeds in $\mathbb Z^{a+6}$, with orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_{a+6}$.
Since $a > 2$, up to automorphisms of $\mathbb Z^{a+6}$, the only possible embedding for the chain of $-2$s is $e_1-e_2, \dots, e_{a+1}-e_{a+2}$, and the only possible embedding for $v_{a+4}$ is $e_{a+3}-e_{a+4}$.
The vector $v_{a+3}$ is now forced to be $e_{a+2}-e_{a+3}-e_{a+5}$.

Finally, the vector $v_1$ cannot use any of the basis vectors $e_2,\dots,e_{a+2}$ (by looking at orthogonality to $v_3, \dots, v_{a+2}$ and $v_1\cdot v_1 = -a > -a-1$), and contains the vector $-e_1$. By orthogonality to $v_{a+3}$ and $v_{a+4}$, it contains a multiple of $e_{a+5}-e_{a+3}-e_{a+4}$.
Summing up, $v_1 = -e_1 + m(e_{a+5}-e_{a+3}-e_{a+4}) + ne_{a+6}$, which implies
\[
a = -v_1\cdot v_1 = 1 + 3m^2 + n^2,
\]
but $a \equiv 7 \pmod 8$ and squares modulo 8 are $0$, $1$, or $4$ and thus a direct inspection yields that the right-hand side modulo 8 is either $0$, $1$, $2$, $4$, $5$, or $6$, but never 7.

We now look at $Q_2$.
Call $w_1, \dots, w_a$ the basis vectors of $Q_2$ coming from the plumbing: $w_{a-1}\cdot w_{a-1} = -a-4$, $w_a\cdot w_a = -3$, $w_j \cdot w_j = -2$ for $j = 1, \dots, a-2$, and $w_j \cdot w_{j+1} = 1$.
Suppose that $Q_2$ embeds in $\mathbb Z^{a+2}$, with orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_{a+2}$.
Since $a > 5$, up to automorphisms of $\mathbb Z^{a+2}$, the only possible embedding for the chain of $-2$s is $e_1-e_2, \dots, e_{a-2}-e_{a-1}$, and the only possible embedding for $w_a$ is $e_a - e_{a+1} - e_{a+2}$.
Let us look at $w_{a-1}$: using the intersection information with the other basis vectors, we see that $w_{a-1}$ has to be of the form
\[
(m-1)(e_1 + \dots + e_{a-2}) + me_{a-1} + (x+y-1)e_a + xe_{a+1} + ye_{a+2},
\]
but the square of this class is
\[
(a-2)(m-1)^2 + m^2 + (x+y-1)^2 + x^2 + y^2 \equiv m-1 + m + x+y-1 + x+y \equiv 0 \pmod 2,
\]
while $w_{a-1}\cdot w_{a-1} = -a-4 \equiv 1 \pmod 2$.

We have therefore shown that $L_a$ admits no integral surgery presentation along a 2-component link. This also rules out a surgery along a single knot, since any integral surgery presentation on a knot can be stabilized by adding a split $(\pm1)$-framed unknot.

Indefinite binary quadratic forms of determinant $-112$ with cyclic cokernel are classified as explained in Section~\ref{ss:classification}.

\[

\]

To see this, we need to show that the congruences
\[
15 = \pm k^2 \pmod{112}, \qquad 15 = \pm 3 k^2 \pmod{112}
\]
have no solution.

We start with the second congruence: reducing modulo $8$, we have $15 \equiv \pm 3k^2 \pmod 8$, which is impossible since the right-hand side is $0$, $3$, $4$, or $5$ modulo $8$.

Let us look at the first congruence.
By the Chinese remainder theorem, we need to show that for $\varepsilon = -1,+1$ there is no solution to the system:
\[
\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
    15 \equiv \varepsilon k^2 \pmod 8\\
    15 \equiv \varepsilon k^2 \pmod 7.
\end{array}
\right.
\]
The first congruence has a solution if and only if $\varepsilon = -1$, while the second has a solution if and only if $\varepsilon = +1$.

Hence, by Theorem~\ref{thm:criteria2}(0), $L(112,15)$ does not have an indefinite surgery presentation along a 2-component link.

To show that $L(112,15)$ has no definite surgery presentation along a $2$-component link either, we again use Proposition~\ref{p:donaldson}.

For the first, we are looking at an embedding in $\mathbb Z^5$, with orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_5$.
Call $v$ and $w$ the two vectors of square $8$.
Since they both intersect the vectors of square $2$ once, they are primitive.
The only ways to write $8$ as a square of at most five integers is $8 = 4 + 4 = 4 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1$.
The first is not allowed, since the corresponding vector would be primitive.
In the second case, there are two possibilities: either $v$ and $w$ have the coefficient $2$ in front of two different $e_i$s, or they have it in front of the same.
The former case is impossible, since then
\[
0 = v\cdot w \equiv 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 2.
\]
In the second case, up to automorphisms of $\mathbb Z^5$ we can write:
\[
v = 2e_1 + e_2 + \dots + e_5, \quad w = \pm 2e_1 \mp (e_2 + \dots + e_5).
\]
(The signs for $w$ are opposite because $v \cdot w = 0$.)
But then the vector of square 2 has to be of the form $\pm e_1 \pm e_2$, which is easily seen to be incompatible with the constraints.

For the other embedding, we actually see that the corresponding lattice has only one embedding (up to automorphism) in $\mathbb Z^n$ for $n \ge 16$, and that embedding uses all sixteen basis vectors.
