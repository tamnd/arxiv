\label{thm:main}
    Let $\Sigma$ be a locally conformally flat free boundary minimal hypersurface in $B^n$ with $n \geq 4$. Then $\Sigma$ is congruent to either the equatorial ball or the critical catenoid.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:main}
    Let $\Sigma$ be a locally conformally flat free boundary minimal hypersurface in $B^n$ with $n \geq 4$. Suppose $\Sigma$ is homeomorphic to $B^{n - 1}$. Then $\Sigma$ is congruent to the equatorial ball.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:rotational}
    Let $\Sigma$ be a free boundary minimal submanifold in $B^n$. Suppose that at least one component of $\partial \Sigma$ is $G$-invariant under a connected subgroup $G \subset SO(n)$. Then $\Sigma$ is $G$-invariant.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $x: M \to B^n$ be an immersion with $x(M) = \Sigma$, and let $\Gamma$ be a component of $\partial \Sigma$ invariant under $G \subset SO(n)$. For each Killing vector field $X$ generating a one-parameter subgroup of $G$, we have that $X$ is tangent to $\Gamma$ along it and that $X^\perp$ is Jacobi by minimality of $\Sigma$. Now, let $\nu = x$ denote the unit conormal of $\partial \Sigma$. Since $X(x) = Q(x)$ for some $Q \in \mathfrak{so}(n)$,
    \[\bar{\nabla}_\nu X = Q(\nu) = Q(x) = X(x),\]
    where $\bar{\nabla}$ is the connection of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and so free boundary condition gives that along $\Gamma$
    \[\nabla^\perp_\nu X^\perp = \bigl(\bar{\nabla}_\nu X\bigr)^\perp - \bigl(\bar{\nabla}_\nu X^\top\bigr)^\perp = X^\perp - \alpha_\Sigma(X, \nu) = 0,\]
    where $\nabla^\perp$ is the normal connection of $\Sigma$ and $\alpha_\Sigma$ is the vectorial second fundamental form of $\Sigma$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Therefore $X^\perp$ satisfies
    \[\left\{
        \begin{aligned}
            L(X^\perp) &= 0\, \mbox{ in }\, \Sigma\\
            X^\perp &= 0\, \mbox{ in }\, \Gamma\\
            \nabla_\nu^\perp X^\perp &= 0\, \mbox{ in }\, \Gamma,
        \end{aligned}
    \right.\]
    where $L$ is the Jacobi operator on the normal bundle of $\Sigma$. It follows by unique continuation that $X^\perp$ vanishes identically, which means that $X$ is tangent to $\Sigma$, so that $G(\Sigma) = \Sigma$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
Let $\Sigma$ be a locally conformally flat free boundary minimal hypersurface in $B^4$. Then $\Sigma$ is congruent to either the equatorial ball or the critical catenoid.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $x: M \to B^4$ be an immersion such that $x(M) = \Sigma$, and let $\nu = x$ be the outward unit conormal of $\partial \Sigma$ in $\Sigma$. Choose a local unit normal vector field $N$ along it, and define the scalar second fundamental form of $\Sigma$ in $\mathbb{R}^4$ as
    \[A_\Sigma(X, Y) = \langle \bar{\nabla}_XY, N\rangle,\]
    where $\bar{\nabla}$ is the connection of $\mathbb{R}^4$. By free boundary condition, we know that $\nu$ is a principal direction of $\Sigma$ along $\partial \Sigma$. Fix $p \in \partial \Sigma$, choose an orthonormal basis $\{e_1, e_2\}$ of $T_p(\partial \Sigma)$ that diagonalizes $A_\Sigma|_{T_p(\partial \Sigma) \times T_p(\partial \Sigma)}$ and set
    \[\lambda_1 = A_\Sigma(e_1, e_1),\quad \lambda_2 = A_\Sigma(e_2, e_2),\quad \mu = A_\Sigma(\nu, \nu).\]
    Let $S$ denote the Schouten tensor of $\Sigma$. By minimality of $\Sigma$,
    \[S = -\, A_\Sigma^2 + \frac{|A_\Sigma|^2}{4}g,\]
    where $g$ is the induced metric of $\Sigma$, and then $S(\nu, e_1) = S(\nu, e_2) = 0$ by free boundary condition. Consider an extension of $e_1$ and $e_2$ in $\partial \Sigma$ such that they are geodesic at $p$ with respect to the induced metric on $\partial \Sigma$. On one hand, since $e_i$ is unit, we have $\nabla_{e_i}e_i = -\, \nu$, where $\nabla$ is the connection induced on $\Sigma$, and thus
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:schouten1}
        \begin{aligned}
            (\nabla_{e_i}S)(\nu, e_i) &= e_i\big(S(\nu, e_i)\big) - S(\nabla_{e_i}\nu, e_i) - S(\nu, \nabla_{e_i}e_i)\\
            &= -\, S(e_i, e_i) + S(\nu, \nu) = \lambda_i^2 - \mu^2.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    On the other hand, extending $\{e_1, e_2, \nu\}$ with $\nabla_\nu e_1 = \nabla_\nu e_2 = \nabla_\nu \nu = 0$ at $p$, one has
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:schouten2}
        (\nabla_\nu S)(e_i, e_i) = \nu\big(S(e_i, e_i)\big) - 2S(\nabla_\nu e_i, e_i) = \nu\Bigl(-\, \lambda_i^2 + \frac{|A_\Sigma|^2}{4}\Bigr).
    \end{equation}
    Moreover, again at $p$, after extending $\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$ and $\mu$ using the above extension of $\{e_1, e_2, \nu\}$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:sff1}
        \frac{1}{2}\nu(|A_\Sigma|^2) = \lambda_1\, \nu(\lambda_1) + \lambda_2\, \nu(\lambda_2) + \mu\, \nu(\mu).
    \end{equation}
    Since $A_\Sigma$ is Codazzi,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:sff2}
        \begin{aligned}
            \nu(\lambda_i) &= (\nabla_\nu A_\Sigma)(e_i, e_i) = (\nabla_{e_i} A_\Sigma)(e_i, \nu) \\
            &= e_i\big(A_\Sigma(e_i, \nu)\big) - A_\Sigma(\nabla_{e_i} e_i, \nu) - A_\Sigma(e_i, \nabla_{e_i} \nu) \\
            &= \mu - \lambda_i
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    and then by minimality of $\Sigma$,
    \[(\nabla_\nu A_\Sigma)(\nu, \nu) = -\, (\nabla_\nu A_\Sigma)(e_1, e_1) - (\nabla_\nu A_\Sigma)(e_2, e_2) = -\, 3\mu.\]
    As a consequence, equation \eqref{eq:sff1} becomes
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:sff3}
        \nu(|A_\Sigma|^2) = -\, 2(|A_\Sigma|^2 + 3\mu^2).
    \end{equation}
    Plugging equations \eqref{eq:sff2} and \eqref{eq:sff3} into \eqref{eq:schouten2}, we get
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:schouten3}
        (\nabla_\nu S)(e_i, e_i) = 2\lambda_i(\lambda_i - \mu) - \frac{1}{2}(|A_\Sigma|^2 + 3\mu^2).
    \end{equation}
    Since $S$ is Codazzi, equations \eqref{eq:schouten1} and \eqref{eq:schouten3} must coincide. In particular,
    \[(\lambda_i - \mu)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(|A_\Sigma|^2 + 3\mu^2),\]
    which shows that $(\lambda_1 - \mu)^2 = (\lambda_2 - \mu)^2$. Thus, by minimality of $\Sigma$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:zero1}
        \mu(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2) = 0.
    \end{equation}
    Note that, by free boundary condition, the second fundamental form of $\partial \Sigma$ as a surface in $S^3$ is just $A_{\partial \Sigma} = A_\Sigma|_{T(\partial \Sigma) \times T(\partial \Sigma)}$, which means that $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are principal curvatures of $\partial \Sigma$ in $S^3$ as well, so that the mean curvature of $\partial \Sigma$ in $S^3$ is $H_{\partial \Sigma} = -\, \mu$. Hence \eqref{eq:zero1} implies that $\partial \Sigma$ is the union of pieces of minimal and umbilical surfaces of $S^3$.\smallskip

    Let $\Gamma$ be a connected component of $\partial \Sigma$. Assume that $H_{\partial \Sigma}$ vanishes identically along it. Since locally $x^\perp = \langle x, N \rangle N$, minimality of $\Sigma$ implies that $x^\perp$ is Jacobi. Moreover, on $\Gamma$ free boundary condition shows
    \[\nabla^\perp_\nu x^\perp = \nu \langle x, N\rangle N + \langle x, N\rangle \nabla^\perp_\nu N = \bigl(\langle \bar{\nabla}_\nu\, x, N\rangle + \langle x, \bar{\nabla}_\nu N\rangle\bigr)N.\]
    However,
    \[\langle \bar{\nabla}_\nu\, x, N\rangle = \langle \nu, N \rangle = 0\]
    and on $\Gamma$
    \[\langle x, \bar{\nabla}_\nu N\rangle = -\, A_\Sigma(\nu, \nu) = -\, \mu = H_{\partial \Sigma} = 0.\]
    Therefore,
    \[\left\{
        \begin{aligned}
            L(x^\perp) &= 0\, \mbox{ in }\, \Sigma\\
            x^\perp &= 0\, \mbox{ in }\,\partial\Sigma\\
            \nabla^\perp_\nu x^\perp &= 0\, \mbox{ in }\, \Gamma,
        \end{aligned}
    \right.\]
    where $L$ is the Jacobi operator on the normal bundle of $\Sigma$. By unique continuation $x^\perp$ vanishes identically, and then $\Sigma$ is a flat ball.\smallskip

    If $H_{\partial \Sigma}(p) \neq 0$ for some $p \in \Gamma$, there exists a connected open subset $U \subset \Gamma$ containing $p$ such that $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda$ on it, so that it is totally umbilical in $S^3$. By the Codazzi equations, $\lambda$ is constant on $U$, and by continuity it is also constant in $\bar{U}$. It follows that if $H_{\partial \Sigma}$ is zero at some point of $\partial U$, it would vanish on the whole of $U$, contradicting its choice. Consequently, by taking $U$ maximal, one gets $\partial U = \emptyset$, and thus $U = \Gamma$ is totally umbilical in $S^3$. This means that $\Gamma$ is a round two-sphere, and therefore $\Sigma$ is rotational by Lemma \ref{lem:rotational}. Since $\Sigma$ cannot be an equatorial ball in that case, the uniqueness of the critical catenoid among non-planar rotational free boundary minimal hypersurfaces of the ball concludes our assertion.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Our computations above can be applied also to the case $n \geq 5$ in the following way. Let $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_{n - 2}, \mu$ be the principal curvatures of $\Sigma$ along $\partial \Sigma$, where $\mu = A_\Sigma(\nu, \nu)$. As before, one shows that equation \eqref{eq:zero1} takes the general form
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:zero2}
        (\lambda_i - \lambda_j)(\lambda_i + \lambda_j - 2\mu) = 0
    \end{equation}
    for all $i \neq j$. Since one of the principal curvatures of $\Sigma$ has multiplicity at least $n - 2$, for a given boundary component $\Gamma \subset \partial \Sigma$ we have that either $\lambda_1 = \ldots = \lambda_{n - 2}$ or $\lambda_2 = \ldots = \lambda_{n - 2} = \mu$ at each $p \in \Gamma$. In this second case \eqref{eq:zero2} implies $\lambda_1 = \mu$. Since the second fundamental form of $\partial \Sigma$ inside of $S^{n - 1}$ coincides with $A_\Sigma|_{T(\partial \Sigma) \times T(\partial \Sigma)}$ by free boundary condition, one concludes that in either case $\Gamma$ is totally umbilical in $S^{n - 1}$, which means that $\Gamma$ is a round $(n - 2)$-sphere. The result now follows from Lemma \ref{lem:rotational} as before.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
