<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The reason we choose to base all subsequent developments on \eqref{eq:deRham_dual} and \eqref{discrete-delta-complex}, rather than the more conventional \eqref{eq:deRham}, originates from the MHD problem \eqref{eq:MHD}. In that system, $\bm B$ is a 2-form satisfying $d\bm B = 0$ (i.e., $\operatorname{div} \bm B = 0$). The diffusion term in \eqref{eq:MHD} reduces to $d\delta \bm B$, which we discretize in weak form as $(\delta \bm B, \delta \bm C)$ using a test 2-form $\bm C$. This approach naturally requires a conforming discretization built around the dual de Rham complex \eqref{eq:deRham_dual}.

The preference for \eqref{eq:deRham_dual} over \eqref{eq:deRham} is largely notational rather than substantial. In three-dimensional vector proxy notation, the dual complex \eqref{eq:deRham_dual} corresponds to $-\nabla$, $\operatorname{curl}$, and $-\operatorname{div}$, which is essentially equivalent to the standard complex \eqref{eq:deRham}. In the broader framework of finite element exterior calculus (FEEC), conforming discretizations of \eqref{eq:deRham} are well established \cite{2006ArnoldFalkWinther,2010ArnoldFalkWinther}. One can then apply a Hodge star to obtain a conforming discretization of \eqref{eq:deRham_dual}. Similarly, composing bounded cochain projections of \eqref{eq:deRham} with a Hodge star yields the corresponding projections for \eqref{eq:deRham_dual}.

<!-- assumption-1: body_md -->
\label{assump:interpolation}
We assume that there exist bounded projections $\tilde\pi_h^k: \0 H\Lambda^k(\Omega)\rightarrow \widetilde{\Lambda}^{k}_{h}$ such that
\begin{equation*}
    d^k\tilde\pi_h^{k} = \tilde\pi_h^{k+1
    }d^k,
\end{equation*}
i.e., the following diagram commutes:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{tikzcd}
    0\ar[r,""]&\mathring{H}\Lambda^0(\Omega) \arrow[r, "d^0"] \arrow[d, "\tilde\pi_h^0"]   & \cdots\arrow[r, "d^{n-1}"] \arrow[d, ""]  &  \mathring{H}\Lambda^n(\Omega)\ar[r,""] \arrow[d, "\tilde\pi_h^n"] &   0\\
    0\ar[r,""]& \widetilde\Lambda_h^0\arrow[r, "d^0"] & \cdots\ar[r,"d^{n-1}"] &  \widetilde\Lambda_h^n \ar[r,""]&0.
\end{tikzcd}
\end{equation*}
Moreover, for any $u\in \mathring{H}\Lambda^k(\Omega)\cap H^t\Lambda^k(\Omega)$ with $1/2<t\leq 1$, we have
\begin{equation*}
    \|u -\tilde{\pi}_h^ku\|_{L^2}\leq
        Ch^t|u|_{H^t},
\end{equation*}
where the constant $C>0$ depends on $t$ but can grow unboundedly as $t\rightarrow 1/2$.

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:pseudospectrum}
    Let $X$ be a complex Hilbert space and $\mathcal{A}:D(\mathcal{A})\subset X\to X$ a closed linear operator. For any $\varepsilon>0$, the $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum of $\mathcal{A}$ is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:pseudospectrum}
        \sigma_\varepsilon(\mathcal{A}) := \left\{\lambda\in \mathbb C : \|(\lambda I - \mathcal{A})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X;X)} > \varepsilon^{-1}\right\},
    \end{equation}
    with the convention that $\|(\lambda I -\mathcal A)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)} = +\infty$ whenever $\lambda \in \sigma(\mathcal{A})$, so that $\sigma(\mathcal{A})\subset \sigma_\varepsilon(\mathcal{A})$ for every $\varepsilon>0$.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:gearhart-pruss}
    Let $\mathcal{L}:D(\mathcal{L})\subset X\to X$ be a closed linear operator on a Hilbert space $X$ that generates a $C_0$ semigroup $\{e^{t\mathcal{L}}\}_{t\geq 0}$. Then, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:gearhart-pruss}
        \varepsilon\sup_{t\geq 0} \|e^{t\mathcal{L}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X;X)} \;\geq\; \alpha_\varepsilon(\mathcal{L}) := \sup\left\{ \Re(\lambda) : \lambda \in \sigma_\varepsilon(\mathcal{L})\right\},
    \end{equation}
    where $\alpha_\varepsilon(\mathcal{L})$ is the \emph{$\varepsilon$-pseudospectral abscissa} of $\mathcal{L}$.

<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:transient}
The simplest illustration of the gap between $\alpha(\mathcal{L})$ and $\alpha_\varepsilon(\mathcal{L})$ is finite dimensional. Fix $\lambda>0$ and $N>0$ and consider on $X=\mathbb C^2$ the two operators
\begin{equation*}
    A \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & N \\ 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}
so that $\sigma(A)=\sigma(B)=\{\lambda\}$ and the spectral abscissa $\alpha(A)=\alpha(B)=\lambda$ is identical: according to \eqref{eq:growth:bound}, both semigroups should display the same asymptotic exponential factor $e^{\lambda t}$. A direct computation gives
\begin{equation*}
    e^{tA} \;=\; e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & Nt \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad e^{tB} \;=\; e^{\lambda t}\,I,
\end{equation*}
so that $\|e^{tB}\|_{\mathcal L(\mathbb C^2,\mathbb C^2)} = e^{\lambda t}$ realizes the eigenvalue prediction exactly, while
\begin{equation*}
    \|e^{tA}\|_{\mathcal L(\mathbb C^2,\mathbb C^2)} \;=\; e^{\lambda t}\,\frac{\sqrt{(Nt)^2+4}+Nt}{2} \;\sim\; Nt\,e^{\lambda t} \qquad \text{as $Nt\to\infty$,}
\end{equation*}
so that the prefactor multiplying the common asymptotic factor $e^{\lambda t}$ can, on any finite time scale, be made arbitrarily large by increasing $N$. With $\lambda=10^{-1}$ and $N=10^{2}$ at $t=1$ already $\|e^{A}\|\approx 110$ while $\|e^{B}\|\approx 1.1$: the spectrum predicts the same asymptotic growth for both operators, but the semigroup generated by $A$ has already produced two orders of magnitude of amplification before $B$ has even doubled. The two semigroups do reach a common amplification, but on radically different time scales: on the dynamo-relevant time scale $B$ grows so slowly that the eigenvalue-driven exponential is essentially invisible, whereas $A$ produces order-of-magnitude effective growth essentially from $t=0$.

Direct computation of the resolvent yields the asymptotic relations
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_\varepsilon(B) \;=\; \lambda + \varepsilon, \qquad \alpha_\varepsilon(A) \;=\; \lambda + \sqrt{N\varepsilon} + o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}) \qquad \text{as $\varepsilon\to 0^+$.}
\end{equation*}
For the values above and $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$ this gives $\alpha_\varepsilon(B)\approx 0.11$ but $\alpha_\varepsilon(A)\approx 1.1$, an order-of-magnitude difference that \Cref{thm:gearhart-pruss} and the transient bound \eqref{eq:transient:bound} translate into a non-trivial lower bound on $\sup_{0\leq t\leq \tau}\|e^{tA}\|_{\mathcal L(\mathbb C^2,\mathbb C^2)}$ vastly exceeding the eigenvalue prediction $e^{\lambda\tau}$, while leaving the slow growth of $\|e^{tB}\|$ untouched. The pseudospectral abscissa thus separates, on the dynamo-relevant transient time scale, the two cases that the spectral abscissa cannot.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:resolvent-factorization}
    Let $X$ be a complex Banach space, let $S,T\in\mathcal{L}(X;X)$, and let $\lambda\in\mathbb C$ be such that $(\lambda I-S)^{-1} \in \mathcal L(X;X)$. Then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:resolvent-factorization}
        \lambda I - T \;=\; \bigl(I - (T-S)(\lambda I - S)^{-1}\bigr)\,(\lambda I - S).
    \end{equation}

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Distributing the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:resolvent-factorization} and using $(\lambda I-S)^{-1}(\lambda I-S)=I$ gives $(\lambda I - S) - (T-S) = \lambda I - T$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:pseudospectra:perturb}
    Let $X$ be a complex Hilbert space and let $S,T\in\mathcal{L}(X;X)$. Then, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:pseudospectra:perturb}
        \sigma_\varepsilon(T) \;\subseteq\; \sigma_{\varepsilon+\|T-S\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)}}(S) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sigma_\varepsilon(S) \;\subseteq\; \sigma_{\varepsilon+\|T-S\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)}}(T).
    \end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
By symmetry between $S$ and $T$, it suffices to establish the first inclusion. Set $\eta:=\|T-S\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)}$ and let $\lambda\in\sigma_\varepsilon(T)$. If $\lambda\in\sigma(S)$, then $\lambda\in\sigma_{\varepsilon+\eta}(S)$ by Definition~\ref{def:pseudospectrum}. Otherwise the resolvent $(\lambda I-S)^{-1}$ is bounded, and we suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that $\lambda\notin \sigma_{\varepsilon+\eta}(S)$, i.e., \ $\|(\lambda I-S)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)} \leq (\varepsilon+\eta)^{-1}$. Notice that
    \begin{equation*}
        \|(T-S)(\lambda I - S)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)} \;\leq\; \eta(\varepsilon+\eta)^{-1} \;<\; 1,
    \end{equation*}
    so that, by a Neumann-series argument,\footnote{Let $M\in\mathcal L(X;X)$ with $q:=\|M\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)} < 1$. The geometric series $\sum_{k\geq 0} M^k$ converges absolutely in $\mathcal L(X;X)$, since by submultiplicativity $\sum_{k\geq 0}\|M^k\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)} \leq \sum_{k\geq 0} q^k = (1-q)^{-1}$. Its sum is a two-sided inverse of $I-M$: the partial sums satisfy $(I-M)\sum_{k=0}^n M^k = I-M^{n+1}$, and since $\|M^{n+1}\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)}\leq q^{n+1}\to 0$ one obtains $(I-M)^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq 0} M^k$ with $\|(I-M)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)} \leq (1-q)^{-1}$.} the operator $I-(T-S)(\lambda I - S)^{-1}$ is invertible in $\mathcal L(X;X)$ with
    \begin{equation*}
        \|(I-(T-S)(\lambda I - S)^{-1})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)} \;\leq\; \frac{1}{1-\eta(\varepsilon+\eta)^{-1}} \;=\; \frac{\varepsilon+\eta}{\varepsilon}.
    \end{equation*}
    The resolvent factorization \eqref{eq:resolvent-factorization} of Lemma~\ref{lem:resolvent-factorization} then yields $(\lambda I-T)^{-1} = (\lambda I-S)^{-1}\bigl(I-(T-S)(\lambda I-S)^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}$, so that
    \begin{equation*}
        \|(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)} \;\leq\; \|(\lambda I-S)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal L(X;X)}\,\frac{\varepsilon+\eta}{\varepsilon} \;\leq\; \frac{1}{\varepsilon+\eta}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon+\eta}{\varepsilon} \;=\; \varepsilon^{-1},
    \end{equation*}
    which contradicts $\lambda\in\sigma_\varepsilon(T)$. Therefore $\lambda\in\sigma_{\varepsilon+\eta}(S)$ and the inclusion \eqref{eq:pseudospectra:perturb} holds.

    & = (\psi_{h_n}-\psi(n),\pi_{h_n}^{k-1}\delta^k u(n)- \delta^{k}\tilde{ u}_{h_n}) = 0.
    \end{align*}
Consequently,

        \| u(n) - \tilde{ u}_{h_n}\|_{L^2}\leq \| u(n) -\pi_{h_n}^k u(n)\|_{L^2}\leq Ch_{n}^s\| u(n)\|_{H^s}\leq Ch_{n}^s\|\delta^k\tilde{ u}_{h_n}\|_{L^2}.

Hence the discrete subsequence $\{ u_{h_n}\}$  converges to  $ u + q$ in $L^2$, which completes the proof.

\[

\]

r_{jm}:=p_j-p_m,\qquad

=|(g_{jm},e_{jm})|,

where the multiplier term vanishes since

Consequently, $\{v_j\}$ is a Cauchy sequence in

since for the problem \eqref{tilde-T-problem}, $d$-free right-hand sides imply $d$-free solutions. Likewise, $T_h f= \tilde{T}_h  f$. Then $(T-T_h) u = (\tilde{T}-\tilde{T}_h) u$ for any $ u\in E$ and
\begin{equation*}
       \sup_{\substack{ v\in E\\ \| v\|_{L^2}=1}}\|(T-T_h) v\|_{L^2}\leq  \sup_{\substack{ v\in E\\ \| v\|_{L^2} =1}} \| (T - T_h  )v\|_{H^*\Lambda}\leq Ch^{r_{1}}.
\end{equation*}

\emph{Second},  consider the term
\begin{equation*}
    \|(T^*-T_h^*)_{/E^*}\|:= \sup_{ \substack{v\in E^*\\ \| v\|_{L^2}=1}}\|(T^*-T_h^*) v\|_{L^2}.
\end{equation*}
Recall that $T^* f$ satisfies
\begin{equation*}
    a( w,T^* f)  =( w, f),\quad \forall  w \in \mathbb{K}^k,
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
    a( w,\tilde{T}^* f) = ( w, f),\quad \forall  w\in \mathring{H}^*\Lambda^{k}(\Omega).
\end{equation*}
Set $\tilde{ u} = \tilde{T}^* f-\Pi_{\delta}^k \tilde{T}^* f \in \mathbb{K}^k$, since $a(w,\delta^{k+1}q) = \nu(w,\delta^{k+1}q) = 0$ holds for any $w\in \mathbb{K}^k$ and $q\in \mathring{H}^*\Lambda^{k+1}(\Omega)$, we have
\begin{equation*}
    a( w,\tilde{ u})  = a( w,\tilde{T}^* f) - a(w,\Pi_{\delta}^k \tilde{T}^* f)
     = (w,f),\quad \forall  w\in\mathbb{K}^k.
\end{equation*}
This implies that for any $ f\in L^2\Lambda^k(\Omega)$
\begin{equation*}
    T^* f= \tilde{T}^* f-\Pi_{\delta}^k \tilde{T}^* f,
\end{equation*}
and, by a similar argument
\begin{equation*}
    T^*_h f= \tilde{T}^*_h f-\Pi_{\delta,h}^k \tilde{T}^*_h f.
\end{equation*}
Therefore,
\begin{align*}
    \|T^* v-T_h^* v\|_{L^2}&\leq \|\tilde{T}^* v-\tilde{T}^*_h v\|_{L^2} + \|\Pi_{\delta}^k \tilde{T}^* v-\Pi_{\delta,h}^k \tilde{T}^*_h v\|_{L^2}\\
    &\leq \|\tilde{T}^* v-\tilde{T}^*_h v\|_{L^2} + \|\Pi_{\delta}^k \tilde{T}^* v -\Pi_{\delta,h}^k \tilde{T}^* v\|_{L^2} + \|\Pi_{\delta,h}^k (\tilde{T}^* v - \tilde{T}^*_h v)\|_{L^2}\\
    &\leq 2\|\tilde{T}^* v-\tilde{T}^*_h v\|_{L^2}+ \|(I-\Pi_{\delta,h}^k)\Pi_{\delta}^k \tilde{T}^* v\|_{L^2},
\end{align*}
which implies that
\begin{equation*}
        \sup_{\substack{ v\in E^*\\ \| v\|_{L^2} =1}} \| (T^*   - T_h^*)  v\|_{L^2}\leq Ch^{\min\{r_{2}, r_{3}\}}.
    \end{equation*}

\emph{Finally}, we need to estimate the term
\begin{equation*}
    \sup_{\substack{ u\in E\\ \| u\|_{L^2} =1}}\sup_{\substack{ v\in E^*\\ \| v\|_{L^2} =1}}| ((T-T_h) u, v)|.
\end{equation*}
Note that
\begin{align*}
    | ((T-T_h) u, v)| &= |a((T-T_h) u,\tilde{T}^* v)| = |a(\tilde{T} u-\tilde{T}_h u,\tilde{T}^* v - \tilde{T}^*_h v)|\\
    &\leq C\|\tilde{T} u-\tilde{T}_h u\|_{H^*\Lambda}\|\tilde{T}^* v - \tilde{T}^*_h v\|_{H^*\Lambda},
\end{align*}
where we used $(T-T_h) u = (\tilde{T}-\tilde{T}_h) u$ for any $ u\in E$ and
\begin{equation*}
    a((\tilde{T}-\tilde{T}_h) u, v_h)= 0,\quad \forall  v_h\in \Lambda_h^k.
\end{equation*}
Hence
\begin{equation*}
    \sup_{\substack{ u\in E\\ \| u\|_{L^2} =1}}\sup_{\substack{ v\in E^*\\ \| v\|_{L^2} =1}}| ((T-T_h) u, v)|\leq Ch^{{r_{1}+\min\{r_{2}, r_{3}\}}}.
\end{equation*}
The result now follows from Lemma \ref{lem:eigenvalue_err}.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rmk:alternative-formulation}
The Maxwell eigenvalue problem
\begin{eqnarray}
\label{curl-curl}
\nabla\times \nabla\times \bm B&=\lambda\bm B, \
\nabla\cdot \bm B&=0,
\end{eqnarray}
admits several equivalent formulations. For example, one may apply an additional curl operator and solve for $\bm j=\nabla\times \bm B$, which satisfies the same eigenvalue problem as $\bm B$. Alternatively, one may introduce a magnetic potential $\bm A$ satisfying $\bm B=\nabla\times  \bm A$ (ignoring harmonic forms) and impose the gauge condition $\nabla\cdot \bm A=0$. Then $\bm A$ also satisfies the $\operatorname{curl}$–$\operatorname{curl}$ system \eqref{curl-curl}. These formulations, together with their finite element discretizations based on de~Rham complexes, are all equivalent \cite{2010Boffi}. This equivalence extends more generally to half Hodge–Laplacian problems.

It is well known that the MHD eigenvalue (or source) problem can be reformulated in terms of a vector potential with an appropriate gauge condition \cite{heumann2012fully}. More precisely, the evolution problem \eqref{eq:MHD} can be rewritten as
\begin{eqnarray}\label{A-formulation}
\bm A_t-\bm u\times \nabla\times \bm A+ R_m^{-1}\nabla\times \nabla\times \bm A+\nabla p&=0, \
\nabla\cdot \bm A&=0.
\end{eqnarray}
Here, $p$ acts as a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the divergence-free gauge condition on $\bm A$, and \eqref{A-formulation} is precisely a half-Hodge formulation of advection–diffusion for the 1-form $\bm A$. However, for the MHD eigenvalue problem, and more generally for advection–diffusion problems of differential forms, the equivalence between discrete formulations can be lost. First, the cohomology information is not preserved when one applies an exterior derivative or passes to a potential formulation. Second, the Lie derivative of a finite element differential form need not belong to the finite element space. Therefore suitable projections are required in order to obtain equivalent finite element formulations.
