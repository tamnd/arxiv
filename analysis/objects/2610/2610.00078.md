\label{ex:standardS6}
Identify $\mathbb R^7$ with the imaginary Cayley numbers and let $\times$ denote the corresponding cross product. On the unit sphere
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
S^6=\{x\in\mathbb R^7:\langle x,x\rangle=1\},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
the standard nearly K\"ahler structure is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
J_xY=x\times Y,\qquad Y\in T_xS^6.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
For the unit round metric this is a strict nearly K\"ahler structure of constant type $\alpha=1$; see Gray \cite{Gray1970} and the hypersurface descriptions in \cite{AnarellaDhaene2026,BerndtBoltonWoodward1995}. Differentiating $J_xY=x\times Y$ in $\mathbb R^7$ and projecting tangentially gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G(X,Y)=X\times Y-\langle X\times Y,x\rangle x.
\label{eq:S6G}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus the general identities above can be checked directly in the octonionic model. We will use this sphere repeatedly below to give genuine hypersurface examples rather than only tangent-space illustrations.

\label{def:induced-acms}

\xi=-JN,\qquad \eta(X)=g(X,\xi),
\end{aligned}

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
JX=\phi X+\eta(X)N.
\end{aligned}

\phi^2X&=-X+\eta(X)\xi,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\begin{aligned}
\phi\xi&=0,\qquad \eta(\phi X)=0,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\end{aligned}

\begin{aligned}
\mathcal D=\ker\eta.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\label{ex:latitude-family}
Fix a unit vector $e_7\in\mathbb R^7$ and a constant $c\in(-1,1)$. Put
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
M_c=\{x\in S^6:\langle x,e_7\rangle=c\},\qquad s=\sqrt{1-c^2}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The restriction of $f(x)=\langle x,e_7\rangle$ to $S^6$ has gradient $e_7-cx$ along $M_c$, whose norm is $s>0$. Hence $c$ is a regular value and $M_c$ is a smooth embedded $5$-dimensional hypersurface. It is the geodesic hypersphere of radius $r=\arccos c$ centered at $e_7$.

A unit normal field in $S^6$ is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
N_c(x)=\frac{e_7-cx}{s}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Indeed, if $X\in T_xM_c$, then $\langle X,e_7\rangle=\langle X,x\rangle=0$, so $\langle X,N_c\rangle=0$, while
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\langle N_c,N_c\rangle=\frac{1-c^2}{1-c^2}=1.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Since $c$ and $s$ are constant along $M_c$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde\nabla_XN_c=-\frac{c}{s}X,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
and the Weingarten formula gives
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
A=\frac{c}{s}I=(\cot r)I.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The induced Reeb field is also explicit:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\xi_c=-J_xN_c=-\frac{x\times e_7}{s}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Thus $\eta_c(X)=\langle X,\xi_c\rangle$ and $\phi_cX=J_xX-\eta_c(X)N_c$.

For $c=0$ we obtain the equatorial hypersphere
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
M_0=S^6\cap e_7^\perp\cong S^5.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Here $N=e_7$ and $A=0$, so $M_0$ is totally geodesic. With the standard Cayley basis chosen so that $e_1\times e_7=-e_6$, at the point $p=e_1$ one has
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\xi_p=-e_1\times e_7=e_6,\qquad
\mathcal D_p=\operatorname{span}\{e_2,e_3,e_4,e_5\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
This gives a concrete realization of every object in Definition~\ref{def:induced-acms}.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:GN}
For every $X\in\mathcal D$, one has $G_NX\in\mathcal D$. Moreover, on $\mathcal D$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G_N\phi=-\phi G_N,
\label{eq:anticommute}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G_N^2=-\alpha I_{\mathcal D}.
\label{eq:GNsquare}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In particular, $G_N|_{\mathcal D}$ is invertible.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
First, $G(X,N)$ is tangent to $M$. From \eqref{eq:G3form}, with $Y=Z=N$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde g(G(X,N),N)=-\widetilde g(G(X,N),N),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
so $\widetilde g(G(X,N),N)=0$.

It remains to show that $G(X,N)$ is orthogonal to $\xi$. Since $\xi=-JN$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
g(G(X,N),\xi)=-\widetilde g(G(X,N),JN).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Let $a=\widetilde g(G(X,N),JN)$. Using \eqref{eq:G3form} and then \eqref{eq:GJ},
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
a=-\widetilde g(G(X,JN),N)=\widetilde g(JG(X,N),N).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Because $J$ is skew-adjoint with respect to $\widetilde g$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde g(JG(X,N),N)=-\widetilde g(G(X,N),JN)=-a.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Thus $a=-a$, so $a=0$. Hence $G_NX\perp\xi$ and therefore $G_NX\in\mathcal D$.

Now let $X\in\mathcal D$. Then $JX=\phi X$. Using \eqref{eq:Gskew} and \eqref{eq:GJ},
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
G_N(\phi X)=G(JX,N)=-G(N,JX)=JG(N,X)=-JG(X,N).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Since $G_NX\in\mathcal D$, the action of $J$ on $G_NX$ equals $\phi$. Consequently
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
G_N(\phi X)=-\phi G_NX,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
which proves \eqref{eq:anticommute}.

Putting $Y=Z=N$ in \eqref{eq:GG}. For $X\in\mathcal D$ we have
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde g(X,N)=0,\qquad \widetilde g(JX,N)=0,\qquad \widetilde g(JN,N)=0,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
so
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
G(G(X,N),N)=-\alpha X.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The left-hand side is $G_N^2X$, proving \eqref{eq:GNsquare}. Since $\alpha>0$, the equation $G_NX=0$ implies $X=0$. Thus $G_N$ is invertible on $\mathcal D$.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:reebform}
Let $M^5$ be an oriented real hypersurface of a six-dimensional strict nearly K\"ahler manifold of positive constant type $\alpha$. Then, at every $p\in M$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{
\left\|(d\eta)_p|_{\Lambda^2\mathcal D_p}\right\|^2
=\frac12\left\|\phi B_p+B_p\phi\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2+8\alpha.}
\label{eq:quantitative-main}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left\|(d\eta)_p|_{\Lambda^2\mathcal D_p}\right\|^2\ge8\alpha>0,
\label{eq:quantitative-lower}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and hence $(d\eta)_p\neq0$ for every $p\in M$. Equality in \eqref{eq:quantitative-lower} holds if and only if
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\phi B_p+B_p\phi=0.
\label{eq:quantitative-equality}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Since $\eta(Y)=g(Y,\xi)$ and $\nabla g=0$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla_X\eta)(Y)=g(\nabla_X\xi,Y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Because the Levi-Civita connection is torsion-free,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
d\eta(X,Y)
&=(\nabla_X\eta)(Y)-(\nabla_Y\eta)(X)\\
&=g(\nabla_X\xi,Y)-g(\nabla_Y\xi,X).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Using \eqref{eq:nablaxi},
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
d\eta(X,Y)
={}&g(\phi AX,Y)-g(\phi AY,X)\\
&-g(G_NX,Y)+g(G_NY,X).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Now $A$ is self-adjoint and $\phi$ is skew-adjoint. Hence
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
g(\phi AY,X)=-g(A\phi X,Y),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
while $G_N$ is skew-adjoint by \eqref{eq:G3form}. Therefore
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{d\eta(X,Y)=g((\phi A+A\phi)X,Y)-2g(G_NX,Y).}
\label{eq:deta}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

We now restrict to $X,Y\in\mathcal D_p$. By the definition of $B_p$, the components of $AX$ and $A\phi X$ in the $\xi$-direction do not contribute after applying $\phi$ or pairing with $Y$. Thus \eqref{eq:deta} becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(d\eta)_p(X,Y)
=g(K_pX,Y),
\qquad
K_p:=L_p-2G_N,
\label{eq:K-horizontal}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
L_p:=\phi B_p+B_p\phi.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Both $L_p$ and $G_N$ are skew-adjoint on $\mathcal D_p$. Indeed,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
L_p^*=(\phi B_p+B_p\phi)^*
=-B_p\phi-\phi B_p=-L_p,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
and Lemma~\ref{lem:GN} gives the corresponding property for $G_N$.

Next, because $\phi^2=-I$ on $\mathcal D_p$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
L_p\phi
&=\phi B_p\phi+B_p\phi^2
=\phi B_p\phi-B_p,\\
\phi L_p
&=\phi^2B_p+\phi B_p\phi
=-B_p+\phi B_p\phi.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Hence
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L_p\phi=\phi L_p.
\label{eq:Lcomm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, Lemma~\ref{lem:GN} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G_N\phi=-\phi G_N,
\qquad
G_N^2=-\alpha I_{\mathcal D_p}.
\label{eq:GNanti-square-main}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus $L_p$ is $\phi$-complex-linear and $G_N$ is $\phi$-anti-complex-linear.

We use the Hilbert--Schmidt inner product
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\langle S,T\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\operatorname{tr}(S^*T)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
on $\operatorname{End}(\mathcal D_p)$. Since $\phi$ is orthogonal on $\mathcal D_p$, conjugation by $\phi$ preserves this inner product. Equations \eqref{eq:Lcomm} and \eqref{eq:GNanti-square-main} therefore give
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\langle L_p,G_N\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}
&=\langle \phi^{-1}L_p\phi,\phi^{-1}G_N\phi\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\\
&=\langle L_p,-G_N\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}
=-\langle L_p,G_N\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\langle L_p,G_N\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=0.
\label{eq:HSorth}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, because $G_N^*=-G_N$ and $\dim\mathcal D_p=4$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|G_N\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
&=\operatorname{tr}(G_N^*G_N)
=-\operatorname{tr}(G_N^2)\\
&=4\alpha.
\label{eq:GN-HS}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:K-horizontal}, \eqref{eq:HSorth}, and \eqref{eq:GN-HS}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|K_p\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
&=\|L_p-2G_N\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\\
&=\|L_p\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2+4\|G_N\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\\
&=\|L_p\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2+16\alpha.
\label{eq:K-HS}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Finally, if a two-form $\omega$ on a Euclidean vector space is represented by a skew-adjoint endomorphism $K$ through $\omega(X,Y)=g(KX,Y)$, then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|\omega\|^2=\frac12\|K\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
\label{eq:twoform-HS}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Applying \eqref{eq:twoform-HS} to the restriction of $(d\eta)_p$ to $\mathcal D_p$ and using \eqref{eq:K-HS} gives exactly \eqref{eq:quantitative-main}. The lower bound follows because the first term on the right-hand side is nonnegative, and equality holds precisely when $L_p=\phi B_p+B_p\phi=0$.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:notexact}
The Reeb one-form $\eta$ is not locally exact on any nonempty open subset of $M$. Equivalently, the Reeb vector field $\xi$ cannot be a local gradient vector field.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
If $\eta=df$ on a nonempty open set, then $d\eta=d^2f=0$ there, contradicting Theorem~\ref{thm:reebform}. Since $\eta=g(\,\cdot\,,\xi)$, the equality $\eta=df$ is equivalent to $\xi=\operatorname{grad}f$ with respect to the induced metric.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:closedclasses}
The induced almost contact metric structure cannot satisfy, on any nonempty open subset, any geometric class whose defining equations include $d\eta=0$. In particular, it cannot be co-K\"ahler or almost cosymplectic in the standard sense $d\eta=d\Phi=0$, where $\Phi(X,Y)=g(X,\phi Y)$.

Both co-K\"ahler and almost cosymplectic structures require the Reeb one-form to be closed; see, for example, Blair \cite{Blair2010}. Theorem~\ref{thm:reebform} rules out this necessary condition pointwise.

\label{rem:not-contact}
The lower bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:reebform} should not be confused with the contact condition. In dimension five, $\eta$ is a contact form only when

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:skew-general}
Let $(M^n,g)$ carry a unit vector field $\xi$, let $\eta=g(\,\cdot\,,\xi)$, and define
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\nabla_XY=\nabla_XY+\eta(Y)X-g(X,Y)\xi.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\operatorname{Skew}\widehat{\operatorname{Ric}}=-\frac{n-2}{2}\,d\eta.}
\label{eq:skew-general}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Write
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\nabla_XY=\nabla_XY+Q(X,Y),
\qquad
Q(X,Y)=\eta(Y)X-g(X,Y)\xi.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
For two connections differing by a $(1,2)$-tensor $Q$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat R(X,Y)Z={}&R(X,Y)Z+(\nabla_XQ)(Y,Z)-(\nabla_YQ)(X,Z)\\
&+Q(X,Q(Y,Z))-Q(Y,Q(X,Z)).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Because $\eta(Z)=g(Z,\xi)$ and $\nabla g=0$,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla_XQ)(Y,Z)=(\nabla_X\eta)(Z)Y-g(Y,Z)\nabla_X\xi.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Moreover,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
Q(X,Q(Y,Z))={}&\{\eta(Y)\eta(Z)-g(Y,Z)\}X\\
&-\{\eta(Z)g(X,Y)-g(Y,Z)\eta(X)\}\xi.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Substitution gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat R(X,Y)Z={}&R(X,Y)Z+(\nabla_X\eta)(Z)Y-(\nabla_Y\eta)(Z)X\\
&-g(Y,Z)\nabla_X\xi+g(X,Z)\nabla_Y\xi\\
&+\{\eta(Y)\eta(Z)-g(Y,Z)\}X\\
&-\{\eta(X)\eta(Z)-g(X,Z)\}Y\\
&+\{g(Y,Z)\eta(X)-g(X,Z)\eta(Y)\}\xi.
\label{eq:general-curv-ssmc}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Choose a local orthonormal frame $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ and use
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\operatorname{Ric}(X,Y)=\sum_{i=1}^n g(\widehat R(e_i,X)Y,e_i).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Contracting \eqref{eq:general-curv-ssmc} term by term yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\operatorname{Ric}(X,Y)
={}&\operatorname{Ric}(X,Y)-(n-2)(\nabla_X\eta)(Y)\\
&-(\operatorname{div}\xi+n-2)g(X,Y)
+(n-2)\eta(X)\eta(Y).
\label{eq:general-ricci-ssmc}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For completeness, the derivative terms contribute
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
-(n-2)(\nabla_X\eta)(Y)-g(X,Y)\operatorname{div}\xi,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
and the remaining quadratic terms contribute
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
-(n-2)g(X,Y)+(n-2)\eta(X)\eta(Y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The ordinary Ricci tensor, $g$, and $\eta\otimes\eta$ are symmetric. Hence
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\operatorname{Ric}(X,Y)-\widehat\operatorname{Ric}(Y,X)
&=-(n-2)\{(\nabla_X\eta)(Y)-(\nabla_Y\eta)(X)\}\\
&=-(n-2)d\eta(X,Y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Dividing by two proves \eqref{eq:skew-general}.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:ricci}
On a real hypersurface of a strict nearly K\"ahler six-manifold, the Ricci tensor of $\widehat\nabla$ is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\operatorname{Ric}^{\widehat\nabla}(X,Y)=\operatorname{Ric}(X,Y)-3g(\phi AX,Y)+3g(G_NX,Y)-3g(\phi X,\phi Y).}
\label{eq:hRic}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Set $n=5$ in \eqref{eq:general-ricci-ssmc}. From \eqref{eq:nablaxi},
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{div}\xi=\operatorname{tr}(\phi A)-\operatorname{tr}(G_N).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Because $A$ is self-adjoint and $\phi$ is skew-adjoint,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr}(\phi A)=0,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
and $G_N$ is skew-adjoint, so $\operatorname{tr}(G_N)=0$. Thus $\operatorname{div}\xi=0$. Also,
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla_X\eta)(Y)
=g(\nabla_X\xi,Y)
=g(\phi AX,Y)-g(G_NX,Y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Substitution into \eqref{eq:general-ricci-ssmc} gives
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Ric}^{\widehat\nabla}(X,Y)={}&\operatorname{Ric}(X,Y)-3g(\phi AX,Y)+3g(G_NX,Y)\\
&-3g(X,Y)+3\eta(X)\eta(Y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Using $g(\phi X,\phi Y)=g(X,Y)-\eta(X)\eta(Y)$ proves \eqref{eq:hRic}.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:decomp}
The symmetric and skew-symmetric parts of $\operatorname{Ric}^{\widehat\nabla}$ are
\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.90\linewidth}{!}{$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Sym}\operatorname{Ric}^{\widehat\nabla}(X,Y)
={}&\operatorname{Ric}(X,Y)-\frac32 g((\phi A-A\phi)X,Y)-3g(\phi X,\phi Y)
\end{aligned}
$}
\label{eq:symric}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Skew}\operatorname{Ric}^{\widehat\nabla}(X,Y)
={}&-\frac32 g((\phi A+A\phi)X,Y)+3g(G_NX,Y).
\label{eq:skewric}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Ric}^{\widehat\nabla}\ \text{is symmetric}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\phi A+A\phi=2G_N.
\label{eq:symcondition}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The tensors $\operatorname{Ric}$ and $(X,Y)\mapsto g(\phi X,\phi Y)$ are symmetric. For
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
C(X,Y)=g(\phi AX,Y),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
we have $(\phi A)^*=-A\phi$, and hence
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
C(X,Y)+C(Y,X)&=g((\phi A-A\phi)X,Y),\\
C(X,Y)-C(Y,X)&=g((\phi A+A\phi)X,Y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Since $G_N$ is skew-adjoint, \eqref{eq:symric} and \eqref{eq:skewric} follow from \eqref{eq:hRic}. The equivalence \eqref{eq:symcondition} follows by setting the skew part equal to zero.

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For the Yano semi-symmetric metric connection \eqref{eq:ssmc},
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\operatorname{Skew}\operatorname{Ric}^{\widehat\nabla}_p|_{\Lambda^2\mathcal D_p}\right\|^2
&=\frac94\left\|(d\eta)_p|_{\Lambda^2\mathcal D_p}\right\|^2\\
&=\frac98\left\|\phi B_p+B_p\phi\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2+18\alpha\\
&\ge18\alpha>0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
In particular, $\operatorname{Ric}^{\widehat\nabla}$ is nowhere symmetric. Equality in the last inequality holds if and only if $\phi B_p+B_p\phi=0$.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
The standard homogeneous nearly K\"ahler product has an additional almost product structure $P$, which plays a central role in its detailed hypersurface theory \cite{Hu2021,HuYao2020,HuYaoZhang2018}. The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:reebform}, however, does not use $P$. Thus every oriented real hypersurface of the homogeneous nearly K\"ahler $S^3\times S^3$ satisfies
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
(d\eta)_p\neq0
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
at every point. In classification problems this supplies an ambient-independent preliminary test: any candidate family whose induced structure would force $d\eta=0$ is excluded before the $P$-dependent equations are considered.
<!-- end remark-4 -->
<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
The same pointwise obstruction applies to the standard strict nearly K\"ahler structures on
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb CP^3=Sp(2)/(Sp(1)\times U(1))
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
F_{1,2}(\mathbb C^3)=SU(3)/T^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The twistor-space hypersurface geometry of these examples has been studied by Deschamps and Loubeau \cite{DeschampsLoubeau2021}. The present result is complementary: it uses no twistor-specific curvature formula and rules out a closed induced Reeb form before any finer classification condition is imposed.
<!-- end remark-5 -->
<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
Blair's classical result excludes compact orientable cosymplectic hypersurfaces under a positive sectional-curvature assumption on the nearly K\"ahler ambient manifold \cite{Blair1971}. Anarella--D'haene prove that the distinguished family of almost contact structures with Reeb field $\xi=-JN$ on a strict nearly K\"ahler six-manifold is never co-K\"ahler \cite{AnarellaDhaene2026}. Theorem~\ref{thm:reebform} addresses a narrower tensorial feature but does so pointwise and without compactness, homogeneity, or a sectional-curvature hypothesis. More precisely, it gives the quantitative lower bound $\|(d\eta)_p|_{\Lambda^2\mathcal D_p}\|^2\ge8\alpha$. The result is therefore complementary to, rather than a replacement for, those richer structure theorems.
<!-- end remark-6 -->
<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
Butruille's classification \cite{Butruille2005} lists the four simply connected homogeneous strict nearly K\"ahler six-manifolds, while Foscolo--Haskins \cite{FoscoloHaskins2017} show that strict nearly K\"ahler geometry also admits complete inhomogeneous examples. Since the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:reebform} is pointwise, the obstruction applies equally to those nonhomogeneous structures. Homogeneity is therefore useful for examples and subsequent classification problems, but not for the obstruction itself.
