<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{main}
As $n\to\infty$ through even integers,
\[
 P_n\sim C_*n^{-1/4},\qquad
 C_*=\frac{e\,2^{1/4}\Gamma(3/4)}{\sqrt\pi}
     =2.234914123\ldots.
\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
A fixed-point-free permutation $\pi$ of $[n]$ is stable if
\[
 U_{i,\pi^{-1}(i)}\le U_{i,\pi(i)}\qquad(i\in[n]),
\]
and there are no distinct $i,j$ such that
\[
 U_{ij}<U_{i,\pi(i)}\quad\text{and}\quad
 U_{ji}<U_{j,\pi(j)}.
\]
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{exact}
For $\pi\in\mathcal E_n$, its stability probability is
\begin{equation}\label{integral}
 p_\pi=\int_{[0,1]^n}\prod_{i\in A(\pi)}x_i
       \prod_{\substack{i<j\\\{i,j\}\notin D(\pi)}}(1-x_ix_j)\,dx.
\end{equation}
The solvability probability satisfies the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{identity}
 P_n=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal E_n}(-1)^{\ell(\pi)}p_\pi.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Condition on the successor scores $x_i=U_{i,\pi(i)}$. At a long-cycle
vertex, the predecessor score is an independent variable distinct from
the successor score; integrating it over $[0,x_i]$ contributes $x_i$.
A cyclic-neighbor pair cannot block, since at least one endpoint names
the other as its successor.
For each pair outside $D(\pi)$, the two directed scores remain
independent uniforms. The forbidden rectangle has area $x_ix_j$, so the
probability of no block is $1-x_ix_j$. These pairs use disjoint score
variables. Multiplication and integration prove~\eqref{integral}.

Appendix~\ref{app:identity} proves the pointwise version of
\eqref{identity}; taking expectations gives the stated formula.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{domination}
If $D$ is a graph on $[n]$ with maximum degree at most two, then on $[0,1]^n$,
\[
 \prod_{\substack{i<j\\\{i,j\}\notin D}}(1-x_ix_j)
 \le e^{81/16}e^{-S^2/2}.
\]
For a fixed set $A$ of $m$ vertices, with $n+m$ even, define
\[
 J_{n,m}=\int_{\mathbb R_+^n}e^{-S^2/2}\prod_{i\in A}x_i\,dx.
\]
Then $J_{n,m}=(n+m-1)!!^{-1}$. Under the probability measure
\[
 d\mu_{n,m}(x)=J_{n,m}^{-1}e^{-S^2/2}\prod_{i\in A}x_i\,dx,
\]
$S^2$ has the $\chi^2_{n+m}$ distribution and is independent of
$u=x/S$, which has law $\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n)$, with
$\alpha_i=2$ on $A$ and $1$ on its complement.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For nonnegative $y_i$, the degree bound gives
\[
 \sum_{\{i,j\}\in D}y_iy_j
 \le\frac12\sum_i\deg_D(i)y_i^2\le\sum_i y_i^2.
\]
Apply this first to $y_i=x_i$ and then to $y_i=x_i^2$. Subtracting from
the corresponding sums over all pairs yields
\[
 \sum_{\substack{i<j\\\{i,j\}\notin D}}x_ix_j
 \ge\frac{S^2}{2}-\frac{3q}{2},\qquad
 \sum_{\substack{i<j\\\{i,j\}\notin D}}x_i^2x_j^2
 \ge\frac{q^2}{2}-\frac32\sum_i x_i^4.
\]
Using $\log(1-z)\le-z-z^2/2$ and $\sum_i x_i^4\le q$ on the cube,
the logarithm of the product is at most
\[
 -\frac{S^2}{2}+\frac94 q-\frac14q^2
 \le-\frac{S^2}{2}+\frac{81}{16}.
\]
Cases with a zero factor follow by continuity.

Set $N=n+m=\sum_i\alpha_i$ and change variables to $x_i=su_i$, where
$s>0$, $u_i\ge0$ and $\sum_i u_i=1$. In the simplex coordinates
$u_1,\ldots,u_{n-1}$, the Jacobian is $s^{n-1}$. Consequently
\[
 e^{-S^2/2}\prod_{i\in A}x_i\,dx
 =e^{-s^2/2}s^{N-1}\,ds\prod_i u_i^{\alpha_i-1}\,du.
\]
The simplex integral is $\prod_i\Gamma(\alpha_i)/\Gamma(N)=1/\Gamma(N)$.
Hence
\[
 J_{n,m}=\frac1{\Gamma(N)}\int_0^\infty e^{-s^2/2}s^{N-1}\,ds
 =\frac{2^{N/2-1}\Gamma(N/2)}{\Gamma(N)}
 =\frac1{(N-1)!!}.
\]
After normalization, the density factors into a density proportional to
$e^{-s^2/2}s^{N-1}$ and the stated Dirichlet density. Changing variables
from $s$ to $s^2$ gives $S^2\sim\chi^2_N$; the factorization gives
independence.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{cutoff}
Uniformly for $0\le m\le n$ with $n+m$ even,
\begin{equation}\label{tail}
 \mu_{n,m}(G^c)\le e^{-c\log^2 n}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, the contribution of $[0,1]^n\setminus G$ to
\eqref{integral} is at most $J_{n,m}e^{-c\log^2 n}$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Since $N=n+m\le2n$, the exponential moment of the chi-square law at $1/4$
gives
\[
 \mathbb P_{\mu_{n,m}}(S^2>4n)
 \le e^{-n}2^{N/2}\le e^{-(1-\log2)n}.
\]
Each coordinate $u_i$ has a Beta$(1,N-1)$ or Beta$(2,N-2)$ law.
For $0<t<1$ their respective upper tails are exactly
\[
 (1-t)^{N-1},\qquad
 (1-t)^{N-2}\bigl(1+(N-2)t\bigr).
\]
At $t=\log^2 n/n$, each tail is at most
$C(1+Nt)e^{-ctN}$. Since $n\le N\le2n$, a union bound over the
$n$ coordinates gives a bound of $e^{-c\log^2 n}$ after reducing $c>0$.
Together with the radial bound, this proves~\eqref{tail} uniformly in $m$.
Finally, Lemma~\ref{domination} bounds the original integrand by
$e^{81/16}$ times the unnormalized reference density, which proves the
last assertion.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{moments}
Let $B$ be any perfect matching on $A^c$, with $r=(n-m)/2$ edges, and put
\[
 h=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in B}x_ix_j,\quad
 N=n+m,\quad
 Q_2=2n+4m,\quad Q_4=20n+64m.
\]
Under $\mu_{n,m}$,
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E q&=\frac{Q_2}{N+1}, &
 \mathbb E q^2&=\frac{Q_2^2+Q_4}{(N+1)(N+3)},\label{qmom}\\
 \mathbb E h&=\frac r{N+1}, &
 \mathbb E h^2&=\frac{r^2+3r}{(N+1)(N+3)}.\label{hmom}
\end{align}
If $v,w\in A$ are distinct, then $\mathbb E x_vx_w=4/(N+1)$.
Uniformly for $0\le m\le n$,
$\operatorname{Var}(q),\operatorname{Var}(h)=O(n^{-1})$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For nonnegative integers $j_i$ and $d=\sum_i j_i$, use the independence
in Lemma~\ref{domination} and integrate the Dirichlet density with its
parameters shifted by $j_i$. This gives
\begin{equation}\label{generalmoment}
 \mathbb E\prod_i x_i^{j_i}
 =2^{d/2}\frac{\Gamma((N+d)/2)}{\Gamma(N/2)}
   \frac{\prod_i\alpha_i^{\overline{j_i}}}{N^{\overline d}}.
\end{equation}
In particular $\mathbb E S^2=N$ and $\mathbb E S^4=N(N+2)$.
Since $\sum_i\alpha_i^{\overline2}=Q_2$, the first formula
in~\eqref{qmom} follows. For the second moment, the diagonal correction to
$(\sum_i\alpha_i^{\overline2})^2$ is
\[
 \sum_i\left(\alpha_i^{\overline4}
             -(\alpha_i^{\overline2})^2\right)
 =20(n-m)+84m=Q_4.
\]
This proves the second formula in~\eqref{qmom}. In~\eqref{generalmoment},
each matching edge contributes numerator $1$ to $\mathbb E h$, numerator $4$
for its square, and numerator $1$ for its product with a distinct
edge. This gives~\eqref{hmom}; the formula for $\mathbb E x_vx_w$ follows from
$\alpha_v\alpha_w=4$. Subtracting the squared means gives
\begin{align*}
 \operatorname{Var}(q)&=\frac{12n^2+52nm+32m^2+20n+64m}{(N+1)^2(N+3)},\\
 \operatorname{Var}(h)&=\frac{r\,[3(N+1)-2r]}{(N+1)^2(N+3)}.
\end{align*}
The numerators are $O(n^2)$ and the denominators are of order $n^3$,
uniformly for $0\le m\le n$.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{topology}
There are positive numbers $b_{n,m}$ depending only on $n,m$ such that,
uniformly for $m\le m_*$ and every admissible long-cycle graph $H$,
\begin{equation}\label{toperror}
 p_H=b_{n,m}+O\left(J_{n,m}
       \left[\frac{m^2\log^8 n}{n^2}+e^{-c\log^2 n}\right]\right).
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
On $G$, each edge product is at most $a^2$, and
$ma^2=O(n^{-1/2}\log^5 n)=o(1)$ for $m\le m_*$.
For $z_e=x_ix_j$ on the edges of $H$, put $Z=\sum_e z_e$.
The expansion $-\log(1-z)=z+O(z^2)$ gives
\[
 \prod_{\{i,j\}\in H}(1-x_ix_j)^{-1}
 =1+\sum_{\{i,j\}\in H}x_ix_j+R_H,
 \qquad |R_H|\le C m^2a^4.
\]
Indeed $Z\le ma^2=o(1)$, and the exponential remainder is bounded by
$C(Z^2+\sum_e z_e^2)\le C m^2a^4$.

Both $G$ and $W_{n,m}$ are invariant under relabelings within $A$.
Thus $\int_G W_{n,m}(x)x_vx_w\,dx$ is the same for every distinct
$v,w\in A$. For $m\ge2$, choose any such pair and define
\begin{equation}\label{bdefinition}
 b_{n,m}=\int_G W_{n,m}(x)(1+m x_vx_w)\,dx.
\end{equation}
Since $H$ has $m$ edges, the constant and linear terms integrate to
exactly $b_{n,m}$ for every $H$. When $m=0$, define
$b_{n,0}=\int_G W_{n,0}(x)\,dx$.
The term at $m=2$ will have coefficient zero in the enumeration.
Lemma~\ref{domination} applied to $B$ gives
\[
 \int_{[0,1]^n}W_{n,m}(x)\,dx\le CJ_{n,m}.
\]
Thus the integrated remainder on $G$ is at most $CJ_{n,m}m^2a^4$.
Outside $G$, cancel the inverse factors in~\eqref{Hintegral} and apply
Lemma~\ref{cutoff} to the original kernel, whose excluded graph is
$D=B\cup H$. Its maximum degree is at most two, so this contribution is at most
$J_{n,m}e^{-c\log^2n}$. Since $a^4=16\log^8 n/n^2$, we obtain
\eqref{toperror}.
Relabeling $A$ and the matching $B$ leaves~\eqref{bdefinition} unchanged,
so $b_{n,m}$ depends only on $n,m$.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{profile}
Uniformly for $m\le m_*$,
\begin{equation}\label{commonprofile}
 \frac{b_{n,m}}{J_{n,m}}
 =e^{1/2}+O\left(n^{-1/2}+\frac mn+\frac{\log^8 n}{n}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular the error is $O(\varepsilon_n)$, where
\[
 \varepsilon_n=n^{-1/2}\log n+n^{-1}\log^8 n=o(n^{-1/4}).
\]
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
All expectations in this proof are under $\mu_{n,m}$. Recall
$q=\sum_i x_i^2$ and $h=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in B}x_ix_j$. On the cube, define
\[
 T_W=e^{S^2/2}\prod_{\substack{i<j\\\{i,j\}\notin B}}(1-x_ix_j).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{domination}, $0\le T_W\le e^{81/16}$ there. We expand
the logarithm to second order, following Chin and Michelen's proof of
Lemma~17 in Appendix~A~\cite{ChinMichelen}.
On $G$, uniformly for $m\le m_*$,
\begin{equation}\label{expansion}
 \log T_W=f+O(\log^8 n/n),\qquad
 f=q/2+h-q^2/4.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the linear part of the expansion is $q/2+h$, and its quadratic
part is $-q^2/4$ up to diagonal and matching-edge corrections. To bound
these corrections and the cubic remainder, use
\[
 \sum_{i<j}x_i^2x_j^2=\frac12\left(q^2-\sum_i x_i^4\right),
 \quad \sum_i x_i^4\le a^2q,
 \quad \sum_{i<j}(x_ix_j)^3\le\frac12a^2q^2.
\]
The matching-edge square sum is at most $na^4/2$. Also
$q\le aS\le4\log^2 n$ on $G$. The diagonal, matching-edge and cubic
terms are therefore all $O(\log^8 n/n)$, proving~\eqref{expansion}.

We now bound the expected absolute difference between $e^f$ and $e^{1/2}$.
For $m\le m_*$, Lemma~\ref{moments} gives
\[
 \mathbb E q=2+O(m/n+n^{-1}),\qquad
 \mathbb E h=\frac12+O(m/n+n^{-1}),
\]
with variances $O(n^{-1})$. The identity
\[
 f-\frac12=h-\frac12-\frac12(q-2)-\frac14(q-2)^2
\]
therefore implies
\begin{equation}\label{fconcentration}
 \mathbb E\left|f-\frac12\right|
 \le\mathbb E\left|h-\frac12\right|+\frac12\mathbb E|q-2|
                      +\frac14\mathbb E(q-2)^2
 =O(n^{-1/2}+m/n).
\end{equation}
Here Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the mean absolute deviations, and
$(m/n)^2\le m/n$ bounds the quadratic shift.
To compare the exponentials, use $h\le q/2$, since $B$ is a matching.
Thus $f\le q-q^2/4\le1$ for every nonnegative $x$, and the mean value
theorem gives
$|e^f-e^{1/2}|\le e|f-1/2|$ throughout the positive orthant.
On $G$,~\eqref{expansion} also gives
$|T_W-e^f|=O(\log^8 n/n)$. Hence

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{coeff}
The coefficients satisfy $d_0=1$, $d_1=0$, and $d_k<0$ for $k\ge2$.
Moreover,
\begin{gather}
 \sum_{k\ge2}|d_k|=1,\qquad \sum_{k\ge0}|d_k|=2,\label{abscoeff}\\

 0\le v_k\le C(k+1)^{-1/2}.\label{vbound}
\end{gather}

\begin{equation}\label{Abounds}
 A_{n,k}\le e^{-k^2/(4n)},\qquad
 1-A_{n,k}\le\min(1,2k^2/n).
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Differentiation gives
\[
 d'(t)=-\frac t2 e^{t/2}(1-t)^{-1/2},\qquad
 k d_k=-\frac12[t^{k-2}]e^{t/2}(1-t)^{-1/2}\quad(k\ge2).
\]
All coefficients on the right are positive, proving the signs.
Let $c_j=\binom{2j}j/4^j$. Stirling's formula gives
$c_j\sim(\pi j)^{-1/2}$, and hence
\[
 [t^j]e^{t/2}(1-t)^{-1/2}
 =\sum_{r=0}^j\frac{1}{2^r r!}c_{j-r}
 \sim\frac{e^{1/2}}{\sqrt{\pi j}}.
\]
To justify the convolution asymptotic, split the sum at $r=j/2$.
For each fixed $r$, $\sqrt j\,c_{j-r}\to1/\sqrt\pi$; for all
$r\le j/2$, the terms multiplied by $\sqrt j$ are bounded
by $C/(2^r r!)$. The remaining factorial tail is $o(j^{-1/2})$.
Dominated convergence proves the asymptotic, and the same split gives
a uniform $O((j+1)^{-1/2})$ bound.
The derivative identity proves~\eqref{dasympt}.
Consequently $(d_k)$ is absolutely summable, and $d(1^-)=0$ gives
\eqref{abscoeff}.

The series in the exponents defining $v(t)$ and $(1-t)^{-1/2}$ have
nonnegative coefficients, and coefficientwise
\[
 \exp\left(\sum_{j\ge2}\frac{t^j}{2j}\right)
 \preceq\exp\left(\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{t^j}{2j}\right)
 =(1-t)^{-1/2}.
\]
Here $\preceq$ denotes coefficientwise inequality. This proves~\eqref{vbound}.

For the product bounds, set $z_j=(4j+1)/(n+2j+1)$ in~\eqref{ratio}.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{reduction}
With $\varepsilon_n$ as in Proposition~\ref{profile},
\begin{equation}\label{scalar}
 P_n=e^{1/2}\sum_{k=0}^{n/2}d_k A_{n,k}
       +O(\varepsilon_n+n^{-3/4}\log^8 n)
       +O(e^{-c\log^2 n}).
\end{equation}
In particular the total error is $o(n^{-1/4})$.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Recall $m_*$ from Section~\ref{sec:topology} and put $K=m_*/2$.
By Lemma~\ref{domination}, the total absolute contribution to
\eqref{identity} from permutations with more than $2K$ long vertices
is at most
\[
 C\sum_{k>K}^{n/2}v_k A_{n,k}\le e^{-c\log^2 n},
\]
using~\eqref{vbound}--\eqref{Abounds} and
$K\asymp\sqrt n\log n$. Any polynomial factor is absorbed by
decreasing $c>0$.

For $k\le K$, Proposition~\ref{topology} replaces every $p_\pi$ by the
same $b_{n,2k}$. We sum these remainders in absolute value and
bound their total by
\[
 \frac{C\log^8 n}{n^2}
        \sum_{k=0}^{n/2}(2k)^2v_k A_{n,k}
 +e^{-c\log^2 n}\sum_{k=0}^{n/2}v_k A_{n,k}.
\]
The coefficient and product bounds give
\begin{equation}\label{unsignedmoments}
 \sum_{k=0}^{n/2}v_k A_{n,k}=O(n^{1/4}),\qquad
 \sum_{k=0}^{n/2}k^2v_k A_{n,k}=O(n^{5/4}).
\end{equation}
These estimates follow by comparison with
$\sum_{k\ge1}k^{-1/2}e^{-k^2/(4n)}$ and
$\sum_{k\ge1}k^{3/2}e^{-k^2/(4n)}$, respectively.
Thus their total contribution is
$O(n^{-3/4}\log^8 n)+O(e^{-c\log^2 n})$.

Grouping the common terms by $k$ now gives exactly
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^K d_k A_{n,k}\frac{b_{n,2k}}{J_{n,2k}}.
\]
Applying Proposition~\ref{profile} introduces an error of at most
\[

\]
Since $\sum_k|d_k|=2$, this error is $o(n^{-1/4})$.
Restoring the scalar terms $K<k\le n/2$
costs $e^{-c\log^2 n}$ by~\eqref{dasympt}--\eqref{Abounds}.
Combining the estimates proves~\eqref{scalar}.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{scalarlimit}
As $n\to\infty$ through even integers,
\[
 n^{1/4}\sum_{k=0}^{n/2}d_k A_{n,k}
 \longrightarrow \frac{e^{1/2}2^{1/4}\Gamma(3/4)}{\sqrt\pi}.
\]
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Extend $A_{n,k}=0$ for $k>n/2$. Since $d_0=1$, $d_1=0$, and for $k\geq2$, $d_k<0$ with $\sum_{k\geq2} d_k=-1$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{positive}
 \sum_{k=0}^{n/2}d_kA_{n,k}
 =\sum_{k\ge2}|d_k|(1-A_{n,k}).
\end{equation}
Fix $T>0$. Expanding each logarithm in~\eqref{ratio} gives, uniformly
for $k\le T\sqrt n$,
\[
 \log A_{n,k}=-\frac{2k^2-k}{n}+O_T(k^3/n^2).
\]
In particular, $A_{n,\lfloor t\sqrt n\rfloor}\to e^{-2t^2}$, uniformly
for $t$ in compact subsets of $(0,\infty)$.
Together with~\eqref{dasympt}, this yields the Riemann-sum limit on each
interval $\varepsilon\le t\le T$:
\[
 n^{1/4}\!\sum_{\varepsilon\sqrt n\le k\le T\sqrt n}
 |d_k|(1-A_{n,k})
 \longrightarrow\frac{e^{1/2}}{2\sqrt\pi}
 \int_\varepsilon^T t^{-3/2}(1-e^{-2t^2})\,dt.
\]
The bounds~\eqref{dasympt}--\eqref{Abounds} control the sums near zero
and infinity, allowing us to pass from compact intervals to $(0,\infty)$.
Near zero,
\[
 n^{1/4}\sum_{2\le k<\varepsilon\sqrt n}|d_k|(1-A_{n,k})
 \le Cn^{-3/4}\sum_{k<\varepsilon\sqrt n}k^{1/2}
 =O(\varepsilon^{3/2}).
\]
At infinity,
\[
 n^{1/4}\sum_{k>T\sqrt n}|d_k|(1-A_{n,k})
 \le Cn^{1/4}\sum_{k>T\sqrt n}k^{-3/2}
 =O(T^{-1/2}).
\]
The second estimate includes $k>n/2$, where $A_{n,k}=0$.
Both bounds are uniform for sufficiently large $n$. Letting
$\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and then $T\uparrow\infty$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{limitintegral}
 n^{1/4}\sum_{k=0}^{n/2}d_k A_{n,k}
 \longrightarrow\frac{e^{1/2}}{2\sqrt\pi}
       \int_0^\infty t^{-3/2}(1-e^{-2t^2})\,dt.
\end{equation}
The integral converges at both endpoints. Integration by parts gives
\[
 \int_0^\infty t^{-3/2}(1-e^{-2t^2})\,dt
 =8\int_0^\infty t^{1/2}e^{-2t^2}\,dt
 =2^{5/4}\Gamma(3/4).
\]
Substitution in~\eqref{limitintegral} proves the lemma.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{app-interval}
For stable matchings $M,N,L$, we have $L\in[M,N]_{\mathcal G}$ if and only if,
for every participant $i$, the partner $L(i)$ lies in the closed
preference interval between $M(i)$ and $N(i)$.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
In a median graph, $L\in[M,N]_{\mathcal G}$ if and only if
$\operatorname{med}_{\mathcal G}(M,N,L)=L$: in the forward direction $L$ belongs to all three
pairwise intervals, and in the reverse direction the median belongs to
$[M,N]_{\mathcal G}$ by definition. Property~\textup{(\ref{app-fact-partnermedian})}

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{app-edgecycle}
The endpoints of an edge of $\mathcal G$ differ on exactly one simple
alternating cycle of even length at least four.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Let the exchanged dual pair be $\rho,\bar\rho$, where
$\rho=((x_i,y_i))_{i\in\mathbb Z/r\mathbb Z}$.
The sets $X_\rho$ and $Y_\rho$ are disjoint. A participant in their
intersection would belong to the $X$-sets of both $\rho$ and $\bar\rho$.
Property~\textup{(\ref{app-fact-chain})} would make these rotations
comparable, contrary to property~\textup{(\ref{app-fact-duality})}.

Interchange the endpoints if necessary so that
$S_N=(S_M\setminus\{\bar\rho\})\cup\{\rho\}$.
For each $x_i$, the prefix described in
property~\textup{(\ref{app-fact-chain})} gains only the rotation
$\rho$. Consequently,
\[
 M(x_i)=y_i,\qquad N(x_i)=y_{i+1}.
\]
Participants outside $X_\rho\cup Y_\rho$ have unchanged prefixes and
hence unchanged partners. The displayed pairs form one alternating cycle
on $2r$ distinct participants. We have $r\ge2$, since the first and
second choices $y_i,y_{i+1}$ are distinct.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{app-restriction}
For stable matchings $M,N$, let $b_i,w_i$ be the better and worse of
$M(i),N(i)$. Retain a pair $\{i,j\}$ precisely when each endpoint ranks
the other within the closed preference interval between its partners in
$M$ and $N$. Any perfect matching that is stable in the restricted
instance is stable in the original instance.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Consider a deleted pair. If one endpoint ranks the other below the worse
of its partners in $M$ and $N$, it cannot prefer that pair to any retained
partner.
Otherwise, deletion means that some endpoint, say $i$, ranks $j$ above
$b_i$. Then $i$ prefers $j$ to both $M(i)$ and $N(i)$. Stability of $M$
and $N$ forces $j$ to prefer both $M(j)$ and $N(j)$ to $i$. Thus $j$
ranks $i$ below $w_j$, reducing to the first case. No deleted pair can
block a perfect matching using retained pairs.
<!-- end proof-13 -->
<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{app-mergededge}
Merge the alternating cycle of adjacent matchings $M,N$ into a long
cycle, with the worse partner as successor, and retain their common
pairs as transpositions. The resulting permutation is stable.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
Let $C$ be the alternating cycle, of length $2r$. Partition its vertices
into $X$, those preferring their $M$-partner, and $Y$, those preferring
their $N$-partner. Every $M$-edge has an endpoint in $Y$, since otherwise
it would block $N$; hence $|Y|\ge r$. Similarly every $N$-edge has an
endpoint in $X$, so $|X|\ge r$. Since $|X|+|Y|=2r$, both inequalities
are equalities and every cycle edge joins $X$ to $Y$. The assignments
\[
 \pi(x)=N(x)\quad(x\in X),\qquad
 \pi(y)=M(y)\quad(y\in Y)
\]
therefore orient $C$ consistently, with worse partner as successor and
better partner as predecessor. This proves~\eqref{app-comparison}.

Suppose that a pair blocks $\pi$. If one endpoint is outside $C$, its
partner is common to $M,N$; choosing the matching that gives the other
participant its worse partner would give a blocking pair for that
matching. A cycle edge also cannot block, because one endpoint
has the other as its successor. Thus a blocker must be a chord of $C$.
At each endpoint the chord lies strictly between its two cycle partners.
Indeed, if an endpoint ranked the chord above both partners, stability
of $M,N$ would force the other endpoint to rank it below both partners.
The chord joins $X$ to $Y$: a chord within $X$ would block $N$, and one
within $Y$ would block $M$.

Apply the restriction in Lemma~\ref{app-restriction}. Outside $C$ only
the common pairs remain. Within $C$, every retained pair joins $X$ to
$Y$, since a pair within $X$ would block $N$, and a pair within $Y$ would
block $M$. The restricted problem on $C$ is therefore
bipartite. Each $x\in X$ has $M(x)$ first and $N(x)$ last among retained
choices; each $y\in Y$ has $N(y)$ first and $M(y)$ last. The assumed
blocking chord is a retained intermediate choice.

For $x\in X$, let $q(x)$ be its second retained choice and define
\[
 \sigma(x)=M^{-1}(q(x))\in X.
\]
There are at least two retained choices, and $q(x)\ne M(x)$, so
$\sigma$ has no fixed points. A map of a finite set to itself has a
directed cycle. Choose one, $x_1,\ldots,x_t$, so that
$q(x_i)=M(x_{i+1})$, with cyclic indices. Replace the $M$-pairs on these
vertices by $x_i\leftrightarrow q(x_i)$ and keep every other $M$-pair.
Call the resulting perfect matching $L$.

To check stability in the restricted instance, consider an endpoint in
$X$. If unchanged, it retains its first choice. If changed, it has its
second choice and prefers only its former partner $M(x_i)$. That former
partner is also rematched along the chosen cycle and strictly prefers
its new partner to $x_i$, its last choice among the retained partners. No retained
pair can therefore block $L$.

The chosen cycle changes partners, so $L\ne M$. It also differs from $N$:
the map $x\mapsto M^{-1}(N(x))$ consists of one directed cycle on all of $X$,
because the restriction of $M\cup N$ to the vertices of $C$ is the
single alternating cycle $C$. The intermediate
chord ensures $q(x)\ne N(x)$ for at least one $x$. If the chosen cycle
of $\sigma$ used only $N$-edges, it would have to be that full cycle,
contradicting the existence of the intermediate choice. Thus $L$ uses a
chord and cannot equal $N$.

By Lemma~\ref{app-restriction}, $L$ is stable in the original instance.
Each participant's partner in $L$ lies between its partners in $M,N$, so
Lemma~\ref{app-interval} places $L$ in $[M,N]_{\mathcal G}$. The interval of
an edge contains only its two endpoints. This contradiction rules out
the blocker and proves~\eqref{app-blocking}.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{app-disjoint}
Distinct coordinate directions of a cube affect disjoint participant
sets. The partner changes along parallel edges are identical.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
Consider a square with corners $A,B,C,D$, where $A$ is adjacent to $B,C$
and $D$ is opposite $A$. Their coordinatewise medians give
\[
 \operatorname{med}_{\mathcal G}(A,B,C)=A,\qquad
 \operatorname{med}_{\mathcal G}(A,B,D)=B,\qquad
 \operatorname{med}_{\mathcal G}(A,C,D)=C.
\]
Suppose participant $i$ changes partner on both $AB$ and $AC$.
Write $r_i(P)$ for the rank of $P(i)$, with smaller ranks preferred.
By the partner-median property, the first equality puts $r_i(A)$
strictly between $r_i(B)$ and $r_i(C)$. Interchanging $B,C$ if necessary,
\[
 r_i(B)<r_i(A)<r_i(C).
\]
The second and third median equalities force, respectively,
$r_i(D)\le r_i(B)$ and $r_i(D)\ge r_i(C)$, a contradiction.

Thus incident directions have disjoint supports. If $i$ changes on $AB$,
disjointness at $A$ gives $C(i)=A(i)$, and disjointness at $B$ gives
$D(i)=B(i)$. Conversely, apply the same argument starting with $CD$.
The parallel edges $AB,CD$ consequently have the same support and the
same partner changes. Any two parallel edges of a cube are connected by
a sequence of such squares, so this conclusion holds throughout the
cube. All directions can now be compared at one corner, where their
supports are pairwise disjoint.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{app-bijection}
There is a bijection between $k$-cubes of $\mathcal G$ and stable all-even
permutations with $k$ long cycles. The cube vertices are exactly the
matchings obtained by choosing either alternating matching on each long
cycle and retaining all transpositions.
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
Start with a $k$-cube. Lemmas~\ref{app-edgecycle} and~\ref{app-disjoint}
give $k$ disjoint, fixed alternating cycles, with common matching pairs
elsewhere. Orient each cycle as in Lemma~\ref{app-mergededge}, retaining
the fixed pairs as transpositions, and call the resulting permutation
$\pi$. It satisfies~\eqref{app-comparison}. A blocker whose endpoints
lie on one cycle is excluded by Lemma~\ref{app-mergededge} applied to
an edge in that direction. For endpoints on different cycles, choose
the cube corner that gives each its worse partner; a blocker of $\pi$
would block that stable matching. The same choice works when one or
both participants have fixed partners. Thus $\pi$ is stable and has
$k$ long cycles.

Conversely, let $\pi$ be stable with $k$ long even cycles. Each choice of
alternating matchings on its long cycles gives a stable matching, since
every assigned partner is at least as good as the successor in $\pi$.
Any blocking pair would therefore violate~\eqref{app-blocking}. There
are $2^k$ distinct such \emph{resolutions}.

Choose opposite resolutions $M,N$, using opposite alternating matchings
on every long cycle. Every resolution belongs to $[M,N]_{\mathcal G}$ by
Lemma~\ref{app-interval}. Conversely, consider a stable matching $L$ in
that interval. If $L$ used an edge outside the cycle edges and fixed
pairs of $\pi$, its endpoints would both strictly prefer that edge to
their worse cyclic neighbor: the interval condition places each
endpoint's partner between its two cyclic neighbors, and that partner
equals neither. This contradicts stability of $\pi$. Thus $L$ uses only
cycle edges and fixed pairs. A perfect matching on an even cycle must
be one of its two alternating matchings,
so $L$ is one of the $2^k$ resolutions. These resolutions therefore
constitute the whole interval $[M,N]_{\mathcal G}$.

For two resolutions differing on exactly one cycle, the same argument
shows that their interval has only two vertices. They are therefore
adjacent. Resolutions differing on more than one cycle are not adjacent,
by Lemma~\ref{app-edgecycle}. Hence the subgraph on the resolutions is
the abstract $k$-dimensional cube.

It is also a coordinate face of the encoding hypercube. At one corner,
its $k$ incident edges change $k$ distinct coordinates. In a square of
a hypercube, opposite edges change the same coordinate: the two length-two
paths to the opposite corner must change the same pair of coordinates.
Following opposite edges through the cube's squares identifies one
encoding coordinate for each direction. Its vertices therefore
give all $2^k$ choices of those coordinates, with the others fixed.

Finally, these constructions are inverse. The cube's matchings recover
all fixed pairs and all edges of its long cycles. Strict preferences
uniquely determine the orientation in which the worse partner is the
successor. The case $k=0$ simply identifies a stable matching with its
transposition permutation.

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{app-Euler}
The alternating sum of all coordinate cubes of a nonempty finite median
graph with an isometric Hamming encoding is $1$.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
Choose a root and complement encoding coordinates so that the root is
$0$. By isometry, distance from the root equals Hamming weight.
For a vertex $v$, let $D_-(v)$ be the coordinates changed by its neighbors
closer to the root, and put $d_-(v)=|D_-(v)|$. These coordinates all
have value one at $v$.

The coordinates in any subset $J\subseteq D_-(v)$ can be set to zero
while staying in the graph. Write $v-J$ for the resulting encoding. These encodings exist for
$|J|\le1$. Inductively, if $v-J$ exists and $j\in D_-(v)\setminus J$,
the coordinatewise median of $v-J$, $v-\{j\}$, and the root is
$v-(J\cup\{j\})$. The graph median lies in all three pairwise graph
intervals; isometry puts it in the corresponding hypercube intervals,
whose intersection is exactly
the coordinatewise median. Thus all these encodings occur in the graph,
and every choice of $k$ downward directions spans a coordinate $k$-cube.

Each cube has a unique corner farthest from the root, namely the one
with value one in all its free coordinates. The $k$-cubes assigned to
$v$ are consequently counted by $\binom{d_-(v)}k$. Summing their signs,
\[
 \sum_{Q\text{ a cube}}(-1)^{\dim Q}
 =\sum_v\sum_{k=0}^{d_-(v)}(-1)^k\binom{d_-(v)}k
 =\sum_v(1-1)^{d_-(v)}=1.
\]
Every vertex other than the root has a closer neighbor on a shortest
path to the root. Thus only the root has $d_-(v)=0$, and its inner sum
is the single $k=0$ term, equal to $1$.
<!-- end proof-17 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
If $I$ is solvable, Proposition~\ref{app-bijection} identifies the
signed permutation sum with the signed cube sum of the nonempty graph
$\mathcal G$. Lemma~\ref{app-Euler} makes that sum equal to $1$.
If $I$ is unsolvable, there is no stable all-even permutation: resolving
its even cycles would give a stable matching, by the argument in
Proposition~\ref{app-bijection}. Every term in the permutation sum is
therefore zero. This proves the pointwise identity in both cases.
Taking expectations of the finite sum proves~\eqref{identity}.
