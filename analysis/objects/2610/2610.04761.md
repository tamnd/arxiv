<!-- abstract-1: body_md -->
aussian elimination with partial pivoting is the standard
algorithm for solving a dense $n\times n$ system of linear
equations $Ax=b$.  The ``growth factor'' $\rho\,$ for this
process may be as large as $2^{n-1}$, and when $\rho$ is
large, the algorithm is unstable.  Nevertheless, long
experience has shown that Gaussian elimination is resoundingly
stable in practice.  Experiments
indicate that among random matrices with independent normally
distributed entries, growth factors $\rho \gg n^{1/2}$ are
in a precise sense exponentially rare.
Here we take a step toward confirming this observation by proving
it not for the full growth factor, which depends
on all the entries of the upper-triangular factor $U$, but for the 
corner entry $u_{nn}^{}$.
<!-- end abstract-1 -->
<!-- keywords-1: body_md -->
aussian elimination, growth factor, stability

<!-- head-1: body_md -->
A {\em random $n$-vector\/} is an $n$-vector
whose entries are independent samples from the standard normal
distribution $N(0,1)$.  A {\em random\ $n\times n$ matrix\/}
is an $n\times n$ matrix whose entries are independent samples
from this distribution.
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- head-2: body_md -->
Let $f$ and $g$ be quantities associated
with random $n\times n$ matrices
for all sufficiently large $n$.  We say that it is\/
{\em exponentially unlikely that $f\gg g$},
or in words, {\em exponentially unlikely that $f$ is
much greater than $g$}, if for any $\alpha >0$ and any $M>0$,
\begin{equation}
\hbox{\rm probability} \left( |f(n)| > |g(n)| n^\alpha \right) < n^{-M}
\label{highly}
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $n$.
<!-- end head-2 -->
<!-- head-3: body_md -->
GE produces a factorization $P\kern -.9pt A = L\kern .8pt U$, where
each subdiagonal entry of $L$ is no greater than $1$.
By induction, it can be shown that this implies that the entries in
columns $n, n-1, n-2, \dots$ of $L^{-1}$ are no
greater than $1, 1, 2, 4, 8, \dots.$

\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\vskip .3cm
\includegraphics[scale=.65]{thm1.eps}
\end{center}
\caption{\label{fig-thm1}Sketch of the key step of the proof of
Theorem\/~$1$.  The last row of $L^{-1}$, $m$, is a random\/
$n$-vector\/ $w$, ``diabolically pivoted,'' then normalized to have
last entry $1$, and its final entries are bounded
by $1,1,2,4,8,\dots.$  If the largest entry of\/ $m$ is of
size $n\kern .4pt \varepsilon^{-1}$ with $\varepsilon \ll 1$,
such a configuration
requires the vector $w$ to have $O(\log \varepsilon)$ entries
of size $\ll \varepsilon n^{-1}$, which is
exponentially unlikely.}
\end{figure}

Let $m$ denote the last row of $L^{-1}$.  The equation $P\kern -.9pt A=L\kern .8pt U$
implies $U = L^{-1} P\kern -.9pt A$.  Thus $u_{nn}^{}$ is the product
of $m$ with the last column of $P\kern -.9pt A$, which consists of $n$
independent samples from $N(0,1)$, unaffected by pivoting since
the elimination process has not made use of data from this
column.
This implies that $u_{nn}^{}$ is a normally distributed
random variable of mean zero whose standard deviation is no greater
than $n^{1/2} \max_j |m_j|$, and therefore it
is exponentially unlikely that $u_{nn}^{}$ exceeds 
$\max_j |m_j|$ by much more than a factor of $n^{1/2}$.  Thus to
prove Theorem 1, it is enough to show that it is exponentially
unlikely that any entry of $m$ is much greater than~$n$.

In the remainder of the argument, we make no careful analysis
of pivoting.  On the contrary, we assume that matrices may be
``diabolically pivoted,'' that is, we derive probabilistic
bounds that apply even in the worst case in which the pivoting
happens to be as adverse as possible.  Nevertheless, pivoting
enters the analysis in a crucial way in implying that the final
entries of $m$ are no greater than $1,1,2,4,8,\dots.$

Now since the last row of $U$ is zero except in the $(n,n)$
position, $m$ is orthogonal to the first $n-1$ columns of $P\kern -.9pt A$.
These columns span a random $(n-1)$-dimensional space, apart
from the pivoting.  Therefore $m$, being orthogonal to these,
is a permuted and then normalized random $n$-vector.\footnote{For
Gaussian elimination without pivoting, $m$ is not permuted---it
is simply $m = w/w(n)$, where $w$ is a random vector.  This
observation immediately makes probabilistic analysis of the
entries of $L^{-1}$ and consequently $U$ straightforward for
elimination without pivoting.} We can describe its structure
like this:

\medskip 

\begin{enumerate}
\par\hangindent 40\p@
Let $w$ be a random $n$-vector, with entries arbitrarily permuted;
\par\hangindent 40\p@ 
$m = w/w(n)$.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip 

If $w$ is a random $n$-vector, its smallest entries scale as
$n^{-1}$ and its largest as $1$.  The probability that
$w$ has an entry of size $\varepsilon\kern .4pt n^{-1}$ is $O(\varepsilon)$
as $\varepsilon \to 0\kern .7pt $; the probability that
it has two such entries
is $O(\varepsilon^2)$, and so on.  (For complex
vectors, these probabilities apply
to entries of size $\varepsilon^{1/2}n^{-1/2}$.)
For its pivoted normalized
vector $m$ to have an entry greater
than $n\kern .4pt \varepsilon^{-1}$ with
$\varepsilon \ll 1$, $w$ would
need to have $O(\log \varepsilon)$ such small entries because of
the $1,1,2,4,8,\dots$ constraint.  To be specific, suppose
we define
\begin{equation}
\mu = \hbox{\rm round}({\textstyle{1\over 2}}|\log_2 \varepsilon\kern .5pt |).
\label{mudef}
\end{equation}
Then $w$ would have to have at least $\mu$ entries smaller
than $\varepsilon^{1/2} n^{-1}$, and for $\varepsilon\ll 1$, this
is exponentially unlikely.\footnote{Discussion
with a reader suggests the following analogy.
Suppose somebody has tossed 100 coins
and ordered the results in a diabolical fashion you know
nothing about.  If the first 50 results are all heads, this cannot
count as surprising, because it could be due to
the ordering.  If the first 75 results are all heads, however,
that is surprising indeed, for even diabolical ordering would be
unlikely to be able to achieve that with 100 coin tosses.}~~\hbox{\vrule width 3.5pt depth 5 pt height 2.5 pt}

We repeat the argument of Theorem 1, but now, we improve the
bound on the entries of $m$, the final row of $L^{-1}$, from $n$
to $1$.  Specifically, to establish Theorem~2, it is enough to
prove---as seems clear in numerical experiments---that it is
exponentially unlikely that any entry of $m$ is much greater
than~$1$.

Suppose $m$ contains an entry of size $\varepsilon^{-1}$ for
some $\varepsilon \ll 1$.  The key idea is to consider the
configuration $\mu$ steps before the end of elimination, with
$\mu$ defined by (\ref{mudef}).  At this stage, the elimination
has not yet made use of data from the last $\mu$ columns of $A$.
Therefore, we can apply a random orthogonal operation to these
last $\mu$ columns without changing the probability distribution.
From this point on, data from these columns will begin to be
brought in, and further row interchanges will take place, but
all these row interchanges will be confined to the final $\mu$
rows of~$A$.

This enables us to set down a sharper characterization of $m$
than before, sketched in Figure~\ref{fig-thm2}.  Note that like
the vector $w$ of the last section, the matrix $W$ defined in
line 1 below is not one that appears explicitly during GE.\ \
Rather, it is an artifice that spans subspaces of
${\mathbb R}^n$ with the appropriate probability distribution.
Line 2 corresponds to applying a random orthogonal operation
to the $\mu$-dimensional space still in play, before selecting
a particular representative vector.

\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\vskip .1cm
\includegraphics[scale=.65]{thm2.eps}
\end{center}
\caption{\label{fig-thm2}Sharpening of the
key step of the proof of Theorem\/~$1$ to establish
Theorem\/~$2$.  At a stage $\mu = O(\log \varepsilon)$
steps from the end of elimination, we know that all further pivoting
will affect only the final $\mu$ columns of $L^{-1}$.  Therefore,
for the final vector $m$ to have a reasonable chance of
having an entry much larger
than $1$, an entire $\mu\times \mu$
block of a diabolically pivoted random $\mu\times n$ matrix would have
to be small or at least close to low
rank.  This is exponentially unlikely.}
\end{figure}

\medskip

\begin{enumerate}
\par\hangindent 40\p@
Let $W$ be a random $\mu\times n$ matrix, with columns arbitrarily permuted;
\par\hangindent 40\p@ 
Let $w_0 = q^TW$, where $q$ is a random $\mu$-vector normalized
to $\|q\|=1$;
\par\hangindent 40\p@
Let $w$ be a further arbitrary permutation of $w_0$, in its
final $\mu$ entries only;
\par\hangindent 40\p@
$m = w/w(n)$.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip

\noindent
As before, $m$ has the additional property that its final
entries are bounded by $1,1,2,$ $4,8,\dots .$ Specifically,
if $\mu$ is defined by (\ref{mudef}), the final $\mu$
entries of $m$ must all be bounded by $\varepsilon^{-1/2}$.
Now this condition is unaffected by step 3 in the list above,
a permutation of just the final $\mu$ entries of $w_0$.  We are
thus done if we can prove the following assertion extracted from
steps 1, 2, and 4: if $W$ is a random $\mu\times n$ matrix,
it is exponentially unlikely that $W$ has a $\mu\times \mu$
submatrix $W_\mu$ (i.e., a selection of some $\mu$ distinct
columns of $W$) with the property that $q^TW_\mu$, where $q$
is a random $\mu$-vector normalized by $\|q\|=1$, has all its
entries smaller than $\varepsilon^{1/2}$ for $\varepsilon \ll 1$.

Intuitively speaking, here is why such a configuration is
exponentially unlikely.  Though $W$ has some small entries (of
order $n^{-1}$), it is exponentially unlikely to have any small
{\em columns,} i.e., columns all of whose entries are small.
So any matrix $W_\mu$ will consist of columns that are not small.
It is algebraically unlikely that the inner product of a fixed
random $q$ with such a column is small, and one would accordingly
expect it to be exponentially unlikely that its inner product
with all $\mu$ of the columns is small.  This last expectation,
however, cannot immediately be justified, since the columns of
$W_\mu$ are not independent due to the pivoting.

To make a rigorous argument, we proceed as follows.
Suppose $\mu/2$ or more of the singular values of $W_\mu$
are greater than, say, $\varepsilon^{1/3}$.  Then it is
exponentially unlikely that $q^T W_\mu$ has all entries
${\le}\, \varepsilon^{1/2}$, since this would require $\mu/2$
independent random inner products of $q$ with a left singular
vector of $W_\mu$ each to be of order $\varepsilon^{1/6}$,
which is ${\ll}\,1$.  So we may assume that $\mu/2$ of the
singular values of $W_\mu$ are ${\le}\,\varepsilon^{1/3}$.
But it is exponentially unlikely that a random $\mu\times n$
matrix has such a $\mu\times \mu$ submatrix.  This is established
in Lemma~1 of the Appendix.~~\hbox{\vrule width 3.5pt depth 5 pt height 2.5 pt}

<!-- head-5: body_md -->
The total number of $\mu\times \mu$ submatrices of $A$ is
${n\choose \mu}$, which is typically about
$(e\kern .4pt n/\mu)^\mu$ and 
can be shown to be always ${\le}\,(3n/\mu)^\mu$.
Rather than needing a finer analysis taking into account
dependencies of the columns of $A$, it is accordingly enough for
us to show that among $(3n/\mu)^\mu$ random
$\mu\times \mu$
matrices, it is exponentially unlikely that there will be
one with $\mu/2$ or more singular values ${\le}\,\varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon \ll 1$.  (We are employing what is
known as a union bound.)
More precisely, following the definition
(\ref{highly}), it is enough to show that for any $\alpha
>0$ and any $M>0$, with $\varepsilon = n^{-\alpha}$,
for a $\mu\times \mu$ random matrix with $\mu\sim C|\log \varepsilon|$,
the probability that $\mu/2$ or more of its singular values are
${\le}\, \varepsilon$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
p < n^{-M} / (3n/\mu)^\mu \quad \hbox{for all sufficiently large $n$}.
\label{probbound1}
\end{equation}
Since $\mu\to\infty$ as $n\to\infty$, a tighter condition
that it is accordingly sufficient to establish is
\begin{equation}
p < n^{-\mu}  = \varepsilon^{\mu/\alpha}\quad \hbox{for all sufficiently large $n$}.
\label{probbound2}
\end{equation}
Now in fact, by Theorem~1.2 of~\cite{sza}, the probability $p$
satisfies
\begin{equation}
p < C \varepsilon^{\mu^2/4}
\label{probbound3}
\end{equation}
for a universal constant $C$, and this implies 
(\ref{probbound2}).~~\hbox{\vrule width 3.5pt depth 5 pt height 2.5 pt}
