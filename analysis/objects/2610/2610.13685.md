<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Put $A\triangleq P_{X|Y}$ and let $\Delta_{\mathcal Y}$ denote the probability simplex on $\mathcal Y$.  A release mechanism can be described by its output probabilities $p_u\triangleq P_U(u)$ and its posterior vectors $q_u\triangleq P_{Y|U=u}$.  Bayes' rule and the law of total probability give
\begin{align}
q_u&\in\Delta_{\mathcal Y},\notag\\
\sum_up_uq_u&=P_Y,\notag\\
P_{X|U=u}&=Aq_u.                                           \label{posterior-mixture}
\end{align}
Conversely, because $P_Y$ has full support, any finite collection satisfying \eqref{posterior-mixture} induces the channel
\begin{align}
P_{U|Y}(u|y)=\frac{p_uq_u(y)}{P_Y(y)}.                     \label{bayes-channel}
\end{align}
The entries in \eqref{bayes-channel} are nonnegative, and the barycenter identity in \eqref{posterior-mixture} shows that they sum to one for every $y$.

Define the exact feasible-posterior set
\begin{align*}
\mathcal Q_\epsilon
\triangleq\left\{q\in\Delta_{\mathcal Y}:
\sum_xP_X(x)\left(\frac{(Aq)(x)}{P_X(x)}\right)^\alpha
\leq e^{\alpha\epsilon}\right\}.
\end{align*}
It is a closed subset of the compact simplex and is therefore compact.  The objective contribution
\begin{align*}
g(q)\triangleq\sum_y\frac{(q(y)-P_Y(y))^2}{P_Y(y)}
\end{align*}
is continuous. To prove attainment and the cardinality bound without assuming finite support, write $n=|\mathcal Y|$ and consider the compact graph
\begin{align*}
\mathcal S=\{(q(1),\ldots,q(n-1),g(q)):q\in\mathcal Q_\epsilon\}
\subset\mathbb R^n.
\end{align*}
Carath\'eodory's theorem says that every point of $\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S)$ is a convex combination of at most $n+1$ graph points. In particular, this convex hull is compact: it is the continuous image of the compact set of $n+1$ graph points and their simplex of mixing weights. Any posterior distribution, including one with infinitely many outputs, has its vector of averaged coordinates and utility in this closed convex hull. Conversely, every such convex combination with barycenter $P_Y$ reconstructs a finite channel by \eqref{bayes-channel}.

The section of $\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S)$ whose first $n-1$ coordinates equal those of $P_Y$ is nonempty, since $P_Y\in\mathcal Q_\epsilon$, and compact. Its last coordinate therefore has a maximum. Carath\'eodory's theorem gives an attaining channel with at most $n+1$ outputs.

We can reduce this support to $n$. For any finite optimum with $m>n$ positive weights $p_i$, the vectors $(1,q_i(1),\ldots,q_i(n-1))$ are linearly dependent. Hence some nonzero coefficients $a_i$ satisfy
\begin{align*}
\sum_i a_i=0,\qquad \sum_i a_iq_i=0.
\end{align*}
Both $p_i+ta_i$ and $p_i-ta_i$ remain nonnegative for small $t>0$ and preserve the barycenter. Optimality then forces $\sum_i a_i g(q_i)=0$; otherwise one sign would increase the utility. Since the $a_i$ have both signs, increasing $t$ in $p_i+ta_i$ up to
$\min_{i:a_i<0}p_i/(-a_i)$ removes at least one positive weight while preserving utility and feasibility. Repeating yields an optimum with at most $n$ outputs. This argument uses only full support of $P_Y$ and compactness, so it also applies to arbitrary leakage matrices.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:uniform}
Fix $\rho\in(0,1)$ and suppose $\|h_u\|_\infty\leq\rho$. Define
\begin{align*}
A_\alpha&\triangleq\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}2,\
M_{\alpha,\rho}\triangleq
\max\{(1-\rho)^{\alpha-3},(1+\rho)^{\alpha-3}\},\\
K_{\alpha,\rho}&\triangleq
\frac{|\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)|}{6}M_{\alpha,\rho}.
\end{align*}
Then, we have
\begin{align}
\bigl(\mathcal L^\alpha(X\!\to\!u)\bigr)^\alpha
&=1+A_\alpha r_u^2+R_u,\nonumber\\
|R_u|&\leq K_{\alpha,\rho}\|h_u\|_\infty r_u^2.\label{uniform}
\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Fix $u$ and abbreviate $h=h_u$.  By \eqref{likelihood-coordinate},
\begin{align}
\bigl(\mathcal L^\alpha(X\!\to\!u)\bigr)^\alpha
=\sum_xP_X(x)(1+h(x))^\alpha.                            \label{lift-h}
\end{align}
Because $\|h\|_\infty\leq\rho<1$, all arguments $1+h(x)$ are strictly positive.  For $f(t)=(1+t)^\alpha$, Taylor's theorem at $t=0$ gives, for every $x$,
\begin{align}
f(h(x))
=1+\alpha h(x)+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}2h(x)^2
+\frac{f^{(3)}(\xi_x)}6h(x)^3,                           \label{pointwise-taylor}
\end{align}
where $\xi_x$ lies between $0$ and $h(x)$.  The third derivative is
\begin{align*}
f^{(3)}(t)=\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)(1+t)^{\alpha-3}.
\end{align*}
Since $|\xi_x|\leq\rho$,
\begin{align}
|f^{(3)}(\xi_x)|
&\leq |\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)|
\max_{t\in[-\rho,\rho]}(1+t)^{\alpha-3}\notag\\
&=6K_{\alpha,\rho}.                                      \label{third-derivative-bound}
\end{align}
Substituting \eqref{pointwise-taylor} in \eqref{lift-h}, the constant terms sum to one and the linear terms vanish by \eqref{weighted-moments}.  The quadratic terms equal
$A_\alpha\sum_xP_X(x)h(x)^2=A_\alpha r_u^2$.  If $R_u$ denotes the sum of the third-order remainders, then \eqref{third-derivative-bound} yields
\begin{align*}
|R_u|
&\leq K_{\alpha,\rho}\sum_xP_X(x)|h(x)|^3\\
&\leq K_{\alpha,\rho}\|h\|_\infty
      \sum_xP_X(x)h(x)^2\\
&=K_{\alpha,\rho}\|h_u\|_\infty r_u^2.
\end{align*}
This proves \eqref{uniform}.  Importantly, the constants depend only on $\alpha$ and the fixed neighborhood radius $\rho$; they do not depend on $u$, $\epsilon$, or the direction of the perturbation.  The estimate is therefore uniform over any optimizing sequence $h_u=h_u(\epsilon)$ that stays inside the prescribed neighborhood.  For $\alpha=2$, $f^{(3)}\equiv0$, so $K_{2,\rho}=0$ and the expansion is exact.

\label{lem:coordinates}
For every $u$,
\begin{align}
P_{Y|U=u}-P_Y
&=P_{X|Y}^{-1}D_X^{1/2}Z_u,                              \label{posterior-inverse}\\
\chi^2(P_{Y|U=u}\|P_Y)&=\|WZ_u\|_2^2.                  \label{utility-coordinate}
\end{align}

\begin{align}
W\sqrt{P_X}=\sqrt{P_Y},\qquad
W^T\sqrt{P_Y}=\sqrt{P_X},                               \label{prior-singular-pair}
\end{align}

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The Markov chain gives $P_{X|U=u}=P_{X|Y}P_{Y|U=u}$, while $P_X=P_{X|Y}P_Y$.  Subtracting these identities and using invertibility gives
\begin{align*}
P_{Y|U=u}-P_Y
&=P_{X|Y}^{-1}(P_{X|U=u}-P_X)\\
&=P_{X|Y}^{-1}D_X^{1/2}Z_u,
\end{align*}
which is \eqref{posterior-inverse}.  Consequently,
\begin{align*}
\chi^2(P_{Y|U=u}\|P_Y)
&=(P_{Y|U=u}-P_Y)^TD_Y^{-1}(P_{Y|U=u}-P_Y)\\
&=\|D_Y^{-1/2}P_{X|Y}^{-1}D_X^{1/2}Z_u\|_2^2
=\|WZ_u\|_2^2.
\end{align*}

For the first identity in \eqref{prior-singular-pair}, use
$D_X^{1/2}\sqrt{P_X}=P_X$, $P_{X|Y}^{-1}P_X=P_Y$, and
$D_Y^{-1/2}P_Y=\sqrt{P_Y}$.  For the second, column stochasticity gives
$P_{X|Y}^T\bm1=\bm1$, hence $P_{X|Y}^{-T}\bm1=\bm1$, and therefore
\begin{align*}
W^T\sqrt{P_Y}
=D_X^{1/2}P_{X|Y}^{-T}D_Y^{-1/2}\sqrt{P_Y}
=D_X^{1/2}\bm1=\sqrt{P_X}.
\end{align*}
Thus, if $z\perp\sqrt{P_X}$, then
$\langle Wz,\sqrt{P_Y}\rangle
=\langle z,W^T\sqrt{P_Y}\rangle=0$.

Finally, let $B\triangleq D_X^{-1/2}P_{X|Y}D_Y^{1/2}=W^{-1}$.  For every $a\perp\sqrt{P_Y}$, the vector $q=P_Y+D_Y^{1/2}ta$ is a probability distribution for sufficiently small $|t|$.  For completeness, put $a_y=a(y)$ and $A=P_{X|Y}$. Since $P_X(x)=\sum_y A_{xy}P_Y(y)$, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\begin{align*}
\|Ba\|_2^2
&=\sum_x\frac{\bigl(\sum_y A_{xy}\sqrt{P_Y(y)}a_y\bigr)^2}{P_X(x)}\\
&\leq\sum_{x,y}A_{xy}a_y^2=\sum_y a_y^2,
\end{align*}
where the last identity uses column stochasticity. Thus $\|Ba\|_2\leq\|a\|_2$ directly.  Hence every singular value of $B$ on the centered subspace is at most one, and every singular value of $W$ on the corresponding centered subspace is at least one.  Equation \eqref{prior-singular-pair} supplies a singular value equal to one in the prior direction.  Therefore the largest singular value of $W$ is attained on the centered subspace (with a centered maximizer also available in the tied case).

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:feasible}
Fix an auxiliary radius $\rho\in(0,1)$. Suppose $0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{\max}(\rho)$ and define
\begin{align}
r_-^2(\epsilon;\rho)\triangleq
\frac{b_\epsilon}{A_\alpha+K_{\alpha,\rho}\delta_0(\epsilon)}.\label{saferadius}
\end{align}
The binary mechanism with $P_U(u_1)=P_U(u_2)=1/2$ and
\begin{align}
P_{X|U=u_{1,2}}&=P_X\pm r_-D_X^{1/2}v^\star,\label{safex}\\
P_{Y|U=u_{1,2}}&=P_Y\pm r_-d^\star\label{safey}
\end{align}
is a valid channel and satisfies the original $\alpha$-lift constraint exactly. If $V_{\chi^2}(\epsilon,\alpha)$ denotes the optimum of \eqref{problem}, then
\begin{align}
\frac{b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)}{A_\alpha+K_{\alpha,\rho}\delta_0}
\leq V_{\chi^2}(\epsilon,\alpha)
\leq\frac{b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)}{A_\alpha-K_{\alpha,\rho}\bar\delta_\epsilon}.
\label{sandwich}
\end{align}
Consequently, with
\begin{align*}
G_-\triangleq
\frac{K_{\alpha,\rho}\delta_0}
{A_\alpha(A_\alpha+K_{\alpha,\rho}\delta_0)},\
G_+\triangleq
\frac{K_{\alpha,\rho}\bar\delta_\epsilon}
{A_\alpha(A_\alpha-K_{\alpha,\rho}\bar\delta_\epsilon)},
\end{align*}
we have
\begin{align}
|V_{\chi^2}-V_0|\leq b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)
\max\{G_-,G_+\}.\label{gap}
\end{align}
For fixed $P_{XY},\alpha,\rho$, the absolute gap is $O(\epsilon^{3/2})$ and $|V_{\chi^2}-V_0|/V_0=O(\sqrt\epsilon)$ as $\epsilon\downarrow0$.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
We divide the argument into five steps.

\emph{1) Consequences of the explicit budget range.}
The inequality $\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{\rm dir}$ gives
\begin{align*}
b_\epsilon=e^{\alpha\epsilon}-1\leq A_\alpha m_\rho^2,
\end{align*}
and hence
\begin{align}
r_0(\epsilon)&\leq m_\rho
\leq\frac{\rho}{\kappa_\star},\notag\\
r_0(\epsilon)&\leq r_Y^\star.                             \label{direction-range-proof}
\end{align}
Thus $\delta_0=\kappa_\star r_0\leq\rho$.  Next consider the remainder range.  If $\alpha\geq2$, then
\begin{align*}
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{\rm rem}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
e^{\alpha\epsilon/(\alpha-1)}-1\leq p_{\min}c_\rho^2,
\end{align*}
so $\bar\delta_\epsilon\leq c_\rho$.  If $1<\alpha<2$, then
\begin{align*}
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{\rm rem}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{1}{p_{\min}}\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha\epsilon}{\alpha-1}}
\leq c_\rho,
\end{align*}
which gives the same conclusion.  Therefore, in both cases,
\begin{align}
\bar\delta_\epsilon&\leq c_\rho\leq\rho,\notag\\
K_{\alpha,\rho}\bar\delta_\epsilon&\leq\frac{A_\alpha}{2},             \label{remainder-range-proof}
\end{align}
where the last inequality is automatic if $K_{\alpha,\rho}=0$.  In particular,
$A_\alpha-K_{\alpha,\rho}\bar\delta_\epsilon\geq A_\alpha/2>0$.

\emph{2) Validity of the proposed posteriors.}
Since the denominator in \eqref{saferadius} is at least $A_\alpha$, we have $r_-\leq r_0$.  For the two signs in \eqref{safex},
\begin{align*}
P_{X|U=u_{1,2}}(x)
=P_X(x)\left(1\pm r_-\frac{v^\star(x)}{\sqrt{P_X(x)}}\right).
\end{align*}
The absolute value of the perturbation in parentheses is at most
$r_-\kappa_\star\leq\delta_0\leq\rho<1$, so these vectors are nonnegative.  They sum to one because
$\langle v^\star,\sqrt{P_X}\rangle=0$.  Likewise,
$r_-\leq r_0\leq r_Y^\star$ ensures that the two vectors in \eqref{safey} are nonnegative.  They are normalized because
\begin{align*}
\bm1^Td^\star
=\bm1^TP_{X|Y}^{-1}D_X^{1/2}v^\star
=\bm1^TD_X^{1/2}v^\star
=\langle\sqrt{P_X},v^\star\rangle=0,
\end{align*}
where $\bm1^TP_{X|Y}^{-1}=\bm1^T$ follows from column stochasticity.  Finally,
\begin{align*}
P_{X|Y}(P_Y\pm r_-d^\star)
=P_X\pm r_-D_X^{1/2}v^\star,
\end{align*}
so the $X$- and $Y$-posteriors are consistent with the Markov chain.  The equiprobable average of either pair equals its prior.  Therefore \eqref{bayes-channel} constructs a valid binary channel $P_{U|Y}$ having precisely these posteriors.

\emph{3) Exact feasibility and the achievable lower bound.}
For the proposed $X$-posteriors,
\begin{align*}
h_{u_{1,2}}&=\pm r_-D_X^{-1/2}v^\star,\\
\|h_{u_i}\|_\infty&=r_-\kappa_\star\leq\delta_0\leq\rho.
\end{align*}
Lemma~\ref{lem:uniform} and the upper sign of its remainder estimate give
\begin{align*}
\bigl(\mathcal L^\alpha(X\!\to\!u_i)\bigr)^\alpha
&\leq1+A_\alpha r_-^2
  +K_{\alpha,\rho}\|h_{u_i}\|_\infty r_-^2\\
&\leq1+(A_\alpha+K_{\alpha,\rho}\delta_0)r_-^2\\
&=1+b_\epsilon=e^{\alpha\epsilon}.
\end{align*}
Thus the original, unexpanded privacy constraint holds for both outputs.  By \eqref{utility-coordinate} and the singular-vector property,
\begin{align*}
\chi^2(Y;U)
&=\tfrac12\|Wr_-v^\star\|_2^2
 +\tfrac12\|W(-r_-v^\star)\|_2^2\\
&=r_-^2\sigma_{\max}^2(W),
\end{align*}
which is the lower bound in \eqref{sandwich}.

\emph{4) A uniform outer bound for every exactly feasible mechanism.}
Fix any output $u$ of any mechanism feasible for \eqref{problem}.  The lift--R\'enyi identity gives
\begin{align}
D_\alpha(P_{X|U=u}\|P_X)\leq\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\epsilon.           \label{renyi-from-lift-proof}
\end{align}
If $\alpha\geq2$, monotonicity of R\'enyi divergence in its order \cite[Theorem~3]{vanerven2014} implies
$D_2\leq D_\alpha$.  Moreover,
\begin{align*}
D_2(P_{X|U=u}\|P_X)
&=\log\sum_x\frac{P_{X|U}(x|u)^2}{P_X(x)}\\
&=\log(1+r_u^2).
\end{align*}
Combining this identity with \eqref{renyi-from-lift-proof} yields
\begin{align*}
r_u^2\leq e^{\alpha\epsilon/(\alpha-1)}-1,
\end{align*}
and hence, for every $x$,
\begin{align*}
|h_u(x)|=\frac{|Z_u(x)|}{\sqrt{P_X(x)}}
\leq\frac{r_u}{\sqrt{p_{\min}}}
\leq\bar\delta_\epsilon.
\end{align*}
If $1<\alpha<2$, monotonicity instead gives
$D(P_{X|U=u}\|P_X)\leq D_\alpha(P_{X|U=u}\|P_X)$, where $D$ is relative entropy.  Pinsker's inequality \cite[Theorem~31]{vanerven2014} (with natural logarithms) and \eqref{renyi-from-lift-proof} then give
\begin{align*}
\|P_{X|U=u}-P_X\|_1
\leq\sqrt{2D(P_{X|U=u}\|P_X)}
\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha\epsilon}{\alpha-1}}.
\end{align*}
Therefore
\begin{align*}
\|h_u\|_\infty
\leq\frac{\|P_{X|U=u}-P_X\|_1}{p_{\min}}
\leq\bar\delta_\epsilon.
\end{align*}
Together with \eqref{remainder-range-proof}, both cases show that
$\|h_u\|_\infty\leq\bar\delta_\epsilon\leq\rho$.  The lower side of Lemma~\ref{lem:uniform} now gives
\begin{align*}
e^{\alpha\epsilon}
&\geq\bigl(\mathcal L^\alpha(X\!\to\!u)\bigr)^\alpha\\
&\geq1+\bigl(A_\alpha-K_{\alpha,\rho}\|h_u\|_\infty\bigr)r_u^2\\
&\geq1+\bigl(A_\alpha-K_{\alpha,\rho}\bar\delta_\epsilon\bigr)r_u^2.
\end{align*}
Thus
\begin{align}
r_u^2\leq
\frac{b_\epsilon}{A_\alpha-K_{\alpha,\rho}\bar\delta_\epsilon}.       \label{exact-radius-outer}
\end{align}
Using \eqref{utility-coordinate}, the singular-value inequality, and $\sum_uP_U(u)=1$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
\chi^2(Y;U)
&=\sum_uP_U(u)\|WZ_u\|_2^2\\
&\leq\sigma_{\max}^2(W)\sum_uP_U(u)r_u^2\\
&\leq\frac{b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)}
{A_\alpha-K_{\alpha,\rho}\bar\delta_\epsilon}.
\end{align*}
Taking the maximum over feasible mechanisms proves the upper bound in \eqref{sandwich}.

\emph{5) Gap and asymptotic orders.}
The surrogate value is
$V_0=b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)/A_\alpha$.  Its difference from the achievable lower bound equals
\begin{align*}
b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)
\frac{K_{\alpha,\rho}\delta_0}
{A_\alpha(A_\alpha+K_{\alpha,\rho}\delta_0)}
=b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)G_-.
\end{align*}
The difference between the outer bound and $V_0$ is similarly
$b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)G_+$.  Since the exact optimum lies between those two bounds, \eqref{gap} follows.

Finally,
$b_\epsilon=\alpha\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2)$,
$r_0=O(\sqrt\epsilon)$, and $\delta_0=O(\sqrt\epsilon)$.  The definitions of $\bar\delta_\epsilon$ in both order regimes also give
$\bar\delta_\epsilon=O(\sqrt\epsilon)$.  Hence $G_-=O(\sqrt\epsilon)$ and $G_+=O(\sqrt\epsilon)$, so the absolute gap is $O(\epsilon^{3/2})$.  Since $V_0=\Theta(\epsilon)$ for $\sigma_{\max}(W)>0$, $|V_{\chi^2}-V_0|/V_0=O(\sqrt\epsilon)$. The lower bound is also $\Theta(\epsilon)$, so normalization by $V_{\chi^2}$ gives the same order. These orders are for fixed $P_{XY},\alpha,\rho$.

The actual suboptimality of the constructed channel should be distinguished from the surrogate-value error. If $V_-=r_-^2\sigma_{\max}^2(W)$, then
\begin{align}
0\leq V_{\chi^2}-V_-
\leq b_\epsilon\sigma_{\max}^2(W)(G_-+G_+)
=O(\epsilon^{3/2}).\label{mechanism-gap}
\end{align}
This follows by subtracting the achievable lower bound from the upper bound in \eqref{sandwich}.

By \eqref{renyilift}, this substitution maps the exact R\'enyi constraint \eqref{renyiconstraint} to the exact lift constraint \eqref{local1}; no approximation is involved.  Under \eqref{renyi-substitution},
\begin{align*}
b_\epsilon=e^{\alpha\epsilon}-1
=e^{(\beta-1)\eta}-1=b_\eta^{\rm R},
\end{align*}
so $r_0=r_{0,\rm R}$ and $\delta_0=\delta_{0,\rm R}$.  The two cases in the outer neighborhood bound transform as
\begin{align*}
e^{\alpha\epsilon/(\alpha-1)}-1&=e^\eta-1,
&&\beta\geq2,\\
\frac{2\alpha\epsilon}{\alpha-1}&=2\eta,
&&1<\beta<2,
\end{align*}
which gives exactly $\bar\delta_\eta^{\rm R}$.

Next, the directional budget condition
\begin{align*}
\epsilon\leq\frac1\alpha\log(1+A_\alpha m_\rho^2)
\end{align*}
becomes $\eta\leq\eta_{\rm dir}^{\rm R}$.  Similarly, the two cases defining $\epsilon_{\rm rem}$ become the two cases defining $\eta_{\rm rem}^{\rm R}$.  Hence
$\eta\leq\eta_{\max}^{\rm R}(\rho)$ is precisely the sufficient range needed to invoke Theorem~\ref{thm:feasible} after the substitution.

Applying that theorem gives the radius \eqref{renyiradius}, exact feasibility, and the two bounds in \eqref{renyisandwich}.  Applying its gap calculation gives \eqref{renyigap}.  Because
$b_\eta^{\rm R}=O(\eta)$ and both neighborhood radii are $O(\sqrt\eta)$, the same calculation yields the stated absolute and relative orders.  Finally, when $\beta=2$, Corollary~\ref{cor:alpha2} applies with
$\epsilon=\eta/2$; its range becomes
$\eta\leq\log(1+(r_{\rm val}^\star)^2)$ and its value becomes
$(e^\eta-1)\sigma_{\max}^2(W)$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{baghal5}
For any leakage matrix $A$, the original problem \eqref{problem} is exactly equivalent to
\begin{align}\label{generalproblem}
\max_{P_U,\{q_u\}}\quad
&\sum_uP_U(u)\left\|D_Y^{-1/2}(q_u-P_Y)\right\|_2^2\\[-1mm]
\mathrm{s.t.}\quad &q_u\in\mathcal Q_\epsilon^{\rm ex},\quad
\sum_uP_U(u)q_u=P_Y,\notag\\
&P_U(u)\geq0,\quad\sum_uP_U(u)=1,\quad
|\mathcal U|\leq|\mathcal Y|.\notag
\end{align}
Moreover, an optimizer can be selected with every $q_u$ an extreme point of $\mathcal Q_\epsilon^{\rm ex}$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
We first prove equivalence in both directions.  Given a channel $P_{U|Y}$, set
$p_u=P_U(u)$ and $q_u=P_{Y|U=u}$.  The Markov chain gives
\begin{align*}
P_{X|U=u}=Aq_u,
\end{align*}
and total probability gives $\sum_up_uq_u=P_Y$.  Furthermore,
\begin{align*}
\bigl(\mathcal L^\alpha(X\!\to\!u)\bigr)^\alpha
&=\sum_xP_X(x)\left(\frac{P_{X|U}(x|u)}{P_X(x)}\right)^\alpha\\
&=\sum_xP_X(x)\left(\frac{(Aq_u)(x)}{P_X(x)}\right)^\alpha
=\Phi_\alpha(q_u).
\end{align*}
Thus the original point-wise constraint is exactly $q_u\in\mathcal Q_\epsilon^{\rm ex}$.  The utility is
\begin{align*}
\chi^2(Y;U)
&=\sum_up_u\sum_y\frac{(q_u(y)-P_Y(y))^2}{P_Y(y)}\\
&=\sum_up_u\left\|D_Y^{-1/2}(q_u-P_Y)\right\|_2^2,
\end{align*}
which is the objective in \eqref{generalproblem}.

Conversely, suppose $\{p_u,q_u\}$ is feasible for \eqref{generalproblem}.  Since $P_Y$ has full support, define
\begin{align*}
P_{U|Y}(u|y)=\frac{p_uq_u(y)}{P_Y(y)}.
\end{align*}
Nonnegativity is immediate, and
\begin{align*}
\sum_uP_{U|Y}(u|y)
=\frac{\sum_up_uq_u(y)}{P_Y(y)}=1.
\end{align*}
Hence this is a valid channel.  Bayes' rule gives posterior $q_u$, the Markov chain produces $Aq_u$, and membership in
$\mathcal Q_\epsilon^{\rm ex}$ gives the original exact privacy constraint.  This proves the equivalence.  Proposition~\ref{car1} supplies existence and the cardinality bound.

It remains to prove the extreme-point claim.  Let
\begin{align*}
g(q)=\left\|D_Y^{-1/2}(q-P_Y)\right\|_2^2.
\end{align*}
This is a convex function of $q$.  If an optimal posterior $q_u$ is not extreme in $\mathcal Q_\epsilon^{\rm ex}$, finite-dimensional convexity gives a representation
\begin{align*}
q_u&=\sum_{j=1}^{m_u}\lambda_{u,j}e_{u,j},\\
e_{u,j}&\in\operatorname{ext}(\mathcal Q_\epsilon^{\rm ex}),\\
\lambda_{u,j}&\geq0,\qquad \sum_j\lambda_{u,j}=1.
\end{align*}
Split output $u$ into symbols $(u,j)$ with probabilities
$p_u\lambda_{u,j}$ and posteriors $e_{u,j}$.  The barycenter is unchanged because
\begin{align*}
\sum_{u,j}p_u\lambda_{u,j}e_{u,j}
=\sum_up_uq_u=P_Y,
\end{align*}
and every new posterior remains exactly private.  Convexity gives
\begin{align*}
g(q_u)\leq\sum_j\lambda_{u,j}g(e_{u,j}),
\end{align*}
so splitting cannot decrease utility.  Since the original mechanism was optimal, the split mechanism is also optimal.

This produces a finite optimal mechanism supported entirely on extreme points, although it may use too many outputs.  Restrict attention to the finite collection of extreme points appearing in that split mechanism and maximize the linear objective over their mixing weights subject to the same barycenter.  The split weights are feasible and globally optimal.  An extreme solution of this finite linear program has at most $|\mathcal Y|$ positive weights by the linear-dependence argument in the proof of Proposition~\ref{car1}.  Selecting such a solution preserves optimality, the barycenter, and support on extreme points while restoring the cardinality bound.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:null-space}
Let $A$ be arbitrary and define
$\Delta_u\triangleq A(q_u-P_Y)$.  Every posterior admits the representation
\begin{align}
q_u&=P_Y+A^\dagger\Delta_u+z_u,\notag\\
z_u&\in\operatorname{null}(A),                            \label{null-decomposition}
\end{align}
subject to normalization and nonnegativity.  Its exact utility contribution is
\begin{align}
\left\|D_Y^{-1/2}(A^\dagger\Delta_u+z_u)\right\|_2^2.    \label{null-utility}
\end{align}
If there exists nonzero $z\in\operatorname{null}(A)$ with
$\bm1^Tz=0$ and $P_Y\pm tz\geq0$ for some $t>0$, then positive utility is achievable at perfect privacy.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Because $\Delta_u=A(q_u-P_Y)$ belongs to the range of $A$, the Moore--Penrose identity $AA^\dagger\Delta_u=\Delta_u$ holds.  Define
\begin{align*}
z_u\triangleq q_u-P_Y-A^\dagger\Delta_u.
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align*}
Az_u&=A(q_u-P_Y)-AA^\dagger\Delta_u\\&=\Delta_u-\Delta_u=0,
\end{align*}
so $z_u\in\operatorname{null}(A)$ and \eqref{null-decomposition} follows.  Substituting this expression into
$\chi^2(q_u\|P_Y)=\|D_Y^{-1/2}(q_u-P_Y)\|_2^2$ gives \eqref{null-utility}.

For the final claim, take equiprobable posteriors
$q_1=P_Y+tz$ and $q_2=P_Y-tz$.  They are normalized and nonnegative by assumption, and their average is $P_Y$.  Since $Az=0$,
\begin{align*}
Aq_1&=Aq_2\\&=AP_Y\\&=P_X,
\end{align*}
so the lift equals one for both outputs: privacy is perfect.  Their average utility is
\begin{align*}
\tfrac12\|D_Y^{-1/2}tz\|_2^2
+\tfrac12\|D_Y^{-1/2}(-tz)\|_2^2
=t^2\|D_Y^{-1/2}z\|_2^2>0.
\end{align*}
In the present full-support model, the side conditions hold for every nonzero $z\in\operatorname{null}(A)$: $\bm1^Tz=\bm1^TAz=0$, and any $0<t\leq\min_{y:z(y)\neq0}P_Y(y)/|z(y)|$ ensures nonnegativity. Conversely, at $\epsilon=0$, strict convexity of $t^\alpha$ and $\sum_xP_X(x)(Aq_u)(x)/P_X(x)=1$ imply that lift at most one forces $Aq_u=P_X$. If the utility is positive, some $q_u\neq P_Y$, so $q_u-P_Y$ is a nonzero null vector. Thus positive utility at perfect privacy is possible if and only if $\operatorname{null}(A)\neq\{0\}$.

When $A$ is invertible, its null space is trivial and \eqref{null-decomposition} reduces to the inverse-coordinate representation used in Lemma~\ref{lem:coordinates}.  When the null space is nontrivial, Proposition~\ref{prop:null-space} shows exactly how utility can survive even at $\epsilon=0$.
