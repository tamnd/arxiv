We have created generic Python libraries that can be plugged into existing Python-based data reduction pipelines or used as a standalone calibration tool. <!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:zernike} \textbf{(Zernike polynomials)}\\
Let \(n,m \in \mathbb{N}_0\) with \(n \geq m\). 
The even Zernike polynomials are defined as
\begin{equation}
    Z_n^m(\rho,\phi) \quad:=\quad R_n^m(\rho) \cdot \cos(m\phi)
\end{equation}
and the odd ones as 
\begin{equation}
    Z_n^{-m}(\rho,\phi) \quad:=\quad R_n^m(\rho) \cdot \sin(m\phi).
\end{equation}
The radial polynomials \(R_n^m(\rho)\) are defined by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zernike_radial_poly}
    R_n^m(\rho) \quad:=\quad 
    \begin{cases}
    \sum\limits_{k=0}^{(n-m)/2} \frac{(-1)^k\,(n-k)!}{k!\,\left(\frac{n+m}{2}-k\right)!\,\left(\frac{n-m}{2}-k\right)!}\rho^{n-2k}, & \text{if \(n-m\) even}\\
    0, & \text{if \(n-m\) odd.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\textnormal{(Orthogonality of the Zernike Polynomials)}\\
The Zernike Polynomials are orthogonal. In particular, the following equation holds:
\begin{equation}
   \frac{1}{\pi}\; \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 \Psi_i(\rho, \phi) \, \Psi_j(\rho, \phi) \,\rho \; d\rho \, d\phi \quad = \quad \delta_{i,j}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We can split the claimed property in the orthogonality of the radial and angular parts. For the angular part the following cases exist: 
The mixed term is always zero: 
\begin{align*}
    \int_0^{2\pi} \,\sin(m\phi)\,\cos(m'\phi) d\phi \quad=\quad&  \frac{m \sin(2m\pi)\sin(2m'\pi) + n (cos(2m\pi)\cos(2m'\pi) -1)}{m^2 - m'^2 } \\
    =\quad& 0 \qquad  \forall\; m,m'\in\mathbb{N}_{0},
\end{align*}
since \(\sin(2z\pi) = 0\) and \(\cos(2z\pi) = 1\) for all \(z\in\mathbb{Z}\). 
If we assume \(m=0\) and \(m'\in\mathbb{N}_0\), we get 
\[ \int_0^{2\pi} 0 \cdot \sin(m'\phi) \,d\phi \;=\; 0 \quad\textnormal{ and }\quad \int_0^{2\pi} \cos(m'\phi) \,d\phi \;=\; 2\pi\,\delta_{m,m'},\]
and the case where \(m=m'=0\) is always the \((\cos,\cos)\) case. 
Let's assume that \(m\in\mathbb{N}\), then
\[
  \int_0^{2\pi} \,\sin(m\phi)\,\sin(m'\phi)\, d\phi \;=\; \pi\,\delta_{m,m'} \;=\; \int_0^{2\pi} \,\cos(m\phi)\,\cos(m'\phi) \,d\phi, 
\]
as we always get a result of the form "\(a\sin(2z\pi)-b\sin(2z'\pi)\)", with \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\) and \(z,z'\in\mathbb{Z}\), if \(m\neq m'\). 
And the integral over \(\cos^2\) and \(\sin^2\) is \(\pi\).

For the radial part we now just need to check for cases where \(m=m'\). The Zernike polynomials were designed to be orthogonal in this case
by subtracting appropriate low order terms. Thus,
\[
    \int_0^1 R_n^m(\rho)\,R_{n'}^m(\rho)\,\rho\,d\rho \quad=\quad  \frac{ \delta_{n,n'}}{2(n+1)}.
\]
With the choose of \(h_m(n)\) as in Equation~\ref{eq:hmn} we have shown the claimed condition.

<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\textnormal{(Hilbert-Schmidt kernel \& integral operator)} \\
Let \(T\subset\mathbb{R}\) be a bounded domain. The function \(k:T\times T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) is called a \textnormal{Hilbert-Schmidt kernel} if \(k\in L^2(T\times T)\), that is 
\[\int_{T\times T} \big| k(t,s) \big|^2 \,dt\,ds \quad <\quad \infty.\] 
Further we define the \textnormal{Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator} as 
\[\big(Ku\big)(t) \;:=\; \int_T k(t,s)\,u(s)\,ds, \quad\forall\,u\in L^2(T).\]
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\textnormal{(Self-adjoint, positive operator)}\\
Let \(H\) be a Hilbert space. We say a bounded linear operator \(K:H\rightarrow H\) is \textnormal{self-adjoint} if
\[\langle Ku,v\rangle \;=\; \langle u,Kv\rangle \quad\forall\,u,v\in H.\]
Further, we say \(K\) is \textnormal{positive semidefinite} if \(\langle Ku,u\rangle \geq 0\) for all \(u\in H\).
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\textnormal{(Mercer's Theorem)} \label{th:mercers_theorem}\\
Let \(T\subset\mathbb{R}\) be a compact interval and \(k:T\times T \rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) be a continuous function. Further, let \(k\) be a symmetric Hilbert-Schmidt kernel and the corresponding Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator \(K\) be positive semidefinite.
Then there exist real \(\lambda_j\geq 0\) and continuous functions \(\varphi_j \in L^2(T)\) that satisfy \(\big(K\varphi_j\big)(t) = \lambda_j\varphi_j(t)\) such that
\[ k(t,s) \quad=\quad \sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\;\lambda_j\,\varphi_j(t)\,\varphi_j(s)\]
uniformly on \(T\times T\). Moreover, \(\{\,\varphi_j\,|\,j\in\mathbb{N}\,\}\) forms an orthonormal basis of \(L^2(T)\).

\textnormal{(Karhunen-Loève Expansion)}\\
The expansion defined by Equations~\ref{eq:x_expansion} and \ref{eq:kl_exp_coefficients} converges and is uniformly in \(T\). That is, it does not depend on the particular value chosen in \(T\). Moreover, the coefficients are of mean zero and uncorrelated. In formulas

<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\textnormal{(counting process)}\\
Let \(\mathcal{X}\) be a measurable space (e.g., \(\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^d\)) and \(\omega = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots\}\) where each \(x_i\) denotes a coordinate in \(\mathcal{X}\). Let \(A\subset\mathcal{X}\) and 
\begin{equation*}
N(A:\omega) \quad:=\quad \sum_i I_A(x_i)
\end{equation*}
the number of points in \(\omega\) that lie in \(A\), with 
\begin{equation*}
      I_A(x) \quad := \quad  
     \begin{cases}
    1, & \text{ if } x \in A\\
    0, &\text{ if } x \notin A,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
then we call \(N(A:\omega)\) a \textnormal{counting process}.
<!-- end definition-4 -->
<!-- definition-5: body_md -->
\textnormal{(Poisson counting process)}\\
Let \(\mathcal{X}\) be a measurable space. A \textnormal{Poisson counting process} (or simply \textnormal{Poisson process}) on \(\mathcal{X}\) with a monotonically increasing intensity function \(\lambda:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_0\) is a counting process \(N(\cdot)\) on \(\mathcal{X}\) such that, for any measurable subset \(A \subseteq \mathcal{X}\):
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] \(N(A)\) is Poisson-distributed with mean \(\lambda(A)\), that is
   \[
   P\big[N(A) = k\big] = \frac{\lambda(A)^k}{k!} \exp(-\lambda(A))
   \]
   for \(k\in\mathbb{N}_0\).
\item[(b)]
   For any collection of disjoint, measurable subsets \(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n \subset \mathcal{X}\) the counts 
   \(N(A_1),\, N(A_2),\, \ldots,\, N(A_n)\) are independent random variables.
\end{itemize}
<!-- end definition-5 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\textnormal{(A translated Poisson process is a Poisson process)} \label{th:translated_poisson}\\
Let \(\{N(A)\,|\,A\subset\mathcal{X}\}\) be a Poisson counting process
with the integrable intensity function \(\lambda(x)\)  in the input space \(\mathcal{X}\). 
If an input location \(x\in\mathcal{X}\) is randomly translated to an output location \(y\in\mathcal{Y}\) via a conditional probability density function \(\rho(y|x)\) to form an output point-process \(\{M(B)\,|\,B\subset\mathcal{Y}\}\), then the latter is also 
a Poisson counting process with intensity function
\begin{equation}
  \mu(y) \quad:=\quad \int_{X}\rho(y|x) \, \lambda(x) \, dx.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\textnormal{(Conditional mean for count-record data)} \label{th:conditional_mean}\\
Let \(\{N(A)\,|\,A\subset\mathcal{X}\}\) a Poisson process with an integrable intensity function \(\lambda(x)\) and  \(\{M(B)\,|\,B\subset\mathcal{Y}\}\) an output point-process with a translating conditional probability density function \(\rho(y|x)\). Denote the count-record data by \(M = \{\mathcal{Y}_1,...,\mathcal{Y}_{M(\mathcal{Y})},\,M(\mathcal{Y})\}\), where \(\mathcal{Y}_m\) is the location of the observed point in the output space. 

Then the conditional expectation given the count-record data \(M\) is given by 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\big[N(A)\,|\,M\big] \quad=\quad 
    \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \frac{\int_A \rho(y|x) \lambda(x) dx}{\int_{\mathcal{X}} \rho(y|x) \lambda(x) dx} M(dy).
\end{equation}

Let the sequence \(\{\theta^{(k)}\,|\, k\in\mathbb{N}\}\) be defined according to the \textbf{E} and \textbf{M} steps above, then the corresponding sequence of

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\textnormal{ \cite[Theorem 1]{Thimons18}} \\
The diffraction plane and image plane errors do not increase, as the algorithm progresses. In particular the following inequality holds
\begin{equation}
    E_{k+1}\quad\leq\quad e_k\quad\leq\quad E_k \qquad \forall\, k\in\mathbb{N}_0.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Define \(W_k := |U_0|\cdot\exp(\textnormal{i} \psi_{k})\) and \(w_k := |u_0|\cdot\exp(\textnormal{i} \phi_{k-1})\). We start with a proof of the right inequality. 
\begin{align} \label{eq:gs_mult_exp}
    \left(N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big|U_k[n] - W_k[n]\big|^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}}  =& \left(N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \Big|\exp(\textnormal{i} \psi_{k}) \cdot \big(|U_k[n]| - |W_k[n]|\big)\Big|^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}} \nonumber \\
    =& \left(N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \Big(\big|\exp(\textnormal{i} \psi_{k})\big| \cdot \big||U_k[n]| - |W_k[n]|\big|\Big)^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}}  \nonumber  \\
    =& \left(N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big(|U_k[n]| - |W_k[n]|\big)^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}}.
\end{align}
Since \(|W_k[n]| = |U_0[n]|\) this is \(E_k\).
From the step definition in Equation~\ref{eq:gs2} we see that \(\textnormal{FFT}(U_k) = w_k = |u_0|\exp(\textnormal{i}\phi_{k-1})\) and \(\textnormal{FFT}(W_k) = u_k =  |u_k|\exp(\textnormal{i}\phi_{k}) \).
As FFT is a linear operation and with Parseval's Theorem we can transfer the left hand side of the equation above to the image domain. We get 
\begin{equation*}
    E_k \;=\; \left(N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big|U_k[n] - W_k[n]\big|^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}} \;=\;\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \Big|w_k[n] - u_k[n]\Big|^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}}. 
\end{equation*}
For the ease of notation let's focus on the \(n\)-th term \(t_k[n] := \big|w_k[n] - u_k[n]\big|^2\) in the sum above.
\begin{align*}
   t_k  =\;&  \Big||u_0|\exp(\textnormal{i}\phi_{k-1}) - |u_k|\exp(\textnormal{i}\phi_{k})\Big|^2  \\
    \stackrel{(*)}{=}\;& \Big||u_0|\cos(\phi_{k-1}) + \textnormal{i}|u_0|\sin(\phi_{k-1})  - |u_k|\cos(\phi_{k})  - \textnormal{i}|u_k|\sin(\phi_{k})\Big|^2  \\
    \stackrel{(**)}{=}&\Big[|u_0|\cos(\phi_{k-1}) - |u_k|\cos(\phi_{k})\Big]^2 + \Big[|u_0|\sin(\phi_{k-1})  -  |u_k|\sin(\phi_{k})\Big]^2  \\
    =\;& \big|u_k\big|^2 + \big|u_0\big|^2 - 2 \big|u_k\big|\big|u_0\big|\big[\cos(\phi_{k-1})\cos(\phi_{k}) + \sin(\phi_{k-1})\sin(\phi_{k})\big] \Big. \\
    =\;& \big|u_k\big|^2 + \big|u_0\big|^2 - 2 \big|u_k\big|\big|u_0\big|\cos(\phi_{k-1}- \phi_{k}) \Big.,
\end{align*}
where \((*)\) is the definition of complex exponentials and \((**)\) the definition of the complex magnitude. Since \(\cos \leq 1\) we get 
\[a \geq a \cdot \cos(\theta), \qquad \forall\, a\in[0,\infty),\;\forall\,\theta\in\mathbb{R},\]
regardless of the sign of \(\cos(\theta)\). And therefore
\begin{align*}
    E_k \;\geq\;& \left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}  \big|u_0[n]\big|^2 + \big|u_k[n]\big|^2 - 2 \big|u_0[n]\big|\big|u_k[n]\big| \right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}} \\
    \;=\;&  \left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big(|u_k[n]| - |u_0[n]|\big)^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}} \\
    \;=\;& e_k.
\end{align*}
Which is what we wanted to show in the first part. For the other inequality we start by multiplying \(|\exp(\textnormal{i}\phi_k)|\) analog to Equation~\ref{eq:gs_mult_exp} and see that
\[e_k \quad = \quad  \left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big|u_k[n] - w_{k+1}[n]\big|^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}}. \]
We use Parseval's Theorem once again and translate this to the diffraction plane:
\[\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big|u_k[n] - w_{k+1}[n]\big|^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}}  \quad = \quad  \left(N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big|W_k[n] - U_{k+1}[n]\big|^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}}. \]
This result can be derived with the same rationals as above to 
\begin{align*}
    e_k =\;& \left(N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big|W_k[n] - U_{k+1}[n]\big|^2\right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}} \\
    \geq\;& \left(N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \big|U_0[n]\big|^2 + \big|U_{k+1}[n]\big|^2 - 2 \big|U_0[n]\big|\big|U_{k+1}[n]\big| \right)^{\nicefrac{1}{2}} \\
    =\;& E_{k+1}.
\end{align*}
The combination of both results yields the proof of the theorem.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{th:theta} \textnormal{ \cite[compare][Theorem 5.1]{Schulz93}}\\
The Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimate for \(\theta_j\) is 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:theta}
   \Theta_j := \big|\exp(\textnormal{i}\Tilde{\theta}_j)\big|^2 \quad=\quad \frac{\sum\limits_{v\in\mathcal{V}} f_j(v)}{G_j}
\end{equation}
with 
\begin{equation}
    G_j \quad:=\quad \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\sum\limits_{u\in\mathcal{U}} \, B(u) \cdot \exp\Big(\textnormal{i} \cdot \sum\limits_{m\in\mathcal{M}} a_{j,m}\cdot\Psi_m(u)\Big) \cdot \exp(-\textnormal{i}2\pi vu)\right|^2.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
From Equations~\ref{eq:OTF} and \ref{eq:zernike} we can write \(\hat{p}_j\) as 
\begin{align} \label{eq:def_phat}
    \hat{p}_j(v) &=\quad \left|\int_{u\in\mathcal{U}} \,B(u) \cdot \exp(\textnormal{i}\theta_j)  \cdot \exp\Big(\textnormal{i} \cdot \sum\limits_{m\in\mathcal{M}} a_{j,m}\cdot\Psi_m(u)\Big) \cdot \exp(-\textnormal{i}2\pi vu) \; du\right|^2 \nonumber\\ 
    &=: \quad  \big|\exp(\textnormal{i}\theta_j)\big|^2 \cdot g_j(v),
\end{align}
using discrete Fourier Transform on \(\mathcal{U}\). We also note that \(G_j=\sum_{v} g_j(v-\tau)\) is not a function in \(\tau\). With that we can re-write the log likelihood as
\begin{align}
     \mathbf{L}(\alpha, \Tilde{\theta}) &=  \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} \left[ -\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \Theta_j\hat{g}_j(v-\tau)s(\tau) + \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} f_j(v)\cdot\ln\big(\Theta_j \cdot \big(s\circledast{}g_j\big)(v)\big)\right] \nonumber\\
     &= - \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} \Theta_j \left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \hat{g}_j(v-\tau) s(\tau) \right) \nonumber\\ 
     &\qquad + \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} f_j(v) \ln(\Theta_j) + \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} f_j(v)\cdot \ln\big(\big(s\circledast{}g_j\big)(v))\big) \nonumber \\
     &= - \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} \Theta_j G_j + \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} f_j(v) \ln(\Theta_j) + \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} f_j(v)\cdot \ln\big(\big(s\circledast{}g_j\big)(v)\big).
\end{align}
Partial differentiation for any given \(k\in\mathcal{J}\) yields 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial\mathbf{L}(\alpha, \Tilde{\theta})}{\partial\Tilde{\theta}_k}   \quad=\quad -G_k +  \frac{\sum\limits_{v\in\mathcal{V}} f_j(v)}{\Theta_k}. \quad 
\end{equation}
Thus, the derivative is zero for any \(\Theta_k\) as defined in Equation~\ref{eq:theta} and the second derivative is negative, since all \(f_j\) are strictly non-negative.

<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{th:GEM} \textnormal{\cite[compare][Theorem 5.2]{Schulz93}}\\
A sequence of estimates of the phase parameters updated as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:def_shat}
    \hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}(v) \quad=\quad \sigma \cdot \hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}}(v) \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \frac{\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau-v)}{\hat{d}_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau)}f_j(\tau),
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma := \left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} f_j(v)\right)^{-1}\) is the scaling factor to ensure Equation~\ref{eg:s_eq_one} holds and \(\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{new}}\) chosen such that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:condition_to_choose_pj}
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \xi(\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}(v)) \cdot \ln\big(\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{new}}(v)\big) \quad \geq \quad \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \xi(\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}(v)) \cdot \ln\big(\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}(v)\big),
\end{equation}
with 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:def_xi}
\xi(\hat{p}_j(v)) \quad:=\quad \hat{p}_j(v) \left[\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}  \frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau-v)}{\hat{d}_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau)}f_j(\tau) \right]
\end{equation}
is an EM algorithm with
\begin{equation} \label{eq:req_update}
    \mathbf{L}(\alpha^{\textnormal{new}}) \quad\geq\quad \mathbf{L}(\alpha^{\textnormal{old}}).
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-8 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For the same scaling argument as above, we can restrict our estimates of the true solar scene \(\hat{s}_j\) to the set \(\mathcal{A} := \{\,s\,| \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s(v) = 1\}\). 
Further we express the observed data \(\{f_j(v)\}\) via an arbitrary set of Poisson-distributed random variables \(\Tilde{f}_j\) via 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ftilde}
    f_j(v) \quad=\quad \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v).
\end{equation}
The expected value of this data is 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:exp_tilde_f}
    \mathbb{E}[\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)] \quad=\quad s(\tau) \cdot p_j(\tau-v).
\end{equation}
Hence,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:exp_f}
    \mathbb{E}[f_j(v)] \quad=\quad \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}  \mathbb{E}[\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)] \quad=\quad \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}  s(\tau) \cdot p_j(\tau-v) \quad=\quad d_j(v).
\end{equation}
The new set does not reveal any new physical meaningful information.  Important is that it is statistically consistent with the observed data as a mathematical model. With this we now can apply the terminology of Section \ref{sec:em} of complete data \(\{\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)\} = \mathcal{Z}\) and incomplete data \(\{f_j(v)\} = \mathcal{Y}\). The many-to-one relation of complete to incomplete data \(h\) is given by Equation~\ref{eq:ftilde}.
As above we can derive a log likelihood function for the complete data ignoring all terms, that do not depend on \(s\) or \(p_j\)
\begin{align}
    \Tilde{\mathbf{L}}(s, p_j) \;=& - \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} s(\tau) p_j(\tau-v) \nonumber\\
    & \quad + \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v) \ln\big( p_j(\tau-v)s(\tau) \big).
\end{align}
Now we follow the EM-algorithm description from Section \ref{sec:em} and define 
\begin{equation}
    Q(s, p_j) \quad:=\quad \Tilde{Q}(s, p_j,\hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}},\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}) \quad=\quad \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\Big[\Tilde{\mathbf{L}}(s, p_j) \Big|\Big. \{f_j(v)\}\Big],
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}[\,\cdot\,|\{f_j(v)\}]\) denotes a conditioned expectation given the observed Poisson-distributed data \(\{f_j(v)\}\) and using the previous functions \(\hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}}\) and \(\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}\) for computing \(f_j\) and \(\Tilde{f}_j\). With this notation we have changed the requirement for updating the estimates from Equation~\ref{eq:req_update} to 
\begin{equation}
    Q(\hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}, \hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{new}}) \quad\geq\quad Q(\hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}}, \hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}).
\end{equation}
From Equation~\ref{eq:discrete_q} we get a concrete formulation of \(Q\) and since \(s,\hat{s}_j \in \mathcal{A}\) we can remove one \(s\) from the formula. Further, we separate \(Q\) to a sum of two independent functions of \(s\) and \(p\) respectively:
\begin{align}
Q(s, p_j)    \;=& - \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_j(v) \nonumber\\
    & \quad + \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big] \ln\big( p_j(\tau-v)s(v) \big) \nonumber \\
    =& \underbrace{- \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_j(v) + \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big] \ln\big( p_j(\tau-v) \big)}_{=:\,Q_p(p_j)}  \nonumber \\
    & \underbrace{\quad + \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \left[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big]\right] \ln\big(s(v) \big)}_{=:\,Q_s(s)} \nonumber\\
    =& \;Q_p(p_j) + Q_s(s).
\end{align}
Now, since all \(\Tilde{f}\) are mutually independent random Poisson distributed variables, we see from Equations~\ref{eq:discrete_q_expanded}, \ref{eq:exp_tilde_f},  and \ref{eq:exp_f} that 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:exp_tilde_f_cond}
     \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big] \;=\;  \frac{\mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}[\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)]}{\mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}[f_j(\tau)] }  f_j(\tau)
     \;=\; \frac{p_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau-v)\hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}}(v)}{d_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau) } f_j(\tau).
\end{equation}
By summing over all observed points and all frames we get 
\begin{align}
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big] \;=\;& 
            \hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}}(v) \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \frac{p_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau-v)}{d_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau) } f_j(\tau) \nonumber \\
            \;=\;& \frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}(v)}{\sigma}.
\end{align}
This allows us to re-write \(Q_s\) as 
\begin{align}
    Q_s(\hat{s}) \;=\;& \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \left[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big]\right] \ln\big(\hat{s}v) \big) \nonumber \\
    =\;& \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}(v)}{\sigma} \ln\big(\hat{s}(v) \big).
\end{align}
For this reformulation it is easy to apply the Laplace operator 
\begin{equation}
    \nabla O_s(\hat{s}) \;=\; \left( \frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}(v)}{\sigma \,\hat{s}(v)} \right)_{v\in\mathcal{V}}.
\end{equation}
Since all scene function estimates must be positive, it is obvious that \(\nabla O_s \neq 0\) and \mbox{\(\Delta Q_s < 0\)} for all \(\hat{s}\). 
Define  
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(\hat{s},\lambda) \quad:=\quad Q_s(\hat{s}) + \lambda \left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \hat{s}(v)\right) - \lambda,
\end{equation}
for \(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\). For critical points of \(\Lambda\) we get the conditions
\begin{equation} \label{eq:lap_op_cond1}
    \frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial \hat{s}(w)} \;=\; \frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}(w)}{\sigma \,\hat{s}(w)} + \lambda\;=\; 0 
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation} \label{eq:lap_op_cond2}
    \frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial \lambda} \;=\; \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \hat{s}(v) - 1 = 0.
\end{equation}
For \(\lambda\neq0\) Condition~\ref{eq:lap_op_cond1} can be rearranged to 
\begin{equation} \label{eq_sw}
    \hat{s}(w) \quad=\quad -\frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}(w)}{\sigma\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Plugging that into Condition~\ref{eq:lap_op_cond2} yields 
\begin{alignat}{2}
        & 0 &\;=\;& 1 +  \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}(v)}{\sigma\lambda} \nonumber \\
\Leftrightarrow\qquad & - \lambda &\;=\;& \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{new}}(v)}{\sigma} \quad =\quad \frac{1}{\sigma}.
\end{alignat}
Now we plug this result into Equation~\ref{eq_sw} and found the maximum at \(s^{\textnormal{new}}\). 


\noindent
Revisiting Equation~\ref{eq:exp_tilde_f_cond} for \(\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|\tau-v)\) we get
\begin{align}
     \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}} \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|\tau-v)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big] 
     \;=\;&  \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}  \frac{\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}(v)\hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau-v)}{d_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau) } f_j(\tau) \nonumber\\
     =\;& \hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}}(v) \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{\hat{s}^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau-v)}{d_j^{\textnormal{old}}(\tau) } f_j(\tau) \nonumber \\
     =\;& \xi(\hat{p}^{\textnormal{old}}_j(v)).
\end{align}
With this  above we can rearrange \(Q_p\) in the form below:
\begin{align}
    Q_p(p_j) \;=\quad& \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\left[ - \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_j(v) + \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}  \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|v)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big]  \ln\big( p_j(\tau-v) \big)\right] \nonumber \\
    \stackrel{v=\tau-w}{=}& \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} \left[ - \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_j(v) +  \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{V}}  \mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{old}}\big[\,\Tilde{f}_j(\tau|\tau-w)\,\big|\,f_j(\tau)\,\big]  \ln\big( p_j(w) \big)\right] \nonumber \\
    =\quad& \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\left[ - \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_j(v) + \sum_{w\in\mathcal{V}} \xi(\hat{p}^{\textnormal{old}}_j(w)) \ln\big(p_j(w)\big) \right].
\end{align}
We see that for \(\Tilde{p}_j := \xi(\hat{p}_j^{\textnormal{old}})\) we find a maximum, since 
\[
\frac{\partial Q_p(p_j)}{\partial p_l} \quad=\quad - \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} 1 + \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} \frac{\xi(\hat{p}^{\textnormal{old}}_l(v))}{p_l(v)},
\]
and the second derivative is always negative.
