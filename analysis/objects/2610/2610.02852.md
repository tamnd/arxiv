\label{lem:defect-identities}
Every binary Zinbiel algebra satisfies the identities
\begin{equation}\label{iden-2}\phi(ab,c,d)=a\phi(b,c,d).\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{iden-3}\phi(\phi(a,b,c),d,e)=0.\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Set
$$
q(a,b,c)
=
\phi(a,b,c)+\phi(b,a,c),
$$
and $$
\begin{aligned}
S(a,b,c,d)=&
2\phi(ab,c,d)
+2\phi(ba,c,d)
+\phi(a,b,cd)
\\
&-2\phi(a,c,bd)
-2a\phi(b,c,d)
-c\phi(a,b,d).
\end{aligned}
$$
A direct calculation gives 
$$
S(a,b,c,d)=
q(ab,c,d)+q(ba,c,d)-q(a,c,bd)-a\,q(b,c,d).$$
Indeed,
$$q(ab,c,d)
=(ab)(cd)+c((ab)d)-2((ab)c)d-2(c(ab))d,$$
$$q(ba,c,d)=
(ba)(cd)+c((ba)d)-2((ba)c)d-2(c(ba))d,$$
$$q(a,c,bd)=a(c(bd))+c(a(bd))-2(ac)(bd)-2(ca)(bd),$$
$$aq(b,c,d)=
a(b(cd))+a(c(bd))-2a((bc)d)-2a((cb)d).$$
This linear combination is precisely the expansion of $S(a,b,c,d)$.
Since $q=0$ by alternation, we obtain $S(a,b,c,d)=0$.


We now prove \eqref{iden-2}.
Since $S=0$, every permutation of its variables is also an identity.
Consider
$$
\begin{aligned}
0={}&
2S(a,b,c,d)
+2S(a,b,d,c)
+4S(b,d,c,a)
\\
&-4S(c,a,b,d)
-2S(c,a,d,b)
-S(c,d,a,b)
\\
&+4S(d,a,b,c)
+4S(d,b,a,c).
\end{aligned}
$$
Substituting the definition of $S$ and using the alternating property
of $\phi$, all terms cancel except
$$
3\phi(ab,c,d)-3a\phi(b,c,d).
$$
Hence
$$
3\phi(ab,c,d)-3a\phi(b,c,d)=0.
$$
Since $char\mathbb{F}=0$,
$$
\phi(ab,c,d)=a\phi(b,c,d).
$$
We next prove \eqref{iden-3}. 
First, by alternation and \eqref{iden-2},
$$
\phi(x,y,zt)
=
\phi(zt,x,y)
=
z\phi(t,x,y)
=
z\phi(x,y,t).
$$
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi-third-product}
\phi(x,y,zt)=z\phi(x,y,t).
\end{equation}

Now calculate $\phi(bc,d, ae)$ in two ways. On the one hand,
$$
\phi(bc,d,ae)
=
b\phi(c,d,ae)
=
b\bigl(a\phi(c,d,e)\bigr).
$$
On the other hand, by \eqref{eq:phi-third-product},
$$
\phi(bc,d,ae)
=
a\phi(bc,d,e)
=
a\bigl(b\phi(c,d,e)\bigr).
$$
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:commuting-on-defects}
a\bigl(b\phi(c,d,e)\bigr)
=b\bigl(a\phi(c,d,e)\bigr).
\end{equation}

Put $u=\phi(c,d,e)$. By \eqref{eq:commuting-on-defects},
we have $a(bu)=b(au)$. Hence
$$
0=q(a,b,u)
 =2\bigl(a(bu)-(ab)u-(ba)u\bigr)
 =2\phi(a,b,u).
$$
Since $\operatorname{char}\mathbb{F}=0$, it follows that
$$
\phi(a,b,\phi(c,d,e))=0.
$$
Finally, by alternation,
$$
\phi(\phi(a,b,c),d,e)
=\phi(d,e,\phi(a,b,c))
=0.
$$

\label{lem:spanning}
Every element of the free binary Zinbiel algebra $\mathcal F(X)$ is a linear
combination of left-normed products
$$
(\cdots((z_1z_2)z_3)\cdots)z_m,
\qquad z_1,\ldots,z_m\in Z.
$$
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We use induction on the degree of monomials in the generators $X$.

The statement is clear in degrees $1$ and $2$. For degree $3$, by the
definition of $\phi$,
$$
a(bc)=(ab)c+(ba)c+\phi(a,b,c),
$$
and hence the statement holds.

Assume that the statement is true for all monomials of degree less
than $n$. Let $w$ be a monomial of degree $n$. Write
$$
w=uv,
\qquad
\deg u<n,\quad \deg v<n.
$$
By the induction assumption, it is sufficient to consider
$$
u=(\cdots((z_1z_2)z_3)\cdots)z_p, \quad v=(\cdots((t_1t_2)t_3)\cdots)t_q,
$$
where $z_i,t_j\in Z$.

We prove by an auxiliary induction on the number $p+q$ of
$Z$-factors that $uv$ is a linear combination of left-normed products
in $Z$.

If $q=1$, then $uv$ is already left-normed. Let $q\geq2$ and write
$$
v=v't_q.
$$
By the definition of $\phi$,
$$
u(v't_q)
=
(uv')t_q+(v'u)t_q+\phi(u,v',t_q).
$$
By the induction assumption on $p+q$, the first two terms belong to
the required linear span.

It remains to consider $\phi(u,v',t_q)$. If $p\geq2$, write
$$
u=u'z_p.
$$
By \eqref{iden-2},
$$
\phi(u,v',t_q)
=
u'\phi(z_p,v',t_q).
$$
If $q\geq3$, write
$$
v'=v''t_{q-1}.
$$
Using \eqref{iden-1} and \eqref{iden-2}, we obtain
$$
\phi(z_p,v',t_q)
=
-\phi(v',z_p,t_q)
=
-v''\phi(t_{q-1},z_p,t_q).
$$
Therefore,
$$
\phi(u,v',t_q)
=
-u'\bigl(v''\phi(t_{q-1},z_p,t_q)\bigr).
$$

If $r,s,t\in X$, then $\phi(r,s,t)=0$ when two arguments
coincide; otherwise, by alternation, $\phi(r,s,t)=\pm y$ for some
$y\in Y$. If one of the arguments belongs to $Y$, then
\eqref{iden-1} and \eqref{iden-3} give
$$
\phi(r,s,t)=0.
$$
Thus, for $p\geq2$ and $q\geq3$, the term $\phi(u,v',t_q)$ is either
zero or is, up to sign, of the form
$$
u'(v''y),
\qquad y\in Y.
$$
The latter involves fewer than $p+q$ elements of $Z$, so the induction
assumption applies again.

If $p=1$ and $q\geq3$, then
$$
\phi(u,v',t_q)
=
-v''\phi(t_{q-1},u,t_q),
$$
which is either zero or a scalar multiple of a left-normed product
in $Z$. If $p\geq2$ and
$q=2$, then
$$
\phi(u,t_1,t_2)
=
u'\phi(z_p,t_1,t_2),
$$
and the same conclusion holds. Finally, if $p=1$ and $q=2$, then
$\phi(u,t_1,t_2)$ is zero or equals $\pm y$ for some $y\in Y$.

Hence $uv$ is a linear combination of left-normed products in $Z$.
The induction is complete.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:annihilated-defect}
If $D\neq0$, then there exists $0\neq a\in D$ such that $Aa=0$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For $a\in D$ and $x,b\in A$, the vanishing in
\eqref{eq:defect-space-properties} gives
$$
x(ba)=(xb+bx)a=b(xa).
$$
It follows that
$$
C(a)\subseteq C(ba).
$$

Choose $0\neq a_0\in D$ for which $\dim C(a_0)$ is maximal.
Since $ba_0\in D$, the preceding inclusion and maximality give
$$
C(a_0)=C(ba_0)
\qquad\text{whenever }ba_0\neq0.
$$
Suppose that $C(a_0)\neq A$. Choose $b_0\in A$ such that
$b_0a_0\neq0$, and define
$$
b_{k+1}=b_kb_k
\qquad(k\ge0).
$$
We claim that $b_ka_0\neq0$ for all $k$. The assertion holds
for $k=0$. If $b_ka_0\neq0$ but $b_{k+1}a_0=0$, the identity
$\phi(b_k,b_k,a_0)=0$ gives
$$
b_k(b_ka_0)=2(b_kb_k)a_0=2b_{k+1}a_0=0.
$$
Hence
$$
b_k\in C(b_ka_0)=C(a_0),
$$
contrary to $b_ka_0\neq0$. This proves the claim.

Let $B=\langle b_0\rangle$ be the one-generated subalgebra of $A$.
By the definition of binary Zinbiel algebras, $B$ is a right
Zinbiel algebra. It is finite-dimensional, so the ordinary
Zinbiel nilpotency theorem \cite[Theorem~2.4]{Towers},
applied to $B^{\mathrm{op}}$, implies that $B$ is nilpotent.
Thus sufficiently long products in $B$ vanish with arbitrary
parentheses. Since $b_k$ is a product of $2^k$ copies of $b_0$,
we obtain $b_k=0$ for all sufficiently large $k$.
This contradicts $b_ka_0\neq0$. Therefore $C(a_0)=A$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:proper-ideal}
If $\dim A>1$, then $A$ has a nonzero proper two-sided ideal.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
If $D=0$, then $A$ is a right Zinbiel algebra and is nilpotent
by \cite[Theorem~2.4]{Towers}, applied to $A^{\mathrm{op}}$.

Suppose that $D\neq0$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:annihilated-defect},
choose $0\neq a\in D$ such that $Aa=0$, and put
$$
I=aA=\{ay:y\in A\}.
$$
Since every defect with an argument in $D$ vanishes,
for all $x,y\in A$ we have
$$
(ay)x=a(yx)-(ya)x=a(yx)\in I
$$
and
$$
x(ay)=(xa+ax)y=(ax)y=a(xy)\in I.
$$
Thus $I$ is a two-sided ideal.

The nonzero vector $a$ lies in the kernel of
$$
L_a:A\longrightarrow A,\qquad L_a(y)=ay,
$$
because $aa=0$. Therefore
$$
\dim I=\dim\operatorname{im}L_a\le\dim A-1.
$$
If $I\neq0$, this is the required ideal. If $I=0$, then
$aA=Aa=0$, and $\mathbb{F}a$ is a nonzero proper two-sided ideal.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:finite-dimensional-solvability}
Every finite-dimensional right binary Zinbiel algebra over
a field of characteristic zero is solvable.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
We argue by induction on $n=\dim A$. The case $n=0$ is immediate.
If $n=1$, write $A=\mathbb{F}e$ and $ee=\alpha e$. The identity
$e(ee)=2(ee)e$ yields $\alpha^2e=2\alpha^2e$, so $\alpha=0$.
Thus $A^{(1)}=0$.

Let $n>1$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:proper-ideal}, $A$ has a nonzero
proper two-sided ideal $I$. Both $I$ and $A/I$ are binary
Zinbiel algebras of dimension less than $n$. By induction,
there exist $r,s\ge1$ such that
$$
(A/I)^{(r)}=0,
\qquad I^{(s)}=0.
$$
For the quotient homomorphism $\pi:A\to A/I$, induction on $k$
gives
$$
\pi\bigl(A^{(k)}\bigr)=(A/I)^{(k)},
$$
because $\pi(UV)=\pi(U)\pi(V)$ for all subspaces $U,V\subseteq A$.
Hence $A^{(r)}\subseteq I$. A further induction gives
$$
A^{(r+k)}\subseteq I^{(k)}
\qquad(k\ge0).
$$
In particular, $A^{(r+s)}\subseteq I^{(s)}=0$.
Therefore $A$ is solvable.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:mono-not-solvable}
The solvability conclusion of
Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-dimensional-solvability} does not extend to
finite-dimensional mono Zinbiel algebras. Note that every
anticommutative algebra over a field of characteristic different from
$2$ is mono Zinbiel. For every element $x$, anticommutativity gives
$x^2=0$, so the subalgebra generated by $x$ has zero multiplication
and is therefore a Zinbiel algebra. In particular, consider the three-dimensional simple Lie algebra
$A=\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb F)$, with multiplication $xy=[x,y]$. Since $A^2=A$, it follows that
$A^{(n)}=A$ for every $n\geq 0$. Thus $A$ is not solvable.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:towers-ideal}
Let $B$ be a left ideal of $A$ such that
$$
\phi(B,A,A)=0.
$$
Then $BA$ is a two-sided ideal of $A$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Since $B$ is a left ideal, $AB\subseteq B$.
For $b\in B$ and $x,y\in A$, the equality
$\phi(b,x,y)=0$ gives
$$
(bx)y=b(xy)-(xb)y.
$$
Both terms on the right belong to $BA$, since $xb\in B$.
Therefore
$$
(BA)A\subseteq BA.
$$

By alternation, $\phi(x,b,y)=0$, and hence
$$
x(by)=(xb)y+(bx)y.
$$
The first term belongs to $BA$ because $xb\in B$,
and the second belongs to $BA$ by the inclusion just proved.
Thus
$$
A(BA)\subseteq BA.
$$
Consequently, $BA$ is a two-sided ideal.

<!-- prp-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:towers-minimal-left-ideal}
Let $M$ be a minimal nonzero two-sided ideal of $A$.
Suppose that $B$ is a minimal nonzero left ideal of $A$
such that
$$
B\subseteq M,
\qquad
\phi(B,A,A)=0.
$$
Then $M=B$.
<!-- end prp-1 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:towers-ideal}, $BA$ is a two-sided ideal.
Since
$$
BA\subseteq MA\subseteq M,
$$
minimality of $M$ implies
$$
BA=0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
BA=M.
$$
If $BA=0$, then $B$ is a two-sided ideal, because
$AB\subseteq B$. Hence $B=M$.

Suppose that $BA=M$.

For $x,y\in A$ and $b\in B$, the vanishing of the defect
and its alternation give
$$
x(yb)=(xy+yx)b=y(xb).
$$
It follows that $A^2B$ is a left ideal: for $s\in A^2$,
$$
x(sb)=s(xb)\in A^2B.
$$
Moreover, $A^2B\subseteq B$. Thus minimality of $B$ gives
$$
A^2B=0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
A^2B=B.
$$

Suppose that $A^2B=B$. If $B\subseteq A^{(k)}B$, then
$$
B
\subseteq A^{(k)}B
\subseteq A^{(k)}\bigl(A^{(k)}B\bigr)
\subseteq A^{(k+1)}B.
$$
The last inclusion follows from
$$
u(vb)=(uv+vu)b
\qquad(u,v\in A^{(k)},\ b\in B).
$$
Starting with $B=A^{(1)}B$, induction gives
$$
B\subseteq A^{(k)}B
\qquad(k\ge1).
$$
This contradicts solvability of $A$ and $B\neq0$.
Therefore
$$
A^2B=0.
$$

The equality $x(yb)=y(xb)$ also shows that $AB$
is a left ideal contained in $B$.
If $AB=B$, then
$$
B=A(AB)\subseteq A^2B=0,
$$
a contradiction. Consequently,
$$
AB=0.
$$

We next show that $BA^2$ is a two-sided ideal.
For $b\in B$, $s\in A^2$, and $x\in A$, we have
$$
(bs)x=b(sx)-(sb)x=b(sx)\in BA^2,
$$
because $sb=0$.
Similarly,
$$
x(bs)=(xb+bx)s=(bx)s=b(xs)\in BA^2.
$$
Thus $BA^2$ is a two-sided ideal contained in $M$.
Minimality of $M$ gives
$$
BA^2=0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
BA^2=M.
$$

Suppose that $BA^2=M$. Then $B\subseteq BA^{(1)}$.
Since $AB=0$ and $\phi(B,A,A)=0$, we have
$$
(bu)v=b(uv)
\qquad(b\in B,\ u,v\in A).
$$
Therefore, if $B\subseteq BA^{(k)}$, then
$$
B
\subseteq BA^{(k)}
\subseteq \bigl(BA^{(k)}\bigr)A^{(k)}
\subseteq BA^{(k+1)}.
$$
Induction and solvability of $A$ yield $B=0$,
a contradiction. Hence
$$
BA^2=0.
$$

Finally, $M=BA$ implies
$$
MA=(BA)A\subseteq BA^2=0
$$
and
$$
AM=A(BA)\subseteq (AB)A+(BA)A=0.
$$
Every subspace of $M$ is therefore a two-sided ideal of $A$.
Minimality of $M$ forces $\dim M=1$.
Since $0\neq B\subseteq M$, we conclude that $B=M$.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:towers-minimal-ideal-annihilator}
Every minimal nonzero two-sided ideal $M$ of $A$ satisfies
$$
AM=MA=0,
\qquad
\dim M=1.
$$
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We first show that $M$ is a minimal nonzero left ideal
and that
$$
\phi(M,A,A)=0.
$$

Suppose first that $M\cap D\neq0$.
Since $M$ is an ideal and $AD\subseteq D$,
the subspace $M\cap D$ is a nonzero left ideal.
Choose a minimal nonzero left ideal $B\subseteq M\cap D$.
The inclusion $B\subseteq D$ gives
$$
\phi(B,A,A)=0.
$$
By Proposition~\ref{prop:towers-minimal-left-ideal},
$M=B$. Thus $M$ is a minimal nonzero left ideal,
and $\phi(M,A,A)=0$.

Now suppose that $M\cap D=0$.
For $m\in M$ and $x,y\in A$, the ideal property gives
$$
\phi(m,x,y)=m(xy)-(mx)y-(xm)y\in M.
$$
By definition, this element also belongs to $D$.
Therefore
$$
\phi(M,A,A)\subseteq M\cap D=0.
$$
Choose a minimal nonzero left ideal $B\subseteq M$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:towers-minimal-left-ideal}
again gives $M=B$.

Thus, in both cases, $M$ is a minimal nonzero left ideal
and all defects with an argument in $M$ vanish.

For $x,y\in A$ and $m\in M$, we have
$$
x(ym)=(xy+yx)m=y(xm).
$$
Hence $AM$ is a left ideal contained in $M$, so
$$
AM=0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
AM=M.
$$
Suppose that $AM=M$.
If $A^{(k)}M=M$, then
$$
M
=A^{(k)}\bigl(A^{(k)}M\bigr)
\subseteq A^{(k+1)}M
\subseteq M.
$$
Starting with $A^{(0)}M=M$, induction gives
$$
A^{(k)}M=M
\qquad(k\ge0),
$$
contradicting solvability of $A$.
Therefore
$$
AM=0.
$$

For $x,y\in A$ and $m\in M$, we now obtain
$$
x(my)=(xm+mx)y=(mx)y=m(xy).
$$
Thus $MA$ is a left ideal contained in $M$.
By minimality,
$$
MA=0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
MA=M.
$$
Suppose that $MA=M$.
The identities $AM=0$ and $\phi(M,A,A)=0$ imply
$$
(mu)v=m(uv)
\qquad(m\in M,\ u,v\in A).
$$
If $MA^{(k)}=M$, it follows that
$$
M
=\bigl(MA^{(k)}\bigr)A^{(k)}
\subseteq MA^{(k+1)}
\subseteq M.
$$
Induction again contradicts solvability.
Consequently,
$$
MA=0.
$$

Since $AM=MA=0$, every subspace of $M$ is a two-sided
ideal of $A$. Minimality of $M$ therefore gives
$$
\dim M=1.
$$

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:binary-zinbiel-nilpotency}
Every finite-dimensional right binary Zinbiel algebra
over a field of characteristic zero is nilpotent.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
We argue by induction on $n=\dim A$.
The assertion is immediate if $n=0$.
If $n=1$, the calculation in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-dimensional-solvability} gives $A^2=0$.

Let $n>1$, and assume that the assertion holds for algebras
of smaller dimension.
Choose a minimal nonzero two-sided ideal $M$ of $A$.
By Corollary~\ref{cor:towers-minimal-ideal-annihilator},
$$
AM=MA=0,
\qquad
\dim M=1.
$$
In particular, $M$ is proper.

The quotient $A/M$ is a binary Zinbiel algebra of smaller
dimension. By induction, it is nilpotent.
Using the left-normed powers defined in \eqref{eq:series},
we obtain
$$
A^r\subseteq M
$$
for some positive integer $r$. Consequently,
$$
A^{r+1}=A^rA\subseteq MA=0.
$$

By Lemma~\ref{lem:spanning}, the vanishing of all sufficiently
long left-normed products implies the vanishing of all
sufficiently long products, regardless of the placement
of parentheses. Thus $A$ is nilpotent, completing the induction.

<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:integral}
The polynomial algebra
$P=\mathbb{F}[x]$, equipped with the multiplication
$$
(f*g)(x)=g(x)\int_0^x f(t)\,dt,
$$
where integration is understood formally, is a right Zinbiel algebra.

The left-normed powers of the constant polynomial $1$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:integral-powers}
1^{[n]}=\frac{x^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\neq0
\qquad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
Indeed, the formula holds for $n=1$, and the induction step follows from
$$
1^{[n+1]}
=1^{[n]}*1
=\int_0^x \frac{t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,dt
=\frac{x^n}{n!}.
$$

Therefore the constant polynomial $1$ is not nilpotent,
and $P$ is not nil.

<!-- prp-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:bounded-nil}
Let $\mathcal W$ be a subvariety of binary Zinbiel algebras
over a field of characteristic zero.
The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\operatorname{Zinb}\nsubseteq\mathcal W$.
\item Every algebra in $\mathcal W$ is nil.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end prp-2 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Assume first that $\operatorname{Zinb}\nsubseteq\mathcal W$. We must show that
every algebra $A\in\mathcal W$ is nil. Consider the intersection
$$
\mathcal U=\mathcal W\cap\operatorname{Zinb}.
$$
The assumption $\operatorname{Zinb}\nsubseteq\mathcal W$
implies that $\mathcal U$ is a proper subvariety of
$\operatorname{Zinb}$. By the theorem of Naurazbekova and
Umirbaev~\cite[Theorem~2]{NU}, $\mathcal U$ is nilpotent. Hence there exists
an integer $N$ such that every product of $N$ elements
in every algebra belonging to $\mathcal U$, with arbitrary
parentheses, is zero.

Let $A\in\mathcal W$ and $a\in A$. Since $A$ is binary
Zinbiel, its one-generated subalgebra
$$
B=\langle a\rangle
$$
is a Zinbiel algebra. Moreover, varieties are closed under
taking subalgebras, so $B\in\mathcal W$. Therefore,
$$
B\in\mathcal W\cap\operatorname{Zinb}=\mathcal U.
$$

By the choice of $N$, every product of $N$ copies of $a$,
with arbitrary parentheses, vanishes. In particular,
$$
a^{[N]}=0.
$$
Thus every element of $A$ is nilpotent, so $A$ is nil. Since $A$ was an arbitrary member of $\mathcal W$, the variety $\mathcal W$ is nil.

Conversely, suppose that $\mathcal W$ is nil.
By Example~\ref{ex:integral}, the integral Zinbiel algebra $P$
is not nil, since \eqref{eq:integral-powers} gives
$1^{[n]}\neq0$ for every $n\ge1$.
If $\operatorname{Zinb}\subseteq\mathcal W$, then $P\in\mathcal W$,
a contradiction. Therefore $\operatorname{Zinb}\nsubseteq\mathcal W$.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:ito-binary-zinbiel}
Let $Z$ be a right binary Zinbiel algebra over $\mathbb F$.
Suppose that
$$
Z=A+B,
\qquad
A^2=B^2=0,
$$
where $A$ and $B$ are subalgebras of $Z$. Then $Z$ is metabelian:
$
(Z^2)^2=0.
$
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Let $C$ be either $A$ or $B$. We first show that, for
$u,v\in C$ and $x\in Z$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ito-same-summand}
\begin{aligned}
u(vx)&=-v(ux),\\
u(xv)&=-2u(vx),\\
(ux+xu)v&=v(ux).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Since $uv=vu=0$, the definition of the defect gives
$$
\phi(u,v,x)=u(vx),
\qquad
\phi(v,u,x)=v(ux).
$$
The first equality in~\eqref{eq:ito-same-summand} follows from
alternation. Invariance under cyclic permutations also gives
$$
-(xu+ux)v
=\phi(x,u,v)
=\phi(u,v,x)
=u(vx).
$$
Together with the first equality, this proves the third equality.
Finally,
$$
u(xv)-(ux+xu)v
=\phi(u,x,v)
=-\phi(u,v,x)
=-u(vx).
$$
Substituting $(ux+xu)v=-u(vx)$ yields the second equality.

Let $p\in A$, $q\in B$, and $z\in Z$. Choose a decomposition
$z=z_A+z_B$, where $z_A\in A$ and $z_B\in B$. Since $B^2=0$,
the second equality in~\eqref{eq:ito-same-summand} gives
$$
p(qz)=p(qz_A)=-2p(z_Aq)=-2p(zq).
$$
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ito-different-summands}
p(qz)=-2p(zq)
\end{equation}
whenever $p$ and $q$ belong to different summands. The same
argument applies with $A$ and $B$ interchanged.

Fix $a,c\in A$ and $b,d\in B$, and put
$$
r=c((ab)d),
\qquad
s=c((ba)d).
$$
Applying~\eqref{eq:ito-different-summands} three times, we obtain
$$
-2r=c(d(ab))=-2c(d(ba))=4s.
$$
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ito-r-two-s}
r=-2s.
\end{equation}
On the other hand, the third equality
in~\eqref{eq:ito-same-summand}, applied to $b,d\in B$, gives
$$
(ba+ab)d=d(ba).
$$
Multiplying on the left by $c$ and
using~\eqref{eq:ito-different-summands}, we obtain
$$
r+s=c(d(ba))=-2s,
$$
so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ito-r-three-s}
r=-3s.
\end{equation}
Comparing~\eqref{eq:ito-r-two-s} and~\eqref{eq:ito-r-three-s}
gives $s=0$, and then $r=0$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ito-fourfold-AB}
c((ab)d)=c((ba)d)=0
\end{equation}
for all $a,c\in A$ and $b,d\in B$. Interchanging $A$ and $B$
also gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ito-fourfold-BA}
d((ab)c)=d((ba)c)=0.
\end{equation}

Let $u=ab$ or $u=ba$, and choose a decomposition
$$
u=p+q,
\qquad
p\in A,\quad q\in B.
$$
For $c\in A$ and $d\in B$, identities
\eqref{eq:ito-same-summand}, \eqref{eq:ito-fourfold-AB},
and~\eqref{eq:ito-fourfold-BA} imply
$$
\begin{aligned}
p(cd)&=-c(pd)=-c(ud)=0,\\
q(cd)&=-2q(dc)=2d(qc)=2d(uc)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $pd=ud$ because $qd=0$, and $qc=uc$ because $pc=0$.
Therefore
$$
u(cd)=p(cd)+q(cd)=0.
$$
Moreover, the second equality in~\eqref{eq:ito-same-summand}
gives $p(dc)=-2p(cd)=0$, while its first equality gives
$$
q(dc)=-d(qc)=-d(uc)=0.
$$
Thus $u(dc)=0$ as well.

Finally, $A^2=B^2=0$ implies
$$
Z^2=(A+B)(A+B)=AB+BA.
$$
Every product of two spanning elements of $AB+BA$ is zero by
the preceding calculation. By bilinearity, $
(Z^2)^2=0$.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:ito-counterexamples}
The analogue of It\^o's theorem does not hold for
mono Zinbiel algebras. Let $Z$ be the three-dimensional
algebra over $\mathbb F$ with basis $e_1,e_2,e_3$ and nonzero
products
$$
e_1e_3=e_3,\qquad e_2e_3=e_1,\qquad
e_3e_1=-e_3,\qquad e_3e_2=-e_1.
$$
All other products of basis elements are zero.

The algebra $Z$ is anticommutative, so $x^2=0$ for every $x\in Z$.
Consequently, every one-generated subalgebra
$\langle x\rangle=\mathbb F x$ has zero multiplication and is
therefore a Zinbiel algebra. Thus $Z$ is mono Zinbiel.

Set
$
A=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F}\{e_1,e_2\}$
and $B=\mathbb F e_3$. 
Then $Z=A\oplus B$ and $A^2=B^2=0$.
Nevertheless,
$$
(e_2e_3)(e_1e_3)=e_1e_3=e_3\neq0,
$$
so $Z$ is not metabelian. More explicitly,
$
Z^2=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F}\{e_1,e_3\}$
and $(Z^2)^2=\mathbb F e_3\neq0$.
Hence the binary assumption in
Theorem~\ref{thm:ito-binary-zinbiel} cannot be replaced by
the mono assumption.
