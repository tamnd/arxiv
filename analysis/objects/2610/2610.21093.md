<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:exact-flow}
For a fixed point of \(U\cap W\), the nonlinear factor \(y \mapsto y(1+kcy^k)^{-1/k}\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:inverse-general} is the local flow at time one of \(-cy^{k+1}\partial/\partial y\); its inverse is the local flow at time one of \(cy^{k+1}\partial/\partial y\). On the complement of the zero section, the functions \(u_U=y_U^{-k}\) and \(u_W=y_W^{-k}\) satisfy
 
\begin{equation*}                             
 y_W^{-k}=g^k(y_U^{-k}+kc).
\end{equation*}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Solving \(dy/ds=cy^{k+1}\), \(y(0)=y_0\), gives \(y(s)=y_0(1-kscy_0^k)^{-1/k}\).  Replacing \(c\) by \(-c\) gives the nonlinear factor in Equation~\eqref{eq:inverse-general}.  The affine identity follows by raising Equation~\eqref{eq:inverse-general} to the power \(-k\).

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:convergent-realization}
After shrinking the fiber directions, Equation~\eqref{eq:inverse-general} defines a smooth Hausdorff surface \(X_\zeta\) containing a compact zero section \(E \simeq C\).  The product projections induce a submersion \(\rho:X_\zeta \to C\), and
 
\[
 \mathcal{I}_E/\mathcal{I}_E^2 \simeq A,\quad
 N_{E/X_\zeta} \simeq A^{-1},\quad E^2=-d.
\]
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For \(x \in U\cap W\), write
 
\[
 \phi_x(y)=g(x)^{-1}y(1+kc(x)y^k)^{-1/k}.
\]
 
The inverse map is
 
\[
 \psi_x(w)=g(x)w (1-kc(x)g(x)^kw^k )^{-1/k}.
\]
 
The functions \(g^{\pm1}\) and \(c\) are bounded on a neighborhood of the compact set \(\overline{U\cap W}\). Since the chosen roots have value one at the zero section, direct substitution gives \(\psi_x(\phi_x(y))=y\) and \(\phi_x(\psi_x(w))=w\) whenever the compositions are defined sufficiently near zero.  We may therefore choose \(\epsilon>0\), uniformly in \(x\), so that both displayed maps are holomorphic and their fiber derivatives are nonzero whenever the relevant fiber coordinate has modulus less than \(2\epsilon\).

Let \(\mathbb D_\epsilon=\{y \in \mathbb C:|y|<\epsilon\}\). Put \(\mathcal U=U\times\mathbb D_\epsilon\) and \(\mathcal W=W\times\mathbb D_\epsilon\), and set
 
\[
 \Omega_U=\{(x,y) \in (U\cap W)\times\mathbb D_\epsilon:
                   |\phi_x(y)|<\epsilon\}.
\]
 
Then
 
\[
 \Phi:\Omega_U \to \Omega_W:=\Phi(\Omega_U),
 \quad (x,y) \mapsto (x,\phi_x(y)),
\]
 
is a biholomorphism, with inverse induced by \(\psi_x\).  Glue the two charts along this pair of open sets.  The graph of \(\Phi\) is closed in \(\mathcal U\times\mathcal W\): if \(((x_n,y_n),\Phi(x_n,y_n))\) converges in the product, then the limiting base point lies in \(U\cap W\), the formula for \(\Phi\) extends there, and both limiting fiber coordinates still have modulus \(<\epsilon\), so the limit belongs to the graph.  Together with the diagonal pieces, this graph and its inverse form a closed equivalence relation on \(\mathcal U\sqcup\mathcal W\).  The quotient map is open, because saturation under an open biholomorphic identification preserves open sets.  Hence the quotient is a Hausdorff complex surface. Its zero sections glue to a compact curve \(E \simeq C\), and the product projections glue because the transition preserves the base.

Modulo \(\mathcal{I}_E^2\), Equation~\eqref{eq:inverse-general} reads \(y_U=g y_W\).  Thus the conormal transition agrees with that of \(A\), so \(\mathcal{I}_E/\mathcal{I}_E^2 \simeq A\).  Dualizing gives \(N_{E/X_\zeta} \simeq A^{-1}\), and hence \(E^2=-d\).

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:ruled-cyclic-root}
After shrinking the neighborhood of \(E\), the quotient \(Y_\zeta\) is biholomorphic over \(C\) to a neighborhood of \(D_\zeta\) in \(\mathbb P_C(\mathcal E_\zeta)\). The map \(q\) is a cyclic cover of degree \(k\), locally obtained by adjoining a \(k\)-th root of a defining function for \(D_\zeta\). For \(k>1\), it is totally ramified along \(E\); for \(k=1\), it is an isomorphism.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The transition is \(\mu_k\)-equivariant, and the quotient coordinates \(z_i=y_i^k\) satisfy
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ruled-z-transition}
 z_W=\frac{g^{-k}z_U}{1+kcz_U}.
\end{equation}
 
Equivalently, \(u_i=z_i^{-1}\) satisfies \(u_W=g^k(u_U+kc)\). Consider the rank-two bundle with transition
 
\begin{equation*}                                        
 \begin{pmatrix}Z_W\\ T_W\end{pmatrix}
 =
 \begin{pmatrix}g^{-k}&0\\ kc&1\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}Z_U\\ T_U\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
 
The invariant line \(Z=0\) has identity transition and hence defines the subbundle \(\mathcal{O}_C\). The quotient has transition \(g^{-k}\), so it is \(A^{-k}\). The off-diagonal entry gives the difference of the two local splittings. Under \(\operatorname{Ext}^1(A^{-k},\mathcal{O}_C) \simeq H^1(C,A^k)\), this difference is represented by the cocycle with local coefficient \(kc\).  Its class is therefore \(k\zeta\), up to the Čech sign convention.  On \(T_i \ne 0\), projectivization with \(z_i=Z_i/T_i\) recovers Equation~\eqref{eq:ruled-z-transition}, identifying \(Y_\zeta\) with the asserted neighborhood of the distinguished section. The quotient map is locally \((x_i,y_i) \mapsto (x_i,y_i^k)\), hence finite of degree \(k\) and unramified away from \(E\).  For \(k>1\) it has ramification index \(k\) along \(E\); for \(k=1\) it is an isomorphism. The covering is defined on a neighborhood of the section; an extension to the entire ruled surface is not needed here.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:dependence-class}
Up to isomorphism over \(C\), the neighborhood is independent of the auxiliary discs and local generators used in its construction. Cohomologous cocycles give isomorphic neighborhoods.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
First fix the cover and its local generators.  Suppose that \(c'\) and \(c\) represent the same class, and write
 
\[
 c'-c=h_U-g^{-k}h_W
\]
 
for holomorphic zero-cochains \(h_U,h_W\).  On the punctured fibers, set \(u_i=y_i^{-k}\) and put
 
\[
 u_i'=u_i-kh_i.
\]
 
Then
 
\begin{align*}
 u_W'
 &=g^k(u_U+kc)-kh_W\\
 &=g^k (u_U'+k(c+h_U-g^{-k}h_W) )
 =g^k(u_U'+kc').
\end{align*}
 
Although \(y_i^{-k}\) is not a local coordinate along the zero section, the corresponding changes in the original fiber coordinates are holomorphic there. They are given by
 
\[
 y_i'=y_i(1-kh_i y_i^k)^{-1/k}.
\]
 
After shrinking the fiber directions, they are holomorphic biholomorphisms fixing the zero section and preserving the retraction.

Next replace the local generators by
 
\[
 a_U'=s_Ua_U,\quad a_W'=s_Wa_W,
 \quad s_U \in \mathcal{O}^*(U),\quad s_W \in \mathcal{O}^*(W).
\]
 
Then
 
\[
 g'=s_Ug s_W^{-1},\quad c'=s_U^{-k}c.
\]
 
With \(y_U'=s_Uy_U\) and \(y_W'=s_Wy_W\), one has
 
\[
 1+kc'(y_U')^k=1+kcy_U^k,
\]
 
and the transition in the primed coordinates is again Equation~\eqref{eq:inverse-general}.  Thus changing local generators does not change the neighborhood over \(C\).

Two choices of covers and local generators admit a finite common refinement. On this refinement, the preceding coordinate changes identify the constructions. Shrinking the charts while retaining a cover of the compact curve makes the coefficients and their inverses bounded on the relevant compact closures. A common fiber radius then makes all changes holomorphic, and the overlap identities glue them to a biholomorphism of smaller neighborhoods over \(C\).

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:contraction-general}
The curve \(E\) contracts to a normal surface \((V_\zeta,0)\). The image point is smooth if and only if \(C \simeq \mathbb P^1\) and \(d=1\). In every other case the image point is an isolated normal surface singularity, and \(X_\zeta \to V_\zeta\) is its minimal good resolution.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Grauert's theorem \cite[\S3, Satz~9]{Grauert1962} gives a contraction
 
\[
 f:X_\zeta \to Y
\]
 
whose exceptional set is \(E\).  The restriction of \(f\) away from \(E\) is a biholomorphism, so \(Y\setminus\{f(E)\}\) is smooth.  Let
 
\[
 \nu:\widetilde Y \to Y
\]
 
be the normalization.  Since \(X_\zeta\) is normal and \(f\) is bimeromorphic, the universal property of normalization gives a lift
 
\[
 \widetilde f:X_\zeta \to \widetilde Y,
 \quad \nu\circ\widetilde f=f.
\]
 
The map \(\widetilde f\) is proper and is a biholomorphism away from \(E\). Its image contains \(\widetilde Y\setminus\nu^{-1}(f(E))\) and is closed because \(\widetilde f\) is proper; hence it is surjective.  Since the normalization map is finite, \(\nu^{-1}(f(E))\) is finite.  Since \(E\) is connected and
 
\[
 \widetilde f(E) \subset \nu^{-1}(f(E)),
\]
 
the set \(\widetilde f(E)\) consists of a single point.  Surjectivity then shows that \(\nu^{-1}(f(E))\) consists of that point alone.  Replacing \(Y\) by \(\widetilde Y\), we obtain the asserted contraction to a normal surface \((V_\zeta,0)\).

If the image point were smooth, the factorization theorem for modifications of smooth surfaces would force the exceptional set to contain a rational \((-1)\)-curve.  Since the exceptional set consists only of \(E\), this gives \(C \simeq \mathbb P^1\) and \(d=1\).  Conversely, in that case \(A \simeq \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb P^1}(1)\) and \(H^1(\mathbb P^1,A^k)=0\), so \(\zeta=0\).  Proposition~\ref{prop:dependence-class} identifies the neighborhood over \(C\) with a neighborhood of the zero section in \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb P^1}(-1)\), whose contraction is smooth.

In all remaining cases the image point is singular.  Its punctured representative is isomorphic to the smooth surface \(X_\zeta\setminus E\), so the singularity is isolated.  Since \(E\) is smooth, the resolution is good.  In the remaining cases \(E\) is either nonrational or has \(E^2=-d \leq -2\), so the exceptional set contains no rational \((-1)\)-curve.  Hence the resolution is minimal good.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:smooth-cutoff-comparison}
The map \(E_N \to C\) is finite flat of degree \(N\), and the restrictions of the two charts to \(E_N\) form a Leray cover.  If \(\mathcal G\) is locally free on \(X_\zeta\), then
 
\[
 H^q(X_\zeta,\mathcal G\mathcal{I}^N) = 0\quad(q \geq 1, N \gg 0),
\]
 
and restriction induces an isomorphism
 
\[
 H^1(X_\zeta,\mathcal G)
 \xrightarrow{ \mathrm{res} }
 H^1(E_N,\mathcal G|_{E_N})
\]
 
for \(N \gg 0\).  Moreover \(H^2(X_\zeta,\mathcal G) = 0\).
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Locally, \(\mathcal{O}_{E_N}\) is free over the base with basis \(1,y_i,\ldots,y_i^{N-1}\), and its transition matrix is triangular with invertible diagonal.  Thus \(E_N \to C\) is finite flat of degree \(N\). The inverse images of \(U\), \(W\), and \(U\cap W\) under this finite map are Stein.  Hence the restrictions of the two charts form a Leray cover.

Since the normal bundle \(A^{-1}\) has negative degree, the curve \(E\) satisfies the negativity hypothesis in Grauert's vanishing theorem for neighborhoods of exceptional analytic sets. After shrinking the fixed representative, we may assume that \(X_\zeta\) is a sufficiently small strongly pseudoconvex neighborhood of \(E\).  Grauert's vanishing theorem \cite[\S4, Satz~2, p.~357]{Grauert1962} for neighborhoods of exceptional analytic sets, applied to the coherent sheaf \(\mathcal G\), gives
 
\[
 H^q(X_\zeta,\mathcal G\mathcal{I}^N) = 0
 \quad(q \geq 1, N \gg 0).
\]
 
The exact sequence
 
\[
 0 \to \mathcal G\mathcal{I}^N
\to \mathcal G
\to \mathcal G|_{E_N}
\to 0
\]
 
therefore gives
 
\[
 H^1(X_\zeta,\mathcal G)
 \xrightarrow{ \simeq }
 H^1(E_N,\mathcal G|_{E_N})
\]
 
for \(N \gg 0\).  Since \(E_N\) is a compact complex space of dimension one, every coherent sheaf on it has vanishing cohomology in degrees at least two. Consequently, the same exact sequence also gives
 
\[
 H^2(X_\zeta,\mathcal G) = 0.\qedhere
\]

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:first-differential}
Put \(q = j-\alpha\).  The first possible nonlinear differential is
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:d-k-general}
 d_k = (k\beta-\alpha-q)\mu_q^\zeta:
 H^0(C,A^q) \to H^1(C,A^{q+k}).
\end{equation}
 
Every differential whose filtration length is not divisible by \(k\) vanishes.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Equation~\eqref{eq:H-column-general} becomes
 
\[
 y_W^j\sigma_W = g^{-q}y_U^jG^{\beta-j/k}\sigma_U.
\]
 
The first term of positive fiber degree in \(G^{\beta-j/k}\) is \((k\beta-j)cy_U^k\).  With the preceding sign convention, its Čech class is exactly \((k\beta-\alpha-q)\zeta \smile f\). Every term of the binomial expansion changes the fiber degree by a multiple of \(k\).  Thus the complex is the direct sum of its subcomplexes indexed by the fiber degree modulo \(k\), and the same is true of its spectral sequence.  This proves the last assertion.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:successive-cochain-corrections}
For every \(v \in H^0\), there is a unique \(u \in C^0\) such that \(\pi u=v\) and \(h\widetilde D u=0\). It is given by
 
\[
u=A_Ri(v),
\quad
\widetilde D u=i\mathfrak K v.
\]
 
In particular, \(u\) is a section if and only if \(\mathfrak K v=0\).
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
The condition \(h\widetilde D u=0\), together with \(\pi u=v\), is equivalent to \(u=i(v)+hRu\). Since \(hR\) is nilpotent, this equation has the unique solution \(u=A_Ri(v)\). Conversely, \(\pi h=0\) gives \(\pi A_Ri(v)=v\), so this solution satisfies both required conditions.

On \(C^1\), the identity \(\delta h=1-i\pi\) shows that a cochain killed by \(h\) belongs to \(i(H^1)\). Hence \(\widetilde D u=i\pi\widetilde D u\). Since \(\pi\delta=0\), we obtain
 
\[
\widetilde D u=i\pi Ru=i\pi R A_Ri(v)=i\mathfrak K v.
\]
 
The last assertion follows because \(i\) is injective.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:complete-schur}
The \v Cech complex of \(\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}|_{E_N}\) with respect to the cover \(\{U,W\}\) is filtered chain homotopy equivalent to
 
\[
H^0 \xrightarrow{\mathfrak K} H^1.
\]
 
In particular,
 
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
H^0(E_N,\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}|_{E_N})
& \simeq \ker\mathfrak K,\\
H^1(E_N,\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}|_{E_N})
& \simeq \operatorname{coker}\mathfrak K.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
 
Consequently,
 
\begin{equation*}                    
h^1(E_N,\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}|_{E_N})
= \sum_{j = 0}^{N-1}h^1(C,A^{q_j})-\operatorname{rk}\mathfrak K.
\end{equation*}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The chosen maps \(i,\pi,h\) satisfy the hypotheses of the Main Perturbation Lemma for \((C^\bullet,-\delta)\) and \((H^\bullet,0)\). Since \(R\) strictly raises the filtration degree, \(hR\) and \(Rh\) are nilpotent. Crainic~\cite[\S2.2, \S2.4, and \S3.2]{Crainic2004} therefore gives the transferred differential
 
\[
\pi B_RRi=\mathfrak K
\]
 
and filtered chain maps
 
\[
I:(H^\bullet,\mathfrak K) \to (C^\bullet,\widetilde D),
\quad
P:(C^\bullet,\widetilde D) \to (H^\bullet,\mathfrak K),
\]
 
where
 
\[
\begin{aligned}
I^0 &= A_Ri, & I^1 &= i,\\
P^0 &= \pi, & P^1 &= \pi B_R.
\end{aligned}
\]
 
They satisfy
 
\[
PI=1,\quad
IP=1+\widetilde D H+H\widetilde D,
\quad H=hB_R=A_Rh.
\]
 
Thus they give a filtered chain homotopy equivalence. Their associated graded maps are \(i\) and \(\pi\), respectively, and induce the stated identifications on the \(E_1\)-page. Taking cohomology gives the kernel and cokernel formulas. The dimension formula follows.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:schur-differentials}
Let \(q = j-\alpha\), where \(0 \leq j < N\), and let \(n \geq 1\). A class \(v_0 \in H^0(C,A^q)\) defines a class on the \(E_{nk}\)-page if and only if there exist

\]
 
such that
 
\[

\quad 1 \leq m < n.
\]

d_{nk}[v_0]
=
\left[
\sum_{r = 1}^{n}\mathfrak K_rv_{n-r}
\right]
\end{equation}
 
in \(E_{nk}^{j+nk,1}\). This target is the quotient of \(H^1(C,A^{q+nk})\) by the boundaries from the earlier pages; it is zero if \(j+nk \geq N\). In particular,
 
\[
\mathfrak K_1|_{H^0(C,A^q)}
=
(k\beta-\alpha-q)\mu_q^\zeta
\]
 
for \(q = j-\alpha\) with \(0 \leq j < N-k\).

\label{prop:transferred-diagonal-equivalence}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q-L-rank-equality}

<!-- proposition-9: body_md -->
\label{prop:exceptional-column-rank}
One has
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Jk-tail-kernel}
J_k = \{v \in H^0(C,A^k):
      \mathfrak C_kv \in \operatorname{im}\mathfrak T_k\}.
\end{equation}
 
Consequently,
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exceptional-column-rank}
\widehat\rho_k = \operatorname{rk}[\mathfrak C_k\quad\mathfrak T_k]-\operatorname{rk}\mathfrak T_k.
\end{equation}
 
Moreover,
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exceptional-column-rank-bounds}
\rho_k \leq \widehat\rho_k \leq h^0(C,A^k).
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-9 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
For \(M \geq N\), the exact sequence
 
\[
 0 \to A^M \to \mathcal{O}_{E_{M+1}}
\to \mathcal{O}_{E_M} \to 0
\]
 
and \(H^1(C,A^M) = 0\) show that \(H^0(E_{M+1},\mathcal{O}_{E_{M+1}}) \to H^0(E_M,\mathcal{O}_{E_M})\) is surjective. Consequently every function on \(E_N\) lifts recursively to a compatible formal function, and restriction gives the converse. Since \(k < N\), the space \(J_k\) is therefore determined on \(E_N\).

The maps \(I^0=A_Ri\) and \(P^0=\pi\) in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-schur} restrict to mutually inverse maps between \(\ker\mathfrak K_{0,0}\) and the space of functions on \(E_N\). Both maps preserve the filtration. Moreover, \(I^0-i\) strictly increases the filtration degree, so these maps preserve the leading coefficient of a function. \[
 \frac{\operatorname{im}\mathfrak C_k+
       \operatorname{im}\mathfrak T_k}
      {\operatorname{im}\mathfrak T_k}.
\]
 
Its dimension is the rank difference in Equation~\eqref{eq:exceptional-column-rank}, which proves the codimension formula. Since \(J_k\) is defined by extension of formal functions, this formula also proves independence from the homotopy data.

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:units-picard-injection}
Let \(C\) be a smooth projective curve in a smooth surface \(Y\), put \(A = \mathcal O_Y(-C)|_C\), and let \(C_j\) be defined by \(\mathcal O_Y(-jC)\). Suppose that, for some \(k \geq 1\), there is an isomorphism of \(\mathcal O_C\)-algebras
 
\[
\mathcal O_{C_{k+1}} \simeq \mathcal O_C\oplus A\oplus\cdots\oplus A^k,
\]
 
with the usual multiplication truncated in degree \(k+1\), under which restriction to \(C_k\) discards the summand \(A^k\). Then restriction is surjective on global units, and the exact sequence of units induces an injection
 
\[
H^1(C,A^k) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Pic}(C_{k+1}).
\]
 
Its image is the kernel of \(\operatorname{Pic}(C_{k+1}) \to \operatorname{Pic}(C_k)\).
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-25: body_md -->
A global section of \(\mathcal O_{C_k}\) lifts to \(C_{k+1}\) by taking its degree-\(k\) coefficient to be zero. A section of either truncated algebra is a unit precisely when its degree-zero coefficient is a unit on \(C\). Thus every global unit on \(C_k\) lifts to a global unit on \(C_{k+1}\).

Let \(\mathcal I = \mathcal O_Y(-C)\). Since \(\mathcal I^k/\mathcal I^{k+1}\) has square zero, the map \(u \mapsto 1+u\) identifies its additive group with \(1+\mathcal I^k/\mathcal I^{k+1}\). The exact sequence of units therefore gives
 
\[
\begin{aligned}
H^0(C_{k+1},\mathcal O_{C_{k+1}}^*)& \to H^0(C_k,\mathcal O_{C_k}^*)\\
& \to H^1(C,A^k) \to \operatorname{Pic}(C_{k+1}) \to \operatorname{Pic}(C_k).
\end{aligned}
\]
 
Surjectivity of the first map proves both assertions about the Picard groups.

<!-- lemma-13: body_md -->
\label{lem:equivariant-linearization-general}
Let \(\pi:(X,E) \to (V,0)\) be the minimal good resolution of a normal surface singularity with an effective good \(\mathbb C^*\)-action. Suppose that \(E\) is a smooth irreducible curve of genus at least two. Then a neighborhood of \(E\) is equivariantly biholomorphic to a neighborhood of the zero section in \(N_{E/X}\), with respect to the induced action on the normal bundle.
<!-- end lemma-13 -->
<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
The action lifts to the minimal good resolution; see \cite[Section~2.1]{CamachoMovasatiScardua2009}. Since \(\operatorname{Aut}(E)\) is finite and \(\mathbb C^*\) is connected, the lifted action fixes \(E\) pointwise. Its action on each normal space is a character \(\lambda \mapsto \lambda^m\). The integer \(m\) is locally constant, and hence constant on \(E\).

Fix a point of \(E\) and choose a holomorphic coordinate map to its tangent space whose derivative is the identity. Averaging this map over \(S^1\) after composing with the inverse tangent representation gives an equivariant holomorphic map with the same derivative. The holomorphic inverse function theorem therefore gives equivariant local coordinates, as in \cite{Bochner1945}. Thus each point of \(E\) has an \(S^1\)-invariant neighborhood with holomorphic coordinates \((z_i,y_i)\) such that
 
\[
E = (y_i = 0),\quad
\lambda\cdot(z_i,y_i) = (z_i,\lambda^m y_i)
\quad(\lambda \in S^1).
\]
 
If \(m = 0\), the action is trivial on such a neighborhood. The identity theorem, first in the group parameter and then on \(X\), would make the entire action trivial, contrary to effectiveness. Thus \(m \ne 0\).

On an overlap, write the coordinate change as
 
\[
z_j = F_{ij}(z_i,y_i),\quad
y_j = G_{ij}(z_i,y_i).
\]
 
Equivariance gives
 
\[
\begin{aligned}
F_{ij}(z_i,\lambda^m y_i)
& = F_{ij}(z_i,y_i),\\
G_{ij}(z_i,\lambda^m y_i)
& = \lambda^m G_{ij}(z_i,y_i)
\end{aligned}
\quad(\lambda \in S^1).
\]
 
Expanding in powers of \(y_i\) and using \(m \ne 0\), we obtain
 
\[
z_j = f_{ij}(z_i),\quad
y_j = a_{ij}(z_i)y_i,
\]
 
where \(a_{ij}\) is nowhere zero. The functions \(f_{ij}\) are the coordinate changes on \(E\), and the functions \(a_{ij}\) are the transition functions for the fiber coordinates of \(N_{E/X}\).

Thus the local identifications with the normal bundle agree on overlaps. After shrinking about \(E\), they give a biholomorphism from a neighborhood of \(E\) to a neighborhood of the zero section in \(N_{E/X}\), restricting to the identity on \(E\). This biholomorphism is \(S^1\)-equivariant by construction. The identity theorem in the group parameter makes it \(\mathbb C^*\)-equivariant wherever the local actions are defined.

<!-- lemma-14: body_md -->
\label{lem:hyper-curve}
The curve \(C\) has genus \(g\) and a unique point \(p\) over infinity. There is a local parameter \(t\) at \(p\) for which
 
\[
 x=t^{-2},\quad
 z=t^{-(2g+1)}(1-t^{4g+2})^{1/2}.
\]
 
The semigroup of pole orders at \(p\) is \(\langle 2,2g+1\rangle\). If \(A=\mathcal{O}_C(p)\), then
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hyper-canonical}
 K_C \simeq A^a,\quad
 \frac{dx}{z}=-2t^a(1-t^{4g+2})^{-1/2} dt.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-14 -->
<!-- proof-30: body_md -->
The map \(x:C \to \mathbb P^1\) is a double cover, simply branched at the \(2g+1\) roots of unity and at infinity. Thus there is one point \(p\) over infinity, and the Riemann--Hurwitz formula gives genus \(g\). Since \(x\) has a pole of order two at \(p\), we may choose \(t\) so that \(x=t^{-2}\). The equation of the curve then gives the stated expression for \(z\), with the holomorphic square root equal to one at \(t=0\), after replacing \(t\) by \(-t\) if necessary.

The functions \(x\) and \(z\) have no poles away from \(p\), and their pole orders are \(2\) and \(2g+1\). Hence the pole semigroup contains \(\langle 2,2g+1\rangle\). The latter has precisely the \(g\) gaps \(1,3,\ldots,2g-1\). By the Weierstrass gap theorem, the pole semigroup at \(p\) also has precisely \(g\) gaps, so the two semigroups agree. The differential \(dx/z\) is regular and nonzero away from \(p\), including at the finite branch points. Its expansion at \(p\) is the one in Equation~\eqref{eq:hyper-canonical}, so its divisor is \(ap\). This proves \(K_C \simeq A^a\).

<!-- lemma-16: body_md -->
\label{lem:hyper-ranks}
For \(0 \leq q \leq a-k\),
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hyper-rank-formula}
 \rho_q(\zeta)=
 \min\left\{
 \left\lfloor\frac q2\right\rfloor+1,
 \left\lfloor\frac{a-k-q}{2}\right\rfloor+1,
 s+1
 \right\}.
\end{equation}
 
In particular,
 
\begin{equation*}                               
 \rho_0=1,\quad \rho_k=s+1.
\end{equation*}
<!-- end lemma-16 -->
<!-- proof-32: body_md -->
After omitting the common nonzero factor \(-2\), the matrix of the Serre-dual pairing has entry \(b_{i+j}\), where \(b_v=0\) for \(v>s\). Put
 
\[
 I=\left\lfloor\frac q2\right\rfloor,\quad
 J=\left\lfloor\frac{a-k-q}{2}\right\rfloor.
\]
 
Then \(\max\{I,J\} \geq s\). Indeed, \(a-k=k+2r-2 \geq 4r-4 \geq 4s\). If both \(I\) and \(J\) were at most \(s-1\), then \(q \leq 2s-1\) and \(a-k-q \leq 2s-1\), contrary to \(a-k \geq 4s\). The case \(s=0\) is immediate.

If \(J \geq s\), use the rows \(0,\ldots,\min\{I,s\}\) and the columns \(s,s-1,\ldots,s-\min\{I,s\}\), in that order. The resulting square matrix is triangular with diagonal entries \(b_s\). Its determinant is nonzero because \(b_s \ne 0\). The case \(I \geq s\) is symmetric. On the other hand, the matrix has \(I+1\) rows and \(J+1\) columns, and every row or column with index greater than \(s\) is zero. Thus its rank is exactly \(\min\{I+1,J+1,s+1\}\), proving Equation~\eqref{eq:hyper-rank-formula}.

The equality \(\rho_0=1\) follows either from this formula or from \(\mu_0^\zeta(1)=\zeta \ne 0\). At \(q=k\), both monomial bases contain \(1,x,\ldots,x^s\), since \(k \geq 2r-2 \geq 2s\) and \(a-2k=2r-2 \geq 2s\). Hence \(\rho_k=s+1\).

 R_1v_1 &= \theta(\theta-k)\phi(Z-\phi x^i),\\

 \end{aligned}

Adding these terms, we obtain
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hyper-second-residual}
 R_1v_1+R_2v_0

With the sign convention fixed before Lemma~\ref{lem:first-differential}, its class represents \(d_{2k}[x^i]\).

\[
H^0(C,K_C\otimes A^{-(q+2k)})
=H^0(C,K_C(-(q+2k)p)).

 \quad 0 \leq w \leq \left\lfloor\frac{a-q-2k}{2}\right\rfloor.

By Equation~\eqref{eq:hyper-canonical}, the coefficient of \(dt\) in each basis differential has only even exponents. <!-- lemma-19: body_md -->
\label{lem:hyper-collapse}
For every \(\zeta \in W_s\setminus W_{s-1}\) and every
 
\[
 (\alpha,\beta) \in \{(0,0),(0,1),(a,0),(a,1)\},
\]
 
the spectral sequence associated with the filtration of \(\mathcal H_{\alpha,\beta}\) by powers of \(\mathcal I_E\) has first possible nonzero differential given by Equation~\eqref{eq:d-k-general}. Every later differential vanishes.
<!-- end lemma-19 -->
<!-- proof-35: body_md -->
Only \(q \geq 0\) can contribute to a differential, since its source is a subspace of \(H^0(C,A^q)\). A differential of filtration degree \(\ell k\) can have a nonzero target only if \(q+\ell k \leq a\), because \(H^1(C,A^n) = 0\) for \(n > a\). Thus, for \(\ell \geq 2\),
 
\[
 0 \leq q \leq a-2k = 2r-2.
\]
 
Moreover,
 
\[
 a-k-q \geq a-k-(2r-2) = k \geq 2r-2 \geq 2s.
\]
 
Consequently, the monomial basis of the second factor in the Serre-dual pairing contains \(1,x,\ldots,x^s\). The triangular minor used in Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-ranks} shows that \(\mu_q^\zeta\) is injective on the span of \(1,x,\ldots,x^{\min\{s,\lfloor q/2\rfloor\}}\), whereas \(\mu_q^\zeta(x^i) = 0\) for every \(i > s\). Hence
 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hyper-kernel}
 \ker\mu_q^\zeta
 = \operatorname{Span}\{x^i:s < i \leq \lfloor q/2\rfloor\}.
\end{equation}
 
Put \(j = q+\alpha\). If \(k\beta-\alpha-q = 0\), then \(\beta-j/k = 0\), so the factor \(G^{\beta-j/k}\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:H-column-general} is identically one. A section in this summand therefore extends with no higher correction. Otherwise, the kernel of the first differential is \(\ker\mu_q^\zeta\). By Equation~\eqref{eq:hyper-kernel}, this kernel is spanned by the sections treated in Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-first-two-obstructions}. Thus \(d_{2k} = 0\) in every source degree.

Finally,
 
\[
 a-3k = 3r-g-2 \leq 0.
\]
 
Equality occurs only when \(g \equiv 1\pmod 3\), in which case \(a = 3k\) and \(q = 0\) is the only possible source degree for \(d_{3k}\).  \widehat\rho_k=\operatorname{codim}J_k
 =\sum_{\ell \geq 1}

For a function with coefficient of positive order \(j\), the first obstruction is \(-j\mu_j^\zeta\). Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-collapse} shows that all later obstructions vanish. Thus \(J_j=\ker\mu_j^\zeta\). For \(1 \leq j \leq N\), Serre duality identifies this kernel with the annihilator of \(S_{N-j}\) under the pairing \((f,h) \mapsto \lambda(fh)\). For \(j>N=a-k\), the first obstruction target \(H^1(C,A^{j+k})\) is zero. This proves Equation~\eqref{eq:hyper-embedding-coefficients}. Since \(N<2g+1\), the pole semigroup in Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-curve} gives \(\dim S_j=\lfloor j/2\rfloor+1\) for \(0 \leq j \leq N\). The dimension formula now follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-ranks}. The ideal assertion is Lemma~\ref{lem:coefficient-ideal}.
<!-- end proof-47 -->
<!-- proposition-14: body_md -->
\label{prop:hyper-negative-embedding}
Every member of the hyperelliptic family in Theorem~\ref{thm:hyper-interval} with \(s \geq 1\) has embedding dimension at least six.
<!-- end proposition-14 -->
<!-- proof-48: body_md -->
Use the notation of Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-function-coefficients}.

Assume \(s \geq 1\). Since \(N \geq 4s\), the dimension formula in Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-function-coefficients} gives \(J_1=J_2=0\). It also gives \(J_3=0\), except when \((g,s)=(4,1)\). Outside this exceptional case, \(\nu \geq 4\). Choose \(0 \ne f \in J_\nu\). Since \(J_+\) is an ideal and \(S\) is a domain, multiplication by \(f\) embeds \(S_j\) in \(J_{\nu+j}\) for \(0 \leq j \leq 3\). These degrees are less than \(2\nu\), so Equation~\eqref{eq:hyper-embedding-first-orders} gives
 
\[
 \operatorname{edim}(V_\zeta,0) \geq \sum_{j=0}^3h^0(C,A^j)=1+1+2+2=6.
\]
 
When \((g,s)=(4,1)\), one has \(N=4\), \(\nu=3\), and \(\dim J_3=1\), \(\dim J_4=2\), \(\dim J_5=3\). The same inequality again gives the lower bound six.

<!-- proposition-15: body_md -->
\label{prop:hyper-zero-embedding}
Every member of the hyperelliptic family in Theorem~\ref{thm:hyper-interval} with \(s=0\) has embedding dimension at least five.
<!-- end proposition-15 -->
<!-- proof-49: body_md -->
Use the notation of Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-function-coefficients}.

Assume \(s=0\). By Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-residue}, \(\lambda\) is a nonzero multiple of the constant-coefficient functional. Equation~\eqref{eq:hyper-embedding-coefficients} gives
 
\[
 J_1=0,\quad J_j=xS_{j-2}\quad(2 \leq j \leq N),\quad J_j=S_j\quad(j>N).
\]
 
Here \(x\) is regarded as a section of \(A^2\), and the same meromorphic function in different degrees represents distinct homogeneous elements of \(S\). In every case \(\nu=2\). Multiplication \(\operatorname{Sym}^2J_2 \to J_4\) is injective: \(J_2\) is one-dimensional when \(g \geq 3\), while for \(g=2\) the products of the basis \(1,x\) are the independent sections \(1,x,x^2\). Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-cotangent-degrees} therefore computes the graded cotangent space through degree \(k+4\).

If \(g \geq 4\), then \(N \geq 4\) and \(k \geq 2\). For \(2 \leq q \leq 6\), the section represented by \(x\) belongs to \(J_q\). Every product contributing to \((J_+^2)_q\) has two factors of degrees between two and four. Both factors are divisible by \(x\), since their degrees are at most \(N\). Thus the class of \(x\) in \(J_q/(J_+^2)_q\) is nonzero. The five degrees \(2,3,4,5,6\) all satisfy \(q \leq k+4\), and give the required lower bound five.

For \(g=2,3\), the dimensions of \(J_q/(J_+^2)_q\) in the degrees needed below are as follows:
 
\[
 \begin{array}{c|cccc|c}
 g\backslash q&2&3&4&5&\text{sum}\\
 \hline
 2&2&2&0&1&5\\
 3&1&2&2&1&6
 \end{array}
\]
 
We check these dimensions directly. If \(g=3\), then \((k,N)=(2,2)\), and the pole semigroup \(\langle 2,7\rangle\) gives
 
\[
 J_2=\operatorname{Span}\{x\},\quad J_3=\operatorname{Span}\{1,x\},\quad J_q=\operatorname{Span}\{1,x,x^2\}\quad(q=4,5).
\]
 
The products in degrees four and five span \(\{x^2\}\) and \(\{x,x^2\}\), respectively. This gives the second row of the table, and Lemma~\ref{lem:hyper-cotangent-degrees} gives the lower bound six.

If \(g=2\), then \((k,N)=(1,1)\), and \(J_j=S_j\) for every \(j \geq 2\). The pole semigroup \(\langle 2,5\rangle\) shows that both \(J_2\) and \(J_3\) have bases represented by \(1,x\), while \(J_4\) has basis \(1,x,x^2\). Its three basis elements are products from \(J_2\), so the quotient in degree four is zero. Moreover,
 
\[
 J_5=\operatorname{Span}\{1,x,x^2,z\},\quad (J_+^2)_5=\operatorname{Span}\{1,x,x^2\}.
\]
 
This gives the first row of the table. The degrees two, three, and five are at most \(k+4=5\), so their quotient dimensions give the lower bound five.

<!-- lemma-27: body_md -->
\label{lem:plane-products}
For every integer \(m \geq 2\),
 
\begin{equation*}                          
W^m=P_{m\nu}.
\end{equation*}
<!-- end lemma-27 -->
<!-- proof-51: body_md -->
We use the following consequence of Lira--Ramos--Simis~\cite[arXiv version, Theorem~4.2(b) and Corollary~4.3]{LiraRamosSimis2023}: over a field of characteristic different from two, a homogeneous Gorenstein ideal \(I \subset \mathbb k[x_0,x_1,x_2]\) of height three, generated by \(2d+1\) forms of degree \(d \geq 2\), satisfies \(I^m=(x_0,x_1,x_2)^{md}\) for every \(m \geq 2\). Their corollary verifies the additional hypothesis of the theorem, namely that the natural map \(\operatorname{Sym}^2_{\mathbb k[x_0,x_1,x_2]}I \to I^2\) is an isomorphism.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:plane-algebra}, \(\mathfrak a\) is a homogeneous Gorenstein ideal of height three, generated in degree \(\nu\). Its minimal number of generators is \(\dim W=2s+3=2\nu+1\), and \(\nu \geq 3\). The cited result therefore applies over \(\mathbb C\). With \(\mathfrak n=(x_0,x_1,x_2)\), it gives
 
\[
 \mathfrak a^m=\mathfrak n^{m\nu}\quad(m \geq 2).
\]
 
Since \(\mathfrak a\) is generated by \(W\), the degree-\(m\nu\) component on the left is \(W^m\). The component of the same degree on the right is \(P_{m\nu}\), which proves the assertion.
<!-- end proof-51 -->
<!-- proposition-16: body_md -->
\label{prop:plane-embedding}
The singularity \((V_s,0)\) has embedding dimension
 
\[
 \operatorname{edim}(V_s,0)=2s+3 \geq 7.
\]
<!-- end proposition-16 -->
<!-- proof-52: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:plane-function-coefficients}, \(J_1=W\) and \(J_p=H^0(C_s,A^p)\) for \(p \geq 2\). Lemma~\ref{lem:plane-products} shows that products of \(p\) elements of \(W\) span \(P_{p\nu}\). Restriction to \(C_s\) is surjective, so these products span \(J_p\). Thus \(J\) is generated by \(J_1=W\).

Choose a basis \(w_1,\ldots,w_{2s+3}\) of \(W\) and lifts \(f_i \in \widehat{\mathfrak m}\). Lemma~\ref{lem:lift-homogeneous-generators} gives a surjection \(\mathbb C[[T_1,\ldots,T_{2s+3}]] \to \widehat R\). This homomorphism maps \((T_1,\ldots,T_{2s+3})\) onto \(\widehat{\mathfrak m}\), so the classes of the \(f_i\) span \(\widehat{\mathfrak m}/\widehat{\mathfrak m}^2\). Every element of \(\widehat{\mathfrak m}^2\) has order at least two along the exceptional curve. Hence a linear combination of the \(f_i\) lies in \(\widehat{\mathfrak m}^2\) only if the same combination of their degree-one coefficients is zero. Their classes form a basis of \(\widehat{\mathfrak m}/\widehat{\mathfrak m}^2\), and \(\operatorname{edim}(V_s,0)=\dim W=2s+3\).
<!-- end proof-52 -->
