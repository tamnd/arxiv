<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Fix $r\ge3$. For all sufficiently large $n$ and all integers $1\le q<n/r$ the following statements hold.

If $(r,s)\ne(3,2)$, then $\Lambda_r^<(n,q)=\lambda(E_{n,r,q})$,
and $E_{n,r,q}$ is the unique extremal graph up to isomorphism.

If $r=3$ and $s=2$, the unique extremal graph is $U_{m,q}$ when $q=1,2$, and it is $M_m$ when $q=3$. For $4\le q\le m$, $\Lambda_3^<(3m+2,q)=\max\{\lambda(U_{m,q}),\lambda(V_{m,q})\}$, and no other graph attains equality up to isomorphism. The real response functions of $U_{m,q}$ and $V_{m,q}$ have one crossing
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossing-location}
 q_*(m)=\frac{\sqrt{41}-5}{4}\,m+
 \frac{887-19\sqrt{41}}{128\sqrt{41}}+O(m^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Within $4\le q\le m$, only $U$ is extremal below this crossing, only $V$ above it, and both at an integer crossing.

\label{thm:closed}
Fix $r\ge3$. For all sufficiently large $n$ and every integer $1\le q<n/r$, $\Lambda_r^{\le}(n,q)=\lambda(T^*_{n,r,q})$,
and $T^*_{n,r,q}$ is the unique extremal graph up to isomorphism. For every fixed $0<\eta<1$, there is $c_{r,\eta}>0$ such that, for sufficiently large $n$ and $\eta m\le q<n/r$, every other graph with $N_{K_{r+1}}(G)\le q c_r(n)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:closed-gap}
 \lambda(T^*_{n,r,q})-\lambda(G)\ge c_{r,\eta}/n.
\end{equation}

\label{thm:matching-threshold}
Fix $r\ge3$. The supremum of the constants $\delta>0$ for which, for all sufficiently large $n$ and every integer $1\le q<\delta\sqrt n$,
\[
 \lambda(G)\ge\lambda(Y_{n,r,q})
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{K_{r+1}}(G)\ge q c_r(n)
\]
is $\delta_r^*=\sqrt2\,\frac{r-1}{r}$.
For each value of $s$, the real star and matching response roots have a unique crossing in every fixed window $a\sqrt n\le q\le b\sqrt n$ with $0<a<\delta_r^*<b$. Its location is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matching-location}
 \theta_{r,s}(n)=\delta_r^*\sqrt n+\beta_{r,s}+O_r(n^{-1/2}),
 \qquad
 \beta_{r,s}=\begin{cases}11/6,&(r,s)=(3,2),\\[1mm]1/2,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Here the strict star graph is $E_{n,r,q}$ when $(r,s)\ne(3,2)$ and is $U_{m,q}$ when $(r,s)=(3,2)$. The two small-$q$ exceptions are outside these windows.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:standard-completions}
Let
\[
 G=K_r(n_1,\ldots,n_r)+H-D,
 \qquad p_i=e(H_i),\quad p=\sum_i p_i,\quad z=|D|.
\]
Then, for a constant $C_r$ depending only on $r$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:standard-completions}
 \begin{split}
 N_{K_{r+1}}(G)\ge{}&\sum_i p_i\prod_{j\ne i}n_j\\
 &-C_r n^{r-2}\sum_{ab\in D}\bigl(d_H(a)+d_H(b)\bigr)
 -C_rpzn^{r-3}.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
In particular, the error in \eqref{eq:standard-completions} is at most $C_rpzn^{r-2}$. If a vertex $v$ meets all but $k$ internal edges, then one missing edge meeting $v$ destroys at most $C_rpn^{r-2}$ standard completions, while one missing edge avoiding $v$ destroys at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:standard-completions-hub}
 C_r\bigl((k+1)n^{r-2}+pn^{r-3}\bigr)
\end{equation}
standard completions.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The standard-completion families of distinct internal edges are disjoint: a clique in one such family has a unique part containing two vertices, and those two vertices recover the internal edge. Fix a missing cross-edge $ab$. If a standard completion containing $ab$ is assigned to an internal edge incident with $a$ or $b$, then after that internal edge and $ab$ are fixed there are at most $n^{r-2}$ choices. There are at most $d_H(a)+d_H(b)$ such internal edges. For every other internal edge there are at most $n^{r-3}$ choices, since both ends of $ab$ are then fixed among the vertices chosen outside the doubled part. A union bound over $D$ proves \eqref{eq:standard-completions}.

The crude bound follows from $d_H(a)+d_H(b)\le2p$. If all but $k$ internal edges meet $v$, every vertex other than $v$ has internal degree at most $k+1$. Applying the first estimate separately to missing edges meeting and avoiding $v$ gives \eqref{eq:standard-completions-hub}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:perron-coordinates}
Fix $r\ge3$. Let $A_\omega$ be obtained from $K_r(n_1,\ldots,n_r)$ by adding internal edges of total weight $P=O(n)$ and lowering the weights of a set of $Z=o(n)$ cross-edges. Suppose
\[
 \max_i|n_i-n/r|\le A=o(n),\qquad
 \lambda(A_\omega)=\alpha n+O(1).
\]
For all sufficiently large $n$, the weighted graph is connected. Let $x$ be its positive unit Perron vector and put $M=\max_u x_u$.
Then $M=O(n^{-1/2})$. If $E_0=A+P+Z+1=o(n)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flat-coordinates}
 x_u=\frac{1+O(E_0/n)}{\sqrt n}
\end{equation}
uniformly in $u$.

Suppose instead that a vertex $v$ is incident with internal edges of total weight $P_v$, and that the total weight of the other internal edges is at most $K=o(n)$. Put $E=A+K+Z+1$. Then, uniformly when $E=o(n)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hub-coordinates}
 x_u=\frac{1+O(E/n)}{\sqrt n}\quad(u\ne v),
 \qquad
 x_v=\frac{1+P_v/(\alpha n)+O(E/n)}{\sqrt n}.
\end{equation}
The constants are uniform over all edge weights in $[0,1]$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Since every part has size $\Theta(n)$ and only $Z=o(n)$ cross-edge weights are lowered, every vertex has positive-weight degree at least $n-n_i-Z>(1/2+o(1))n$. Hence the positive-weight skeleton, and therefore the weighted graph, is connected for large $n$. The eigenvector equation and Cauchy--Schwarz give
$\lambda(A_\omega)x_u\le\sqrt n$ for every $u$, and hence $M=O(n^{-1/2})$. Write
\[
 S_i=\sum_{u\in V_i}x_u,\qquad S=\sum_iS_i.
\]
On summing the eigenvector equations over $V_i$, every edited edge contributes the sum of its two endpoint coordinates, with the appropriate sign. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:part-sums}
 (\lambda+n_i)S_i=n_iS+R_i,
 \qquad |R_i|\le2(P+Z)M=O(\sqrt n).
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:part-sums-2}
 S_i=\frac{n_iS}{\lambda+n_i}+O(n^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}

Assume first that $E_0=A+P+Z+1=o(n)$. In this case the sharper form of \eqref{eq:part-sums} is $|R_i|=O((P+Z)n^{-1/2})$, and hence
$S_i=n_iS/(\lambda+n_i)+O((P+Z)n^{-3/2})$. For every vertex $u\in V_i$, the edit contribution in its eigenvector equation has absolute value at most $(P+Z)M$. Equations \eqref{eq:part-sums-2} and $\lambda+n_i=n+O(A+1)$ therefore give
\[
 x_u=\frac Sn+O(E_0n^{-3/2})
\]
uniformly. The normalization condition gives $S=\sqrt n(1+O(E_0/n))$, proving \eqref{eq:flat-coordinates}.

For the second assertion, every vertex other than $v$ has internal degree at most $K+1$. Thus, for $u\in V_i\setminus\{v\}$, \eqref{eq:part-sums-2} and the individual eigenvector equation give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonhub-coordinate-proof}
 x_u=\frac{S}{\lambda+n_i}+O(En^{-3/2})
 =\frac Sn\left(1+O\left(\frac{A+1}{n}\right)\right)+O(En^{-3/2}).
\end{equation}
Let $v\in V_1$. Summing \eqref{eq:nonhub-coordinate-proof} over its internal neighbors, with edge weights included, and then using the equation at $v$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hub-coordinate-proof}
 x_v=\frac Sn\left(1+\frac{P_v}{\alpha n}+O\left(\frac En\right)\right)+O(En^{-3/2}).
\end{equation}
Since $S\le\sqrt n$, equations \eqref{eq:nonhub-coordinate-proof} and \eqref{eq:hub-coordinate-proof}, followed by $\sum_ux_u^2=1$, yield
$S=\sqrt n(1+O(E/n))$. Substitution proves \eqref{eq:hub-coordinates}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:linear-signed}
Fix $r\ge3$, $C_0>0$ and $L_0\ge0$. If $N_{K_{r+1}}(G)\le C_0n^r$ and $\lambda(G)\ge\alpha n-L_0$, then every maximum $r$-cut satisfies $p+z=O(n)$ and $S_2=O(n)$.
Moreover, with $s_v=d_H(v)-d_D(v)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:signed-expansion}
 \lambda(G)=\alpha n-\frac{\alpha S_2}{n}
 +\frac{2(p-z)}n+\frac1{\alpha n^2}\sum_vs_v^2+o(1).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Since $C_0n^r=o(n^{r+1})$, the spectral supersaturation-stability theorem of Fang, Li, Lin and Ma \cite[Theorem~2.8]{FLLM} gives a partition for which $G$ differs from $T_{n,r}$ in $o(n^2)$ edges. A maximum $r$-cut has no more internal edges than this partition. Also $e(G)=e(T_{n,r})+o(n^2)$. Hence, for the chosen maximum cut, $p+z=o(n^2),\qquad n_i=n/r+o(n)$.
Indeed,
\[
 \sum_{i<j}n_in_j\ge e(G)-p=e(T_{n,r})-o(n^2),
 \qquad
 \sum_i(n_i-n/r)^2=\alpha n^2-2\sum_{i<j}n_in_j.
\]

Put $\theta=1/(100r)$ and $\xi=\theta/1000$. Let $X$ be the set of vertices with internal degree at least $\xi n$. If $v\in X$, then the maximum-cut property gives at least $\xi n$ neighbors of $v$ in every part. We choose an internal neighbor of $v$ and one neighbor in each other part. This gives at least $(\xi n)^r$ choices. At most $zn^{r-2}=o(n^r)$ of them are lost because of missing cross-edges. Thus every $v\in X$ lies in at least $\xi^rn^r/2$ copies of $K_{r+1}$ for large $n$. Since one copy contains at most $r+1$ vertices of $X$, double counting the incidences between $X$ and these copies gives $|X|\le 2(r+1)C_0\xi^{-r}=O(1)$.

Delete $X$ and write $N=n-|X|$. Let $p',z'$ be the two edit counts in $G-X$. The remaining maximum internal degree is at most $\beta N$, where $\beta=2\xi$. If $y$ is a unit Perron vector of $G$, then $y_v\le\sqrt n/\lambda(G)=O(n^{-1/2})$. Put $a=\sum_{v\in X}y_v^2=O(n^{-1})$. Using the restriction of $y$ to $G-X$ and the eigenvalue equations on $X$, we obtain $\lambda(G-X)\ge \lambda(G)\frac{1-2a}{1-a}\ge \lambda(G)-O(1)$.
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delete-X}
 \lambda(G-X)\ge \alpha N-L_1
\end{equation}
for a fixed $L_1$.

Let $a_v,b_v$ be the internal and missing-cross degrees in $G-X$, and put $Y=\{v:b_v\ge\theta N\}$.
Then $|Y|\le2z'/(\theta N)=o(N)$. Consider an internal edge $uv$ with both ends outside $Y$. Before cross-edges are removed it has $(r^{-1}+o(1))^{r-1}N^{r-1}$ standard completions. Missing edges incident with $u$ or $v$ destroy at most $2\theta N^{r-1}$ of them, and all other missing edges destroy at most $O_r(z'N^{r-3})=o(N^{r-1})$ of them. Taking $\theta$ sufficiently small, every such edge has at least $c'_rN^{r-1}$ standard completions. These families are disjoint by Lemma~\ref{lem:standard-completions}. Internal edges meeting $Y$ number at most $\beta N|Y|$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count-contraction-short}
 p'\le C_1N+\frac{2\beta}{\theta}z'.
\end{equation}

Let $A_0$ be the complete multipartite base on the same parts and $E=A(G-X)-A_0$. Its unit Perron coordinates are $(1+o(1))/\sqrt N$, because a coordinate in part $i$ is proportional to $(\mu+n_i)^{-1}$. Since $\|E\|\le\sqrt{2(p'+z')}=o(N)$, $x^{\mathsf T}Ex=\frac{2(p'-z')+o(p'+z')}{N}$.
Moreover,
\[
 \|Ex\|^2\le\frac1N\left(\sum_v(a_v-b_v)^2+o\left(\sum_v(a_v+b_v)^2\right)\right).
\]
We have $\sum_va_v^2\le2\beta Np'$. Vertices outside $Y$ contribute at most $2\theta Nz'$ to $\sum_vb_v^2$. On $Y$, $\sum_{v\in Y}b_v\le z'+e_D(Y)\le z'+\tfrac12|Y|^2=z'+o(z')$,
and $b_v\le(\alpha+o(1))N$. Thus $\sum_vb_v^2\le(\alpha+2\theta+o(1))Nz'$.
Using $(a_v-b_v)^2\le a_v^2+b_v^2$ in \eqref{eq:schur-upper}, and then taking $n$ large, gives $\lambda(G-X)\le \alpha N+\frac{3p'}N-\frac{z'}{2N}$.
Together with \eqref{eq:delete-X}, this yields $z'\le6p'+2L_1N$. Putting this in \eqref{eq:count-contraction-short}, with $12\beta/\theta<1/2$, gives $z'=O(N)$ and then $p'=O(N)$. Restoring the bounded set $X$ adds only $O(n)$ edits, so $p+z=O(n)$.

Now we prove $S_2=O(n)$. For the complete multipartite base, if $F_0(x)=\sum_i n_i/(x+n_i)$ and $\mu$ is its root, then $1-F_0(\alpha n)=\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_i\frac{(n_i-n/r)^2}{n+n_i-n/r}$.
Since $|F_0'(x)|=O(n^{-1})$ when $x=\Theta(n)$, this implies $\alpha n-\mu\ge c''_rS_2/n$.
Now $p+z=O(n)$, $|x^{\mathsf T}Ex|=O(1)$, and $\|Ex\|^2=O(n)$. Formula \eqref{eq:schur-upper} gives $\lambda(G)\le\mu+O(1)$; hence $S_2=O(n)$.

It remains to prove the expansion. Let $Q=I-xx^{\mathsf T}$. The Schur formula and the identity for the inverse give
\[
 \lambda(G)=\mu+x^{\mathsf T}Ex+
 \langle QEx,(\mu I-QA_0Q)^{-1}QEx\rangle
 +O\left(\frac{\|E\|\,\|Ex\|^2}{n^2}\right).
\]
Here $\|E\|=O(\sqrt n)$ and $\|Ex\|^2=O(n)$, so the remainder is $O(n^{-1/2})$. The base coordinate in part $i$ is $n^{-1/2}(1+O(n^{-1/2}))$, so
\[
 x^{\mathsf T}Ex=\frac{2(p-z)}n+O(n^{-1/2}),
 \qquad
 \|Ex\|^2=\frac1n\sum_vs_v^2+O(\sqrt n).
\]
On the subspace whose coordinate sum in each part is zero, $A_0=0$ and the inverse is $\mu^{-1}I$. The projection of $Ex$ onto the remaining part-constant directions has norm $O(1)$, so those directions contribute only $O(n^{-1})$. Therefore the quadratic term is $\frac1{\alpha n^2}\sum_vs_v^2+O(n^{-1/2})$.
Expanding the base equation $\sum_i n_i/(\mu+n_i)=1$ gives
$\mu=\alpha n-\alpha S_2/n+O(n^{-1/2})$, which proves \eqref{eq:signed-expansion}.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:profiles}
Let $n=rm+s\to\infty$, $1\le q\le m$, and $q/m\to\gamma$. Suppose $N_{K_{r+1}}(G)\le q c_r(n)$ and $\lambda(G)\ge\alpha n-O(1)$. Then $\limsup(\lambda(G)-\alpha n)\le\phi_r(\gamma)$.
If in addition $\lambda(G)\ge\alpha n+\phi_r(q/m)-o(1)$, then every maximum $r$-cut satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limit-entry}
 z=o(n),\qquad p=q+o(n),\qquad S_2=o(n).
\end{equation}
If $\gamma>0$, all but $o(n)$ internal edges meet one vertex.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Lemma~\ref{lem:linear-signed} applies. Fix an arbitrary sequence of maximum cuts; then $p+z=O(n)$ and $S_2=O(n)$. Order the vertices by decreasing edit degree, say $v_1,v_2,\ldots$, and pass to a subsequence and use a diagonal argument so that $q/m$, $S_2/n$, $p/m$, $z/m$, $\lambda(G)-\alpha n$, and all the ratios of neighborhood sizes below converge. If $v_j$ lies in part $i_j$, set
\[
 x_{j,i}=\lim\frac{|N(v_j)\cap V_i|}{m},\qquad
 a_j=x_{j,i_j},\qquad
 b_j=\sum_{i\ne i_j}(1-x_{j,i}),
\]
Put $t_j=a_j-b_j=\sum_i x_{j,i}-(r-1)$ and $\pi_j=\prod_i x_{j,i}$.
The maximum-cut condition gives $x_{j,i_j}\le x_{j,i}$ for every $i$.

Delete $v_1,\ldots,v_K$, and let $p_K,z_K$ be the remaining edit counts. Edits with both ends among the deleted vertices contribute only $O(K^2)$, so for every fixed $K$,
\[
 \lim\frac{p_K}{m}=\lim\frac pm-\sum_{j\le K}a_j\ge0,
 \qquad
 \lim\frac{z_K}{m}=\lim\frac zm-\sum_{j\le K}b_j\ge0.
\]
Hence $\sum_j(a_j+b_j)<\infty$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limit-remaining}
 P=\lim p/m-\sum_ja_j\ge0,
 \qquad
 Z=\lim z/m-\sum_jb_j\ge0.
\end{equation}
The numbers $P$ and $Z$ count the edges that are not seen at any fixed number of vertices.

Let $d_*(v)=d_H(v)+d_D(v)$. Since $d_*(v_{K+1})\le 2(p+z)/(K+1)$ and $\sum_vd_*(v)=2(p+z)$, $\frac1{m^2}\sum_{j>K}s_{v_j}^2 \le \frac{C_r}{K+1}$.
Writing $S_2/n\to\sigma$ and first taking $n\to\infty$, then $K\to\infty$, formula \eqref{eq:signed-expansion} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limit-spectrum-short}
 \Lambda=-\alpha\sigma+\frac2r\left(P-Z+\sum_jt_j\right)
 +\frac1{r(r-1)}\sum_jt_j^2,
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda=\lim(\lambda(G)-\alpha n)$.

We now use the clique bound. Fix $K$ and $j\le K$. Count the cliques containing $v_j$, no other vertex among $v_1,\ldots,v_K$, and one neighbor of $v_j$ in every part. Divided by $m^r$, their number tends to $\pi_j$; missing cross-edges among the other selected vertices spoil only $O_r(zn^{r-2})=O(n^{r-1})$ choices. These $K$ families are disjoint. Every internal edge outside the first $K$ vertices has $\bigl(1-O_r(K^{-1})-o(1)\bigr)m^{r-1}$
standard completions avoiding those vertices, because every remaining edit degree is $O(n/K)$. Missing cross-edges incident with an endpoint of the internal edge remove $O_r(n^{r-1}/K)$ choices, and all other missing cross-edges remove $O_r(zn^{r-3})=O(n^{r-2})$ choices. Thus the same displayed lower bound remains valid. The resulting families are disjoint from one another and from the first $K$ families by Lemma~\ref{lem:standard-completions}. Since $qc_r(n)/m^r\to\gamma$, taking the limits in the order $n\to\infty$, then $K\to\infty$, yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limit-budget-short}
 P+\sum_j\pi_j\le\gamma.
\end{equation}

For $x_1,\ldots,x_r\in[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product-bound}
 \prod_i x_i\ge\sum_i x_i-(r-1).
\end{equation}
Indeed, with $y_i=1-x_i$, expand $1-\prod_i(1-y_i)$ one by one. Equality holds exactly when at most one $x_i$ is smaller than one. Thus $\pi_j\ge(t_j)_+$. Put $T=\sum_j(t_j)_+, \qquad U=\sum_j(-t_j)_+$.
For $-(r-1)\le t<0$, $\frac{2t}{r}+\frac{t^2}{r(r-1)}\le\frac tr$.
Since $P+T\le\gamma$, equations \eqref{eq:limit-spectrum-short} and \eqref{eq:limit-budget-short} give
\begin{align}
 \Lambda
 &\le-\alpha\sigma-\frac{2Z}{r}-\frac Ur
 +\frac{2(P+T)}r+\frac{\sum_j(t_j)_+^2}{r(r-1)}\notag\\
 &\le-\alpha\sigma-\frac{2Z}{r}-\frac Ur
 +\frac{2(P+T)}r+\frac{T^2}{r(r-1)}\notag\\
 &\le\phi_r(\gamma).\label{eq:limit-chain}
\end{align}
The chain \eqref{eq:limit-chain} proves the upper bound.

Under the stronger lower bound, equality must hold in every step. Hence $\sigma=Z=U=0, \qquad P+T=\gamma, \qquad \sum_j(t_j)_+^2=\gamma^2$.
If $\gamma>0$, then $P=0$ and exactly one number $t_j$ is positive; its value is $\gamma$. If $\gamma=0$, then $P=T=0$. Equality in the count bound gives $\pi_j=t_j\ge0$ for every $j$. By the equality case of \eqref{eq:product-bound}, for each $j$ all but at most one of the numbers $x_{j,i}$ are equal to one. The maximum-cut condition shows that the possible smaller number is $x_{j,i_j}$. Thus $b_j=0$ and $a_j=t_j$ for every $j$. Equation \eqref{eq:limit-remaining} gives $z=o(n)$ and $p=q+o(n)$, and $\sigma=0$ gives $S_2=o(n)$. When $\gamma>0$, the vertex with positive $t_j$ has internal degree $\gamma m+o(n)=p-o(n)$.

The same argument applies to every subsequence. Hence the conclusions hold for the original sequence and for every maximum cut.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:global-asymptotic}
Fix $r\ge3$ and $\Gamma>0$. For $\gamma\ge0$, set $j=\lfloor\gamma\rfloor$, $\vartheta=\gamma-j$, and $\Phi_r(\gamma)=2\gamma/r+(j+\vartheta^2)/[r(r-1)]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-envelope}
 \max\{\lambda(G):|V(G)|=n,\ N_{K_{r+1}}(G)<q c_r(n)\}
 =\alpha n+\Phi_r(q/m)+o(1).
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
The preceding argument remains valid whenever $q/m$ has a bounded limit $\gamma$. Since each positive $t_j\le1$, $\sum_j(t_j)_+^2\le\lfloor T\rfloor+(T-\lfloor T\rfloor)^2$.
For a finite sequence, if two entries lie strictly between zero and one, moving mass from the smaller entry to the larger one until one entry becomes zero or one does not decrease the sum of squares. Iteration proves the displayed inequality; the infinite case follows by truncation. The right side is increasing in $T$. Together with $P+T\le\gamma$, this gives the upper bound.

For the lower bound, the case $q<m$ is given by a $(q-1)$-edge star in a largest Tur\'an part. If $q=jm+s_0\ge m$, where $0\le s_0<m$, take a balanced complete $r$-partite core on $n-j-1$ vertices. Then $p=q+O_{r,\Gamma}(1)$ and $\sum_ud_H(u)^2=jm^2+s_0^2+O_{r,\Gamma}(n)$, so \eqref{eq:fine} gives spectral radius
$\alpha n+\Phi_r(q/m)+O_{r,\Gamma}(n^{-1})$. Its clique count is
$qc_r(n)+O_{r,\Gamma}(n^{r-1})$. Deleting a sufficiently large bounded number of edges from one universal vertex to the first core part makes the inequality strict; each deletion destroys $\Theta_r(n^{r-1})$ cliques and changes the root by $O(n^{-1})$. Finally, if the error were not uniform, a counterexample sequence would have a subsequence for which $q/m$ converges in $[0,\Gamma]$. This would contradict the upper bound already proved. Hence the error in \eqref{eq:general-envelope} is uniform.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:walks}
Let $J$ have $p$ edges and let $w_j=\boldsymbol 1^{\mathsf T}A(J)^j\boldsymbol 1$. Then $w_1=2p,\qquad w_2=p^2+p-2M_2(J),\qquad w_3\le4p^2$,
and $w_4\le2pw_2$. If $p\ge4$ and $J$ is not a star together with isolated vertices, then $M_2(J)\ge p-3$. If $p\ge16$ and $k=p-\Delta(J)$, then $pk/16\le M_2(J)\le pk$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Counting intersecting edge pairs gives $w_2=2p+2\left(\binom p2-M_2(J)\right)$.
If $d=A(J)\boldsymbol 1$, then
\[
 w_3=2\sum_{ab\in E(J)}d(a)d(b)
 \le\left(\sum_ad(a)\right)^2=4p^2,
 \qquad
 w_4=\|A(J)d\|^2\le2p\|d\|^2,
\]
because $\|A(J)\|\le\sqrt{2p}$.

Choose a vertex of maximum degree $d_0=p-k$. Every edge avoiding it is disjoint from at least $d_0-2$ incident edges. If $d_0\ge3$ and $k\ge1$, then $M_2(J)\ge k(p-k-2)\ge p-3$;
the last inequality follows by concavity on $1\le k\le p-3$. If $d_0\le2$, every edge meets at most two other edges, so
$M_2(J)\ge\binom p2-p\ge p-3$. A pairwise-intersecting family of at least four edges has a common endpoint, which proves the stated description of the star case.

Every disjoint pair contains an edge avoiding the chosen vertex, hence $M_2(J)\le pk$. If $M_2(J)\ge p^2/16$, then $M_2(J)\ge pk/16$ because $k\le p$. Otherwise $w_2=p^2+p-2M_2(J)>\frac78p^2$,
whereas $w_2=\sum_vd(v)^2\le2p\Delta(J)=2pd_0$. Thus $d_0>7p/16$ for $p\ge16$, and $d_0-2\ge p/4$. The first paragraph then gives $M_2(J)\ge k(d_0-2)\ge pk/4$.
This completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:response-expansions}
If $\max_i|a_i|=O(1)$ and $p=O(n)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fine}
 \lambda(K+H)=\lambda(K)+\frac{2p}{n}-\frac4{n^2}\sum_i a_ip_i
 +\frac{\sum_vd_H(v)^2}{\alpha n^2}
 +O\left(\frac{p^2+p}{n^3}\right).
\end{equation}
If $p=O(n)$, $S_2=O(n)$ and $A=\max_i|a_i|$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coarse}
 \lambda(K+H)=\alpha n+f_r(p/n)-\frac{\alpha S_2}{n}
 -\frac{2M_2(H)}{\alpha n^2}+O\left(\frac{A+1}{n}\right).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We first prove the coarse formula. By the hypotheses, choose fixed constants $B_0,C_0$ such that $p\le B_0n$ and $S_2\le C_0n$. A direct use of \eqref{eq:R-expansion} and \eqref{eq:base-variance-identity}, evaluated at $x=\alpha n+w$, gives, uniformly for $|w|\le n^{1/4}$, $F(\alpha n+w)=1-\frac{w}{n}+O_{r,B_0,C_0}(n^{-1})$.
Let $C$ bound the last error and choose a fixed $C_1>2C$. Since $F$ is strictly decreasing, $F(\alpha n-C_1)>1>F(\alpha n+C_1)$
for all sufficiently large $n$. Thus the root has the form $x=\alpha n+w$ with $w=O(1)$. Expanding the base response at $\alpha n$ and using \eqref{eq:base-variance-identity} now gives
\[
 F_0(x)=1-\frac wn-\frac{\alpha S_2}{n^2}
 +O\left(\frac{(A+1)S_2}{n^3}+\frac1{n^2}\right).
\]
Let $S=\sum_vd_H(v)^2$. By \eqref{eq:R-expansion}, the change in the $i$th part response is obtained by dividing the increment of $R_{H_i}$ by $(1+n_i/x)^2$, up to a quadratic error. Summing over the parts yields
\[
 F(x)-F_0(x)=\frac{2p}{n^2}+\frac{S}{\alpha n^3}
 +O\left(\frac{A+1}{n^2}\right).
\]
Here the quadratic terms in the changes in the response are $O(n^{-2})$, and changing a denominator by $O(A+1)$ produces the displayed error. Setting $F(x)=1$, multiplying by $n$, and using
$S=p^2+p-2M_2(H)$ gives
\[
 w=f_r(p/n)-\frac{\alpha S_2}{n}
 -\frac{2M_2(H)}{\alpha n^2}
 +O\left(\frac{A+1}{n}\right),
\]
where the term $p/(\alpha n^2)$ is absorbed by the error. This is \eqref{eq:coarse}.

Assume now that $A=O(1)$. Let $D_*=\sum_i\frac{n_i}{(\mu+n_i)^2}$.
The root change is $O(p/n)$. Taylor expansion of the response equation, using \eqref{eq:R-expansion} and its differentiated form, gives
\[
 \lambda(K+H)-\mu
 =\sum_i\frac{2p_i}{D_*(\mu+n_i)^2}
 +\frac1\mu\sum_i
 \frac{\sum_{v\in V_i}d_H(v)^2}{D_*(\mu+n_i)^2}
 +O\left(\frac{p^2+p}{n^3}\right).
\]
The base equation and the normalization condition imply
\[
 \mu=\alpha n-\frac{\alpha S_2}{n}+O(n^{-2}),
 \qquad
 \frac1{D_*(\mu+n_i)^2}
 =\frac1n-\frac{2a_i}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\]
Putting these formulas together proves \eqref{eq:fine}; in the degree-square term the $a_i/n^2$ correction is $O((p^2+p)/n^3)$ and is absorbed.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:walk-defects}
There is an absolute constant $C$ such that every $p$-edge graph $J$, with $p\ge4$, satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:walk-defects}
 |w_3(J)-2p^2|\le CM_2(J),\qquad
 |w_4(J)-(p^3+p^2)|\le CpM_2(J).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Write $M=M_2(J)$. The assertion is clear for a star. The absolute bounds in Lemma~\ref{lem:walks} prove it when $M\ge p^2/16$, and also for $4\le p<16$ after increasing $C$. Suppose $p\ge16$ and $0<M<p^2/16$. Choose a vertex $v$ of degree $d=p-k$. The proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:walks} gives $pk\le4M$.

Delete the edges at $v$, leaving a $k$-edge graph $J_0$. For $z\ne v$, let $e_z$ be the indicator of $N_J(v)$ and put $b_z=d_{J_0}(z)$. If $B_0=\sum_{z\in N_J(v)}b_z\le2k$, then $\frac{w_3(J)}2=d(d+B_0)+ \sum_{xy\in E(J_0)}(e_x+b_x)(e_y+b_y)$.
The right side lies between $d^2$ and $p^2+2k^2+2k$, so
$|w_3(J)-2p^2|\le8pk=O(M)$.

Here $\sum_zt_z\le k^2+3k$ and $\sum_z t_z^2\le2k\sum_z(e_z+b_z)^2 \le2k(p+k^2+5k)$.
Comparison with $p^3+p^2$ gives an error $O(p^2k)=O(pM)$, proving \eqref{eq:walk-defects}.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:concentration}
Fix $r\ge3$ and $A,B>0$. Let $|n_i-n/r|\le A$, and let a $p$-edge graph $H$ lie in one part, where
$4\le p\le\min\{Bn,u-1\}$. If $G_{\rm st}$ replaces $H$ by a $p$-edge star in that part, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spectral-concentration}
 \lambda(G_{\rm st})-\lambda(K+H)
 =\frac{2M_2(H)}{\alpha n^2}\left(1+O_{r,A,B}(n^{-1/2})\right).
\end{equation}
The same conclusion holds when the internal edges lie in several parts of a common size $u$, provided that their total number $p$ satisfies $p\le u-1$ and they are replaced by one $p$-edge star in one of these parts. Here $M_2(H)$ is computed in the disjoint union of the internal graphs.

<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
The two roots are $\alpha n+O_{r,A,B}(1)$ by \eqref{eq:fine}. On the interval between them, the derivatives of the responses are $-1/n+O(n^{-2})$. When the edges lie in one part, the exact change in that part summand is $\frac{R_{\rm st}(x)-R_H(x)} {(1+R_{\rm st}(x))(1+R_H(x))}$.
Each denominator factor is $1/\alpha+O(n^{-1})$. Equation~\eqref{eq:relative-response} and the mean-value theorem therefore give \eqref{eq:spectral-concentration}.

\[
 \left|\psi_h(\delta)-\sum_i\psi_h(\delta_i)\right|
 \le\frac{C}{n^4}\sum_{i<j}p_ip_j
 \le\frac{CM_2(H)}{n^4}.
\]
This error is smaller than the main term. The same mean-value argument completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:deletion}
In the setting of Lemma~\ref{lem:preliminary}, suppose $z=o(n)$ and let $\overline G=K+H$. If $p=o(n)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flat-deletion}
 \lambda(\overline G)-\lambda(G)=\frac{2z}{n}(1+o(1)).
\end{equation}
If $q/m\to\gamma>0$, let $v$ have internal degree $p-k$, let $z_h$ missing cross-edges meet $v$, and put $z_o=z-z_h$. With $\gamma_n=q/m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hub-deletion}
 \lambda(\overline G)-\lambda(G)
 =\frac{(2+2\gamma_n/(r-1))z_h+2z_o}{n}+o(z/n).
\end{equation}
The error is zero when $z=0$.

<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Change the weights of all missing cross-edges continuously from zero to one. Every intermediate graph is connected for large $n$. Its largest eigenvalue lies between those of $G$ and $\overline G$, and both endpoints equal $\alpha n+O(1)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:preliminary} and \eqref{eq:coarse}. Thus Lemma~\ref{lem:perron-coordinates} applies uniformly along the interpolation.

If $p=o(n)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:preliminary} gives $A=o(n)$, and $p+z=o(n)$. Hence \eqref{eq:flat-coordinates} holds throughout. \label{lem:low-core}
Assume $0\le h\le a-2$. In $C_-$, no non-neighbor of $v$ may lie in an $(a+1)$-part. If there is no $a$-part, all $h$ non-neighbors lie in the $(a-1)$-part. If an $a$-part exists, the only possible distributions, up to a permutation of equal parts, are $X:(0,h),\qquad Y:(h-1,1),\qquad Z:(h,0)$,
where the entries count non-neighbors in the $(a-1)$-part and one $a$-part; all other parts have none. The distribution $Y$ requires $h\ge1$; $X=Y$ when $h=1$, and $X=Z$ when $h=0$. All these distributions satisfy the strict inequality.
<!-- end lemma-15 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
The core product is $P_-=P_0(1-a^{-2})$. All factors are positive. The logarithm is concave, so a minimum occurs at a vertex of this set. Its vertices put all $h$ non-neighbors in one larger part, or put one there and the other $h-1$ in one other part. Among the latter choices the smallest product puts the other non-neighbors in the $(a-1)$-part. These two products, minus $qP_0/a$, are respectively $P_0(h-1)/a^2$ and zero. They are nonnegative, contradicting the strict inequality. Thus no larger part contains a non-neighbor.

Let $u$ non-neighbors lie in the $(a-1)$-part. The others lie in $a$-parts. If no such part exists, $u=h$. Otherwise, for fixed $u$, the product of their avoidance factors is minimized by putting all $h-u$ non-neighbors in one $a$-part. Compared with $X$, this minimum increases the count by $\frac{P_0(a+1)}{a^3}\,u(h-1-u)$.
The count of $X$ is below the bound by $P_0(a-h)/a^3$. For $1\le u\le h-2$, we have $h\ge3$ and $u(h-1-u)\ge h-2\ge1$; hence the count exceeds the bound. This leaves $u=0,h-1,h$.

For $u=0$, a distribution among at least two $a$-parts has product at least the value at $(h-1,1)$. This follows by transferring integer mass to the largest entry until only one other positive entry, equal to one, remains; each transfer decreases the product. The resulting count minus the bound, after multiplication by $a^4/P_0$, is $(h-2)a^2+ha-h+1>0\qquad(h\ge2)$.
Thus $u=0$ requires concentration in one $a$-part. For $u=h-1$, only one further non-neighbor remains; $u=h$ leaves none. This proves all assertions, including the small values of $h$.

 &=\frac{3(h-1)}{n^2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{h+1}{n}\right)\right),\label{eq:endpoint-xy}\\
 \lambda(Y_{m,h})-\lambda(Z_{m,h})
 &=\frac3{n^2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{h+1}{n}\right)\right).\label{eq:endpoint-yz}
\end{align}
At $h=1$, $X=Y$ and $Z$ has the second largest spectral radius, with \eqref{eq:endpoint-yz} still valid. At $h=0$, $X=Z$; every other graph has a gap of at least $c/n$.

We first prove the gap from all other graphs. If no fixed $\delta,c,m_0$ worked, there would be a sequence with $h/m\to0$ and graphs other than the listed ones satisfying $n(\lambda(X)-\lambda(G))\to0$. Corollary~\ref{cor:global-entry} and Theorem~\ref{thm:core} apply. After passing to a subsequence, all bounded integer parameters of the core are fixed. The proof of \eqref{eq:general-endpoint-score}, specialized to $r=3,s=1$, shows that a nonnegative limit excludes all core defects at least four and all missing core edges. For a balanced core, only $k=0,1$ and $d_0=k$ remain; Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-core-general} excludes both. Thus the graph is obtained from an almost balanced core by adding one vertex with exactly $h$ non-neighbors. This core is $K_3(m-1,m,m+1)$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:low-core} leaves exactly $X,Y,Z$. This contradiction proves \eqref{eq:endpoint-separated} in a fixed endpoint window, including the equalities at $h=0,1$.

The response calculation in Lemma~\ref{lem:core-order} gives \eqref{eq:endpoint-xy}--\eqref{eq:endpoint-yz} with $\alpha_3=2/3$. Its estimates are uniform also for $h\le\delta m$ once $\delta$ is sufficiently small: the roots remain $2n/3+O(1)$, $W=O(h/n)$, the partial derivative in $W$ is $-3n^{-1}(1+O((h+1)/n))$, the partial derivative in $Q$ is $O(n^{-3})$, and the full root derivative is $-1/n+O(n^{-2})$. The exact differences of $W,Q$ displayed there therefore have the stated relative errors. Shrink $\delta$ so that both errors have absolute value less than one half. This proves the strict order for $h\ge2$ and the order at $h=1$.

Finally, shrink $\delta$ again so that the sum of the two displayed gaps is less than $c/(2n)$. Then \eqref{eq:endpoint-separated} puts every other graph below $Z$. Thus these are the three graphs with largest spectral radii. The equalities at $h=0,1$ follow from the actual neighborhoods and describe the same graph.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:core-example}
Choose integers $h\to\infty$ with $h=o(\sqrt m)$, put $q=m-h$, and set $a=\lfloor(h+1)/2\rfloor,\qquad b=h+1-a$.
Start with $K_3(m+1,m,m)$ on parts $A,B,C$. Add a vertex $v$ adjacent to every core vertex except $a$ vertices of $A$ and $b$ vertices of $B$, and call the graph $W_{m,h}$. Then, for sufficiently large $m$,
\begin{align}
 N_{K_4}(W_{m,h})-qm(m+1)
 &=m\bigl(-m+a(b+1)-1\bigr)<0,\label{eq:example-count}\\
 \lambda(V_{m,q})-\lambda(W_{m,h})
 &=\frac5{3n}+o(n^{-1}),\qquad n=3m+2,\label{eq:example-gap}\\
 z_3(W_{m,h})&=\min\{a,b\}\longrightarrow\infty.\label{eq:example-deletions}
\end{align}
However, deleting $v$ leaves a balanced complete tripartite graph.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-33: body_md -->
The core contains no $K_4$, so $N_{K_4}(W)=(m+1-a)(m-b)m$. Using $a+b=h+1$ gives \eqref{eq:example-count}, whose sign follows from $a(b+1)=O(h^2)=o(m)$.

The core squared deviation of $W$ is $2/3$, whereas the core of $V$ has squared deviation $8/3$. Their added vertices have $h+1$ and $h$ non-neighbors, respectively. There are no core edits. Lemma~\ref{lem:core-expansion} gives
\[
 n(\lambda(W)-\lambda(V))
 =-\frac23\left(\frac23-\frac83\right)-3+o(1)
 =-\frac53+o(1),
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:example-gap} independently of the exact extremal theorem.

Assigning $v$ to the core part containing more of its non-neighbors gives a partition with $\min\{a,b\}$ missing cross-edges. Conversely, if any core part is split by a new partition into three parts, its own non-edges across that split already contribute at least $m-1>\min\{a,b\}$ missing cross-edges. Hence a partition with a smaller count would keep every core part intact. If the three core parts occupy three different new parts, placing $v$ can absorb at most $\max\{a,b\}$ of its non-neighbors. At least $\min\{a,b\}$ remain across parts. If the core occupies two new parts, the third consists of $v$ and all $h+1$ non-neighbors are counted. The core cannot occupy only one part, because a single remaining vertex cannot fill two further nonempty parts. This proves \eqref{eq:example-deletions}.
