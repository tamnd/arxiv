We prove (i)--(iv) in turn.

(i). Since $D\subset\mathbb R^2$, domain
monotonicity for the Dirichlet heat kernel gives \cite{vandenBerg1989}
\[
  0\le K_D(t,x,y)\le K_{\mathbb R^2}(t,x,y),
\]
where
\[
  K_{\mathbb R^2}(t,x,y)=(4\pi t)^{-1}e^{-|x-y|^2/(4t)}.
\]
Hence
\eqref{eq:gaussian-upper-bound} holds with $C_0=(4\pi)^{-1}$ and $b=1/4$.

(ii). By Cauchy--Schwarz and
\eqref{eq:gaussian-upper-bound}, for $0<t\le1$ and $f\in L^2(D)$,
\[
|e^{-tA}f(x)|\le\|K_D(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^2(D)}\|f\|_{L^2(D)}
\le C_1 t^{-1/2}\|f\|_{L^2(D)},
\quad C_1:=C_0\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2b}},
\]
which is \eqref{eq:small-t-estimate}. For $t>1$, writing
$e^{-tA}=e^{-A}e^{-(t-1)A}$ and applying \eqref{eq:small-t-estimate} at $t=1$
to the vector $e^{-(t-1)A}f$ yield
\[
\begin{aligned}
\|e^{-tA}f\|_{L^\infty(D)}
&=\|e^{-A}e^{-(t-1)A}f\|_{L^\infty(D)}
\le C_1\|e^{-(t-1)A}f\|_{L^2(D)}\\
&\le C_1e^{-\lambda_1(t-1)}\|f\|_{L^2(D)},
\end{aligned}
\]
which is \eqref{eq:large-t-estimate} with $C_2=C_1e^{\lambda_1}$. Here, in the last inequality, we have used the spectral theorem.

(iii). Since $D$ is a bounded Lipschitz domain, every boundary point of $D$ is
Wiener regular (see, e.g., \cite{Kenig1994}). Then Example~6.2 of Arendt--ter Elst \cite{ArendtElst2019} establishes the inclusion

Now fix $t>0$ and $f\in L^2(D)$. Since $A$ commutes with $e^{-tA}$ and

$Ae^{-tA}f\in L^\infty(D)$, while $e^{-tA}f\in \mathcal{D}(A)$. Therefore
$e^{-tA}f\in C_\partial(\overline D)$ by \eqref{eq:ATE-inclusion}.

Thus \(e^{-tA}\) maps \(L^2(D)\) into \(C_\partial(\overline D)\).
The same holds for the bounded self-adjoint operator
\(T:=e^{-tA/3}\). Applying Theorem~1.1 of
\cite{ArendtElst2019} to the self-adjoint operator
$T$ on the bounded open set $D$, we deduce that
$T^3=e^{-tA}$ has an integral kernel in $C(\overline D\times\overline D)$,
namely $K_D(t,\cdot,\cdot)\in C(\overline D\times\overline D)$.

Fix $y\in D$ and $0<s<t$. By the semigroup property of the kernel,
\[
  K_D(t,x,y)=(e^{-(t-s)A}g)(x)
\]
for almost every $x\in D$ with
$g:=K_D(s,\cdot,y)$, and $g\in L^\infty(D)\subset L^2(D)$ by
\eqref{eq:gaussian-upper-bound}. By the mapping property just proved, the right-hand side lies in
$C_\partial(\overline D)$; since it agrees with $K_D(t,\cdot,y)$ almost
everywhere in $D$ and both functions are continuous on $\overline D$, we
conclude that $K_D(t,x,y)=0$ for $x\in\partial D$, $y\in D$. The boundary vanishing identity \eqref{eq:kernel-vanishing} then follows by the symmetry and continuity of the Dirichlet heat kernel.

(iv). Fix \(y\in D\) and \(t_0>0\), and choose \(0<s<t_0\).
By (iii), \(K_D(t,\cdot,y)=e^{-(t-s)A}g\) on \(\overline D\) for all
\(t>s\), where \(g:=K_D(s,\cdot,y)\in L^\infty(D)\). Continuity of
\(t\mapsto K_D(t,\cdot,y)\) at \(t_0\) is therefore equivalent to
continuity of \(\tau\mapsto e^{-\tau A}g\) at \(\tau_0:=t_0-s\). For
\(h>0\),
\[
e^{-(\tau_0+h)A}-e^{-\tau_0 A}
=e^{-\tau_0 A/2}\bigl(e^{-hA}-I\bigr)e^{-\tau_0 A/2},
\]
while for \(0<h<\tau_0\),
\[
e^{-\tau_0 A}-e^{-(\tau_0-h)A}
=e^{-(\tau_0-h)A/2}\bigl(e^{-hA}-I\bigr)e^{-(\tau_0-h)A/2}.
\]
By (ii), \(e^{-\tau A}:L^2(D)\to L^\infty(D)\) remains bounded for
\(\tau\) near \(\tau_0/2\). Combined with strong continuity of
\(e^{-tA}\) on \(L^2(D)\), both displays, applied to \(g\), tend to
zero in \(L^\infty(D)\) as \(h\to0^+\). Since
\(e^{-\tau A}g\in C_\partial(\overline D)\) by (iii), this is continuity
in \(C_\partial(\overline D)\). Evaluating at \(x\in\overline D\) yields
continuity of \((t,x)\mapsto K_D(t,x,y)\) on
\((0,\infty)\times\overline D\). The case of fixed \(x\) follows by
symmetry of the kernel.

Each eigenfunction $\phi_{\lambda,j}$ is real-analytic in $D$ by interior analyticity for elliptic equations with analytic coefficients; see, for example, Morrey and Nirenberg \cite{MorreyNirenberg1957} or Morrey \cite{Morrey1966}. Since $P_\lambda(y,y)$ is a finite sum of squares of real-analytic functions, it is real-analytic in $D$.

It remains to show that $P_\lambda(y,y)$ is continuous on $\overline D$ and vanishes on $\partial D$. Fix an eigenfunction $\phi=\phi_{\lambda,j}\in H^1_0(D)$ satisfying $-\Delta\phi = \lambda\phi$ in $D$. It suffices to show that $\phi\in C(\overline D)$ and $\phi|_{\partial D}=0$, which we do via the Dirichlet heat semigroup $e^{-tA}$.

Since $\phi$ is an eigenfunction, $e^{-tA}\phi = e^{-\lambda t}\phi$ for every $t>0$. On the other hand, Lemma~\ref{lem:heat-kernel-boundary}(iii) shows that for each fixed $t>0$ the Dirichlet heat semigroup maps $L^2(D)$ into $C_\partial(\overline D)$, that is, $e^{-tA}f$ extends continuously to $\overline D$ and vanishes on $\partial D$ for every $f\in L^2(D)$. Hence
\[
e^{-tA}\phi \in C(\overline D)
\quad\text{and}\quad
(e^{-tA}\phi)|_{\partial D}=0.
\]
Multiplying by the nonzero scalar $e^{\lambda t}$ gives $\phi\in C(\overline D)$ and $\phi|_{\partial D}=0$.
The diagonal value $P_\lambda(y,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{m_\lambda}\phi_{\lambda,j}(y)^2$ inherits both the continuity on $\overline{D}$ and the pointwise vanishing on $\partial D$.

$u\in\mathcal D(A)$,
\[
(-\Delta_x-\mu)\Gamma_\mu=(-\Delta_y-\mu)\Gamma_\mu=\delta_{\mathrm{Diag}_{x=y}}
\quad\text{in }\mathcal D'(D\times D),
\]
hence $(-\Delta_x-\Delta_y-2\mu)\Gamma_\mu=0$ off the diagonal.
Since $\mu\mapsto(A-\mu)^{-1}$ is holomorphic
with locally bounded operator norm on the resolvent set
$\mathbb C\setminus\sigma(D)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:kernel-holomorphy} with
$T(\mu):=(A-\mu)^{-1}$, $\Lambda:=\mathbb C\setminus\sigma(D)$ and
$F:=0$ shows that $\mu\mapsto\Gamma_\mu$ is holomorphic with values in
$C^\infty_{\mathrm{loc}}\bigl((D\times D)\setminus\mathrm{Diag}_{x=y}\bigr)$.
In particular, for each fixed $k$ with $k^2\notin\sigma(D)$, by
\eqref{eq:green-kernel-def},
$G_D(x,y,k)=\Gamma_{k^2}(x,y)\in C^\infty\bigl((D\times D)\setminus\mathrm{Diag}_{x=y}\bigr)$,
and for fixed $x\neq y$ the map $k\mapsto G_D(x,y,k)$ is holomorphic
on $\{k:k^2\notin\sigma(D)\}$.

\emph{Part~3: Poles and residues.}
For fixed $x\neq y$, all terms of \eqref{eq:laurent-expansion} are smooth
off the diagonal and may be evaluated pointwise: for every $k$ with
$k^2\in B(\lambda,\rho)\setminus\{\lambda\}$, which entails
$k^2\notin\sigma(D)$,
\[
G_D(x,y,k)=\frac{P_\lambda(x,y)}{\lambda-k^2}+K_H(x,y,k^2),
\]
with $K_H(x,y,\cdot)$ holomorphic on $B(\lambda,\rho)$. As a function of
$k\in\mathbb C$ the right-hand side is meromorphic: its second term is
holomorphic on $\{k:k^2\in B(\lambda,\rho)\}$, while $\lambda-k^2$
vanishes simply at $k=\pm\sqrt{\lambda}$. Since the left-hand side is a
priori defined only on $\{k:k^2\notin\sigma(D)\}$, this identity supplies
the meromorphic continuation of $k\mapsto G_D(x,y,k)$ across the spectral
points. Combined with the holomorphy of $k\mapsto G_D(x,y,k)$ on
$\{k:k^2\notin\sigma(D)\}$ established above, this shows that
$k\mapsto G_D(x,y,k)$ is meromorphic on $\mathbb C$, with possible poles only at $k=\pm\sqrt{\lambda}$,
$\lambda\in\sigma(D)$, where the preceding
argument is applied at each such $\lambda$. Moreover, $k=\sqrt{\lambda}$
is an actual pole if and only if $P_\lambda(x,y)\neq0$, since
$K_H(x,y,k^2)$ is holomorphic near $k=\sqrt{\lambda}$.
Substituting $\mu=k^2$ and using
\[
\frac{1}{\lambda-k^2}
=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}-k}
+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}+k}\Bigr)
\]
gives
\[
\operatorname{Res}_{k=\sqrt{\lambda}}G_D(x,y,k)
=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}P_\lambda(x,y),
\quad
\operatorname{Res}_{k=-\sqrt{\lambda}}G_D(x,y,k)
=+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}P_\lambda(x,y).
\]
By \eqref{eq:green-kernel-def} the kernel depends on $k$ only through
$k^2$, so $G_D(x,y,-k)=G_D(x,y,k)$; the same criterion therefore governs
the pole at $k=-\sqrt{\lambda}$.

The proof is divided into two steps: real analyticity in $y$, and meromorphy with the residue formula.

\emph{Step~1: Real analyticity in $y$.}
Fix $k$ with $\Re k>0$ and $k^2\notin\sigma(D)$. For each $w\in D$,
the solution $u^s(\cdot,w,k)\in H^1(D)$ of
\eqref{eq:helmholtz}--\eqref{eq:dirichlet} satisfies the homogeneous
equation \eqref{eq:helmholtz} in all of $D$ and is therefore
real-analytic in $D$ by interior elliptic regularity. Moreover,
standard continuous dependence for
\eqref{eq:helmholtz}--\eqref{eq:dirichlet}, together with the smoothness
of $(\xi,w)\mapsto\Phi_k(\xi,w)$ on $\partial D\times D$, gives that
$w\mapsto u^s(\cdot,w,k)$ is continuous from $D$ into $L^2(D)$; since
differences of solutions solve the homogeneous equation
\eqref{eq:helmholtz}, interior estimates make $u^s(\cdot,\cdot,k)$
jointly continuous on $D\times D$. In particular,
$u^s(\cdot,\cdot,k)\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(D\times D)$ defines a
distribution on $D\times D$. Testing against $\chi\in C_c^\infty(D\times D)$ and using
\eqref{eq:helmholtz} together with Fubini's theorem gives
\[
(-\Delta_z-k^2)u^s=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal D'(D\times D).
\]
For the second variable we use the reciprocity relation
\[
u^s(z,w,k)=u^s(w,z,k),\quad z,w\in D.
\]
Indeed, Weyl's law $\lambda_n\sim 4\pi n/|D|$ gives
$\sum_{n\ge1}|\lambda_n-k^2|^{-2}<\infty$, so $(A-k^2)^{-1}$ is
Hilbert--Schmidt and its kernel admits the $L^2(D\times D)$-convergent
eigenfunction expansion
\[
G_D(z,w,k)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\phi_n(z)\phi_n(w)}{\lambda_n-k^2}
=G_D(w,z,k)\quad\text{a.e. on }D\times D.
\]
Furthermore, by the off-diagonal continuity of the kernel
(Theorem~\ref{theo:spec-expansion}) and the symmetry of $\Phi_k$, the
decomposition \eqref{eq:GD-decomposition} yields the reciprocity
relation for $z\neq w$, and the diagonal case follows from the joint
continuity just established. Applying the previous paragraph with the
roles of $z$ and $w$ interchanged therefore gives
$(-\Delta_w-k^2)u^s=0$ in $\mathcal D'(D\times D)$, and adding the two
identities yields
\[
(-\Delta_z-\Delta_w-2k^2)u^s=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal D'(D\times D).
\]
This is an elliptic equation with constant, hence real-analytic,
coefficients, so analytic hypoellipticity (see, e.g., Morrey and
Nirenberg \cite{MorreyNirenberg1957}) shows that $u^s(\cdot,\cdot,k)$
coincides almost everywhere with a complex-valued real-analytic
function on $D\times D$; being jointly continuous,
$u^s(\cdot,\cdot,k)$ is itself real-analytic on $D\times D$. Finally,
for $z\neq y$ the decomposition \eqref{eq:GD-decomposition} gives
$u^s(z,y,k)=G_D(z,y,k)-\Phi_k(z,y)$, and letting $z\to y$ yields
\[
u^s(y,y,k)=\lim_{z\to y}\bigl(G_D(z,y,k)-\Phi_k(z,y)\bigr),
\]
so that $y\mapsto u^s(y,y,k)$ is real-analytic in $D$.

\emph{Step~2: Meromorphy and residue formula.}
Fix \(y_0\in D\) and \(r>0\) with \(B(y_0,2r)\subset D\), and let
\(\chi\in C_c^\infty(B(y_0,2r))\) satisfy \(\chi\equiv1\) on \(B(y_0,r)\).
Set
\[
v_\chi(\cdot,k):=G_D(\cdot,y_0,k)-\chi\,\Phi_k(\cdot,y_0).
\]
Since \(\chi\equiv1\) near \(y_0\), the singularity of \(G_D\) at \(y_0\)
is canceled by \(\chi\Phi_k\). Writing \([P,\chi]:=P\chi-\chi P\) for
the commutator of a differential operator \(P\) with multiplication by
\(\chi\), we obtain
\[
(-\Delta-k^2)v_\chi
=-\bigl[(-\Delta-k^2),\chi\bigr]\Phi_k(\cdot,y_0)=:f(\cdot,k),
\]
where \(f(\cdot,k)\) is holomorphic in \(k\) as a
\(C_c^\infty(D)\)-valued map, supported in
\(B(y_0,2r)\setminus B(y_0,r)\). We next verify that
\(v_\chi(\cdot,k)\in H^1_0(D)\). Since
\(G_D(\cdot,y_0,k)=\Phi_k(\cdot,y_0)+u^s(\cdot,y_0,k)\) away from \(y_0\),
the definition of \(v_\chi\) gives the decomposition
\[
v_\chi(\cdot,k)=(1-\chi)\,\Phi_k(\cdot,y_0)+u^s(\cdot,y_0,k).
\]
Because \(\chi\equiv1\) on \(B(y_0,r)\), the function \((1-\chi)\Phi_k\)
vanishes near \(y_0\) and is smooth on \(\overline D\); hence
\((1-\chi)\Phi_k(\cdot,y_0)\in H^1(D)\). Moreover
\(u^s(\cdot,y_0,k)\in H^1(D)\) solves \eqref{eq:helmholtz}--\eqref{eq:dirichlet},
so \(v_\chi(\cdot,k)\in H^1(D)\). Finally, \(\chi\) vanishes on a
neighborhood of \(\partial D\), while
\(\operatorname{Tr}u^s=-\operatorname{Tr}\Phi_k\) on \(\partial D\) by
\eqref{eq:dirichlet}; hence
\(\operatorname{Tr}v_\chi=\operatorname{Tr}\Phi_k-\operatorname{Tr}\Phi_k=0\),
and therefore $v_\chi(\cdot,k)\in H^1_0(D)$. Since
$f(\cdot,k)\in C_c^\infty(D)\subset L^2(D)$, the distributional
equation $(-\Delta-k^2)v_\chi=f(\cdot,k)$ yields
$-\Delta v_\chi=f(\cdot,k)+k^2v_\chi\in L^2(D)$, so

For $\lambda \in \sigma(D)$, we say that $\lambda$ is \emph{visible} with respect to ${\mathcal M}$ if $P_\lambda(y,y)$ is not identically zero on ${\mathcal M}$; otherwise, $\lambda$ is called \emph{invisible} with respect to ${\mathcal M}$.
Equivalently, $\lambda$ is invisible with respect to ${\mathcal M}$ if and only if every eigenfunction in the $\lambda$-eigenspace vanishes on ${\mathcal M}$: choosing the eigenfunctions $\phi_{\lambda,j}$ to be real-valued, one has $P_\lambda(y,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{m_\lambda}\phi_{\lambda,j}(y)^2$, a sum of squares, so $P_\lambda(y,y)$ vanishes identically on ${\mathcal M}$ if and only if each $\phi_{\lambda,j}$ does. We say that an eigenspace is \emph{fully invisible} if all of its eigenfunctions vanish on ${\mathcal M}$, and \emph{partially visible} if it contains both eigenfunctions vanishing on ${\mathcal M}$ and eigenfunctions that do not vanish identically on ${\mathcal M}$.
Define $V(D):= \{\lambda \in \sigma(D): \lambda \text{ is visible with respect to } {\mathcal M}\}$.

Invisibility is realized by concrete planar domains. Let $D = B(0,R)$ be a
disk and ${\mathcal M} = \{|x| = r_0\}$ a concentric
circle with $0 < r_0 < R$. The Dirichlet eigenfunctions are, up to

\[
\phi_{m,n}(r,\theta) = J_m(j_{m,n} r/R)
\begin{cases}
\cos(m\theta) \\
\sin(m\theta)
\end{cases}, 
\quad
\lambda_{m,n}=(j_{m,n}/R)^2,
\]
where $j_{m,n}$ is the $n$-th positive zero of the Bessel
function $J_m$. Fix $m\ge0$ and $n\ge2$, choose $1\le\ell<n$, and set
$r_0=R\,j_{m,\ell}/j_{m,n}$, so that $0<r_0<R$. Then
$J_m(j_{m,n}r_0/R)=J_m(j_{m,\ell})=0$; hence the radial factor, and
therefore every eigenfunction of angular order $m$ and eigenvalue
$\lambda_{m,n}$, vanishes identically on ${\mathcal M}$.

For $\lambda_{m,n}$ to be \emph{fully} invisible, one must exclude
accidental coincidences with Bessel zeros of other orders. The full
eigenspace is
\[
E_{\lambda_{m,n}}(D)
=
\operatorname{span}\bigl\{\phi_{m',n'}:j_{m',n'}=j_{m,n}\bigr\},
\]
and if $j_{m',n'}=j_{m,n}$ for some $m'\ne m$, then $\phi_{m',n'}$ belongs
to the eigenspace but does not vanish on ${\mathcal M}$: its radial factor there is
$J_{m'}(j_{m,\ell})$, which is nonzero by Bourget's theorem recalled
below. In that case $\lambda_{m,n}$ is only partially visible. Bourget's
hypothesis, proved by Siegel \cite{Siegel1929}, rules such coincidences
out: for integers $p\ge0$ and $q\ge1$, the functions $J_p$ and $J_{p+q}$
have no common positive zeros. Applying this to
$\{m,m'\}=\{p,p+q\}$ gives $j_{m,n}\ne j_{m',n'}$ whenever $m'\ne m$.

order $m$ (one-dimensional for $m=0$ and two-dimensional for $m\ge1$) and
$\lambda_{m,n}$ is fully invisible from ${\mathcal M}$ for every $m\ge0$ and every
$n\ge2$.

Thus invisible eigenvalues do occur: there exist a domain $D$ and a
measurement curve ${\mathcal M}$ for which some Dirichlet eigenvalue of $D$ is fully
invisible from ${\mathcal M}$. Whether the multiplicity gap of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main-unique} is realized by a pair of \emph{distinct}
domains with identical curve data remains open; see
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

We prove parts \textup{(a)}, \textup{(b1)}, and \textup{(b2)} separately.

\textbf{Proof of (a): Spectral set comparison.}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:visible-spectrum}, the visible eigenvalues of $D_1$ and $D_2$ coincide as sets: $V(D_1) = V(D_2) =: V$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:invisible-interior}, every invisible eigenvalue of $D_i$ is a Dirichlet eigenvalue of $\Omega$, i.e., $I(D_i) \subset \sigma(\Omega)$ for $i = 1, 2$, where $I(D_i) = \sigma(D_i) \setminus V$ is the set of invisible eigenvalues.

Since $\sigma(D_i) = V \cup I(D_i)$ (disjoint union), we have
\[
\sigma(D_1) \triangle \sigma(D_2) = (V \cup I_1) \triangle (V \cup I_2) \subset I_1 \cup I_2 \subset \sigma(\Omega),
\]
where $I_i = I(D_i)$. If $\lambda \in \sigma(D_1) \setminus \sigma(D_2)$, then $\lambda$ cannot be visible; otherwise $\lambda \in V(D_1) = V(D_2) \subset \sigma(D_2)$, a contradiction. Thus $\lambda \in I(D_1) \subset \sigma(\Omega)$. The case $\lambda \in \sigma(D_2) \setminus \sigma(D_1)$ is symmetric. This proves (a).

\textbf{Proof of (b1): Area estimate under multiplicity matching.}
For \(i=1,2\), write
\[
N_{D_i}(E)
= \sum_{\substack{\lambda\in V\\ \lambda\le E}} m_i(\lambda)
  + N_{\mathrm{inv},D_i}(E),
\]
where \(V=V(D_1)=V(D_2)\) by part~\textup{(a)}, \(m_i(\lambda)\) denotes the multiplicity of the visible eigenvalue \(\lambda\) in \(D_i\), and \(N_{\mathrm{inv},D_i}(E)\) counts the fully invisible eigenvalues of \(D_i\) up to energy \(E\) with multiplicity. By assumption, \(m_1(\lambda)=m_2(\lambda)\) for every \(\lambda\in V\). Hence
\[
N_{D_1}(E)-N_{D_2}(E)
=N_{\mathrm{inv},D_1}(E)-N_{\mathrm{inv},D_2}(E).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:invisible-interior} gives the stronger estimate
\[
N_{\mathrm{van},D_i}(E)\le N_\Omega(E), \quad i=1,2,
\]
where \(N_{\mathrm{van},D_i}(E)\) counts all eigenfunctions of \(D_i\) with eigenvalue at most \(E\) whose restrictions to \({\mathcal M}\) vanish, including those belonging to partially visible eigenspaces. Since every fully invisible eigenvalue contributes its full multiplicity to \(N_{\mathrm{van},D_i}(E)\), we have
\[
0\le N_{\mathrm{inv},D_i}(E)\le N_{\mathrm{van},D_i}(E)\le N_\Omega(E), \quad i=1,2,
\]
and therefore
\[
|N_{D_1}(E)-N_{D_2}(E)|\le N_\Omega(E).
\]
Dividing by \(E\) and applying the Weyl law \eqref{eq:weyl-law} to both sides yields
\[
\frac{\bigl||D_1| - |D_2|\bigr|}{4\pi} + o(1) \leq \frac{|\Omega|}{4\pi} + o(1) \quad \text{as } E \to \infty.
\]
Taking $E \to \infty$ yields
\[
\bigl||D_1| - |D_2|\bigr| \leq |\Omega|,
\]
which completes the proof of (b1).

\textbf{Proof of \textup{(b2)}: Area estimate under rank matching.}
For each eigenvalue \(\lambda\) we have
\[
m_i(\lambda) = \operatorname{rank}R_\lambda^{(i)} + \dim\ker R_\lambda^{(i)},
\]
and \(\operatorname{rank}R_\lambda^{(i)}=0\) for \(\lambda\notin V\).
Summing over \(\lambda\le E\) and separating the visible eigenvalues
from the vanishing directions gives the decomposition
\[
N_{D_i}(E)
=
\sum_{\substack{\lambda\in V\\ \lambda\le E}}
\operatorname{rank} R_\lambda^{(i)}
+
N_{\mathrm{van},D_i}(E),
\]
where the first sum counts the visible directions (the rank of the
restriction map) and the second term counts all vanishing directions,
including both fully invisible eigenvalues and the kernel directions
of partially visible eigenspaces.

Under the rank-matching assumption,
\[
\operatorname{rank}R_\lambda^{(1)}=\operatorname{rank}R_\lambda^{(2)}
\quad\text{for every }\lambda\in V(D_1)=V(D_2),
\]
the visible-rank sums cancel when taking the difference:
\[
N_{D_1}(E)-N_{D_2}(E)
=
N_{\mathrm{van},D_1}(E)-N_{\mathrm{van},D_2}(E).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:invisible-interior},
\(0\le N_{\mathrm{van},D_i}(E)\le N_\Omega(E)\), so
\[

\le N_\Omega(E).
\]
The proof is completed by using Weyl asymptotics, exactly as in \textup{(b1)}.

If $D_1\subset D_2$ and $D_2\subset D_1$, then $D_1=D_2$; hence, after
relabeling, $D_1\setminus D_2\neq\varnothing$. Since $D_1$ is a connected
open subset of $\mathbb R^2$ and hence path connected, and since
$D^\ast$ is open and nonempty, we may choose a continuous path
$\gamma:[0,1]\to D_1$ with $\gamma(0)\in D^\ast$ and
$\gamma(1)\in D_1\setminus D_2$. Set
\[
t^\ast:=\inf\{t\in[0,1]:\gamma(t)\notin D^\ast\}.
\]
The set $\{t\in[0,1]:\gamma(t)\notin D^\ast\}$ is nonempty, since it
contains $1$, and it does not contain $0$, so $0<t^\ast\le 1$. Then
$p:=\gamma(t^\ast)\in\partial D^\ast\cap D_1$, and by definition of
$t^\ast$ there are $t_n\nearrow t^\ast$ with $\gamma(t_n)\in D^\ast$; set
$y_n:=\gamma(t_n)$. Since $D^\ast\subset D_1\cap D_2\subset D_2$, we have
$y_n\in D_2$ for every $n$, and by continuity of $\gamma$,
$p=\lim_n y_n\in\overline{D_2}$. If $p\in D_2$, then $p\in D_1\cap D_2$,
and because $D_1\cap D_2$ is open, a small ball centered at $p$ would lie
in $D_1\cap D_2$ and meet $D^\ast$, forcing $p\in D^\ast$, a
contradiction. Combining $p\in\overline{D_2}$ with $p\notin D_2$, we
obtain $p\in\partial D_2$; together with $p\in D_1$, this gives
$p\in\partial D_2\cap D_1$. The symmetric case yields a point in
$\partial D_1\cap D_2$.

<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:diagonal}, for $i=1,2$ the backscattering field
$u_i^s(y,y,k_0)$ is real-analytic with respect to $y\in D_i$. Hence the difference
$u_1^s(y,y,k_0)-u_2^s(y,y,k_0)$ is real-analytic on $D_1\cap D_2$. Choose a nonempty connected open subset $\mathcal U_0\Subset \mathcal U$, and let $D^\ast$ be the connected component of $D_1\cap D_2$ containing $\mathcal U_0$. Since this difference vanishes identically on $\mathcal U_0$, the identity theorem for real-analytic functions implies that
\[
u_1^s(y,y,k_0)=u_2^s(y,y,k_0), \quad y\in D^\ast.
\]

Assume for contradiction that $D_1\neq D_2$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:boundary-crossing}, applied
with the connected component $D^\ast$ of $D_1\cap D_2$ containing
$\mathcal U_0$, there exist a point
\[
p\in (\partial D_1\cap D_2)\cup(\partial D_2\cap D_1)
\]
and a sequence \((y_n)\subset D^\ast\) with \(y_n\to p\).
By symmetry, it suffices to consider the case
\[
p\in \partial D_1\cap D_2.
\]
Since $p\in D_2$, the function $y\mapsto u_2^s(y,y,k_0)$ extends continuously to $p$, and
\[
u_2^s(y,y,k_0)\to u_2^s(p,p,k_0)
\quad\text{as } y\to p,\ y\in D^\ast.
\]
In particular,
\[
\Re\,u_2^s(y,y,k_0)\to \Re\,u_2^s(p,p,k_0)
\quad\text{as } y\to p,\ y\in D^\ast.
\]
On the other hand, Lemma~\ref{lem:diag-regular-part-blowup} gives
\[
\Re\,u_1^s(y,y,k_0)\to -\infty
\quad\text{as } y\to p,\ y\in D^\ast,
\]
because $p\in \partial D_1$. Since the two diagonal values agree on $D^\ast$, it follows that
\[
\Re\,u_2^s(y,y,k_0)\to -\infty
\quad\text{as } y\to p,\ y\in D^\ast,
\]
which contradicts the finite limit $\Re\,u_2^s(p,p,k_0)$. Consequently, \(D_1=D_2\). The proof is complete.
