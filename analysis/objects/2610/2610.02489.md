<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
Parking functions of size $n$ with $\ell$-limited preferences are in bijection with trees in $\mathcal{T}_n$ where each vertex has at most $\ell$ children.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm: 1-ell-avoidance}
    For $\ell \geq 2$, if we let  $A:=A(x,y_2,\dots,y_\ell)$, then
    \[
    A = 1 + xA + \sum_{i = 2}^\ell y_i(xA)^i
    \]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Given such a Dyck path, it is either empty, of the form $UDB$ where $B$ is another such Dyck path, or $U^{i}DB_1D B_2 D \cdots D B_i$ where each $B_j$ is another such Dyck path. As we range over $i = 2,\dots,\ell$, each of these is recorded by $y_i (xA)^i$. This covers all the possible ways such a Dyck path would start and recovers the desired functional equation.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:Pell}
    For $n \geq 0$ and $\ell\geq 2$, $p_n(1^{\ell+1})$ has exponential generating function
    \[
    P_\ell(x) = \sum_{n \geq 0} \frac{p_n(1^{\ell+1})}{n!} x^n = A(x,\tfrac{1}{2!},\tfrac{1}{3!},\tfrac{1}{4!},\dots,\tfrac{1}{\ell!})
    \]
    where $A$ is defined as in Theorem \ref{thm: 1-ell-avoidance}.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The number of parking functions with $n$ cars avoiding $1^{\ell+1}$ is exactly
    \[
    \sum_{(k_2,\dots,k_\ell)\in\mathcal J_n} \frac{n!\, a_{n,k_2,k_3,\dots,k_\ell}}{(2!)^{k_2}(3!)^{k_3}\cdots(\ell!)^{k_\ell}},
    \qquad
    \mathcal J_n=\Big\{(k_2,\dots,k_\ell)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\ell-1}\;:\;\sum_{j=2}^\ell jk_j\le n\Big\},
    \]
    because $a_{n,k_2,k_3,\dots,k_\ell}$ is the number of weakly increasing parking functions of length $n$ in which, for each $i$, exactly $k_i$ distinct values have multiplicity $i$.  Each such weakly increasing parking function can be rearranged in $n!/\prod_i (i!)^{k_i}$ distinct ways. Dividing by $n!$ and summing over $n$ gives $A(x,\tfrac1{2!},\tfrac1{3!},\dots,\tfrac1{\ell!})$ by Theorem~\ref{thm: 1-ell-avoidance}.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Using Theorems \ref{thm:Pell} and \ref{thm: 1-ell-avoidance} in combination with Lagrange inversion, one can show that for $n \geq 1$,
    \[
    p_n(1^{\ell+1}) = (n!)^2 \sum_{\mathcal{J}_n} \frac{1}{\left(1 + \sum_{j=2}^\ell (j-1)k_j\right)! \left(n - \sum_{j=2}^\ell j k_j\right)! \prod_{j=2}^\ell \left(k_j! (j!)^{k_j}\right)}
    \]
    where $\mathcal{J}_n = \{(k_2,\dots,k_\ell) \in \mathbb{Z}^{\ell-1}_{\geq 0} \; | \; \sum_{j=2}^\ell j k_j \le n\}$.
<!-- end remark-1 -->

    \[
    P_2(x) = \sum_{n \geq 0} \frac{p_n(111)}{n!} x^n = \frac{1-x-\sqrt{1-2x-x^2}}{x^2}
    \]

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem: motzkin}
    When $\ell=2$, $A(x,y)$ is a refinement of the Motzkin numbers. Indeed, the map $F\leftrightarrow UD$, $U\leftrightarrow UUD$, $D\leftrightarrow D$ is a bijection between Motzkin paths of length $n$ with $k$ up steps and Dyck paths of semi-length $n$ with no $UUU$ and exactly $k$ occurrences of $UU$. For example, $UFUDD$ corresponds to $UUDUDUUDDD$. Choosing the positions of the $2k$ non-level steps and then a Dyck path of semi-length $k$ on them gives the explicit formula
    \begin{equation}\label{eq: ank}
        a_{n,k}=\binom{n}{2k}C_k ,
    \end{equation}
    where $C_k$ is the $k$-th Catalan number. More generally, for $\ell\geq 2$ the series $A(x,1,\dots,1)$ counts Dyck paths with no $U^{\ell+1}$, equivalently ordered trees in which every vertex has at most $\ell$ children.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm: 212}
    Let $\sigma\in\{212,221,122\}$ and, for $1\le k\le n$, let $p_{n,k}$ denote the number of $\{111,\sigma\}$-avoiding parking functions of size $n$ whose largest entry is $k$, so that $p_n(111,\sigma)=\sum_{k=1}^n p_{n,k}$. Then $p_{1,1}=p_{2,1}=1$, $p_{2,2}=2$, and for $n\ge3$,
    \[
    p_{n,k} =
    \begin{cases}
    0 & k=1,  \\
    p_{n,k-1} + n\, p_{n-1, k-1} + (n-1)\, p_{n-2, k-1} & 1 < k < n,\\
    n\, p_{n-1}(111,\sigma) & k = n.
    \end{cases}
    \]
    In particular $p_n(111,212)=p_n(111,221)=p_n(111,122)$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We will focus on the case where $\sigma = 212$ since the other cases are similar. If $k = 1$ and $n \geq 3$, there are no such parking functions. If $k = n$, we can take any such parking function of size $n-1$ and add $n$ into any of the $n$ positions to obtain a parking function that still avoids $111$ and $212$, but now has $n$ as its largest element. This shows $p_{n,n} = n p_{n-1}(111,\sigma)$. We now focus on the less trivial cases when $1 < k < n$.

    We want to find the number of $\{111,212\}$-avoiding parking functions of size $n$ whose largest element is $k$. First, we can take any $\{111, 212\}$-avoiding parking function $\pi$ of length $n$ with $k-1$ as its largest element and we can replace all the $(k-1)$'s with $k$'s. This is still a parking function. Since $\pi$ avoids $111$, at most two entries of $\pi$ equal $k-1$, so at least $n-2\ge k-1$ entries are smaller than $k-1$. As a result, $|\{i:\pi_i\le m\}|\ge m$ still holds for $m=k-1$, and the counts for every other $m$ are unchanged. Since the relabelling is order preserving, the new word still avoids $111$ and $212$. This accounts for all the $\{111,212\}$-avoiding parking functions of length $n$ with $k$ as the largest element where no $k-1$ is present. There are $p_{n,k-1}$ such parking functions.

    Now we need to account for the $\{111,212\}$-avoiding parking functions that do contain a $k-1$. We first consider those with a single copy of $k$. These parking functions come from inserting a $k$ into any position of a $\{111,212\}$-avoiding parking function of size $n-1$ with largest element $k-1$. There are therefore $n p_{n-1,k-1}$ such parking functions. Next, we consider the parking functions with two copies of $k$; this is the only other case because of the $111$-avoidance condition. To avoid $212$, since $k$ is the maximal element, we must have the two copies of $k$ occur consecutively. We can therefore construct these parking functions by adding $kk$ into any position of a $\{111,212\}$-avoiding parking function of size $n-2$ whose largest element is $k-1$. There are $(n-1)p_{n-2,k-1}$ such parking functions.

    This accounts for all the possibilities and establishes the desired recursion when $1 < k < n$. The proof is almost identical when $212$ is replaced by either $122$ or $221$. The only difference is in the final step, instead of the two $k$ terms occurring consecutively, one of the $k$'s must occur at the beginning or the end of the parking function. The recursion remains the same.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm: 121}
    If we let $p_n$ denote the number of $\{111, \sigma\}$-avoiding parking functions, where $\sigma \in \{121,211,112\}$, then $p_n = \sum_{k=1}^n p_{n,k}$ where $p_{1,1} = 1$, $p_{0} = 1$ and $p_{m,0} = 0$ for all $m$ and for $n \ge 2$:
    \[
    p_{n,k} =
    \begin{cases}
    (n-1) p_{n-2} & k = 1\\
    np_{n-1,k-1} +
    p_{n,k-1}  - np_{n-1,k-2} - (n-1) p_{n-2,k-2} & 1 < k <n \\
    n! & k=n.
    \end{cases}
    \]
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We will consider the case where $\sigma = 121$, the others are similar. Here, $P_{n,k}$ denotes the $\{111,121\}$-avoiding parking functions of size $n$ whose set of used letters is $S$, where $\{1,\dots,k\} \subset S$ and $k+1 \not\in S$. We let $p_{n,k} = |P_{n,k}|$. When $k = n$, we get permutations, so there are $n!$ options. When $k = 1$, we must have two copies of $1$ because the parking function cannot contain $2$. These are obtained by taking any $\{111,121\}$-avoiding parking function of length $n-2$, shifting all its entries up by two and inserting $11$ anywhere. The $11$ must be consecutive to avoid $121$. There are $(n-1)p_{n-2}$ such parking functions. We now consider the case where $1 < k < n$.

    First, we consider the parking functions with a single copy of $1$. We can obtain these by taking any parking function in $P_{n-1,k-1}$, shifting all of the entries up by $1$ and inserting a $1$ into any position. There are $n p_{n-1,k-1}$ such functions. These account for all the desired parking functions with a single copy of $1$.

    We now claim that there are $p_{n,k-1} - np_{n-1,k-2} - (n-1) p_{n-2,k-2}$ parking functions in $P_{n,k}$ with two copies of $1$. To see this, let $X$ denote the set of $\pi \in P_{n,k}$ with two copies of $1$ and let $Y$ denote the set of $\pi \in P_{n,k-1}$ with two copies of $1$. By the previous paragraph we have $|Y| = p_{n,k-1} - n p_{n-1,k-2}$. Now, we split $Y$ into two disjoint subsets, $Y = Y' \sqcup Y''$ where $Y'$ consists of those parking functions that contain $k+1$ and $Y''$ consists of those that do not. We claim $|X| = |Y'|$ and $|Y''| = (n-1) p_{n-2,k-2}$ which gives
    \[
    |X| = p_{n,k-1} - n p_{n-1,k-2} - (n-1) p_{n-2,k-2}
    \]
    as desired. 

    For the first claim, send $\pi \in X$ to the word $\pi'$ obtained by replacing every $k$ by $k+1$, we can do this because $k+1 \not\in S$. Since $k+1 \notin S$, this relabelling is order preserving, so $\pi'$ still avoids $111$ and $121$. It is also still a parking function. Indeed, the number of entries at most $m$ is unchanged for every $m \neq k$, while $|\{j : \pi'_j \le k\}| = |\{j : \pi_j \le k-1\}| \ge k$, because $\pi$ contains $1$ twice and each of $2,\dots,k-1$ at least once. But by construction $\pi'$ contains $\{1,\dots,k-1\}$ and $k+1$ but not $k$, so $\pi' \in Y'$. The inverse map replaces every $k+1$ by $k$, this shows $|X| = |Y'|$.

    Now to prove $|Y''| = (n-1) p_{n-2,k-2}$, let $\pi \in Y''$ so that $\pi$ contains $1$ twice and neither $k$ nor $k+1$. To avoid $121$, the two $1$s are adjacent, so we can delete them and lower every entry in $\{2,\dots, k-1\}$ by one and every entry greater than or equal to $k+2$ by two (possible since $\pi$ does not contain $k$ or $k+1$). The resulting word, $\tau$, is a parking function. Indeed, for $m \le k-2$, $|\{j:\tau_j\le m\}| = |\{j:\pi_j\le m+1\}| - 2 \ge m$, since $\pi$ contains $1$ twice and each of $2,\dots,m+1$ at least once and for $m \ge k-1$, $|\{j:\tau_j\le m\}| = |\{j:\pi_j\le m+2\}| - 2 \ge m$. Since $\tau$ contains $\{1,\dots,k-2\}$ but does not contain $k-1$, we conclude $\tau \in P_{n-2,k-2}$. There were $n-1$ places the repeated $1$s could appear in the original parking function. 
    
    Conversely, given $\pi' \in P_{n-2,k-2}$ we can add $11$ in any of the $n-1$ positions of $\pi'$, shift all the entries $\{1,\dots,k-2\}$ up by one and all the entries greater than or equal to $k$ up by two and we reverse this process. This shows $|Y''| = (n-1) p_{n-2,k-2}$ and establishes the desired recursion. When $k = 2$ both $Y''$ and $P_{n-2,0}$ are empty, and the formula reduces to $p_{n,2} = n\, p_{n-1,1} + p_{n,1}$.

    The cases where $\sigma = 211$ or $112$ are essentially identical except in the final step. Wherever we used that two copies of $1$ must be adjacent, we use instead that the first (resp. last) copy of $1$ must be the first (resp. last) entry of the word, while the other copy is unconstrained. In each case there are again $n-1$ ways to place the pair.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm: 112-121}
    For $n \geq 1$, $p_n(111,112,211) = p_n(111,112,121) = p_n(111,211,121) = p_n$ where $p_n=\sum_k p_{n,k}$, with $p_{1,1} = 1$ and for $n \ge 2$,
    \[
    p_{n,k} = 
    \begin{cases}
         p_{n-2} & k = 1,\\
        np_{n-1,k-1} + \sum_{j=k-1}^{n-2} p_{n-2,j} & 1 < k < n,\\
        n! & k = n.
    \end{cases}
    \]
    In particular, all three sets are Wilf-equivalent.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $P_{n,k}$ denote the set of $\{111, 112,211\}$-avoiding parking functions of size $n$ whose set of used letters is $S$, where $\{1,\dots,k\} \subset S$ and $k+1 \not\in S$. We let $p_{n,k} = |P_{n,k}|$. When $k = 1$, we cannot have a single copy of $1$ and skip $2$, so the only case to consider is when we have two copies of $1$. In this case, the $1$'s must appear at the beginning and end of the parking function. We can then take any parking function of length $n-2$ that avoids $111, 112,$ and $211$, shift all the entries up by two and place it between the two $1$'s. This shows $p_{n,1} = p_{n-2}$.

    When $k = n$, every element of $P_{n,n}$ is a permutation, so $p_{n,n} = n!$. The argument below also covers this case because the second sum will be empty, but we separate to make the argument more straightforward. Now if $1 < k < n$, we have two cases to consider. Either, the parking function contains a single copy of $1$ or two copies of $1$. In the first case, there are $np_{n-1,k-1}$ options because we can take any parking function in $P_{n-1,k-1}$, shift all the entries up by one and add a $1$ anywhere.

    In the second case, if $\pi \in P_{n,k}$ has two copies of $1$ they must appear at the beginning and end of $\pi$ to avoid $112$ and $211$. For the entries between the two $1$'s we can take any parking function in $P_{n-2,j}$ for $k-1 \leq j \leq n-2$, and shift its entries less than $k$ up by one and its entries greater than or equal to $k$ up by two. The $j$ keeps track of where the next jump occurs, making this an easily reversible process.

    For the other cases $\{111,112,121\}$ and $\{111,211,121\}$, the only change is that the two copies of $1$ must be adjacent and at the end (resp.\ the beginning) of $\pi$; again there is exactly one way to place them.

\label{thm: 122-221}

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $p_{n,k}$ be the number of $\{111,122,221\}$-avoiding parking functions with maximal entry $k$.
    If we wish to form $p_{n,k}$, we can add $k$ to any $\{111,122,221\}$-avoiding parking function of length $n-1$ and smaller maximal entry than $k$. We can add this $k$ into any position without creating a $221$ or $122$ pattern. Or we could add two $k$'s to any $\{111,122,221\}$-avoiding parking function of length $n-2$ and smaller maximal entry than $k$. We can, however, only add these two copies of $k$ as the first and last entry, otherwise we would create a $122$ or $221$. This shows that for $k<n$, 
    \[
    p_{n,k} = n\sum_{m<k} p_{n-1,m} + \sum_{m<k} p_{n-2,m},
    \]
    and subtracting the corresponding expression for $p_{n,k-1}$ gives the stated recursion. When $k=n$ the second sum is absent, so $p_{n,n}=n\,p_{n-1}$. The lower bound on $k$ comes from the $111$ avoidance.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm: 121-212}
    For $n \geq 1$, $p_n(111,121,212) = \sum_{k=0}^n (n-k)!\,a_{n,k}$.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Avoiding $121$ and $212$ forces all repeated elements to appear consecutively. If we take a weakly increasing parking function that avoids $111$ with $k$ repeated elements, we can permute all the $n-k$ distinct entries in $(n-k)!$ ways, giving $(n-k)!a_{n,k}$ possible parking functions. Summing over the possible number of repeated elements yields the stated result.

<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{thm: 211-122}
    For $n \geq 1$, $p_n(111,211,122) = p_n(111,112,221) = \frac{(n+1)!}{2}$.
<!-- end theorem-8 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
First, notice such a parking function can have at most one repeated entry. If two entries $a<b$ repeat, this means the parking function contains one of $aabb, abab, baba, baab, abba, bbaa$, all of which contain either a $211$ or $122$ pattern.

    The number of parking functions with no repeated entries is $n!$. If we are allowed to have an entry occur at most two times, the number of weakly increasing parking functions with one entry repeating two times is $\binom{n}{2}$. Indeed, select two entries from $n$, let the repeated entry be the minimal entry we select, remove the other entry we select and keep the remaining $n-2$ entries. 
    
    After selecting the entries, we must arrange them. The initial entry of the parking function must be the repeated entry. We can then permute the remaining entries in any way. This means there are $\binom{n}{2} (n-1)!$ such parking functions with a repeat. In total, we find $p_n(111,211,122) = n! + \binom{n}{2} (n-1)! = \frac{(n+1)!}{2}$.

<!-- theorem-9: body_md -->
\label{thm: 112-122}
    For $n\geq 1$, $p_n=p_n(111,112,122)=p_n(111,112,212)=p_n(111,121,122)$ where $p_n=\sum_{k,\ell} p_{n,k,\ell}$ with 
    \[
    p_{n,k,\ell}=
    \begin{cases}
        (\ell-1)\sum_{m<k}p_{n-1,m,\ell-1} + \sum_{j\geq \ell-1, m<k} p_{n-2,m,j} & 2 \le k < n\\
        (\ell-1)\sum_{m<n}p_{n-1,m,\ell-1} & k = n.
    \end{cases}
    \]
    When $k = 1$, $p_{1,1,2} = 1$, $p_{2,1,2} = 1$, and for all other values of $n$ and $\ell$, $p_{n,1,\ell} = 0$.
<!-- end theorem-9 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
We focus on the case of avoiding $112$ and $212$, the others are similar and we will remark on the similarities at the end of the proof. Let $p_{n,k,\ell}$ denote the number of $\{111,112,212\}$-avoiding parking functions of size $n$, largest element $k$, and $\ell$ places where one could insert a new largest element. We will sometimes refer to these as admissible positions. By definition we must have $2 \le \ell \le n+1$, since it is always possible to insert a new largest element at the start of the parking function or in the second position. Furthermore, in this case, the admissible positions must all appear consecutively at the beginning of the parking function. Indeed, reading from left to right, we can place a new largest element up until we have our first repeat occurrence of any element. As soon as we have a repeated entry, we cannot place a larger element after that spot. This means $\ell$ actually tracks the position of the first repeated element.
    
    If $k = 1$, $p_{n,1,\ell} = 0$ when $n > 2$ to avoid $111$, so we only have a few cases to consider: $p_{1,1,2} = 1$, $p_{2,1,2} = 1$ and $p_{2,1,3} = 0$. Now assume $k > 1$.

    First, we consider how many of these parking function have a single occurrence of $k$. One could build such a parking function by inserting a single $k$ into a parking function with maximal entry less than $k$. The parking functions we start with will therefore have size $n-1$ and largest element $m$ for some $m < k$. If we want the resulting parking function to have $\ell$ admissible positions, we need the parking function we start with to have $\ell-1$ admissible positions. Indeed, if we place $k$ into any of those $\ell-1$ places, we could then place a new largest element on either side of $k$ and in any of the remaining $\ell-2$ places we did not insert $k$. This means the parking functions we desire with a single occurrence of $k$ are enumerated by $(\ell-1) \sum_{m < k} p_{n-1,m,\ell-1}$.

    Next, for $k < n$ we consider how many of these parking function contain two copies of $k$. We need $k < n$ because $n$ cannot appear twice in any parking function; in the case $k = n$, we only get the first sum. To avoid $212$, the two occurrences of $k$ must be consecutive. We will similarly build such a parking function by inserting two consecutive copies of $k$ into a parking function with maximal entry less than $k$ and size $n-2$. Since $\ell$ records the position of the first repeated element and we are introducing a repetition, this means we must add our two copies of $k$ into positions $\ell-1$ and $\ell$. In order to be able to add a largest element in these positions, the first repetition in our starting parking function must occur in a position $\ge \ell-1$. In all, this means the number of desired parking functions with two occurrences of $k$ is precisely $\sum_{j \ge \ell-1, m < k} p_{n-2,m,j}$ as desired.

    Now if we instead considered $112,122$ avoidance, the only change in the argument is when we add two copies of the largest element. In this case instead of appearing consecutively, one must appear at the start of the parking function and we place the second entry in position $\ell$. 
    
    For $121,122$ avoidance the admissible positions look different, so we need to explain this case in more detail. A new largest entry creates a $121$ exactly when some value occurs both before and after it, and it cannot create a $122$. This means position $p$ is admissible if and only if $\pi_1\cdots\pi_{p-1}$ and $\pi_p\cdots\pi_n$ have no value in common. In particular positions $1$ and $n+1$ are always admissible, but the admissible positions don't have to be consecutive anymore. For example, for $\pi=3311$ they are $1$, $3$ and $5$. Also, now two copies of the largest entry don't have to be adjacent, e.g. $3123$ avoids $111$, $121$
    and $122$. Despite this, interestingly, the recursion is the same. If $k$ occurs once, it was inserted into an admissible position of a word $\tau$ with $\ell-1$ admissible positions, and since $k$ occurs only once this creates exactly one new admissible position, the one immediately after $k$, while preserving every other position. This gives the first term. If $k$ occurs twice, avoiding $122$ forces the first copy to be $\pi_1$, and avoiding $121$ forces the second copy into an admissible position of the remaining word $\tau$. Conversely, every such placement avoids $111$, $121$ and $122$. Now, suppose the admissible positions of $\tau$ are $a_1<\dots<a_j$ and the second $k$ is inserted at $a_i$. In $\pi$, position $1$ is admissible, no position between the two copies of $k$ is, the position immediately after the second $k$ is admissible because $a_i$ was, and each later position is admissible if and only if the corresponding position of $\tau$ was. So the number of admissible positions of $\pi$ is $1+1+(j-i)=j-i+2$, and for each $\ell\ge2$ every $\tau$ with $j\ge\ell-1$ gives exactly one $\pi$ with $\ell$ admissible positions, namely $i=j-\ell+2$. This is the second term.

<!-- theorem-10: body_md -->
\label{thm: repeatedAvoidance}
    If $\Lambda$ is a subset of the permutations of $\{1,1,2,2\}$, and $M_k(\Lambda)$ is the number of permutations of $[k] \cup [k]$ avoiding $\Lambda$, then 
    \[
    p_n(111,\Lambda) = \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor} M_k(\Lambda) (n-2k)! \binom{n}{2k} a_{n,k}
    \]
    where $a_{n,k}$ is the number of Dyck paths with no occurrence of $UUU$, and exactly $k$ occurrences of $UU$.
<!-- end theorem-10 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
There are $a_{n,k}$ weakly increasing parking functions with $k$ repeated entries avoiding $111$. The number of ways to permute the repeated entries while still avoiding $\Lambda$ is $M_k(\Lambda)$. We can then permute the non-repeated entries in $(n-2k)!$ ways. We can insert the non-repeated entries anywhere among the repeated entries without creating a pattern in $\Lambda$ because $\Lambda$ consists of permutations of $[2] \cup [2]$. There are $\binom{n}{n-2k} = \binom{n}{2k}$ ways to insert the non-repeated entries. Summing over the number of possible repeated entries gives the result.
<!-- end proof-10 -->
<!-- theorem-11: body_md -->
Let $n\geq 1.$
    The number of parking functions avoiding $111$ and $\Lambda = \{1221, 2112\}$ or $\Lambda = \{1212, 2121\}$ is
\[
p_n(111,\Lambda) = \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor} \frac{n!}{(k+1)!} a_{n,k}.
\]
<!-- end theorem-11 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
We can use Theorem \ref{thm: repeatedAvoidance} here. In this case $\Lambda = \{1221, 2112\}$ or $\{1212,2121\}$ and so, in both cases, $M_k(\Lambda) = k!C_k$, since $C_k$ counts both the number of non-nesting  and the number of non-crossing matchings on $k$ elements. Simplifying
    \[
    \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor} k! C_k (n-2k)! \binom{n}{2k} a_{n,k}
    \]
    gives the desired result.

    It is clear that $p_1 = 1$. Now, notice any such parking function of length $n+1$ can either have one or two copies of $1$. If there is only one copy of $1$, we can form these parking functions by adding a $1$ to $\pi \in P_n(\Lambda)$. <!-- theorem-15: body_md -->
\label{thm: 221-112-1k} For $n\geq 1$ and $\ell\ge1$,
    \[
    p_n(1^{\ell+1},221,112) \;=\; n!\;+\sum_{j=2}^{\ell}\binom{n}{j}\,(n-j+1)! = n!\Big(1+\sum_{j=2}^{\ell}\frac{n-j+1}{j!}\Big).
    \]
<!-- end theorem-15 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
First, notice such a parking function can have at most one repeated entry. If two entries $a,b$ repeat, this means the parking function contains one of $aabb, abab, baba, baab, abba, bbaa$, all of which contain either a $221$ or $112$ pattern.

    The number of parking functions with no repeated entries is $n!$, this corresponds to $j=1$. If we are allowed to have an entry occur at most $j$ times, the number of weakly increasing parking functions with one entry repeating $j$ times is $\binom{n}{j}$. Indeed, if we select $j$ entries from $n$, let the repeated entry be the minimal entry we select, remove the other entries we select and keep the remaining $n-j$ entries. 
    
    After selecting the entries, we must arrange them. Let $a$ be the repeated entry. Any entry following the second copy of $a$ forms a $112$ (if larger) or a $221$ (if smaller) with the first two copies of $a$, so every entry after the second copy of $a$ equals $a$. This means the parking function is $w\,a^{\,j-1}$, where $w$ is any of the $(n-j+1)!$ orderings of the $n-j$ other entries together with one copy of $a$. It is clear that every such word avoids $221$ and $112$. Summing over $j$ gives the first formula, and the second follows from $\binom nj(n-j+1)!=n!\,(n-j+1)/j!$.
