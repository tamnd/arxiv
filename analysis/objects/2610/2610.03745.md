<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:lifts}
Fix $\delta\in\{+1,-1\}$. In the coordinate frame on the cover, the two Pin structures are
\[
 P_{\delta,\varepsilon}=
 (\widetilde M\times\operatorname{Pin}^\delta(2))/\mathbb Z,\qquad \varepsilon\in\{+1,-1\},
\]
where the generator acts by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal}
 (p,g)\longmapsto(\tau p,\varepsilon e_w g).
\end{equation}
Their lifted generators square to $(p,g)\mapsto(\tau^2p,\delta g)$. Changing $\varepsilon$ is the nontrivial torsor action.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
In the coordinate trivialization,
\[
 O(TM)=(\widetilde M\times O(2))/((p,h)\sim(\tau p,Ah)).
\]
Since $p_\delta(\varepsilon e_w)=A$, the map $[p,g]\mapsto[p,p_\delta(g)]$ is a well-defined equivariant double cover of this frame bundle. The action \eqref{eq:principal} commutes with the right Pin action. Its square is as asserted because $(\varepsilon e_w)^2=\delta$; this is allowed because the deck group is $\mathbb Z$, with no order-two relation on its generator.

Conversely, pull a Pin structure back to the contractible strip and lift the coordinate frame to a global Pin frame. The lift of $\tau$ must multiply this frame by an element over $A$. There are exactly two such elements, $e_w$ and $-e_w$, and continuity makes the choice constant. A change of lifted coordinate frame over the same orthogonal frame is a constant sign, which does not change this choice. This gives exactly the two structures. Twisting by the nontrivial real flat line, with character $n\mapsto(-1)^n$, changes $\varepsilon$ to $-\varepsilon$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Inequivalent Pin lifts should not be confused with inequivalent underlying complex vector bundles. Since $M$ retracts onto a circle, the latter can be trivial even when the Pin data or flat connection differs.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:covariance}
For every $g\in\operatorname{Pin}^\delta(2)$ and $v\in\mathbb R^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:covariance}
 \rho_\delta(g)\gamma(v)\rho_\delta(g)^{-1}
 =\gamma(p_\delta(g)v).
\end{equation}
Thus $-i\gamma$ gives a skew-Hermitian Clifford multiplication on the associated bundle.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
It suffices to check a unit vector $u$, since such vectors generate the Pin group. The matrix $\gamma(u)\Gamma$ anticommutes with $\gamma(u)$ and commutes with $\gamma(v)$ for $v\perp u$. Conjugation therefore changes the normal component of $v$ by a sign and fixes its perpendicular component. Multiplication of the conjugating matrix by $-i$ does not affect its conjugation action. This proves \eqref{eq:covariance}. Also $\gamma(v)^2=|v|^2I$, so $(-i\gamma(v))^2=-|v|^2I$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:sections}
Smooth sections of $S_c$ correspond bijectively to smooth functions on the strip satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:equiv}
 \Psi(x+1,-w)=R_c\Psi(x,w).
\end{equation}
This correspondence extends to $L^2$ and to nonnegative geometric Sobolev orders. The $L^2$ norm is the integral over $F=[0,1]\times[-1,1]$. Furthermore,
\[
 R_c^2=c^2I=\delta I,\quad \operatorname{spec}(R_c)=\{c,-c\},\quad
 E_{\pm c}(R_c)=E_{\pm1}(\sigma_1).
\]
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The associated quotient is $(\widetilde M\times\mathbb C^2)/((p,v)\sim(\tau p,R_cv))$. A section gives a unique lift in this trivialization, and equality of its two descriptions at $p$ and $\tau p$ is exactly \eqref{eq:equiv}. Conversely, \eqref{eq:equiv} makes $[p,\Psi(p)]$ independent of the chosen lift of a base point. Smoothness is a local property of these descriptions. Unitarity of $R_c$ makes the norm independent of the fundamental domain. The same argument with measurable functions or weak derivatives gives the stated extensions. The matrix statements follow from $\sigma_1^2=I$ and its two one-dimensional eigenspaces.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:fourier}
Every $L^2$ section has the unique expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fourier}
 \Psi(x,w)=\sum_{j=\pm1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}
 e^{iq_{c,j,k}x}\phi_{j,k}(w),\qquad \phi_{j,k}\in\mathcal K_j,
\end{equation}
with convergence in $L^2(F)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parseval}
 \left\lVert\Psi\right\rVert_{L^2(M)}^2=\sum_{j,k}\left\lVert\phi_{j,k}\right\rVert_{\mathcal K}^2.
\end{equation}
Conversely, every square-summable coefficient family of this form defines a section. The phase assignments are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\toprule
Pin type&$\varepsilon$&$R_c$&$\theta_{c,+1}$&$\theta_{c,-1}$\\
\midrule
$+$&$+1$&$\sigma_1$&$0$&$1/2$\\
$+$&$-1$&$-\sigma_1$&$1/2$&$0$\\
$-$&$+1$&$i\sigma_1$&$1/4$&$3/4$\\
$-$&$-1$&$-i\sigma_1$&$3/4$&$1/4$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Apply $\Pi_j$ to \eqref{eq:one-step}. The result has scalar quasiperiod $cj$. Multiplication by $e^{-2\pi i\theta_{c,j}x}$ converts it into a periodic $\mathcal K_j$-valued function of period one. The ordinary Hilbert-valued Fourier theorem gives \eqref{eq:Fourier} and Parseval on each summand. The summands are orthogonal because $\mathcal K_+\perp\mathcal K_-$, even though the two scalar shifted exponential systems are not mutually orthogonal on $[0,1]$. Reversing the construction proves the converse. The table is obtained by taking the arguments of $cj$.

\label{cor:holonomy}

The tensor-product deck representation multiplies $R_c$ by the scalar holonomy. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:fourier} to the resulting one-step operator.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:sobolev}
For each integer $m\geq0$, the Fourier transform in Theorem~\ref{thm:fourier} identifies $H_c^m$ with the coefficient families satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sobolevnorm}
 \sum_{j,k}\sum_{r=0}^{m}(1+q_{c,j,k}^2)^{m-r}
       \left\lVert\partial_w^r\phi_{j,k}\right\rVert_{L^2(-1,1)}^2<\infty.
\end{equation}
The square root of this expression is equivalent to the geometric norm. All $H_c^s$, $s\geq0$, are complete, and smooth equivariant sections are dense. For $t\geq s\geq0$, $H_c^t\hookrightarrow H_c^s$ continuously; for $t>s$ the inclusion is compact. Translation in $x$ and $J$ act continuously and preserve the sector decomposition at every order.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
For smooth sections, apply Parseval to every $\partial_x^a\partial_w^r$ with $a+r\leq m$. Differentiation in $w$ changes $\mathcal K_j$ to $\mathcal K_{(-1)^rj}$, preserving orthogonality of the two summands. For each fixed $r$, the longitudinal weight is $\sum_{a=0}^{m-r}q^{2a}$, uniformly equivalent to $(1+q^2)^{m-r}$. This proves \eqref{eq:sobolevnorm}; the distributional characterization gives the converse as well.

Here are analytic details concerning descent. On the length-two cylinder consider fields satisfying $\Psi(x+2,w)=c^2\Psi(x,w)$. They are sections of a scalar flat twist of a trivial rank-two bundle. In these spaces
\[
 (G\Psi)(x,w)=R_c^{-1}\Psi(x+1,-w)
\]
is an isometric involution. The bounded projection $(I+G)/2$ has range exactly the descended sections. Integer Sobolev spaces on this cylinder are complete by the weak-derivative definition. Smooth approximation near an interval endpoint can be obtained by a bounded extension, followed by convolution; a finite partition of unity gives global smooth approximation. Projecting these approximants by $(I+G)/2$ preserves convergence and imposes equivariance. This proves completeness and density downstairs. Local trivializations have constant unitary transition matrices, so the derivative norms just used are equivalent to the chart-and-partition definitions of the bundle norms.

For clarity, the compactness assertion uses both variables. On any bounded range of Sobolev orders, extend the transverse interval to a larger circle by a bounded interval extension and a cutoff. Untwist the length-two longitudinal boundary condition by multiplication by a fixed smooth phase. On the resulting torus the Sobolev norms are equivalent to weights $(1+\ell^2+n^2)^s$. The inclusion from order $t$ to $s<t$ is a norm limit of finite Fourier truncations, since the tail bound is $O(N^{s-t})$. Restriction and the equivariance projection prove compactness on $M$. The same extension, restriction and Fourier argument identifies the interpolation spaces with the usual fractional Sobolev spaces. Continuity of embeddings follows from the weights and interpolation. Translation commutes with all derivatives and with $G$. Reflection changes derivative signs, and multiplication by $\sigma_1$ is unitary; hence $J$ preserves the norms and their interpolations.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:anisotropic}
The norm $\sum_{j,k}(1+q_{c,j,k}^2)^s\left\lVert\phi_{j,k}\right\rVert_{L^2}^2$ measures only regularity in $x$. Its inclusion into $L^2$ is not compact: fix one longitudinal mode and choose an orthonormal sequence in its infinite-dimensional $\mathcal K_j$. Also, the product weight $(1+q^2)^s\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{H^s_w}^2$ describes stronger mixed regularity, not the isotropic two-dimensional norm \eqref{eq:sobolevnorm}. Negative orders and boundary traces require a choice of dual or completion; we do not identify all such choices. An all-real operator scale is specified separately in Section~\ref{sec:scale}.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:realization}
The Hilbert space is $\mathcal H_c=L^2(M;S_c)$, with inner product conjugate-linear in the first entry. Define
\[
 \operatorname{Dom}(D_{c,b})=\{\Psi\in H_c^1:(I-b\sigma_1)\Psi(x,\pm1)=0\},
 \qquad D_{c,b}\Psi=D\Psi.
\]
Boundary equalities are trace equalities. For $s\geq0$, write $H_{c,b}^{s+1}$ for the subspace of $H_c^{s+1}$ satisfying the same trace condition.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:boundary}
Condition \eqref{eq:BC} is a local self-adjoint elliptic boundary condition in the boundary-symbol sense.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Green's identity, with the stated inner-product convention, is
\[
 \left\langleDu,v\right\rangle-\left\langleu,Dv\right\rangle=i\int_{\partial M}u^*\gamma(\nu)v,
\]
where $\nu$ is the outward normal. On the two lifted edges $\gamma(\nu)=\pm\sigma_2$. Since $\sigma_2$ interchanges the $\sigma_1$ eigenspaces, the line $E_b(\sigma_1)$ is isotropic and equal to its annihilator for this form. The seam terms cancel by equivariance and $R_c^*\sigma_1 R_c=\sigma_1$.

For the complementing condition, freeze a nonzero tangential frequency $\xi$ and use an inward half-line normal coordinate. The decaying solutions of the zero-energy principal ODE have boundary values in one of the two $\sigma_3$ eigenspaces; changing the sign of $\xi$ or the edge interchanges them. Neither coordinate line $E_{\pm1}(\sigma_3)$ intersects $E_b(\sigma_1)$ nontrivially. Each has dimension one, so the boundary projection is an isomorphism on the decaying subspace. This is the Shapiro--Lopatinski condition. The actual adjoint domain, rather than only its boundary symbol, is proved next.
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:transverse}
On $\mathcal K$, let
\[
 T_b=-i\sigma_2\frac{d}{dw},\qquad
 \operatorname{Dom}(T_b)=\{\phi\in H^1([-1,1];\mathbb C^2):\sigma_1\phi(\pm1)=b\phi(\pm1)\}.
\]
Then $T_b$ is self-adjoint. The involution $J$ preserves its domain and commutes with it. For every $q\in\mathbb R$, the operator
\[
 D_q=q\sigma_1+T_b
\]
with the same domain is self-adjoint, reduces $\mathcal K_\pm$, and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy}
 \left\lVertD_q\phi\right\rVert^2=q^2\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert^2+\left\lVert\phi'\right\rVert^2.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
The boundary form of $T_b$ vanishes on the specified endpoint lines. If $v\in\operatorname{Dom}(T_b^*)$, testing against compactly supported functions shows distributionally that $v'\in L^2$, hence $v\in H^1$. The endpoint values of test functions in $\operatorname{Dom}(T_b)$ vary independently in $E_b(\sigma_1)$. Green's identity therefore forces $v(\pm1)$ into the annihilator of this line for $\sigma_2$, which is the same line. This proves equality of the domains of $T_b$ and its adjoint.

Reflection reverses the derivative, and $\sigma_1\sigma_2=-\sigma_2\sigma_1$; thus $J$ commutes with $T_b$. It also preserves the endpoint condition. The bounded Hermitian operator $q\sigma_1$ preserves this domain and commutes with $J$. The adjoint of its sum with $T_b$ has the domain of $T_b^*$, proving self-adjointness of $D_q$. The projections $(I\pm J)/2$ commute with the operator and preserve the domain, so their restrictions are self-adjoint. Expanding the squared norm gives the cross term
\[
 2q\operatorname{Re}\int_{-1}^1\phi^*\sigma_3\phi'
 =q[\phi^*\sigma_3\phi]_{-1}^{1}=0,
\]
where the last equality follows because $\sigma_3$ interchanges the $\sigma_1$ eigenspaces. This proves \eqref{eq:energy}.
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:SA}
The operator $D_{c,b}$ is densely defined and self-adjoint. Its domain is exactly Definition~\ref{def:realization}, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graph}
 \left\lVert\Psi\right\rVert^2+\left\lVertD_{c,b}\Psi\right\rVert^2
 =\left\lVert\Psi\right\rVert^2+\left\lVert\partial_x\Psi\right\rVert^2+\left\lVert\partial_w\Psi\right\rVert^2.
\end{equation}
Smooth equivariant sections satisfying \eqref{eq:BC} form a core. In particular, their operator is closable and its closure is $D_{c,b}$.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Use Theorem~\ref{thm:fourier}. In its $(j,k)$ summand the differential expression is
\[
 D_{q_{c,j,k}}|_{\mathcal K_j}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:transverse} each of these is self-adjoint on the intersection of $\operatorname{Dom}(T_b)$ with $\mathcal K_j$. Their Hilbert direct sum is self-adjoint on the domain where both the coefficients and their images are square summable. To see this without a domain assumption, the resolvent at $i$ of each summand has norm at most one, and their direct sum is an everywhere-defined inverse; the same is true at $-i$. A densely defined symmetric direct sum with both these ranges equal to the Hilbert space is self-adjoint.

Summing \eqref{eq:energy} identifies its domain with the coefficient condition for $H_c^1$ together with \eqref{eq:BC}, and proves \eqref{eq:graph}. This also proves closedness directly. For the core assertion first truncate the longitudinal expansion in graph norm. In each of the finitely many transverse domains, smooth $H^1$ approximation can be followed by linear endpoint corrections to put both traces in $E_b(\sigma_1)$. The corrections tend to zero in $H^1$ because the trace map is continuous; finally apply $(I+jJ)/2$. The resulting smooth functions converge in the same graph norm. Such a domain is dense in $L^2$, for example by first approximating with functions supported away from the physical boundary and then imposing equivariance.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:compact}
The resolvent of $D_{c,b}$ is compact. Its spectrum is real and discrete, each eigenvalue has finite multiplicity, and there is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Its kernel is finite dimensional, its range is closed, and
\[
 \operatorname{ran} D_{c,b}=(\ker D_{c,b})^\perp,\qquad \operatorname{ind} D_{c,b}=0.
\]
Here the index is the ordinary complex Fredholm index of the ungraded operator.
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Self-adjointness gives $(D_{c,b}-i)^{-1}$ on all of $\mathcal H_c$. The identity
\[
 \left\lVert(D_{c,b}-i)\Psi\right\rVert^2=\left\lVertD_{c,b}\Psi\right\rVert^2+\left\lVert\Psi\right\rVert^2
\]
and \eqref{eq:graph} make this inverse bounded into $H_c^1$. The inclusion $H_c^1\hookrightarrow L^2$ is compact by Theorem~\ref{thm:sobolev}. Thus the resolvent is compact. The compact normal spectral theorem applied to the resolvent gives the stated discrete spectral decomposition. Only finitely many eigenvalues can lie in a bounded interval, so the nonzero spectrum has a positive distance from zero. The inverse on the orthogonal complement of the kernel is bounded, which proves closed range. Self-adjointness identifies the cokernel with the kernel and gives index zero.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:HsFredholm}
For every real $s\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:HsFredholm}
 D:H_{c,b}^{s+1}\longrightarrow H_c^s
\end{equation}
 is bounded and Fredholm of index zero. As an unbounded operator on $H_c^s$ with this domain, it is closed. The kernel consists of smooth sections and is independent of $s$.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
We first prove, for integers $m\geq0$, that $u\in\operatorname{Dom}(D_{c,b})$ and $f=Du\in H_c^m$ imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regularity}
 \left\lVertu\right\rVert_{H_c^{m+1}}\leq C_m(\left\lVertf\right\rVert_{H_c^m}+\left\lVertu\right\rVert_{L^2}).
\end{equation}
For a finite longitudinal truncation apply \eqref{eq:energy} to $q^a u_{j,k}$ for $0\leq a\leq m$. This controls all derivatives with at most one $w$ derivative and total order at most $m+1$. Constants with $q=0$ are controlled by the $L^2$ term. For the remaining derivatives use the distributional equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalode}
 \partial_wu_{j,k}=i\sigma_2 f_{j,k}-q\sigma_3 u_{j,k}.
\end{equation}
Differentiating this identity controls a derivative with $r\geq2$ normal derivatives by derivatives of $f$ of total order at most $m$ and derivatives of $u$ with one fewer normal derivative and the same total order. Induction on $r$ proves \eqref{eq:regularity}, uniformly in the truncation. Passing to the limit proves both the estimate and the asserted gain of regularity.

For $D+i$, the $L^2$ inverse exists by self-adjointness. If $g\in H_c^m$ and $(D+i)u=g$, induction in \eqref{eq:regularity} applied to $Du=g-iu$ gives $u\in H_c^{m+1}$ and a bounded inverse from $H_c^m$ to $H_c^{m+1}$. Interpolation of this single consistent inverse at adjacent integer orders gives the same statement for every real $s\geq0$. The output satisfies the boundary condition because it is the original $L^2$ inverse and its trace is defined. Conversely $D+i$ maps $H_{c,b}^{s+1}$ to $H_c^s$ and is injective. It is therefore an isomorphism between these spaces.

The inclusion $H_{c,b}^{s+1}\hookrightarrow H_c^s$ is compact. The map in \eqref{eq:HsFredholm} is a compact perturbation of the isomorphism $D+i$, so it is Fredholm of index zero. The corresponding estimate with $\left\lVertu\right\rVert_{H_c^s}$ gives equivalence of its graph norm and the $H_c^{s+1}$ norm and proves closedness. Iterating \eqref{eq:regularity} with $f=0$ makes kernel elements smooth.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:transversespectrum}
The functions $f_n$ are an orthonormal eigenbasis of $T_b$, with eigenvalues $\mu_n$. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transverseidentities}
 Jf_n=b(-1)^nf_n,\qquad \sigma_1 f_n=b f_{-n}.
\end{equation}
The zero transverse eigenvalue is simple.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
The equation $T_b\phi=\mu\phi$ gives $\phi(w)=\exp(i\sigma_2\mu(w+1))\phi(-1)$. The condition at $-1$ makes $\phi(-1)$ a multiple of $v_b$. The condition at $1$ is $\sin(2\mu)=0$, because $\sigma_2 v_b$ lies in the opposite $\sigma_1$ eigenspace. Self-adjointness makes $\mu$ real, giving exactly \eqref{eq:transversemodes}. The normalization is correct since the exponential is unitary and the interval length is two. Distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. The graph norm of $T_b$ is the interval $H^1$ norm, so its resolvent is compact. Its spectral theorem and the solved eigenvalue equation imply completeness. Anticommutation of $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, together with $\exp(2i\sigma_2\mu_n)=(-1)^nI$, proves \eqref{eq:transverseidentities}.

<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{thm:spectrum}
The spectrum of $D_{c,b}$, including multiplicities, is the multiset union of
\begin{align}
 \lambda_{0,k}&=bq_{0,k},&&k\in\mathbb Z,\label{eq:zerochannel}\\
 \lambda_{n,k}^{\pm}&=\pm\sqrt{q_{n,k}^2+(n\pi/2)^2},
 &&n\geq1,\ k\in\mathbb Z.\label{eq:pairedchannels}
\end{align}
Each displayed branch contributes one eigenvector for each of its labels; coincident values contribute the sum of these multiplicities. The transverse zero mode occurs only once. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
 \dim\ker D_{c,b}=\begin{cases}1,&cb=1,\\0,&cb\ne1.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-8 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
By \eqref{eq:transverseidentities}, the span of $f_n,f_{-n}$ for $n\geq1$ lies in $\mathcal K_{b(-1)^n}$. Equivariance therefore imposes exactly the phase \eqref{eq:thetan}. On this two-dimensional span the $k$th longitudinal block is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block}
 \begin{pmatrix}\mu_n&bq_{n,k}\\bq_{n,k}&-\mu_n\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Its eigenvalues are \eqref{eq:pairedchannels}. The space spanned by $f_0$ is one dimensional, and there $T_b=0$ and $\sigma_1=b$, giving \eqref{eq:zerochannel}. Completeness follows by first using the longitudinal Fourier decomposition and then the transverse eigenbasis in each $J$ eigenspace. No branch with $n\geq1$ can vanish. In the zero branch, $k+\theta_0=0$ is possible exactly when $\theta_0=0$, equivalently $cb=1$, and then $k=0$ is unique.

\label{prop:symmetry}
The following maps have the indicated domains and intertwining relations:

\midrule

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:classification}
Every translation-invariant rank-one local boundary condition which is self-annihilating for Green's form and compatible with the deck action is of the form \eqref{eq:Bt}. It is elliptic exactly when $\cos t\ne0$. In that case $D_{c,t}$ is self-adjoint with compact resolvent and ordinary index zero.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
An orthogonal rank-one projection in $\mathbb C^2$ has the form $(I+\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2$, $|\mathbf n|=1$. Isotropy for $\sigma_2$ is equivalent to $n_2=0$. Thus the line at the upper boundary is the positive eigenspace of $B_+(t)$ for some $t$. Deck compatibility conjugates this projection by $R_c$, giving $B_-(t)$. Conversely these lines are self-annihilating and compatible.

The decaying boundary lines of the principal ODE are the $\sigma_3$ eigenspaces, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:boundary}. An allowed line equals one of them exactly when $\cos t=0$. Thus the complementing condition holds exactly as stated. For $\cos t\ne0$, the boundary lines form a smooth rank-one subbundle over the compact boundary, are self-annihilating for the normal symbol, and satisfy the local complementing condition at every nonzero tangential covector. The formally self-adjoint Dirac expression acts on a smooth Hermitian Clifford bundle over a compact smooth manifold. These are the hypotheses of the local ellipticity, adjoint-domain and regularity results in \cite[Theorems 4.15, 4.6, 4.9 and 4.11]{BBGuide}, in its specified version. They identify the self-adjoint domain with this $H^1$ boundary domain and give the graph estimate into $H^1$. Compactness of $H^1\hookrightarrow L^2$ then proves compact resolvent; closed range and index zero follow as in Theorem~\ref{thm:compact}. The general local classification is classical; compare \cite{GUV}.

<!-- theorem-9: body_md -->
\label{thm:secular}
Suppose $\cos t\ne0$ and set
\[
 r_t=\frac{1-\sin t}{\cos t}\ne0.
\]
In the sector $j=\pm1$ and longitudinal frequency $q=2\pi(k+\theta_{c,j})$, the eigenvalues are exactly the real zeros of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:secular}
 F_{q,j,t}(\lambda)=(j-r_t)\cos\mu
 +\big[q(r_t+j)-\lambda(1+r_tj)\big]\frac{\sin\mu}{\mu},
 \qquad \mu^2=\lambda^2-q^2.
\end{equation}
The functions of $\mu$ in this formula are their entire extensions in $\mu^2$, including at $\mu=0$. Each sector-frequency eigenspace has dimension one. The full kernel has dimension at most one and is nonzero exactly when, for $j=\operatorname{sgn}(\cos t)$, there is $k\in\mathbb Z$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kercriterion}
 2\pi(k+\theta_{c,j})=\frac12\log|r_t|.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-9 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
The $J=j$ condition reduces the transverse problem to $[0,1]$, with $\sigma_1\phi(0)=j\phi(0)$. Any nonzero solution starts at a multiple of $(1,j)^T$; zero initial data give the zero solution. The equation $D_q\phi=\lambda\phi$ is
\[
 \phi'=(i\lambda\sigma_2-q\sigma_3)\phi,
 \qquad (i\lambda\sigma_2-q\sigma_3)^2=(q^2-\lambda^2)I.
\]
Hence its fundamental matrix at $w=1$ is
\[
 \cos\mu\,I+\frac{\sin\mu}{\mu}(i\lambda\sigma_2-q\sigma_3).
\]
The upper boundary line has component ratio $\phi_2(1)/\phi_1(1)=r_t$, so applying the row $(-r_t,1)$ gives exactly \eqref{eq:secular}. Reflection reconstructs the other half-interval, and the initial $\sigma_1$ condition and the ODE ensure matching at zero. Thus the scalar equation is both necessary and sufficient, including for imaginary $\mu$. It has a one-dimensional solution space at a zero, by uniqueness for the initial-value problem. Self-adjointness already proved in Proposition~\ref{prop:classification} ensures that the spectrum is real and has no algebraic multiplicity beyond its eigenspace multiplicity within a block.

At $\lambda=0$, the solution is proportional to $(e^{-qw},j e^{qw})^T$. The endpoint condition is $j e^{2q}=r_t$. Thus $j=\operatorname{sgn}(r_t)=\operatorname{sgn}(\cos t)$ and $q=\tfrac12\log|r_t|$. This gives \eqref{eq:kercriterion}. For fixed $t$ there is at most one such $j$ and one such $k$.
