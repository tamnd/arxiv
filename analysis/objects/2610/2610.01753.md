<!-- lem-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:rule}
For $x,y,z\in\mathbb{F}_3$,
\[
f(x,y,z)=\begin{cases}(x-z)^2-y & \text{if } y\ne0,\\ xz+2 & \text{if } y=0.\end{cases}
\]
Explicitly, $f(x,1,z)$ is $2$ if $x=z$ and $0$ otherwise, $f(x,2,z)$ is $1$ if $x=z$ and $2$
otherwise, and $f(x,0,z)$ is $2$ if $xz=0$, $0$ if $x=z\ne0$, and $1$ if $\{x,z\}=\{1,2\}$.
<!-- end lem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
If $y\ne0$ then $y^2=1$, so $f=x^2+z^2+xz+2y+3=(x-z)^2+3xz+2y+3=(x-z)^2-y$ in $\mathbb{F}_3$. If $y=0$
then $f=xz+2$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lem-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:unique}
Let $D$ be a function on cells with $D[s,\pm s]=1$ for all $s$ and
$D(c)=f(D(c_{x}),D(c_{y}),D(c_{z}))$ for every cell $c=[s,d]$ with $|d|\le s-2$. Then $D=T$.
<!-- end lem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Both $D$ and $T$ satisfy the same recurrence with the same boundary values, so they agree on
row $s$ if they agree on rows $s-1$ and $s-2$. Induction on $s$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- defn-1: body_md -->
\label{def:lattices}
For $\varepsilon\in\{0,2\}$ and a cell $[s,d]$ let
\[
\Lambda_\varepsilon[s,d]=\begin{cases}1& \text{if } s \text{ is odd and } s\equiv|d|+\varepsilon \pmod 4,\\
2&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\]
\[
\Gamma[s,d]=\begin{cases}1& \text{if } s \text{ is even and } s\equiv d \pmod 4,\\ 2&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
<!-- end defn-1 -->
<!-- lem-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:lattices}
Let $c=[s,d]$ and let $\Pi$ be one of $\Gamma,\Lambda_0,\Lambda_2$. Put $v=f(\Pi(c_{x}),\Pi(c_{y}),\Pi(c_{z}))$.
\begin{alphenum}
\item If $\Pi=\Gamma$ then $v=\Gamma(c)$.
\item If $\Pi=\Lambda_\varepsilon$ and $d\ne0$ then $v=\Lambda_\varepsilon(c)$.
\item If $\Pi=\Lambda_\varepsilon$ and $d=0$ then $v=1$, while $\Lambda_\varepsilon(c)=2$.
\end{alphenum}
<!-- end lem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
(a) Let $s$ be even. Then $c_{x}$ and $c_{z}$ lie on an odd row, so both are blue, and
$f(2,y,2)=1$ if and only if $y=2$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:rule}. Now $\Gamma(c_{y})=2$ if and only if
$s-2\not\equiv d\pmod 4$, which, as $s-d$ is even, is equivalent to $s\equiv d\pmod4$, that is
to $\Gamma(c)=1$. Let $s$ be odd. Exactly one of $s-1-(d+1)$ and $s-1-(d-1)$ is divisible by
$4$, so one of $c_{x},c_{z}$ is red and the other blue, $c_{y}$ is blue because $s-2$ is odd, and
$f(x,2,z)=2$ for $x\ne z$, which agrees with $\Gamma(c)=2$.

(b) Let $s$ be odd. Then $c_{x}$ and $c_{z}$ are blue and $f(2,y,2)=1$ if and only if $y=2$, that
is if and only if $s-2\not\equiv|d|+\varepsilon\pmod4$, which is equivalent to
$s\equiv|d|+\varepsilon$, that is to $\Lambda_\varepsilon(c)=1$. Let $s$ be even and $d\ne0$. Then
$|d|\ge2$, so $|d+1|$ and $|d-1|$ are $|d|+1$ and $|d|-1$ in some order, one of the two
odd-row parents is red and the other blue, $c_{y}$ is blue, and $f(x,2,z)=2$ for $x\ne z$.

(c) If $d=0$ then $c_{x}$ and $c_{z}$ have the same value and $c_{y}$ is blue, so $v=1$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- defn-2: body_md -->
\label{def:box}
For $n\ge1$ and odd $e\ge w_n$, the \emph{box of level $n$ opening at time $e$} is the set of cells
\[
\mathsf{B}(n,e)=\{[s,d]:\ e\le s\le e+L_n,\ |d|\le w_n\}.
\]
Its \emph{border} consists of the two \emph{walls} $|d|=w_n$, the \emph{opening edge} $s=e$ and
the \emph{closing edge} $s=e+L_n$. The other cells form its \emph{interior}. Its \emph{midpoint}
is $\mu=e+L_n/2=e+2^{n+1}-3$, its \emph{half-width} is $w_n$, and its \emph{vertex} is
$A=(u,v)=\big(\tfrac{e-w_n}2,\tfrac{e+w_n}2\big)$.
<!-- end defn-2 -->
<!-- defn-3: body_md -->
\label{def:children}
A box of level $1$ or $2$ has no children. For $n\ge3$ the \emph{children} of $\mathsf{B}(n,e)$ are the
$2(n-2)$ boxes
\[
\mathsf{B}(k,\,e+p_{n,k})\quad\text{and}\quad \mathsf{B}(k,\,e+q_{n,k}),\qquad 1\le k\le n-2,
\]
where
\[
p_{n,k}=2^{n+1}-2^{k+3}-2(n-k)+8,\qquad q_{n,k}=L_n-L_k-p_{n,k}=2^{n+1}+2^{k+2}+2(n-k)-8.
\]
In time order the children are the boxes of levels $n-2,n-3,\dots,1$, which form the
\emph{first half}, followed by the boxes of levels $1,2,\dots,n-2$, which form the \emph{second
half}.
<!-- end defn-3 -->
<!-- defn-4: body_md -->
\label{def:family}
The \emph{main boxes} are $S_n=\mathsf{B}(n,e_n)$ with $e_n=2^{n+2}-2n+3$ for $n\ge1$. The family
$\mathfrak{B}$ consists of the main boxes and of all boxes obtained from them by taking children
repeatedly.
<!-- end defn-4 -->
<!-- lem-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:timeline}
Let $\mathsf{B}(n,e)\in\mathfrak{B}$, $w=w_n$, $L=L_n$ and $\mu=e+L/2$.
\begin{alphenum}
\item $e\equiv w\pmod4$. Consequently $e$ is odd, $e+L\equiv w+2\pmod4$ and $\mu\equiv w+1\pmod4$.
\item Let $n\ge3$. The first child opens at $e+4$. Each child except the first copy of $S_1$ and
the last child closes $4$ rows before the next child opens. The first copy of $S_1$ closes at
$c_{\mathrm{gap}}=\mu-2n-1$, and the second copy opens at $e_{\mathrm{gap}}=\mu+2n+1$.
These times bound the switching gap. The last child closes at $e+L-4$.
The main box $S_n$ closes at $e_{n+1}-4$.
\item The children lie in the region $e+4\le s\le e+L-4$, $|d|\le w-4$. The first half lies in
$s\le\mu-2n-1$ and the second half in $s\ge\mu+2n+1$. Two boxes of $\mathfrak{B}$ are either nested or
disjoint, and two disjoint boxes are separated by at least $4$ rows.
\item Every cell of every box of $\mathfrak{B}$ satisfies $|d|\le s-8$.
\end{alphenum}
<!-- end lem-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
(a) For a main box, $e_n=2^{n+2}-2n+3\equiv 3-2n\equiv 2n-1\pmod4$. For $n\ge3$ and
$1\le k\le n-2$ we have $p_{n,k}\equiv q_{n,k}\equiv 2(n-k)\pmod4$ and $L_n\equiv L_k\equiv2\pmod4$,
so a child of level $k$ of a box opening at $e\equiv 2n-1$ opens at a time
$\equiv 2n-1+2(n-k)\equiv 2k-1\pmod4$. The other congruences follow from $L\equiv 2$ and
$L/2=2^{n+1}-3\equiv1\pmod4$.

(b) $p_{n,n-2}=4$ and $p_{n,k-1}-p_{n,k}=2^{k+2}-2=L_k+4$. The second half is the mirror image
of the first half in time, because $q_{n,k}+L_k=L_n-p_{n,k}$. The first copy of $S_1$ closes at
$e+p_{n,1}+2=e+2^{n+1}-2n-4=\mu-2n-1$, and by the mirror symmetry the second copy opens at
$\mu+2n+1$. Finally $e_n+L_n+4=2^{n+3}-2n+1=e_{n+1}$.

(c) Children have half-width $w_k\le w_{n-2}=w-4$, and the time bounds follow from (b) by
induction over the nesting.

(d) A cell of a box inside $S_n$ satisfies $s-|d|\ge e_n-w_n=2^{n+2}-4n+4\ge8$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- defn-5: body_md -->
\label{def:edges}
An \emph{edge} is a pair $(e,a)$ of a time and a half-width. For a box $H=\mathsf{B}(n,e)\in\mathfrak{B}$, the set
$\mathcal{E}(H)$ consists of the edges $(e,w_n)$ and $(e+L_n,w_n)$ of $H$ and of the edges $(e',w_k)$
and $(e'+L_k,w_k)$ of each child $\mathsf{B}(k,e')$ of $H$. For the \emph{global host} $\ast$,
\[
\mathcal{E}(\ast)=\{(3,1)\}\cup\{(e_n,w_n),\ (e_n+L_n,w_n):n\ge1\}.
\]
The pair $(3,1)$, which we call the \emph{virtual edge}, stands for the two white cells
$t(1,2)$ and $t(2,1)$. The \emph{host} of a cell $c$ is the smallest box of $\mathfrak{B}$ whose interior
contains $c$, and $\ast$ if there is no such box.
<!-- end defn-5 -->
<!-- defn-6: body_md -->
\label{def:D}
For a cell $c=[s,d]$, the value $D(c)$ is given by the first of the following rules that
applies.
\begin{Denum}{-1}
\item\label{D0} If $|d|=s$, then $D(c)=1$. If $|d|=s-2\ge1$, then $D(c)=0$.
\item\label{D1} If $c$ lies on the border of a box of $\mathfrak{B}$, then $D(c)=0$.
\end{Denum}
Otherwise let $H$ be the host of $c$.
\begin{Denum}{1}
\item\label{D2} If $s=e\pm1$ and $|d|\le a-1$ for some $(e,a)\in\mathcal{E}(H)$, then $D(c)=\Gamma(c)$.
\item\label{D3} If $s=e\pm2$ and $|d|\le a-2$ for some $(e,a)\in\mathcal{E}(H)$, then $D(c)=1$.
\item\label{D4} If $H=\mathsf{B}(n,e)$ with $n\ge2$ and $|s-\mu_H|+|d|\le w_n-3$, then
$D(c)=\Gamma(c)$.
\item\label{D5} Otherwise $D(c)=\Lambda_0(c)$ if $H$ is a box and $s<\mu_H$, and
$D(c)=\Lambda_2(c)$ if $H=\ast$ or $s\ge\mu_H$.
\end{Denum}

\label{thm:main}
For all $x,y\ge0$, $t(x,y)=D[x+y,y-x]$.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:borders}
$D$ is correct at every border cell.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
\emph{Wall cells} $c=[s,w]$ with $e+2\le s\le e+L$, which include the closing corner. By
\ref{P1} the parent $c_{y}$ is white, and by \ref{P2} the parents $c_{x}$ and $c_{z}$ are blue, so
the triple is $(2,0,2)$ and $f(2,0,2)=0$.

\emph{Inner cells of the opening edge}, $c=[e,d]$ with $|d|\le w-2$. The parent $c_{y}=[e-2,d]$
is red and $c_{x},c_{z}$ are the $\Gamma$-cells of row $e-1$ by \ref{P3}, of which one is red and the
other blue. The triple is $(1,1,2)$ or $(2,1,1)$, and $f=0$.

\emph{Opening corner} $c=[e,w]$. The parent $c_{y}=[e-2,w]$ is red by \ref{P4}, the parent
$c_{z}=[e-1,w-1]$ has $\Gamma=1$ because $e-1\equiv w-1$, and the parent $c_{x}=[e-1,w+1]$ is
blue by \ref{P2}. The triple is $(2,1,1)$ and $f=0$.

\emph{Inner cells of the closing edge}, $c=[e+L,d]$ with $|d|\le w-2$. As for the opening
edge, using the rows $e+L-1$ and $e+L-2$ of \ref{P3}.

<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:trans}
$D$ is correct at every transition cell.
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $(e,a)\in\mathcal{E}(H)$ be the edge responsible for $c$ in rule \ref{D2} or \ref{D3}.

\emph{Rule \ref{D2} after an edge}: $c=[e+1,d]$ with $|d|\le a-1$. The parents $c_{x}$ and
$c_{z}$ lie on the edge and are white, and $c_{y}=[e-1,d]$ has the value $\Gamma(c_{y})$ by \ref{P3}.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:rule}, $f(0,1,0)=2$ and $f(0,2,0)=1$. Since $\Gamma(c_{y})=2$ if and only if
$e+1\equiv d$, which is the condition $\Gamma(c)=1$, we get $D(c)=\Gamma(c)$. The cell $[4,0]$ is
of this kind, with the virtual edge.

\emph{Rule \ref{D2} before an edge}: $c=[e-1,d]$ with $|d|\le a-1$, and $c$ is not of the
previous kind. Then $(e,a)$ is the opening of a child of $H$, or the closing of $H$, in which
case $H$ has level at least $2$. The cell $[2,0]$, before the virtual edge, is treated
directly: its parents are the axis cells $[1,\pm1]$ and $[0,0]$, so the triple is $(1,1,1)$
and $f=2=\Gamma[2,0]$. In the remaining cases we distinguish the position of $c$.
\begin{romenum}
\item $|d|\le a-3$. Then $c_{x}$ and $c_{z}$ are red by \ref{P3} applied to row $e-2$, and
$f(1,y,1)$ is $2,1,0$ for $y=1,2,0$. It therefore suffices to show $D(c_{y})=\Gamma(c_{y})$ for
$c_{y}=[e-3,d]$. In cases J1, J2, J3 and J5 the cell $c_{y}$ is a \ref{D2}-cell after the
previous edge $(e-4,a')$, and $a'\ge a-2$. In case J4 the same holds for $|d|\le a-5$, and for
$|d|=a-3$ \ref{P2} gives $D(c_{y})=2=\Gamma(c_{y})$, since $e-3\equiv a-1$. Before the closing of a
box of level $2$, $c_{y}=[\mu+2,0]$ has $D(c_{y})=2$ by class (E1) below and $\Gamma(c_{y})=2$.
\item $|d|=a-1$ with $d\ge0$, and $(e,a)$ is an opening. The parent $c_{x}=[e-2,a]$ is red by
\ref{P4}. The parent $c_{z}=[e-2,a-2]$ is red, by \ref{P3} if $a\ge3$ and as the mirror
image of $c_{x}$ if $a=1$. The parent $c_{y}=[e-3,a-1]$ is blue. In cases J1 and J2,
this follows from \ref{P3} because $\Gamma=2$ as $e-3\equiv a+1$.
In cases J3 and J5, use \ref{P2}. After the switching gap,
$c_{y}=[\mu+w_H-1,0]$, and for $S_1$ at $e=9$, $c_{y}=[6,0]$.
Both cells are blue by class (E1) below.
The triple is $(1,2,1)$, so $f=1=\Gamma(c)$, since
$e-1\equiv a-1$.
\item $|d|=a-1$ with $d\ge0$, and $(e,a)$ is the closing of $H$, so $a\ge3$. The parent
$c_{x}=[e-2,a]$ is a wall cell, $c_{z}$ is red by \ref{P3}, and $c_{y}$ is blue by \ref{P2}. The
triple is $(0,2,1)$, so $f=2=\Gamma(c)$, since $e-1\equiv a+1$.
\end{romenum}

\emph{Rule \ref{D3} after an edge}: $c=[e+2,d]$ with $|d|\le a-2$. The parent $c_{y}$ is on the
edge, and $c_{x},c_{z}$ are $\Gamma$-cells of row $e+1$, one red and one blue. The triple is
$(1,0,2)$ or $(2,0,1)$, and $f=1$.

\emph{Rule \ref{D3} before an edge}, not of the previous kind: $c=[e-2,d]$ with $|d|\le a-2$.
This happens in three situations. In cases J3 and J5 with $|d|=a-2$, and in case J4 with
$|d|=a-4$, the cell belongs to class (E3) below and is treated there. In case J4 with $|d|=a-2$,
the parent $c_{y}=[e-4,a-2]$ has $\Lambda_2=2$, since $e-4\equiv a+2$, and $c_{x},c_{z}$ are blue by
\ref{P2}, so the triple is $(2,2,2)$ and $f=1$. Before the closing of a box $\mathsf{B}(2,e')$ of level
$2$, $c=[e'+8,\pm1]$, the parent $c_{y}=[e'+6,1]$ has $\Lambda_2=2$, the parent $c_{z}=[e'+7,0]$
is blue by class (E1), and $c_{x}$ is blue by \ref{P2}, so the triple is $(2,2,2)$ and $f=1$.

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:diamond}
$D$ is correct at every cell of a switching diamond.
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let $H=\mathsf{B}(n,e)$ with $n\ge2$, $\mu=\mu_H$, $r=w_n-3$, and let $\delta(c)=|s-\mu|+|d|$. Then
$\delta$ takes even values only and $\mu-r\equiv0$.

If the three parents of $c$ lie in the diamond, Lemma~\ref{lem:lattices}(a) applies. For
$s>\mu$ the parents satisfy $\delta\le\delta(c)$, and for $s\le\mu$ and $\delta(c)\le r-2$ they
satisfy $\delta\le\delta(c)+2\le r$. It remains to treat the cells with $s\le\mu$ and
$\delta(c)=r$, for which $s\equiv|d|\pmod4$.

The parents of such a cell that lie outside the diamond lie on rows $\ge\mu-r-2$, later than
the transition rows of the edge that precedes the diamond, so they are environment cells with
value $\Lambda_0$. Take $d\ge0$. If $s$ is even, then $\Gamma(c)=1$, the parent $c_{x}=[s-1,d+1]$
has $\Lambda_0=2$ because $s\not\equiv d+2$, the parent $c_{y}$ is blue because its row is even,
and $c_{z}$ is a diamond cell on an odd row if $d\ge2$ and the mirror image of $c_{x}$ if $d=0$, so
blue in both cases. The triple is $(2,2,2)$ and $f=1$. If $s$ is odd, then $\Gamma(c)=2$, the
parent $c_{x}$ is blue because its row is even, the parent $c_{z}=[s-1,d-1]$ is in the diamond
with $\Gamma=1$, and $c_{y}=[s-2,d]$ has $\Lambda_0=2$ because $s-2\not\equiv d$. The triple is
$(2,2,1)$ and $f=2$.

<!-- lem-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:hosts}
Let $c$ be an environment cell and $p$ a parent of $c$ that is not white. Then $p$ has the
same host as $c$.
<!-- end lem-5 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Suppose first that $p$ lies in the interior of a box $X$ whose interior does not contain $c$.
If $p=c_{y}$, then $e_X<s-2<e_X+L_X$ and $|d|<w_X$. Since $c$ is not white, $c$ is not on the
border of $X$, so $s>e_X+L_X$, hence $s=e_X+L_X+1$ and $c$ is a \ref{D2}-cell after the closing
of $X$, not an environment cell. If $p=c_{x}$ or $p=c_{z}$, then $|d|\le|d\pm1|+1\le w_X$ and
$e_X<s\le e_X+L_X$, so $c$ lies in $X$, and since $c$ is not white it lies in the interior of
$X$, a contradiction.

Suppose next that $c$ lies in the interior of a box $X$ and $p$ does not. Then $p$ is on the
border of $X$ and white, or $p=c_{y}$ with $s-2<e_X$, in which case $s=e_X+1$ and $c$ is a
\ref{D2}-cell after the opening of $X$.

<!-- lem-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:Eclasses}
Let $c$ be an environment cell that has an irregular parent or lies on the diagonal $d=0$.
Then $c$ belongs to one of the classes (E1) to (E6) listed in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:env}.
<!-- end lem-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:hosts}, an irregular parent $p$ of $c$ is white, or lies in the host $H$ of
$c$ and is one of the following: a cell of an axis, a transition cell, a diamond cell, or an
environment cell of the other phase. We go through the successors of each kind of cell and
keep those successors that are environment cells.

\emph{Axis cells.} The successors of $[s',\pm s']$ lie on the axes or on the white lines.

\emph{White lines.} The successors of $[s',s'-2]$ are $[s'+1,s'-1]$ on the white line,
and $[s'+1,s'-3]$ and $[s'+2,s'-2]$, which have $|d|=s-4$ and are $[4,0]$, a
\ref{D2}-cell, or cells of class (E6).

\emph{Walls.} Let $p=[s',w]$ be a wall cell of $\mathsf{B}=\mathsf{B}(n,e)$ with $s'\in[e,e+L]$. The
successor $[s'+1,w+1]$ lies outside $\mathsf{B}$ on a row of $[e+1,e+L+1]$ and belongs to class
(E2). The successor $[s'+1,w-1]$ is a \ref{D2}-cell of $\mathsf{B}$ if $s'+1$ is $e+1$ or $e+L-1$, a
\ref{D2}-cell after the closing if $s'+1=e+L+1$, and otherwise belongs to class (E2). The
successor $[s'+2,w]$ is a wall cell unless $s'=e+L$, in which case it belongs to class (E3).

\emph{Inner edge cells.} The successors of an inner cell $[e',d']$, $|d'|\le a-2$, of an
edge $(e',a)$ are \ref{D2}-cells and \ref{D3}-cells after that edge.

\emph{Transition cells} of an edge $(e,a)\in\mathcal{E}(H)$. The successors of the cells before the
edge, on rows $e-1$ and $e-2$, lie on the rows $e-1$ with $|d|\le a-1$, $e$ with $|d|\le a$,
and $e+1$ with $|d|\le a-1$, so they are \ref{D2}-cells, edge cells, or \ref{D2}-cells after
the edge. The successors of the cells after the edge, on rows $e+1$ and $e+2$, lie on the
rows $e+2$ with $|d|\le a$, $e+3$ with $|d|\le a-1$ and $e+4$ with $|d|\le a-2$. On row $e+2$
the cells with $|d|\le a-2$ are \ref{D3}-cells and the cells $[e+2,\pm a]$ are wall cells if
$(e,a)$ is an opening and cells of class (E3) if it is a closing. For the rows $e+3$ and $e+4$
let $(e+g,b)$ be the next element of $\mathcal{E}(H)$.
\begin{itemize}
\item In cases J3, J4 and J5, where $g=4$ and $b\ge a+2$, the rows $e+3$ with $|d|\le b-1$ and
$e+4$ with $|d|\le b$ consist of \ref{D2}-cells and edge cells.
\item In case J2, where $b=a-2$, the only remaining cells on row $e+3$ are $|d|=a-1$, of class
(E4), and on row $e+4$ all cells with $|d|\le a-2=b$ are edge cells.
\item In case J1, where $b=a-4$, the remaining cells are $[e+3,\pm(a-3)]$ of class (E4),
$[e+3,\pm(a-1)]$ of class (E2), and $[e+4,\pm(a-2)]$ of class (E4).
\item After the opening of a box of level $2$, where $a=3$, row $e+3$ gives $[e+3,0]$ of
class (E1) and $[e+3,\pm2]$ of class (E2). Row $e+4$ gives $[e+4,\pm1]$, whose
non-white parents on rows $e+2$ and $e+3$ are regular, because $D[e+2,\pm1]=1=\Lambda_0$ as
$e+2\equiv1$, and $D[e+3,0]=D[e+3,\pm2]=2$.
\item After the opening of a box of level $1$, where $a=1$, the rows $e+1$ and $e+2$ belong to
the box, and the next cell $[e+3,0]$ is a \ref{D2}-cell after its closing.
\item After the closing of the first copy of $S_1$, before the switching gap, row $e+3$ gives
$[e+3,0]$ of class (E1). There are no \ref{D3}-cells after an edge of half-width $1$.
The cells $[e+2,\pm1]$ are regular, with value $1=\Lambda_0$ by \ref{P5}, so their
successors on row $e+4$ give no further case.
\item For the virtual edge $(3,1)$, the successors of $[4,0]$ are $[5,\pm1]$ of class (E6)
and $[6,0]$ of class (E1).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Diamond cells.} Let $p$ be in the diamond of $H$, so $\delta(p)\le r=w_n-3$ in the
notation of Proposition~\ref{prop:diamond}, and let $c$ be a successor of $p$ outside the
diamond. If $c$ lies on a row $\le\mu$, then $\delta(c)\le\delta(p)$, which is impossible, so
$s>\mu$. Then $\delta(c)\le\delta(p)+2\le r+2$, and since $\delta$ is even and $\delta(c)>r$, we
get $\delta(c)=r+2=w_n-1$, which is class (E5).

\emph{Other phase.} With our convention, a parent of the other phase occurs only for $c$ on
row $\mu+1$ with $c_{y}$ on row $\mu-1$. Outside the diamond, row $\mu+1$ contains only the
cells $|d|=w_n-2$, which belong to class (E5).

\emph{The diagonal.} Let $c=[s,0]$ be an environment cell with host $H=\mathsf{B}(n,e)$, so that $s$ is
not in the time span of a child. In a gap of $4$ rows between consecutive elements
$(e',\cdot)$ and $(e'+4,\cdot)$ of $\mathcal{E}(H)$, the rows $e'+1$ and $e'+3$ are \ref{D2}-rows that
reach $d=0$. In the switching gap $(c_{\mathrm{gap}},e_{\mathrm{gap}})$, or in the gap $(e,e+10)$ for $n=2$, the even
rows are the \ref{D2}-rows $c_{\mathrm{gap}}+1$ and $e_{\mathrm{gap}}-1$, the diamond rows from $\mu-r$ to $\mu+r$, and
the two rows $c_{\mathrm{gap}}+3=\mu-w_n+1$ and $e_{\mathrm{gap}}-3=\mu+w_n-1$, whose diagonal cells belong to class
(E1). In a box of level $1$ the only such row is a \ref{D2}-row. For $H=\ast$ the gaps between
consecutive edges are gaps of $4$ rows, except the gap $(3,9)$, whose even rows $2,4,8$ are
\ref{D2}-rows and whose row $6$ gives $[6,0]$ of class (E1).

<!-- prop-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:env}
$D$ is correct at every environment cell.
<!-- end prop-4 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:Eclasses} it suffices to treat the classes (E1) to (E6). All other
environment cells are covered by Lemma~\ref{lem:lattices}(b).

\textbf{(E1)} \emph{Environment cells on the diagonal.} These are $[\mu-w_n+1,0]$ and
$[\mu+w_n-1,0]$ in each box of level $n\ge2$, and the cell $[6,0]$. For $n\ge3$,
$[\mu-w_n+1,0]=[c_{\mathrm{gap}}+3,0]$, where $c_{\mathrm{gap}}$ is the closing time of the first copy of $S_1$. Its
parents are $[c_{\mathrm{gap}}+2,\pm1]$, red by \ref{P5}, and $[c_{\mathrm{gap}}+1,0]$, which has $\Gamma=1$ since
$c_{\mathrm{gap}}\equiv3$. The triple is $(1,1,1)$ and $f=2=\Lambda_0$. For $n=2$ the same triple arises,
using \ref{P3}. The cell $[\mu+w_n-1,0]$ belongs to class (E5). For $[6,0]$ the parents
$[5,\pm1]$ have $\Lambda_2=2$ and $[4,0]$ is red, so the triple is $(2,1,2)$ and $f=2$.

\textbf{(E2)} \emph{Next to walls.} Inside: $c=[s,w-1]$ with $s$ even and $e+3\le s\le e+L-3$.
The parent $c_{x}$ is a wall cell, $c_{y}$ is blue by \ref{P2}, and $c_{z}$ is not white, so
$f(0,2,z)=2$ for $z\ne0$, which is $D(c)$. Outside: $c=[s,w+1]$ with $s$ even and
$e+1\le s\le e+L+1$. The parent $c_{z}$ is a wall cell or a corner, $c_{y}$ is blue by \ref{P2}, and
$c_{x}$ is not white, so $f(x,2,0)=2$ for $x\ne0$, which is $D(c)$ by \ref{P2}.

\textbf{(E3)} \emph{After a closing corner}: $c=[e+L+2,w]$, with $D(c)=1$ by \ref{P5}. The
parent $c_{y}$ is the corner and white, $c_{x}$ is blue by \ref{P2}, and $c_{z}=[e+L+1,w-1]$ has
$\Gamma=1$ since $e+L+1\equiv w-1$. The triple is $(2,0,1)$ and $f=1$.

\textbf{(E4)} \emph{Behind transition rows.} In case J1, with the opening of $H$ at $e$ and
$a=w$, the cell $c=[e+3,w-3]$ has the parents $[e+2,w-2]$ and $[e+2,w-4]$, both red,
and $[e+1,w-3]$ with $\Gamma=1$. The triple is $(1,1,1)$ and $f=2$. Next, $c=[e+4,w-2]$
has the parents $[e+3,w-1]$ and $[e+3,w-3]$, both blue, and $[e+2,w-2]$, red. The
triple is $(2,1,2)$ and $f=2=\Lambda_0$, since $e+4\equiv w$. The cell $[e+3,w-1]$ belongs to
class (E2). In case J2, with the closing of a child $X$ at $c_0$ and half-width $a$, the cell
$c=[c_0+3,a-1]$ has the parents $[c_0+2,a]$, red by \ref{P5}, $[c_0+2,a-2]$, red, and
$[c_0+1,a-1]$ with $\Gamma=1$. The triple is $(1,1,1)$ and $f=2$. In cases J3, J4 and J5
the rows behind the transition rows are transition rows or edges of the next edge, so there is
nothing to check. After the opening of a box of level $2$ and in the switching gap, the cells
in question belong to classes (E1) and (E2).

\textbf{(E5)} \emph{Below a diamond}: $c$ with $s>\mu$ and $(s-\mu)+|d|=w_n-1$, with $d\ge0$.
If $s$ is even, then $s\equiv d+2$, the parent $c_{y}$ is in the diamond with $\Gamma=1$, the
parent $c_{x}$ has $\Lambda_2=2$, and $c_{z}$ is a diamond cell on an odd row, or the mirror image of
$c_{x}$ when $d=0$, so blue in both cases. The triple is $(2,1,2)$ and $f=2=\Lambda_2(c)$. If $s$ is
odd, then $s\equiv d$, the parent $c_{z}$ is in the diamond with $\Gamma=1$, the parent $c_{x}$ is
blue, and $c_{y}$ is a diamond cell on an odd row if $s\ge\mu+3$, while for $s=\mu+1$ it is the
environment cell $[\mu-1,w_n-2]$ with $\Lambda_0=2$. The triple is $(2,2,1)$ and
$f=2=\Lambda_2(c)$.

\textbf{(E6)} \emph{Next to the white lines}: $c=[s,\pm(s-4)]$ with $s\ge5$, that is, $x=2$ or
$y=2$. The parents $c_{x}$ and $c_{y}$ are on the white line, so $f(0,0,z)=2=\Lambda_2(c)$.

<!-- rem-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:copies}
The recurrence determines the interior of a box $\mathsf{B}(n,e)$ from its opening edge and
the neighbouring cells. By \ref{P3}, the preceding row $[e-1,d]$, $|d|\le w-1$,
is coloured by the central lattice $\Gamma$. By \ref{P2}, the columns $|d|=w\pm1$ are
blue. These conditions do not depend on the position of the box, so every box of
level $k$ in $\mathfrak{B}$ is a translate of $S_k$. In particular, the environment inside a
box has the same phase at every depth of nesting.

<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:boxes}
The ordinals of $t$ are the boxes of $\mathfrak{B}$. The ordinals contained in no other are the
main boxes $S_n$, $n\ge1$, and the ordinals directly inside a box, with no ordinal in between,
are its children.
<!-- end cor-1 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Let $R$ be an ordinal with half-width $W=v-u$ and time span from $s_0$ to $s_0+2k$. By
\ref{P1} the cells of its walls are border cells of boxes or cells of the white lines. A cell of
a white line has no white cell two rows before or after it in the same column, so for $k\ge1$
the wall cells are box cells. A wall of $R$ cannot contain an inner cell of an edge of a box
wider than $R$, because the cells two rows before and after such a cell are red by \ref{P3}. So
the walls of $R$ consist of wall cells of boxes of half-width $W$. Two such boxes are at least
$4$ rows apart, and the cells between them in the columns $|d|=W$ are not white by \ref{P4}
and \ref{P5}, so all wall cells of $R$ belong to one box $\mathsf{B}$. Inside $\mathsf{B}$ no row except the two
edges is white on all of $|d|\le W$, because children have half-width at most $W-4$, and for
$W=1$ the only odd rows of $\mathsf{B}$ are its edges. Hence $R=\mathsf{B}$.

<!-- thm-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:structure}
The sequence $t$ is symmetric and consists of the following parts.
\begin{romenum}
\item The axes $x=0$ and $y=0$ are red. The lines $x=1$ and $y=1$ are white except for
$t(1,0)=t(0,1)=1$ and $t(1,1)=2$.
\item The main ordinal $S_n$, $n\ge1$, has vertices $A_n$, $B_n$, $\sigma(B_n)$, $\sigma(A_n)$
with $\sigma(u,v)=(v,u)$ and
\[
A_n=(2^{n+1}-2n+2,\ 2^{n+1}+1),\qquad B_n=(2^{n+2}-2n-1,\ 2^{n+2}-2).
\]
Its edges parallel to the diagonal have $2^{n+1}-2$ cells and its other edges have $2n$ cells.
The vertices of every ordinal have coordinates congruent to $(0,1)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,0)$
modulo $2$. $S_{n+1}$ follows $S_n$ with $B_n=A_{n+1}-(1,3)$.
\item Let an ordinal of level $n\ge3$ have vertex $A=(u,v)$. Its children are translates of
$S_k$, $1\le k\le n-2$, with vertices
\begin{align*}
&A+\big(2^n-2^{k+2}+4,\ \ 2^n-2^{k+2}-2(n-k)+4\big) &&\text{(first half)},\\
&A+\big(2^n+2^{k+1}+2(n-k)-4,\ \ 2^n+2^{k+1}-4\big) &&\text{(second half)}.
\end{align*}
\item An ordinal of level $n\ge2$ with vertex $(u,v)$ contains the \emph{switching square}
$[u+2^n,\,u+2^n+2n-4]^2$ of side $2n-3$, coloured by the central lattice: a cell of the square is
red if and only if both its coordinates are even. Each side of the square contains $n-1$ red
and $n-2$ blue cells. The two diagonal cells $(u+2^n-2,u+2^n-2)$ and $(u+2^n+2n-2,u+2^n+2n-2)$
are red. For $S_n$ the switching square is $[3\cdot2^n-2n+2,\ 3\cdot2^n-2]^2$.
\item Outside the main ordinals, cells $(x,y)$ with $\min(x,y)\ge2$ have the colour
$\Lambda_2$, except in the transition squares $T_n$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:transition}
and at $(2,2)$ and $(4,4)$.
Inside an ordinal of level $n\ge3$, exclude its children, its transition squares,
its switching square and the two red diagonal cells of (iv). The remaining cells
have the colour $\Lambda_0$ before the switching square and $\Lambda_2$ after the square.
\item The white cells of $t$ are the borders of the ordinals and the two white lines.
\end{romenum}
<!-- end thm-2 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
All parts restate Theorem~\ref{thm:main} in the coordinates $(x,y)$. For (ii),
$A_n=\big(\tfrac{e_n-w_n}2,\tfrac{e_n+w_n}2\big)$ and $B_n=A_n+(2^{n+1}-3)(1,1)$, and the
parities follow from Lemma~\ref{lem:timeline}(a), since $u=(e-w)/2$ is even, $v=u+w$ is odd and

\label{def:three-initial-pattern}
Let $0\le r\le b_n$, $|d|\le n$ and $n+r\equiv d\pmod2$.
The value $H_n(r,d)$ is given by the first applicable rule below. An edge $(a,k)$ means
$(0,n)$ or $(b_n,n)$, or an opening or closing edge of a child, at relative time $a$
and with half-width $k$.
\begin{romenum}
\item On the border, $H_n(r,d)=0$.
\item In a child of level $k$ opening at offset $a$, use $H_k(r-a,d)$.
\item If $1\le|r-a|\le2$ and $|d|\le k-|r-a|$ for an edge $(a,k)$, use $2$.
\item If $n\ge3$, $d=0$ and $|r-b_n/2|=n+1$, use $0$.
\item If $n\ge3$ and $|r-b_n/2|+|d|\le n-1$, use $1$ for even $d$ and $2$ for odd $d$.
\item Otherwise use $1$ if
$\min(r,b_n-r)+|d|\equiv n-2\pmod4$, and $2$ otherwise.
\end{romenum}

M_1=M_2=M_3=1,\qquad M_n=M_{n-1}+2M_{n-3}\quad(n\ge4),
\]
and two representations of these numbers, valid for every $n\ge1$, are
\[
M_n=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/3\rfloor}2^j\binom{n-1-2j}{j},\qquad
M_n=\left\lfloor\frac{3^{n^2+2n}}{3^{3n}-3^{2n}-2}\right\rfloor\bmod 3^n.
\]
The second expression is an arithmetic term, using the least nonnegative remainder.
<!-- end cor-4 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
The children give $M_n=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-3}M_k$ for $n\ge4$. Subtracting successive
equations gives the recurrence, including $n=4$ by the initial values. Its generating
function is $\sum_{n\ge1}M_nz^{n-1}=1/(1-z-2z^3)$. In the expansion
$\sum_{m\ge0}(z+2z^3)^m$, choosing $j$ factors $2z^3$ and $n-1-3j$ factors $z$
gives the displayed coefficient.

For the arithmetic term, we use the generating-function extraction method of
\cite[Section~4]{PS25}. Put $M_0=0$, so that
$G(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}M_nz^n=z/(1-z-2z^3)$.
Induction gives $M_r\le2^{r-2}$ for $r\ge2$, with strict inequality for $r\ge3$:
check $r=2,3,4$ directly and, for $r\ge5$, use
$M_r\le2^{r-3}+2\cdot2^{r-5}<2^{r-2}$.
In particular $G(3^{-n})$ converges for $n\ge1$. For an integer $A_n\ge0$,
\[
3^{n^2}G(3^{-n})=A_n3^n+M_n+\eta_n,\qquad
\eta_n=\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{M_{n+i}}{3^{ni}}.
\]
The tail satisfies
\[
0\le \eta_n<2^{n-2}\sum_{i\ge1}\left(\frac2{3^n}\right)^i
=\frac{2^{n-1}}{3^n-2}\le1,
\]
where the last inequality is $3^n\ge2^{n-1}+2$. Since $M_n<3^n$, taking the floor
and the remainder gives the asserted formula. Because its denominator is positive for
$n\ge1$, its subtractions can be read as truncated subtraction. The remainder can be
written as $a\bmod m=a-m\lfloor a/m\rfloor$. The expression is therefore an arithmetic
term in the sense of \cite{PS25}. The stronger coefficient bound above permits base $3$.
A direct application of \cite[Theorem~2]{PS25} gives the same formula with base $8$.

<!-- thm-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:three-none}
Let $g:\mathbb{F}_3^3\to\mathbb{F}_3$ be symmetric in its first and third argument and let $b\in\mathbb{F}_3$. The
recurrent double sequence with rule $g$ and boundary values $t(x,0)=t(0,y)=b$
cannot satisfy all of the following conditions.
\begin{romenum}
\item Along the diagonal, three consecutive ordinals contained in no other ordinal
are $S_0$, $S_1$ and $S_2$. There is no ordinal strictly inside $S_0$. The ordinals directly
inside $S_1$ are two copies of $S_0$, and those directly inside $S_2$ are copies of
$S_1,S_0,S_0,S_1$, in that order.
\item All three ordinals lie in the square $x,y<192$ and have half-width at most $40$.
\item The ordinal $S_1$ lies in the square $x,y<40$ and has half-width at most $9$.
\item No colour occupies more than $97$ percent of the cells in the square $x,y<40$.
\end{romenum}
<!-- end thm-5 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
We use a finite condition that depends only on the contents of $S_2$. If (i)--(iii)
hold, the small square contains a nested pair, and the large square contains a box
whose direct children have levels $1,0,0,1$, each level-$1$ child having two leaf
children. The test allows this box to have an enclosing ordinal. It also allows
other boxes between the stipulated main boxes. Thus it makes no inference about
global maximality or global consecutiveness from the finite picture.

For the enumeration, write a rectangle as $(e,w,f)$, where $e$ and $f$ are its
opening and closing times. Its border and its interior rows are entirely visible
in the square $x,y<N$ if
\[
1\le w\le W,\qquad w\le e<f,\qquad e\equiv f\equiv w\pmod2,
\qquad f+w\le2N-2.
\]
An ordinal inside such a rectangle has smaller half-width and lies in the same
square, so every descendant needed to test its children is visible as well.
In coordinates, $(e,w,f)$ is inside $(e',w',f')$ if
$e'<e$, $f<f'$ and $w<w'$.

For each possible pair $(e,w)$, the detector checks the opening edge and then
extends the walls in steps of two rows. Each extension checks the newly enclosed
rows for a non-border-coloured cell. It stops at a broken wall, an entirely
border-coloured interior row, the boundary of the square, or the first closing
edge. The last stopping rule is valid because that closing edge would be an
entirely border-coloured interior row of any longer candidate with the same
$(e,w)$. Thus there is at most one ordinal for each opening and half-width.
For fixed $w$ there are at most $N-1-w$ possible openings, so the storage bounds are
\[
\sum_{w=1}^{9}(39-w)=306,\qquad
\sum_{w=1}^{40}(191-w)=6820.
\]
The program allocates these bounds and aborts if either is exceeded.

The test of descendants does not assign each rectangle an arbitrary parent.
For each candidate $B$, it lists all rectangles strictly inside $B$. A leaf has
none. A level-$1$ candidate has two descendants, both leaves. For a level-$2$
candidate, take the containment-maximal rectangles inside $B$ and require four
of them in the order $1,0,0,1$. The outer two must be level-$1$ candidates.
There can be at most eight proper descendants in this configuration. Checking
all candidates with four to eight descendants therefore includes the required
$S_2$, even if some containment relations elsewhere do not form a tree.
The test does not require copies to have matching dimensions or colours in their
interiors, so it accepts every configuration stipulated by (i).

There are $3^{18}$ symmetric rules. If $g(b,b,b)=b$, the whole sequence is
constant and fails (iv). Otherwise there is a unique permutation of the three
colours taking $b$ to $0$ and $g(b,b,b)$ to $1$. Conjugating $g$ by this
permutation preserves symmetry, containment and the density condition.
It is therefore enough to enumerate the $3^{17}=129\,140\,163$ symmetric rules
with boundary $0$ and $g(0,0,0)=1$, testing all three possible border colours.

The exhaustive run tested all these rules. All passed the density condition,
and $649\,981$ pairs of a rule and a border colour passed the small-window nesting test.
For each surviving pair, all ordinals of half-width at most $40$ in the large
square were enumerated. None contained a level-$2$ candidate. The largest
rectangle counts were $170$ in the small square and $3377$ in the large square,
below the proved storage bounds.
As a positive control, the same detector accepts the level-$2$ box of the
four-letter sequence at times $55$ to $105$, with its eight descendants.
Section~\ref{sec:computer} records the independent checks of the detector.

and all its descendants. A virtual box of level $n$ contains exactly $3^n-1$ ordinary boxes.
<!-- end cor-6 -->
<!-- proof-26: body_md -->
Let $N_n$ count the ordinary boxes in an ordinary box of level $n$, including the outer box.
Theorem~\ref{thm:four}(b) gives $N_0=1$ and
$N_n=1+2\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}N_k$ for $n\ge1$. Subtraction gives $N_n=3N_{n-1}$, hence $N_n=3^n$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:four-virtual}, a virtual box has the same ordinary children and their
descendants. Any ordinary box of smaller level has half-width at most $v_n-4$, where
$Q_n=P_n$, so no further ordinary box appears when the outer walls are changed.
The virtual box itself is not an ordinal, so its count is $N_n-1=3^n-1$.

<!-- lem-13: body_md -->
\label{lem:gaps}
Let $u:\mathbb{N}\to A$ be $k$-automatic, let $N$ be the number of elements of its $k$-kernel, and let the
letter $a$ occur in $u$. If $u(n)\ne a$ for all $n$ in an interval of length $2k^m$ with $m\ge2^N$,
then there are integers $q\ge1$ and $p\ge1$ such that $u(n)\ne a$ for all
$n\in[qk^{m+tp},(q+1)k^{m+tp})$ and all $t\ge0$.
<!-- end lem-13 -->
<!-- proof-34: body_md -->
An interval of length $2k^m$ contains an interval $[qk^m,(q+1)k^m)$ with $q\ge0$. For a sequence
$v$ in the $k$-kernel $\mathcal{K}$ of $u$ and a digit $b<k$, the sequence $n\mapsto v(kn+b)$ lies in $\mathcal{K}$.
Let $\mathcal{K}_i\subseteq \mathcal{K}$ be the set of the sequences $n\mapsto u(k^in+b)$ with $0\le b<k^i$. Writing
$b=k^ib_1+b'$ with $b_1<k$ and $b'<k^i$ shows that $\mathcal{K}_{i+1}$ consists of the sequences
$n\mapsto v(kn+b_1)$ with $v\in \mathcal{K}_i$ and $b_1<k$, so $\mathcal{K}_{i+1}$ is determined by $\mathcal{K}_i$. Among the
$2^N+1$ sets $\mathcal{K}_0,\dots,\mathcal{K}_{2^N}$ two coincide, say $\mathcal{K}_i=\mathcal{K}_{i+p}$ with $i+p\le2^N\le m$, and then
$\mathcal{K}_{m+tp}=\mathcal{K}_m$ for all $t\ge0$. By assumption $v(q)\ne a$ for all $v\in \mathcal{K}_m$, hence for all
$v\in \mathcal{K}_{m+tp}$, which is the claim. If $q=0$, the claim says that $u(n)\ne a$ for all
$n<k^{m+tp}$ and all $t$, so $a$ would not occur in $u$. Hence $q\ge1$.
<!-- end proof-34 -->
<!-- proof-35: body_md -->
Suppose that $t$ is $k$-automatic. By Lemma~\ref{lem:line} the sequence
$u(x)=t(x,x+1)=T[2x+1,1]$ is $k$-automatic. By Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, $T[s,1]=0$ exactly when
$s=3$, by rule~\ref{D0}, or when $[s,1]$ lies on the border of a box of $\mathfrak{B}$, by rule~\ref{D1},
since the other rules give the values $1$ and $2$ on odd rows. The cells $[s,1]$ on borders are
the cells of the edges, because the walls $|d|=1$ belong to the boxes of level $1$, whose rows are
their two edges and the row between them. Inside the main box $S_n$ two consecutive edge times
differ by at most $4n+2$, which is the length of the switching gap of $S_n$, and consecutive main
boxes are $4$ rows apart. The initial runs at $x=0$ and $x=2,3$ are finite exceptions.
Every later maximal run of terms of $u$ different from $0$, starting at $x_0\ge5$,
lies between two consecutive edge times $s'=2x_0-1$ and $s''$, and its length is
$(s''-s')/2-1\le2n$, where $n$ is the level of the main box that contains $s'$ or ends just before
it. Since $e_n=2^{n+2}-2n+3>2^{n+1}$, we have $n<\log_2s'$, and the run has length less than
$2\log_2(2x_0)$. On the other hand the switching gap of $S_n$ gives a run of length $2n$, so the
runs are unbounded. Lemma~\ref{lem:gaps}, applied with $a=0$, gives runs of length $k^{m+tp}$
starting at $qk^{m+tp}$ for all $t\ge0$, which contradicts the bound $2\log_2(2qk^{m+tp})$ for
large $t$. Hence $t$ is not $k$-automatic.

For $\tau$ we use the letter $3$ on the same line. By Theorem~\ref{thm:four}(a),
the cells $\tau[s,\pm1]$ with $s\ge56$ are green at offset $12$ in each box
of level $1$ and $12$ rows after each middle block begins. These are the only green
entries on $|d|=1$ in the tables $P_1$ and $\mathcal{M}$. None of the other description rules
gives a green cell on this line: borders, transition rows and junction rows give
$0$ or $1$, rules (iv) and (F6) concern even columns, $I$ takes the values $2,0,0$
on the wall column $a=1$, and $\Lambda$ is white on $|d|=1$.

The middle block of a box of level $n\ge2$ starts at offset $c_{\mathrm{mid}}=\ell_n/2-7$.
Thus every box of level $n\ge1$ has a green cell on $d=1$ at offset
$\ell_n/2+5$, five rows after the middle of the box. The same statement holds
for each virtual box $V_j$ at its level.
Induction on $n$, using the three sub-boxes, shows that each row of a box of level
$n\ge1$ lies within $4n+12$ rows of a green cell on $d=1$ inside the box.
Consecutive main boxes are at most $12$ rows apart. The main boxes that meet the
rows up to $s$ have levels at most $\log_3s+1$: in block $j$, the row index satisfies
$s\ge E_j-1\ge30\cdot27^j$ and the levels are at most $3j+4$.
Consequently a maximal run of terms of $u(x)=\tau(x,x+1)$ different from $3$,
starting at a sufficiently large $x_0$, has length at most $C\log x_0$ for a constant $C$.

These runs are unbounded. In a box of level $n\ge3$, consider the last row with
a green cell on $d=1$ in its first child and the first such row in its second child.
The two rows are $8n-2$ rows apart, with no green cell on $d=1$ between them.
Lemma~\ref{lem:gaps}, applied with $a=3$, contradicts the logarithmic bound as in the
case of $t$.

Finally consider $v(x)=\rho(x,x+1)=1-U[2x+1,1]$, and use the red letter $1$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:three-initial}, its occurrences are exactly the intersections with
the borders of odd-level boxes: the other zero cells of $U$ have $d=0$, even-level
edges have even time, and the wall $d=1$ of a level-$1$ box consists only of its two
edge cells.

For a box of level $n$, let $Z_n$ be the relative times of these red cells on $d=1$.
Then $Z_1=\{0,2\}$, $Z_2=\varnothing$ and $Z_3=\{0,14\}$. For $n\ge3$, the first
red time and the distance of the last red time from $b_n$ are both
\[
a_n=\begin{cases}0,&n\text{ odd},\\3,&n\text{ even},\end{cases}
\]
and the largest difference between consecutive red times is $2n+8$.
To prove these assertions, use the child list of Section~\ref{sec:three-initial}.
For odd $n$, the red cells occur on both edges of the parent. For even $n\ge4$,
the first and last children have odd level and lie $3$ rows from the parent's edges.
Within either half, consecutive
children have successive levels. Their nearest red cells are $6$ rows apart, except
when the empty set $Z_2$ is skipped: the level-$2$ duration $b_2=6$ and its two
neighbouring gaps of $3$ give $12$ rows between the red cells of levels $3$ and $1$.
For an odd-level parent, the gap from its opening to its first red child cell is $12$
at level $5$ and $6$ at odd levels at least $7$, and closing is symmetric.
The two central level-$1$ children, for $n\ge4$, have red edges separated by exactly
$2n+8$, with no red cell between them. This gap exceeds the other gaps and, by induction,
all gaps inside a child. The case $n=3$ follows from $Z_3$ directly.

Consequently $C_n$, for $n\ge3$, contains a run of exactly $n+3$ non-red terms of $v$,
and no longer run between its red cells. Between $C_n$ and $C_{n+1}$, for $n\ge3$,
the nearest red times are $a_n+3+a_{n+1}=6$ rows apart. The initial level-$2$ main box
has no red cell on this line. The surrounding red times $7$ and $19$ give a single
finite exception of $12$ rows.

Since the durations increase, $b_n\ge3b_{n-3}$ for $n\ge4$, and hence
\[
b_m\ge2\cdot3^{\lfloor(m-1)/3\rfloor},\qquad
c_n\ge b_{n-1}\ge2\cdot3^{(n-4)/3}\quad(n\ge2).
\]
A maximal non-red run starting at $x_0\ge10$ has its preceding red cell at time
$s'=2x_0-1$ in a main box $C_n$, with $n\ge3$ and $c_n\le s'$. Its length is at most
\[
n+3\le3\log_3(s'/2)+7<3\log_3x_0+7.
\]
Thus the non-red runs are unbounded, but their lengths are $O(\log(x_0+2))$.
If $\rho$ were $k$-automatic, Lemma~\ref{lem:line} would make $v$ $k$-automatic.
Lemma~\ref{lem:gaps}, with $a=1$, would then give non-red intervals of length
$k^{m+tp}$ starting at $qk^{m+tp}$ for all $t\ge0$. Extend each to a maximal run:
its starting point is no larger, so its length still has a logarithmic upper bound
in $qk^{m+tp}$. This contradicts the displayed interval lengths for large $t$.
