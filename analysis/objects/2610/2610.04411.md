\label{The1}
Let $c_{1}=c_{0}=c>0,$ suppose $\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{0},u_{-1})$ is a solution of \eqref{eq9.4} in $(H^1(\mathbb{R})\setminus \{0\})^{3}$, then there exists a unique vector $\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{0},a_{-1})$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{T1}
U_\mathbf{a}(x) = R M(x)^{-1} \mathcal{A}(x) R^T, \quad
U_\mathbf{a}(x) = \begin{pmatrix} u_1 & u_0 / \sqrt{2} \\ u_0 / \sqrt{2} & u_{-1} \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
 Each set of real parameters yields a smooth \(H^1\) solution on the entire real line, and its parameters are given by
\begin{equation}\label{H1}
a_i = \lim_{x \to -\infty} e^{-\eta_i x} u_i(x), \quad i = 1, 0, -1.
\end{equation}
  In particularly, if $\lambda=0,$ then $\eta=\sqrt{-\mu}$ and there exists a unique vector $\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{0},a_{-1})$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{N4}
U_\mathbf{a} = e^{\eta x} A \left( I_2 + \frac{c}{4\eta^2} e^{2\eta x} A^2 \right)^{-1},\ \text{where} \ \ A = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_0 / \sqrt{2} \\ a_0 / \sqrt{2} & a_{-1} \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and \(I_2\) is the \(2 \times 2\) identity matrix. Each parameter \(A\) yields a globally smooth \(H^1\) solution, and the parameter is uniquely determined by
\begin{equation*}
A = \lim_{x \to -\infty} e^{-\eta x} U_\mathbf{a}.
\end{equation*}

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The Hirota method used here is nonstandard in a precise structural sense.
Besides writing $u_i=g_i/f$, it introduces the auxiliary function $s$ through
\[
    sf=g_0^2-2g_1g_{-1},
\]
and uses the formal parameter $X$ solely to record homogeneous degree in the
amplitudes. The additional variable $s$ isolates the spin-exchange combination
and closes the coupled problem as five bilinear equations whose coefficients
can be solved recursively. This triangular structure is what makes an exact
finite cascade possible and subsequently reveals its $4\times4$ determinant
encoding. Thus the modified Hirota formulation is not merely a convenient way
to compute an explicit solution: it is the structural bridge between the
original coupled system, the finite algebraic identities, and the determinant
representation. Its role is nevertheless confined to the construction stage;
the exhaustion of all $H^1$ solutions still requires the independent
asymptotic and uniqueness arguments.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The determinant formula is not used merely to exhibit a family of solutions.
The argument also assigns a unique left amplitude triple to every real-valued
$(H^1(\mathbb R))^3$ solution and proves that two solutions with the same triple
must coincide. Thus the construction map $\mathbf{a}\mapsto U_\mathbf{a}$ is shown to be both
surjective and injective. This distinction between constructing explicit
solutions and exhausting the entire solution space is a central feature of the
present work.
The middle component first supplies the decay needed to analyze the two side
components, whose equations contain the inhomogeneous spin-exchange terms
$-cu_{-1}u_0^2$ and $-cu_1u_0^2$. A scalar asymptotic lemma therefore yields a
well-defined amplitude triple even when a side amplitude vanishes. The refined
asymptotics lead to an exact Volterra representation, and a componentwise
weighted contraction estimate controls every exchange term. This is the
system-specific step that adapts the general left-uniqueness strategy to the
present spin-$1$ model without imposing a common profile or an additional
spectral-gap ratio.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
The condition $c_0=c_1$ is structural rather than merely technical. In matrix
form, the stationary spin-$1$ BEC system with general coupling constants can be
written as
\[
 U''+\mu U+\frac{\lambda}{2}
 \bigl(\sigma_3U+U\sigma_3\bigr)
 +2c_1U^3
 +(c_0-c_1)\operatorname{tr}(U^2)U=0.
\]
When $c_0=c_1$, the trace-coupling term disappears and the nonlinearity reduces
to the single matrix covariant $U^3$. This reduction is precisely what permits
the closed Hirota bilinear system, its finite truncation, and the associated
$4\times4$ determinant representation.

If $c_0\neq c_1$, the two cubic covariants
$
 U^3
 \ \text{and}\
 \operatorname{tr}(U^2)U
$
are generically independent. Consequently, the second term cannot be absorbed
into $U^3$ by replacing the coupling constant with an effective one. In
particular, if $U_\mathbf{a}$ denotes the determinant solution of the pure matrix-cubic
equation with coefficient $c_1$, then its residual in the general system is
$
 \mathcal R(U_\mathbf{a})
 =(c_0-c_1)\operatorname{tr}(U_\mathbf{a}^2)U_\mathbf{a},
$
which is nonzero for a generic nontrivial three-component solution. At the
bilinear level, the additional trace term generates new nonlinear products that
are not encoded by the original auxiliary function. The finite algebraic
closure is therefore lost, and the Hirota cascade is not expected to terminate
at a fixed order.

The left-asymptotic analysis and the uniqueness of a solution with prescribed
left amplitudes may still remain valid when $c_0\neq c_1$. What fails is the
surjectivity of the construction: an arbitrary amplitude triple need not
produce a global solution decaying at both ends. Instead, the admissible
amplitudes must satisfy an additional right-end matching condition and are
expected, in the nonintegrable case, to form a lower-dimensional subset of the
full amplitude space. Special scalar, fixed-polarization, or rank-one reductions
may still survive because the two cubic covariants become proportional on such
invariant subspaces. Thus, the obstruction for $c_0\neq c_1$ is the loss of the
integrable finite-rank closure, rather than a mere increase in computational
complexity; it does not rule out a classification by different, generally
non-explicit methods.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
If \(c_0=c_1=c\leq0\), then \(u_1\equiv u_0\equiv u_{-1}\equiv0\). Indeed, testing the three equations by \(u_1,u_0,u_{-1}\), respectively, summing, and integrating by parts over \(\mathbb R\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j\in\{1,0,-1\}}
\int_{\mathbb R}
\left(|u_j'|^2+\eta_j^2u_j^2\right)\,dx
=
2c\int_{\mathbb R}\operatorname{tr}(U^4)\,dx.
\label{eq:energy-c-nonpositive}
\end{equation}
Since \(U\) is real and symmetric,
\[
\operatorname{tr}(U^4)
=
\operatorname{tr}\!\left((U^2)^TU^2\right)
=
\|U^2\|_F^2
\geq0.
\]
Thus, the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:energy-c-nonpositive} is nonnegative, while the right-hand side is nonpositive. Hence both sides vanish. Since \(\eta_j>0\), we conclude that \(u_j\equiv0\) for \(j\in\{1,0,-1\}\).

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
\label{rem:physical-admissibility}
The condition $|M|\leq N$ is not an additional technical
assumption, but a necessary consequence of the definitions of the
total particle number and the longitudinal magnetization. Indeed,
$
N+M
=
\int_{\mathbb R}
\left(2u_1^2+u_0^2\right)\,dx\geq0,
$
and
$
N-M
=
\int_{\mathbb R}
\left(u_0^2+2u_{-1}^2\right)\,dx\geq0.
$
Hence $-N\leq M\leq N$. At the two endpoints, this argument gives
the stronger conclusions
$
M=N
\Rightarrow
u_0\equiv u_{-1}\equiv0,
$
and
$
M=-N
\Rightarrow
u_1\equiv u_0\equiv0.
$
Thus the boundary values $M=\pm N$ describe fully magnetized states
supported entirely in the $m_F=\pm1$ Zeeman component.

\label{rem:exact-constraint-inversion}
The first essential feature of the Theorem \ref{The2} is that it converts the
physical constraints into a finite-dimensional inverse problem. In
the noncollision regime, the exact identities
$$
N
=
\frac{2}{c}
\left(
\eta_1\varepsilon_1
+2\eta_0\varepsilon_0
+\eta_{-1}\varepsilon_{-1}
\right),
$$
and
$$
M
=
\frac{2}{c}
\left(
\eta_1\varepsilon_1
-\eta_{-1}\varepsilon_{-1}
\right),
\
\varepsilon_i:=\mathbf 1_{\{a_i\neq0\}},
$$
show that the total particle number and magnetization depend only on
the decay rates and the active asymptotic channels, but not on the
magnitudes or signs of the nonzero amplitudes. Consequently, the unconstrained parametrization
$
(\mu,\lambda,\mathbf{a})\longmapsto U_\mathbf{a}
$
can be inverted at the level of the physical data $(N,M)$ by solving
a finite algebraic system. The resulting constraint map is naturally
set-valued: each individual solution still has a unique asymptotic
amplitude, but a fixed pair $(N,M)$ may correspond to several
different branches or continuous families of solutions.

<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
\label{rem:rank-support-stratification}
A second essential feature is the different geometric mechanism
governing the collision and noncollision regimes. When $\lambda=0$,
the three decay rates coincide, and the asymptotic data must be
described by a symmetric $2\times2$ amplitude matrix. The constrained
classification is then determined by the rank and eigendirections of
this matrix, producing distinct rank-one and rank-two families.

When $\lambda\neq0$, the decay rates are distinct and the
classification is instead determined by the nonzero pattern
$
(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_0,\varepsilon_{-1})
\in\{0,1\}^3\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}.
$
The seven possible support patterns give the three one-amplitude,
three two-amplitude, and one three-amplitude strata. Thus the complete
classification requires the combination of two genuinely different
mechanisms.
In particular, the collision families cannot be obtained by simply
setting $\lambda=0$ in the distinct-rate formulas.

<!-- remark-8: body_md -->
\label{rem:magnetization-phase-diagram}
The complete branch structure is governed by the dimensionless
magnetization ratio
$
m:=\frac{M}{N}\in[-1,1].
$
While the rank-one collision family exists throughout the entire
physical interval $|m|\leq1$, the genuinely coupled noncollision
families occur only in the smaller regime
$
0<|m|<\sqrt2-1.
$
The threshold $\sqrt2-1$ is intrinsic: it is exactly the value at
which one of the reconstructed decay rates ceases to be positive.
The exceptional values
$
m=\pm\frac13
$
correspond to a spectral collision $\lambda=0$ and must therefore be
removed from the appropriate two-amplitude noncollision branches.

Hence the Theorem \ref{The2} provides a complete magnetization-dependent phase
diagram rather than a finite list of isolated normalized states.
Different stationary branches may coexist for the same physical data
$(c,N,M)$ while possessing different multipliers, decay rates, and
internal structures. Selecting the physically preferred branch
requires a further constrained-energy or stability analysis and is
not part of the classification theorem itself.

For positive numbers \(p \neq q\), we have
\begin{equation*}
\det \begin{pmatrix} (2p)^{-1} & (p+q)^{-1} \\ (p+q)^{-1} & (2q)^{-1} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{(p-q)^2}{4pq(p+q)^2} > 0.

coefficients directly. We now explain why the same matching can also be viewed
as order-by-order uniqueness for the cascade equations.

Suppose that two collections of coefficients agree at all lower orders and
both satisfy \eqref{eq9.1d} at the current order. For a denominator
coefficient, their difference~$h=\widetilde f_{2m}-f_{2m}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 h''=0.
 \label{eq:app-en-f-difference-ode}
\end{equation}
For every higher-order denominator, the cascade construction imposes the
left-end normalization
\[
 f_{2m}(x),\ f_{2m}'(x)\longrightarrow0
 \ (x\to-\infty).
\]
This excludes both the constant and linear solutions of
\eqref{eq:app-en-f-difference-ode}, so~$h=0$.

For the auxiliary coefficient~$s_{2m}$, \eqref{eq9.1d} is an algebraic recurrence.
At the current order its left-hand side contains
$s_{2m}f_0=s_{2m}$, while every other term contains only lower-order
coefficients. Since~$f_0=1$, equality at all lower orders immediately implies
equality of the current~$s_{2m}$ coefficients.

For any~$i\in\{1,0,-1\}$, the difference between two numerator coefficients,
$h_i=\widetilde g_{i,2m+1}-g_{i,2m+1}$, satisfies
\begin{equation*}
 h_i''-\eta_i^2h_i=0.
\end{equation*}
Consequently,
\[
 h_i=A_ie^{\eta_ix}+B_ie^{-\eta_ix}.
\]
Left-end decay excludes~$B_ie^{-\eta_ix}$. The convention that no new
leading-order homogeneous mode is introduced at higher cascade orders excludes
$A_ie^{\eta_ix}$. Therefore~$h_i=0$. Induction now shows that the cascade
coefficients with the same first-order leading terms and the same construction
normalization are unique at every order. Combining \eqref{eq:app-en-f-coeff-equality},
\eqref{eq:app-en-g-coeff-equality}, and
\eqref{eq:app-en-s-coeff-equality}, we finally obtain
\begin{equation*}
 \widetilde f=f,
 \
 \widetilde g_i=g_i\ (i=1,0,-1),
 \
 \widetilde s=s.
\end{equation*}
In particular, the fourth-order
determinant representation is not a new candidate family: it is an exact
finite encoding of the finite cascade functions constructed in Lemma \ref{LE1}.

Temporarily denote the cubic term in the original equation as \(F_i(u)\), whose complete expression is given in \begin{equation}\label{M23}
\begin{aligned}
F_1(u) &= -2cu_1^3 - 2cu_0^2u_1 - cu_{-1}u_0^2, \\
F_0(u) &= -cu_0^3 - 2cu_1^2u_0 - 2cu_{-1}^2u_0 - 2cu_1u_{-1}u_0, \\
F_{-1}(u) &= -2cu_{-1}^3 - 2cu_0^2u_{-1} - cu_1u_0^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation} The 1D embedding and product estimates give
\[
H^1(\mathbb{R}) \hookrightarrow L^\infty(\mathbb{R}) \cap C_0(\mathbb{R}), \quad \|u_j u_k u_l\|_{L^2} \le \|u_j\|_\infty \|u_k\|_\infty \|u_l\|_{L^2}.
\]
Therefore
\[
u_i'' = \eta_i^2 u_i + F_i(u) \in L^2, \quad u_i \in H^2, \quad u_i' \in H^1 \hookrightarrow C_0.
\]
Repeatedly substituting into the polynomial equation yields
\[
u_i \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}), \ \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} u_i(x) = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} u_i'(x) = 0.
\]
The central equation can be written as a homogeneous scalar equation
\begin{equation}\label{M24}
u_0'' - \eta_0^2 u_0 = q_0 u_0, \ q_0 = -cu_0^2 - 2c(u_1^2 + u_{-1}^2 + u_1 u_{-1}).
\end{equation}
Each squared term belongs to \(L^1\), and \(\int |u_1 u_{-1}| \le \|u_1\|_{L^2} \|u_{-1}\|_{L^2}\), and
\[
\|q_0\|_{L^1} \le c\|u_0\|_2^2 + 2c\|u_1\|_2^2 + 2c\|u_{-1}\|_2^2 + 2c\|u_1\|_2 \|u_{-1}\|_2 < \infty.
\]
So \(q_0 \in C(\mathbb{R}) \cap L^1(\mathbb{R})\). By Lemma \ref{LE7}, there exists \(a_0\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{M25}
u_0 = a_0 e^{\eta_0 x} + o(e^{\eta_0 x}), \ u_0' = \eta_0 a_0 e^{\eta_0 x} + o(e^{\eta_0 x}).
\end{equation}
And since the non-homogeneous term of this equation is zero, \(a_0 = 0\) if and only if \(u_0 \equiv 0\).

\noindent Next, write the first and third equations as

u_1'' - \eta_1^2 u_1 = q_1 u_1 + h_1, \ q_1 = -2c(u_1^2 + u_0^2), \ h_1 = -cu_1 u_0^2,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation*}
u_{-1}'' - \eta_{-1}^2 u_{-1} = q_{-1} u_{-1} + h_{-1}, \ q_{-1} = -2c(u_{-1}^2 + u_0^2), \ h_{-1} = -cu_1 u_0^2.

\[
|h_1| \le c\|u_1\|_\infty |u_0|^2 = O(e^{2\eta_0 x}), \ |h_{-1}| \le c\|u_1\|_\infty |u_0|^2 = O(e^{2\eta_0 x}).

From \eqref{M13},

2\eta_0 = \eta_1 + \sigma_1 = \eta_{-1} + \sigma_{-1}, \ \sigma_1 := 2\eta_0 - \eta_1 > 0, \quad \sigma_{-1} := 2\eta_0 - \eta_{-1} > 0.

Therefore, Lemma \ref{LE7} can be applied separately. We obtain unique \(a_1, a_{-1}\) satisfying the asymptotic formulas corresponding to \eqref{M25}. \[
3\eta_i - \eta_i = 2\eta_i \geq 2\eta_*, \ (\eta_i + 2\eta_j) - \eta_i = 2\eta_j \geq 2\eta_*.

The weighted indices of the three exchange terms respectively satisfy
\[
\begin{aligned}
(2\eta_0 + \eta_{-1}) - \eta_1 &\geq 2\eta_{-1} \geq 2\eta_*, \\
(2\eta_0 + \eta_1) - \eta_{-1} &\geq 2\eta_1 \geq 2\eta_*, \\
(\eta_1 + \eta_0 + \eta_{-1}) - \eta_0 &= \eta_1 + \eta_{-1} \geq 2\eta_*.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore, every monomial term in \eqref{M23} satisfies the uniform estimate for its respective component
\begin{equation}\label{M27}
F_i(u(x)) = O(e^{(\eta_i + 2\eta_*)x}) \quad (x \to -\infty). \tag{6.6}
\end{equation}
Here for \(x \leq 0\), the larger the index rate, the smaller the exponential function. The integrating factor identity is
\begin{align}\label{M29}
\left(e^{\eta_i x}(u_i' - \eta_i u_i)\right)' = e^{\eta_i x}F_i(u(x)).
\end{align}

\noindent Since
\[
\lim_{t \to -\infty} e^{\eta_i t}(u_i'(t) - \eta_i u_i(t)) = 0,
\]

\noindent integrating from \(-\infty\) yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
u_i' - \eta_i u_i = e^{-\eta_i x} \int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{\eta_i t}F_i(u(t)) \, dt= O(e^{(\eta_i + 2\eta_*)x}).
\end{aligned}
\]

\noindent Moreover,
\[
(e^{-\eta_i x}u_i)' = e^{-\eta_i x}(u_i' - \eta_i u_i) = O(e^{2\eta_*x}).
\]

\noindent From \(a_i = \lim_{x \to -\infty} e^{-\eta_i x}u_i(x)\),
\begin{align}\label{M28}
e^{-\eta_i x}u_i(x) - a_i &= \int_{-\infty}^{x} (e^{-\eta_i t}u_i(t))' \, dt = O(e^{2\eta_*x}).
\end{align}
Multiplying back by \(e^{\eta_i x}\), and combining with \eqref{M28}, we obtain Lemma \ref{LE8}.
Indeed, integrating \eqref{M29} again, we have
\[
e^{-\eta_i x}u_i(x) - a_i = \int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{-2\eta_i z} \left( \int_{-\infty}^{z} e^{\eta_i t}F_i(u(t)) \, dt \right) dz.
\]

\noindent Here \(a_i\) is the already proven left limit, not a new free constant added in this step. First, applying Tonelli's theorem to the absolute values, we have
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{-2\eta_i z} \int_{-\infty}^{z} e^{\eta_i t}|F_i(u(t))| \, dt \, dz \\
&= \int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{\eta_i t}|F_i(u(t))| \left( \int_{t}^{x} e^{-2\eta_i z} dz \right) dt \le \frac{1}{2\eta_i} \int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{-\eta_i t}|F_i(u(t))| \, dt < \infty.
\end{aligned}
\]

\noindent The final integrability comes from the left end \(e^{-\eta_i t}F_i(u(t)) = O(e^{2\eta_* t})\), as well as the continuity of the integrand on any finite interval. Therefore, Fubini's theorem can be used to exchange the order of integration. Using
\[
e^{\eta_i x} e^{\eta_i t} \int_{t}^{x} e^{-2\eta_i z} dz = \frac{e^{\eta_i (x-t)} - e^{-\eta_i (x-t)}}{2\eta_i} = \frac{1}{\eta_i} \sinh(\eta_i (x-t)),
\]

\noindent we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{M30}
u_i(x) = a_i e^{\eta_i x} + \frac{1}{\eta_i} \int_{-\infty}^{x} \sinh(\eta_i (x-t)) F_i(u(t)) \, dt.
\end{equation}
It holds for every finite \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), and is an exact equality, not just an approximation valid near negative infinity.

Let \(u, v\) be any two real-valued \(H^1\) global solutions, and assume they have the same left amplitude triple. Let \(w_i = u_i - v_i\). By Lemma \ref{LE8}, there exist fixed \(b_0 < 0\) and \(B > 0\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{M31}
|u_i(x)|, |v_i(x)| \le B e^{\eta_i x} \quad (x \le b_0), \ w_i = o(e^{\eta_i x}).
\end{equation}

\noindent Define
\begin{equation}\label{M32}
Z(x) = \max_{j=1,0,-1} e^{-\eta_j x} |w_j(x)|.
\end{equation}

\noindent For \(j, k, l \in \{1, 0, -1\}\), denote
\begin{equation}\label{M33}
\Delta_{jkl} := u_j u_k u_l - v_j v_k v_l = w_j u_k u_l + v_j w_k u_l + v_j v_k w_l.
\end{equation}
From \eqref{M31} and \(|w_j| \le Z(x) e^{\eta_j x}\),
\begin{equation}\label{M34}
|\Delta_{jkl}| \le 3B^2 Z(x) e^{(\eta_j + \eta_k + \eta_l)x}.
\end{equation}

\noindent The three nonlinear differences are exactly written as
\[
\begin{aligned}
F_1(u) - F_1(v) &= -2c\Delta_{111} - 2c\Delta_{001} - c\Delta_{-1,0,0}, \\
F_0(u) - F_0(v) &= -c\Delta_{000} - 2c\Delta_{110} - 2c\Delta_{-1,-1,0} - 2c\Delta_{1,-1,0}, \\
F_{-1}(u) - F_{-1}(v) &= -2c\Delta_{-1,-1,-1} - 2c\Delta_{0,0,-1} - c\Delta_{1,0,0}.
\end{aligned}
\]
For the ordinary terms, after removing the target weight \(e^{\eta_i x}\), the remainder is \(2\eta_j \ge 2\eta_*\). The three types of exchange terms respectively satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{M35}
\begin{aligned}
2\eta_0 + \eta_{-1} - \eta_1 &\ge 2\eta_{-1} \ge 2\eta_*, \\
2\eta_0 + \eta_1 - \eta_{-1} &\ge 2\eta_1 \ge 2\eta_*, \\
\eta_1 + \eta_{-1} &\ge 2\eta_*.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent Adding the terms from \eqref{M34} together, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{M36}
|F_i(u(x)) - F_i(v(x))| \le C e^{(\eta_i + 2\eta_*)x} Z(x), \quad x \le b_0.
\end{equation}

\noindent The sums of the absolute values of the coefficients for the three equations are \(5c, 7c, 5c\) respectively, so we can uniformly choose
\[
C = 3B^2 \max\{5c, 7c, 5c\} = 21cB^2,
\]

\noindent \(b_0, B, C\) are fixed once here, and \(C\) does not change when the interval endpoint is subsequently moved to the left. The whole process does not divide by any \(a_i\), so zero amplitudes are allowed.



\noindent Subtracting the two solutions in \eqref{M30}, the same-amplitude leading terms cancel out, yielding
\begin{equation}\label{M37}
w_i(x) = \frac{1}{\eta_i} \int_{-\infty}^{x} \sinh(\eta_i(x-t)) [F_i(u(t)) - F_i(v(t))] \, dt.
\end{equation}

\noindent For \(b \le b_0\), let
\[
W_b = \max_i \sup_{x \le b} e^{-\eta_i x} |w_i(x)| < \infty.
\]
The finiteness is obtained from the left asymptotics and the continuity on compact intervals. Using $$\sinh(\eta_i(x-t)) \le \frac{1}{2} e^{\eta_i(x-t)}\ \ \text{for}\ t\leq x,  $$ we have
\begin{align*}
e^{-\eta_i x} |w_i(x)| &\le \frac{C}{2\eta_i} W_b \int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{2\eta_* t} \, dt = \frac{C}{4\eta_i \eta_*} W_b e^{2\eta_* x} \le \frac{C}{4\eta_*^2} W_b e^{2\eta_* b}.
\end{align*}

\noindent Taking the supremum yields \( W_b \le [C/(4\eta_*^2)] e^{2\eta_* b} W_b\). Since \(C\) does not vary with \(b\), we can take a sufficiently negative \(b\) such that the entire coefficient in the brackets is less than 1, hence \(W_b = 0\). Thus \(u = v\) on \((-\infty, b]\).

To extend the conclusion to the entire axis, note that at a finite point \(b\), the function values and first-order derivatives of the two solutions are the same. Let
\[
Y = (u_1, u_0, u_{-1}, u_1', u_0', u_{-1}') \in \mathbb{R}^6.
\]

\noindent The original system becomes a six-dimensional first-order equation \(Y' = \mathcal{F}(Y)\), where \(\mathcal{F}\) is a polynomial mapping. For any compact set \(K \subset \mathbb{R}^6\),
\[
L_K := \sup_{Y \in K} \|D\mathcal{F}(Y)\| < \infty, \ \|\mathcal{F}(Y) - \mathcal{F}(\tilde{Y})\| \le L_K \|Y - \tilde{Y}\|.
\]
Moreover,
\[
Y_u(b) = Y_v(b).
\]

\noindent Local uniqueness of initial values first gives \(Y_u = Y_v\) in a neighborhood of \(b\); since the initial values are the same at equal interval endpoints, iteratively extending along the common interval of existence of the two solutions yields \(Y_u = Y_v\) on \(\mathbb{R}\). Here, the two solutions are known to exist on the entire axis; it is not the case that the existence on the entire axis is deduced from the fact that ``the right-hand side is a polynomial''.



Within the given range, if two sets of real-valued \(H^1(\mathbb{R})^3\) solutions have the same complete left amplitude triple, then they are identical on the entire real axis.
This conclusion applies to all \(H^1\) solutions, not just to the candidate solutions already written in determinant form. It is precisely the realization of the left-uniqueness method from reference \cite{Guo26} in our system; the new work is to verify \eqref{M36} containing the exchange terms, rather than adding an additional common waveform assumption.


Take any real-valued \(H^1\) solution \(u\) of the original system. Lemma \ref{LE8} gives its unique left amplitude \(a\). Using this set of \(a\), construct the candidate solution \(u^a\) from \eqref{T1}; Lemma \ref{LE6} has already proven that it is smooth on the entire axis, belongs to \(H^1\), satisfies the original equation, and has the same left amplitude \(a\). Thus,  \(u = u^a\) on the entire axis, so there are no \(H^1\) solutions outside the explicit family.

Conversely, Lemma \ref{LE6}  has already proven that every set of \(a \in \mathbb{R}^3\) yields a solution. If two sets of parameters yield the same solution, taking the left limit via \eqref{H1} shows that they are identical. Therefore, the parameters and the solution are in one-to-one correspondence. The zero parameters obviously give the zero solution; if the solution is zero, its left amplitudes are also all zero. The screening conditions for the three components will be proven in the next section, thus completing the parametrization classification of the entire solution space.

\label{thm:lambda-zero}
Fix $c>0$, $N>0$, and $|M|\le N$, and define $m,\kappa$ by
\eqref{eq:normalized-data}.  Among solutions with $\lambda=0$ and $\mu<0$,
the constraints~\eqref{eq9.4}--\eqref{N1} hold exactly in the following
two cases.

\textbf{Case one.}  For every $|m|\le1$, and
\begin{equation}
 \lambda=0,
 \
 \mu=-\kappa^2=-\frac{c^2N^2}{4}.
 \label{eq:rank-one-multipliers}
\end{equation}
All amplitudes are
\begin{equation}
 a_1=\frac{s}{2}(1+m),
 \quad
 a_0=\frac{\varepsilon s}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{1-m^2},
 \quad
 a_{-1}=\frac{s}{2}(1-m),
 \label{eq:rank-one-amplitudes}
\end{equation}
where $s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ and $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$.  When $|m|=1$,
$a_0=0$ and the two choices of $\varepsilon$ give the same matrix.

 \textbf{Case two.}  If $M=0$, and
\begin{equation}
 \lambda=0,
 \
 \mu=-\frac{\kappa^2}{4}=-\frac{c^2N^2}{16}.
 \label{eq:rank-two-multipliers}
\end{equation}
The amplitude matrix may be any invertible real symmetric matrix, equivalently
\begin{equation}
 a_1a_{-1}-\frac{a_0^2}{2}\ne0.
 \label{eq:rank-two-condition}
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Because $N>0$, the zero matrix $A=0$ is impossible: it would give $U_A=0$ and
$N=0$.  A nonzero $2\times2$ matrix has rank one or two, so the two cases below
are exhaustive.

A real symmetric rank-one matrix has exactly one nonzero eigenvalue.  If that
eigenvalue is denoted by $s\ne0$ and $v=(v_1,v_2)^T$ is a corresponding unit
eigenvector, then
\begin{equation*}
 A=svv^T,
 \
 v_1^2+v_2^2=1.
\end{equation*}
Formula~\eqref{eq:J-lambda-zero} becomes
$
 J=\frac{2\eta}{c}vv^T.
$
Thus, by~\eqref{eq:NM-from-J}, we have
\begin{align*}
 N&=tr J
   =\frac{2\eta}{c}tr(vv^T)
   =\frac{2\eta}{c}(v_1^2+v_2^2)
   =\frac{2\eta}{c},\\
 M&=J_{11}-J_{22}
   =\frac{2\eta}{c}(v_1^2-v_2^2).
\end{align*}
Since $\kappa=cN/2$, the first identity gives $\eta=\kappa$.  Since
$\mu=-\eta^2$, this proves~\eqref{eq:rank-one-multipliers}.  Dividing the
second identity by the first gives
\begin{equation}
 v_1^2-v_2^2=\frac{M}{N}=m.
 \label{eq:v-difference}
\end{equation}
Solving the linear system formed by~\eqref{eq:v-difference} and
$v_1^2+v_2^2=1$ gives
\begin{equation}
 v_1^2=\frac{1+m}{2},
 \
 v_2^2=\frac{1-m}{2}.
 \label{eq:v-squares}
\end{equation}
Real $v_1,v_2$ exist exactly when $|m|\le1$.  Furthermore,
\[
 |2v_1v_2|
 =2\sqrt{v_1^2v_2^2}
 =2\sqrt{\frac{1-m^2}{4}}
 =\sqrt{1-m^2}.
\]
Hence, away from the endpoints, there is a sign $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$ such that
\begin{equation}
 2v_1v_2=\varepsilon\sqrt{1-m^2}.
 \label{eq:v-cross}
\end{equation}
At $|m|=1$ the left and right sides are both zero, independently of $\varepsilon$.
Finally, comparing
\[
 svv^T
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 sv_1^2&sv_1v_2\\sv_1v_2&sv_2^2
 \end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 a_1&a_0/\sqrt2\\a_0/\sqrt2&a_{-1}
 \end{pmatrix}
\]
and substituting~\eqref{eq:v-squares}--\eqref{eq:v-cross} yields exactly
\eqref{eq:rank-one-amplitudes}.

Conversely, let $s\ne0$, $|m|\le1$, and choose amplitudes from
\eqref{eq:rank-one-amplitudes}.  A direct determinant calculation gives
\begin{align*}
 \det A=a_1a_{-1}-\frac{a_0^2}{2}=\frac{s^2}{4}(1-m^2)
   -\frac12\left(\frac{s^2}{2}(1-m^2)\right)=0.
\end{align*}
The matrix is not zero because $s\ne0$ and $(1+m)+(1-m)=2$.  Thus it has rank
one.  Standing \eqref{eq9.4}  produces a global $H^1$
solution, and the preceding mass-matrix calculation gives the prescribed
$N,M$.


Both eigenvalues are nonzero.  The two indicators in
\eqref{eq:J-lambda-zero} are therefore one, and orthogonality gives
\begin{equation}
 J=\frac{2\eta}{c}OI_2O^T=\frac{2\eta}{c}I_2.
 \label{eq:J-rank-two}
\end{equation}
Hence
$
 M=J_{11}-J_{22}=0,
 \
 N=tr J=\frac{4\eta}{c}.
$
Thus rank two is possible only for $M=0$, and
$\eta=cN/4=\kappa/2$.  Since $\mu=-\eta^2$, we obtain
\eqref{eq:rank-two-multipliers}.  A $2\times2$ matrix has rank two exactly when
its determinant is nonzero, which is precisely~\eqref{eq:rank-two-condition}.

Conversely, every invertible symmetric $A$ has rank two.  With
$\eta=\kappa/2$, formula~\eqref{eq:J-rank-two} gives
$N=4\eta/c$ and $M=0$.  Standing again gives
the required global $H^1$ solution.  This proves necessity, sufficiency, and
exhaustion.

For every $x\in\mathbb{R}$,

Put
\[
 B_E:=P_E^{-1}
 =\mathcal A_E^{-1}C_E^{-1}\mathcal A_E^{-1}+cC_E.
\]
From~\eqref{eq:A-derivative} and the inverse differentiation rule,
\begin{equation*}
 (\mathcal A_E^{-1})'
 =-\mathcal A_E^{-1}\mathcal A_E'\mathcal A_E^{-1}
 =-\mathcal A_E^{-1}K_E
 =-K_E\mathcal A_E^{-1}.
 \label{eq:Ainv-derivative}
\end{equation*}
The final equality again uses the commutation of $K_E$ and $\mathcal A_E$.
Since $C_E$ is constant in $x$,
\begin{align*}
 B_E'
 &=(\mathcal A_E^{-1})'C_E^{-1}\mathcal A_E^{-1}
   +\mathcal A_E^{-1}C_E^{-1}(\mathcal A_E^{-1})'\\
 &=-\mathcal A_E^{-1}
   (K_EC_E^{-1}+C_E^{-1}K_E)\mathcal A_E^{-1}.
\end{align*}
Multiplying~\eqref{eq:Lyapunov-E} on the left and right by $C_E^{-1}$ gives
\begin{equation*}
 C_E^{-1}K_E+K_EC_E^{-1}
 =C_E^{-1}R_E^TR_EC_E^{-1}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, with $D_E:=\mathcal A_E^{-1}C_E^{-1}$,
\begin{equation*}
 B_E'=-D_ER_E^TR_ED_E^T.
 \label{eq:B-derivative}
\end{equation*}
Differentiating $P_E=B_E^{-1}$ now gives
\begin{equation}
 P_E'=-P_EB_E'P_E
      =P_ED_ER_E^TR_ED_E^TP_E.
 \label{eq:Pprime-intermediate}
\end{equation}
It remains to identify the two outer factors.  From
\begin{align*}
 D_ET_E^{-1}
 &=\mathcal A_E^{-1}C_E^{-1}
   (\mathcal A_E^{-1}+cC_E\mathcal A_EC_E)\\
 &=\mathcal A_E^{-1}C_E^{-1}\mathcal A_E^{-1}+cC_E
 =B_E,
\end{align*}
we get $P_ED_E=T_E$.  Similarly,
\begin{align*}
 T_E^{-1}D_E^T
 &=(\mathcal A_E^{-1}+cC_E\mathcal A_EC_E)
   C_E^{-1}\mathcal A_E^{-1}\\
 &=\mathcal A_E^{-1}C_E^{-1}\mathcal A_E^{-1}+cC_E
 =B_E,
\end{align*}
so $D_E^TP_E=T_E$.  Substituting both identities into
\eqref{eq:Pprime-intermediate} yields
$
 P_E'=T_ER_E^TR_ET_E.
$
Finally, by~\eqref{eq:U-via-T},
\[
 U_a^2
 =R_ET_ER_E^TR_ET_ER_E^T
 =R_EP_E'R_E^T
 =\frac{d}{dx}(R_EP_ER_E^T),
\]
which proves~\eqref{eq:exact-derivative}.

Factoring $P_E^{-1}$ on both sides gives
\begin{align}
 P_E^{-1}
 &=\mathcal A_E^{-1}
   (C_E^{-1}+c\mathcal A_EC_E\mathcal A_E)
   \mathcal A_E^{-1},\notag\\
 P_E
 &=\mathcal A_E
   (C_E^{-1}+c\mathcal A_EC_E\mathcal A_E)^{-1}
   \mathcal A_E.
 \label{eq:P-minus-factor}
\end{align}
As $x\to-\infty$, every entry of $\mathcal A_E(x)$ tends to zero and the
middle inverse in~\eqref{eq:P-minus-factor} tends to $C_E$.  Hence the product
tends to zero.

As $x\to+\infty$, every nonzero entry of $\mathcal A_E$ grows exponentially,
whereas every entry of $\mathcal A_E^{-1}$ decays exponentially.  It follows
directly from~\eqref{eq:T-def} that
\[
 P_E^{-1}
 =\mathcal A_E^{-1}C_E^{-1}\mathcal A_E^{-1}+cC_E
 \longrightarrow cC_E.
\]
Since $C_E$ is positive definite and hence invertible, inversion is continuous
near $cC_E$, giving $P_E\to c^{-1}C_E^{-1}$.

For every solution in the noncollision determinant family,

For positive $p\ne q$, define
\begin{equation*}
 C(p,q):=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 (2p)^{-1}&(p+q)^{-1}\\
 (p+q)^{-1}&(2q)^{-1}
 \end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:Cpq}
\end{equation*}
Its determinant is
\begin{align*}
 \det C(p,q)
 &=\frac1{4pq}-\frac1{(p+q)^2}=\frac{(p+q)^2-4pq}{4pq(p+q)^2}
 =\frac{(p-q)^2}{4pq(p+q)^2}>0.
 \label{eq:Cpq-det}
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
 C(p,q)^{-1}
 =\frac1{\det C(p,q)}
 \begin{pmatrix}
 (2q)^{-1}&-(p+q)^{-1}\\
 -(p+q)^{-1}&(2p)^{-1}
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
 (1,1)C(p,q)^{-1}(1,1)^T
 &=\frac{\dfrac1{2p}+\dfrac1{2q}-\dfrac2{p+q}}
 {\dfrac{(p-q)^2}{4pq(p+q)^2}}=\frac{\dfrac{(p+q)^2-4pq}{2pq(p+q)}}
 {\dfrac{(p-q)^2}{4pq(p+q)^2}}=\frac{\dfrac{(p-q)^2}{2pq(p+q)}}
 {\dfrac{(p-q)^2}{4pq(p+q)^2}}
 =2(p+q).
\end{align*}
If only the first coordinate is active, the restricted matrix is
$[(2p)^{-1}]$ and its inverse contributes $2p$; if only the second coordinate
is active, the contribution is $2q$; if neither is active, the contribution is
zero.

In~\eqref{M1}, the first row of $R$ selects the block
$C(\eta_1,\eta_0)$ and the second row selects
$C(\eta_0,\eta_{-1})$.  Applying the preceding three possibilities to each
block in~\eqref{eq:J-nonzero-lambda} gives
\begin{equation*}
 J_{11}=\frac2c(\eta_1\varepsilon_1+\eta_0\varepsilon_0),
 \
 J_{22}=\frac2c(\eta_0\varepsilon_0+\eta_{-1}\varepsilon_{-1}).
 \label{eq:J-diagonal}
\end{equation*}
Together with~\eqref{eq:NM-from-J}, this complete the  proof of the Proposition \ref{prop:mass-formula}.
