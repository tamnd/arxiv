<!-- head-1: body_md -->
Compared with PIR, PIWR (i) uses all samples, including the end points;
(ii) averages the noise in the integrals instead of amplifying it in a difference quotient; and (iii) needs to
resolve only the \emph{area} under a sharp outbreak, not its slope. Its error still depends on how well the
sampled library resolves the dynamics. It also inherits an errors-in-variables bias, because the library is evaluated
at noisy states.

<!-- head-2: body_md -->
\Cref{tab:tv} compares the errors with the PINN results of \citet[Tables~2--4]{Millevoi2024PINN}
(mean of 10 training runs; ours are medians over 100 noise realizations). In Cases~1--3 PIR and PIWR are more accurate
than both PINN variants for every quantity, mostly by one to two orders of magnitude (the one exception is the
truncation-limited PIR-FD2 estimate of the constant $\beta$), in milliseconds instead of
12--37 minutes of training. The constant $\beta$ of Case~1 is recovered to $6\cdot 10^{-5}$ (PIR-FD6) and
$1\cdot 10^{-4}$ (PIWR), against $3.6\cdot 10^{-4}$ for the better PINN. For the
time-varying cases the full-period error, $0.27$--$0.70$ for the PINNs because they fail in the first weeks, drops
to $0.02$--$0.07$, and after day 20 PIWR reaches $5.0\cdot 10^{-3}$ for $\beta(t)$ and
$2.7\cdot 10^{-3}$ for $\mathcal R_t$, against $1.6\cdot 10^{-2}$ and $6.7\cdot 10^{-2}$ for the better PINN. PIWR
is the most accurate estimator after day 20 in Cases~2 and 3, and it fits the infected curve to $2\cdot 10^{-3}$ and
$5\cdot 10^{-4}$. In Case~4 the $40\,\%$ errors dominate. PIR-FD6 is the most accurate there, with
$\mathcal R_t$ errors of $0.17$ over the whole period and $0.18$ after day 20, against $0.47$--$0.50$ and
$0.31$--$0.33$ for the PINNs. PIWR is comparable
with the PINN ($0.34$ and $0.39$). In the weak form each equation contains one raw sample with its $40\,\%$ error,
while the finite-difference rows, together with the smoothness penalty, average over neighbouring days.
