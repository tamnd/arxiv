\label{lem:supset}
If a YBO $R$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda$, then $R_{(j)}= {\mathbb 1}^{\otimes (j-1)} \otimes R \otimes {\mathbb 1}^{\otimes (n-j -1)} $ on $V^{\otimes n}$ 
has an eigenvalue $\lambda$ for all $j=1, \ldots, n$.
Moreover, if $E_\lambda$ is the eigenspace of $\lambda$ for $R$, 
the eigenspace  $E(R_{(j)}, \lambda)$ of $\lambda$ for $R_{(j)}$ contains
\begin{equation}
E_{(j, \lambda)} := V^{\otimes (j-1)} \otimes E_\lambda  \otimes V^{\otimes (n-j-1)}. \label{eqn:Elambda}
\end{equation}
Similarly, for the inverse  the eigenspace $E(R_{(j)}^{-1}, \lambda^{-1})$ of $R_{(j)}^{-1}$  contains 
\begin{equation}
E^{-1}_{(j, \lambda)} := V^{\otimes (j-1)} \otimes E_\lambda^{-1}  \otimes V^{\otimes (n-j-1)}. \label{eqn:Einv}
\end{equation}
That is, $E(R_{(j)}, \lambda ) \supset  E_{(j, \lambda)}$ and 
$E(R_{(j)}^{-1}, \lambda^{-1} ) \supset E^{-1}_{(j, \lambda)}$. 

Moreover, if $V$ is a flat $\mathbb k$-module, and $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $R$, then $E(R_{(j)}, \lambda ) =  E_{(j, \lambda)}$ and $E(R_{(j)}^{-1}, \lambda^{-1} ) = E^{-1}_{(j, \lambda)}$. In this situation, if $R$ is diagonalizable, the follwing eigenspace decomposition             
holds: $V^{\otimes n} = \bigoplus_{\lambda \in {\rm spec}(R)} E_{(j,\lambda)}$, where ${\rm spec}(R)$ indicates the spectrum of $R$.

Let $v$ be an eigenvector of $R$ for $\lambda$, so that $R v=\lambda v$.
Then for any $u \in V^{\otimes (j-1)} $ and $w \in V^{\otimes (n-j-1)} $,
we have $R_{(j)} (u \otimes v \otimes w)=\lambda u \otimes v \otimes w$, hence $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of 
$R_{(j)}$ with an eigenvector $u \otimes v \otimes w$.
We have that $u \otimes v \otimes w \in E_{(j, \lambda)}$, hence the statement follows.
The case for the inverse is similar. 


Let $R$ have an  eigenvalue $\lambda$ over a flat $\mathbb k$-module $V$. The tensor product $V\otimes V$ is flat over $\mathbb k$ as well. It follows that the right and left tensor product functors by $V$ are exact. The eigenspace $E_\lambda$ fits into the shorth exact sequence 
$$
\begin{tikzcd}
	0\arrow[rr] && E_\lambda \arrow[rr] && V\otimes V\arrow[rr,"R-\lambda \mathbb 1^{\otimes 2}"] && V\otimes V. 
\end{tikzcd}
$$
Tensoring on the left, and using the exactness of the tensor product functor, we obtain the short exact sequence
$$
\begin{tikzcd}
0\arrow[rr] && V \otimes E_\lambda \arrow[rr] && V\otimes V\otimes V\arrow[rr,"\mathbb 1\otimes R-\lambda \mathbb 1^{\otimes 3}"] && V \otimes V\otimes V, 
\end{tikzcd}
$$
which shows that $E(\mathbb 1\otimes R,\lambda) = \ker(\mathbb 1\otimes R-\lambda \mathbb 1^{\otimes 3}) = V\otimes \ker(R-\lambda \mathbb 1^{\otimes 2}) = V\otimes E_\lambda$. A similar procedure by tensoring on the right shows that $E(R\otimes \mathbb 1,\lambda) = E_\lambda\otimes V$. Iterating this process shows that more generally $E(R_{(j)},\lambda) = E_{(j,\lambda)}$. An analogous reasoning can be applied to the case of inverses.

\begin{eqnarray*}
		V^{\otimes n} &=& V^{\otimes (j-1)} \otimes (\oplus_{\lambda \in {\rm spec}(R)} E_\lambda) \otimes V^{\otimes (n-j-1)}\\

		&=& \bigoplus_{\lambda \in {\rm spec}(R)} E_{(j,\lambda)}
\end{eqnarray*}
  
as desired.

{\rm

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:sfc_braid}
The spaces $E(\vec{B}, \lambda)$ and $E_{(\vec{B}, \lambda)}$ are invariants of surface braids, 
which we call {\emph the Yang-Baxter (YB) eigenspace invariant}. 
Therefore we also write $E(S, \lambda)$ and  $E_{(S, \lambda)}$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
It is known~\cite{Kamada:SB} that equivalent surface braids have slide equivalent braid systems. Hence we show that the eigenspaces are invariant under slide equivalence. 
			Set $\vec{b}=(b_1, \ldots, b_m)$
		and let
		\begin{eqnarray*}
				\vec{b}' = (b_1', \ldots, b_m')= (b_1 , \ldots, b_{i-1}, b_i b_{i+1} b_i^{-1} , b_i, b_{i+2}, \ldots, b_m). 
		\end{eqnarray*}
		 For a representation $\rho$ derived from a YBO $R$, let $B_i$ and $B_i'$ denote $\rho(b_i)$ and $\rho(b_i')$ as before. 
				We  
		show that each vector $v\in E(\vec{B}, \lambda)$ is a $\lambda$ eigenvector for each $B_k'$, 
		and similarly, 
		that each vector in $v\in E(\vec{B}', \lambda)$ is a $\lambda$ eigenvector for each $B_k$.          
		Let $v \in E(\vec{B}, \lambda)$. By definition, $v$ is a $\lambda$ eigenvector of $B_k$ for each $k=1, \ldots, m$. It follows directly by definition of $\vec B'$ that $v$ is an eigenvector of $B'_k$ for $k=1,\ldots, i-1, i+1, \ldots , m$. For $k=i$, we have that 
		\begin{eqnarray*}
				B'_iv =  B_i B_{i+1} B_i^{-1}v = \frac{1}{\lambda}B_i B_{i+1}v = B_iv = \lambda v. 
		\end{eqnarray*}
		This shows the inclusion $E(\vec{B}, \lambda) \subset E(\vec{B}', \lambda)$. To show the reverse inclusion, consider $v\in E(\vec{B}', \lambda)$. Then $v$ is a $\lambda$ eigenvector of $B'_k$ for all $k$. It follows that $v$ is a $\lambda$ eigenvector of $B_k$ for $k=1,\ldots ,i, i+2,\ldots, m$. For $k=i+1$, since $B_iB_{i+1}B^{-1}_iv = \lambda v$, we have that $B^{-1}_iv$ is a $\lambda$ eigenvector of $B_{i+1}$. Since $B_i = B'_{i+1}$ by assumption , it follows that $v$ is a $\lambda$ eigenvector of $B_i$, and therefore a $\lambda^{-1}$ eigenvector of $B^{-1}_i$. Therefore, $\frac{1}{\lambda}B_{i+1} v = v$, from which it follows that $E(\vec{B}', \lambda) \subset E(\vec{B}, \lambda)$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:fundQ}
Let $S$ be a surface braid with the set of black vertices $\Sigma=(y_1, \ldots, y_m)$.
Let $\vec{b}=(b_1, \ldots, b_m)$ be its braid system.
Then the fundamental quandle of $S$ is generated by $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ with relations
$b_i (x_1, \ldots, x_n)=(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, $i=1, \ldots, m$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Recall the notation
$$\vec{b}=(b_1, \ldots, b_m)=(w_1^{-1} \sigma^{\epsilon_1}_{k_1} w_1, \ldots, w_m^{-1} \sigma^{\epsilon_m}_{k_m} w_m ). $$
In \cite{CJKLS}, using \cite{Kamada:SB}, the presentation of the fundamental quandle is given by generators 
$x_1, \ldots, x_n$ and relations 
$[ w_i (x_1, \ldots, x_n) ]_{k_i} = [ w_{i} ( x_1, \ldots, x_n)]_{k_{i}+1}$, 
$i=1, \ldots, n$, where the subscript $k_i$ indicates $k_i$-th term of an $n$-tuple. 
Set $(x_1', \ldots, x_n')=w_i (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$. Then the relations are written as 
$x_{k_i}' = x_{k_i +1}'$. 
A small circle around $y_i$ intersects an arc of the chart of $S$ labeled $x_{k_i}$,
and the corresponding monodromy is 
$$
\sigma_{k_i} (x_1', \ldots, x_n') = (x_1', \ldots, x_{k_i-1}', x_{k_{i+1}}' , x_{k_i}' * x_{k_{i+1}}', x_{k_i+2}', \ldots, x_n') ,
$$ 
where the two sheets around a branch point receive the same label, resulting in 
the relation $ x_{k_i}'=x_{k_{i} +1}'$. 
Hence this relation corresponds to $\sigma_{k_i} (x_1', \ldots, x_n') =(x_1', \ldots, x_n') $.
Thus the relation 
$[ w_i (x_1, \ldots, x_n) ]_{k_i} = [ w_{i} ( x_1, \ldots, x_n)]_{k_{i}+1}$ 
 can be rewritten as 
$(w_i^{-1} \sigma_{k_i} w_i )  (x_1, \ldots, x_n)= (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, that is,
$b_i  (x_1, \ldots, x_n)= (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, as desired.

Let $\theta \in Z^3_{\rm Q}(X, \mathbb k)$, where the abelian group of $\mathbb k$ is used as the coefficient of quandle cohomology group of $X$.

$$ 
\psi (e_x \otimes e_y \otimes e_z) :=
\theta(x,y,z)\  e_z \otimes e_{y*z} \otimes e_{ (x*y)*z }, $$
and linearly extended. 
Then $\psi $ is a YB 3-cocycle, $\psi \in Z^3_{\rm YB} (V,V)$.

\lefteqn{ 
\theta(x,y,z)+ \theta(x*z, y*z, w) + \theta(x, z, w) 
} \\
& = & 
\theta(x*y, z,w)+ \theta(x,y,w) + \theta(x*w,y*w,z*w) .

To show that $\mathop{\text{rank}}_\Sigma(\hat S)$ is a well defined invariant of knotted surfaces, it is sufficient to show its invariance under slide equivalence, conjugation and (de)stabilization with respect to the braid system.  By Lemma~\ref{lem:admissible}, $R$ is given by a set-theoretic YBO $\bar R$,  and a YB $2$-cocycle $\sigma$ satisfying the stated properties. 
			
			Suppose that $\vec b' = (b_1, \ldots, b_ib_{i+1} b_i^{-1}, b_i, \ldots, b_m)$ is obtained from $\vec b$ by applying a slide move. As shown in Theorem~\ref{thm:sfc_braid}, we have the equality $E(\vec B',1) = E(\vec B,1)$, and therefore in particular the equality $\Sigma(V^{\otimes n})\cap E(\vec B',1) = \Sigma(V^{\otimes n})\cap E(\vec B,1)$ holds. 
			
			Let us consider the case where $\vec b'$ is obtained from $\vec b$ by a stabilization, which in turn means that $\vec b$ is obtained from $\vec b'$ by a destabilization. By Lemma~\ref{lem:ses}, we have that 
			$$E(\vec B',1) = [E(\vec B, 1)\otimes V] \cap [V^{\otimes (n-1)}\otimes (\ker (\mathbb 1-R))].$$
			Let $e_{x_1}\otimes \cdots \otimes e_{x_n}$ denote an element of $\Sigma(V^{\otimes n})\cap E(\vec b,1)$. Since $R$ is $k$-stabilizing, there exist $s(x_n,j)\in X$, for $j=1, \ldots, k$, such that $e_{x_1}\otimes \cdots \otimes  e_{x_n}\otimes e_{s(x_n,j)}$ is in  $\Sigma(V^{\otimes {n+1}})\cap E(\vec B',1)$. Conversely, if $e_{x_1}\otimes \cdots \otimes  e_{x_n}\otimes e_{x_{n+1}}$ is an element of $\Sigma(V^{\otimes {n+1}})\cap E(\vec B,1)$, from the same equality  
			 it follows that $e_{x_n}\otimes e_{x_{n+1}}$ is a solution to the equation $R(e_{x_n}\otimes e_y) = e_{x_n}\otimes e_y$ which gives $x_{n+1} = s(x_n,j)$ for some $j$, since $R$ is $k$-stabilizing. Therefore the number of elements in $\Sigma(V^{\otimes n})\cap E(\vec B,1)$ and
			 that in  $\Sigma(V^{\otimes {n+1}})\cap E(\vec B',1)$ are proportional by $k$,
              $$
              \sharp \Sigma(V^{\otimes {n+1}})\cap E(\vec B',1)
             = k \ \sharp \Sigma(V^{\otimes {n+1}})\cap E(\vec B,1).
             $$
             Since $\mathop{\text{rank}}_\Sigma(\hat S)$ is normalized by $k^n$ and $n$ increases by $1$, it follows that the simple rank is invariant:
        	$${\rm rank}_\Sigma (\hat S)=
             \frac{1}{k^{n+1}}\sharp (\Sigma(V^{\otimes {n+1}})\cap E(\vec B',1))=\frac{1}{k^{n+1}}\sharp (\Sigma(V^{\otimes {n}})\cap E(\vec B,1))
             ={\rm rank}_\Sigma ( S)
             $$
             as desired.
			
			Let $\vec b'$ be obtained from $\vec b$ by a conjugation as $\vec b' = (\beta^{-1} b_1\beta,\ldots, \beta^{-1} b_n\beta)$, where $\beta$ is an $m$-braid. Then, applying Lemma~\ref{lem:conjeigen} we have $E(\vec b',1) = \rho(\beta^{-1})(E(\vec b,1))$. 
            
            Since $R$ maps simple tensors to simple tensors, so does $\rho (\beta)$. In particular, we have 
            $$\rho (\beta^{-1})(e_{x_1}\otimes \cdots \otimes e_{x_n}) = \alpha(x_1,\ldots, x_n) e_{y_1}\otimes \cdots \otimes e_{y_n}$$ for some nonzero $\alpha(x_1,\ldots, x_n)\in \mathbb k$ determined by the YB 2-cocycle           
			$\sigma$, and some $y_1, \ldots, y_n\in X$. Therefore we have a $1-1$ correspondence between  elements of $\Sigma(V^{\otimes n})\cap E(\vec B,1)$ and elements of $\Sigma(V^{\otimes n})\cap E(\vec B',1)$. This completes the proof.

The definition of the simple rank mimics the coloring invariant of discrete structures such as biquandles and their variants. This attempt is to resolve the difference that occurs under stabilization to the number of simple tensors. Potential invariants beyond discrete cases may be expected, for example, from the construction of YBOs from linearlized ones by YB $2$-cocycles. 
Computations of Yang-Baxter $2$-cocycles, however,  have not been fully developed, and are desirable. 
}
