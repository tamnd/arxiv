\label{maintheorem}
For every $a\in(0,\frac12)$, the coefficient $I_a$ in \eqref{coefficient} satisfies the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{fluxidentity}
I_a=a(1-a)T_a>0,
\end{equation}
and
\[\frac{{\mathrm{d}} T_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}>0,\qquad \frac{{\mathrm{d}} I_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}>0, \qquad \frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} a}\bigl(2T_aI_a\bigr)>0.
\]
In particular, the maps
\[
T_a:\Big(0,\frac12\Big)\rightarrow(2,\pi),\qquad I_a:\Big(0,\frac12\Big) \rightarrow\Big(0,\frac{\pi}{4}\Big),\qquad 2T_aI_a:\Big(0,\frac12\Big)
\rightarrow\Big(0,\frac{\pi^2}{2}\Big)
\]
are strictly increasing bijections.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The identity \eqref{fluxidentity} gives an exact geometric interpretation of the coefficient $I_a$ arising in the scalar solvability condition by expressing it as the product of the axial period and the normalised Delaunay flux. Indeed, with the positive axial orientation and the convention $H\equiv2$, the axial flux across any circular section is given by
\[
\mathcal F_a\mathrel{\mathop:}= 2\pi\bigg(\frac{f_a}{\sqrt{1+(f_a')^2}}-f_a^2\bigg)=2\pi a(1-a),
\]
thus the constant $c=a(1-a)$ in \eqref{conservedquantity} is the normalised axial flux $\frac{\mathcal F_a}{2\pi}$, and the identity $I_a=cT_a$ takes the form
\[
I_a=\frac{T_a}{2\pi}\mathcal F_a.
\]
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The factorisation $T_aI_a=a(1-a)T_a^2$ reduces the conjectured monotonicity of $T_aI_a$ to showing that $\frac{{\mathrm{d}} T_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}>0$. Together with the volume monotonicity $\frac{{\mathrm{d}} V_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}<0$ established in Proposition \ref{volumemonotonicity}, this allows the prescribed-mass argument of \cite{dPMZ} to be applied near any fixed $a_0\in(0,\frac12)$, yielding equilibria of every sufficiently large mass $m$ with reference necks $a_m$ satisfying $a_m\to a_0$ as $m\to\infty$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{pointwiseidentity}
Fix $a\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)$. Then, for every $s\in\mathbb R$ it holds
\begin{equation}\label{fluxidentitypointwise}
\Phi_a(s)=2c+\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} s}\left(\frac{f^2f'}{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{exactidentity}
I_a=cT_a=a(1-a)T_a>0.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We first note that the structure of \eqref{defphialpha} suggests differentiating
$\frac{f^2f'}{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}$, whose derivative contains a term involving $\frac{f''}{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{5}{2}}}$. More precisely, a direct calculation gives
\[
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} s}\left(\frac{f^2f'}{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
\right)=\frac{2f(f')^2\left(1+(f')^2\right)+
f^2f''\left(1-2(f')^2\right)}{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{5}{2}}},
\]
which we combine with \eqref{defphialpha} to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{firstidentitylemma1}
\Phi_a-\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} s}\left(\frac{f^2f'}{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
\right)=\frac{f\left(ff''+1+(f')^2\right)}
{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.
\end{equation}
On the other hand, multiplying \eqref{profileequation} by $f\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}$ yields $
ff''=1+(f')^2-2f\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}$, hence substituting into \eqref{firstidentitylemma1} one finds
\[\Phi_a-\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} s}\left(\frac{f^2f'}{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
\right)=2\frac{f}{\sqrt{1+(f')^2}}-2f^2.\]
The conserved quantity \eqref{conservedquantity} implies that the right-hand side of the above expression is equal to $2c=2a(1-a)$, which proves \eqref{fluxidentitypointwise}.

To conclude, we integrate \eqref{fluxidentitypointwise} over $\left[0,\frac{T_a}{2}\right]$ to obtain
\[I_a=cT_a+\left[\frac{f^2f'}{\left(1+(f')^2\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
\right]_{0}^{\frac{T_a}{2}}=a(1-a)T_a>0,\]
where we have used that $s=0$ and $s=\frac{T_a}{2}$ correspond to a bulge and its adjacent neck, respectively, hence
$f'(0)=f'\left(\frac{T_a}{2}\right)=0$.

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{arclengthrepresentation}
Let $a\in(0,\frac12)$, $b=1-a$ and $c=ab$. For all $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}$, the radial profile in \eqref{rhodefinition} satisfies
\begin{equation*}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^2}{{\mathrm{d}}\sigma^2}\bigl(\rho_a(\sigma)^2\bigr)+4\rho_a(\sigma)^2= 2(a^2+b^2),
\end{equation*}
and hence
\begin{equation}\label{rhoexplicit}
\rho_a(\sigma)^2=\frac{a^2+b^2}{2} +\frac{b^2-a^2}{2}\cos(2\sigma).
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\sigma=0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi$ correspond to a bulge, the adjacent neck, and the next bulge, respectively, with
\[s_a\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)=\frac{T_a}{2},\quad s_a(\pi)=T_a,\]
thus a full axial period $T_a$ corresponds to the fixed arclength interval $[0,\pi]$, independently of $a$.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We first derive the equation satisfied by $\rho_a(\sigma)^2=f_a(s_a(\sigma))^2$. Differentiating 
\eqref{arclengthderivatives} with respect to $\sigma$ and using the chain rule we find
\[
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^2\rho_a}{{\mathrm{d}}\sigma^2}(\sigma)=\frac{f_a''(s_a(\sigma))}
{\left(1+\bigl(f_a'(s_a(\sigma))\bigr)^2\right)^2},
\]
which further yields
\begin{equation}\label{rho2second}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^2}{{\mathrm{d}}\sigma^2}\bigl(\rho_a^2\bigr)=\frac{2\bigl(f_a'(s_a(\sigma))\bigr)^2}
{1+\bigl(f_a'(s_a(\sigma))\bigr)^2}+\frac{2f_a(s_a(\sigma))f_a''(s_a(\sigma))}
{\left(1+\bigl(f_a'(s_a(\sigma))\bigr)^2\right)^2}.
\end{equation}
We now evaluate the Delaunay equation \eqref{profileequation} at
$s=s_a(\sigma)$ and multiply by $
\frac{f_a(s_a(\sigma))}
{\sqrt{1+\bigl(f_a'(s_a(\sigma))\bigr)^2}}$ to get
\[\frac{f_a(s_a(\sigma))f_a''(s_a(\sigma))}
{\left(1+\bigl(f_a'(s_a(\sigma))\bigr)^2\right)^2}=
\frac{1}{1+\bigl(f_a'(s_a(\sigma))\bigr)^2}-
2\frac{f_a(s_a(\sigma))}
{\sqrt{1+\bigl(f_a'(s_a(\sigma))\bigr)^2}}.\]
Substituting the above into \eqref{rho2second} and then using \eqref{conservedquantity} once more, we arrive at the equation
\begin{equation}\label{oscequation}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^2}{{\mathrm{d}}\sigma^2}\bigl(\rho_a^2\bigr)+4\rho_a^2=2-4c=2(a^2+b^2),
\end{equation}
where we have used that $a+b=1$ and $c=ab.$ Since the initial conditions are $\rho_{a}(0)^2=b^2$ and $\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} \sigma}(\rho_{a}^2)(0)=0,$ the solution of  \eqref{oscequation} reads
\[\rho_a(\sigma)^2=\frac{a^2+b^2}{2}+\frac{b^2-a^2}{2}\cos(2\sigma).\]
Finally, in order to identify the corresponding axial coordinates we
differentiate \eqref{rhoexplicit} to get
\begin{equation}\label{derivrhosquared}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\sigma}\bigl(\rho_a(\sigma)^2\bigr)=2\rho_a(\sigma)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\rho_a}{{\mathrm{d}}\sigma}(\sigma)=-(b^2-a^2)\sin(2\sigma),
\end{equation}
hence using that $\rho_a>0$ and $b>a$, \eqref{derivrhosquared} and
\eqref{rhoexplicit} show that $\rho_a$ strictly decreases 
from $b$ to $a$ on $[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]$ and then strictly increases from $a$ to $b$ on $[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi]$. Recalling \eqref{rhodefinition} and using that $s_a$ is strictly increasing with $s_a(0)=0$, we deduce that
$\sigma=\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\sigma=\pi$ correspond to the first neck and the next bulge after $s=0$, respectively, where we conclude that
\[
s_a\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\frac{T_a}{2},\quad s_a(\pi)=T_a.\]

<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{coefficientmonotonicity}
For every $a\in(0,\frac{1}{2})$, it holds
\[
\frac{{\mathrm{d}} T_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}>0, \qquad \frac{{\mathrm{d}} I_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}>0, \qquad \frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} a}\bigl(2T_aI_a\bigr)>0,
\]
and the maps
\[
T_a:\Big(0,\frac{1}{2}\Big)\rightarrow(2,\pi),
\qquad
I_a:\Big(0,\frac{1}{2}\Big)
\rightarrow\Big(0,\frac{\pi}{4}\Big),
\qquad
2T_aI_a:\Big(0,\frac{1}{2}\Big)
\rightarrow\left(0,\frac{\pi^2}{2}\right)
\]
are strictly increasing bijections.
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
As a preliminary step, we define
\[\delta\mathrel{\mathop:}= b-a\in(0,1),\]
so that in view of $a+b=1$ and $c=ab$, we have
\[
a=\frac{1-\delta}{2},\qquad
b=\frac{1+\delta}{2},\qquad
c=\frac{1-\delta^2}{4}.
\]
Invoking Proposition \ref{arclengthrepresentation}, the identity in
\eqref{saderivative}, and the properties
$s_a(0)=0$ and $s_a(\pi)=T_a$, we write
\begin{equation}\label{perioddelta}
T_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}=\int_0^\pi F_\delta(\sigma)\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma,
\quad \mbox{with}\quad F_\delta(\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop:}=
\frac{1+\delta\cos(2\sigma)}
{\sqrt{1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)}}.
\end{equation}
To prove the strict monotonicity of $T_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}$, we fix
$\delta_0\in(0,1)$ and choose $\eta>0$ sufficiently small so that
$[\delta_0-\eta,\delta_0+\eta]\subset(0,1)$. For every $\delta$
in this interval and $\sigma\in[0,\pi]$ it holds
\[
1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)=(1-\delta)^2+4\delta\cos^2\sigma
\geq(1-\delta_0-\eta)^2>0,
\]
thus $F_\delta(\sigma)$ and its derivative with respect
to $\delta$ are uniformly bounded, while differentiation
under the integral sign in \eqref{perioddelta} and the chain rule give
\begin{equation}\label{periodderivative}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\delta}T_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}=-\delta\int_0^\pi
\frac{\sin^2(2\sigma)}
{\left(1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)\right)^{\frac32}}
\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma<0, \quad \frac{{\mathrm{d}} T_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}=
-2\Big.
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\delta}T_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}
\Big|_{\delta=1-2a}>0.
\end{equation}
To determine the range of $T_a$, we first observe that the integrand in \eqref{perioddelta} satisfies $0<F_\delta(\sigma)\leq1$ for every $\delta\in(0,1)$ and
$\sigma\in[0,\pi]$, while one can additionally verify that
\[\begin{aligned}
&F_\delta(\sigma)\rightarrow1 \quad\text{as}\quad \delta\to0^+
\quad\text{for every }\sigma\in[0,\pi],\\
&F_\delta(\sigma)\rightarrow|\cos\sigma|
\quad\text{as}\quad \delta\to1^- \quad\text{for a.e. }\sigma\in[0,\pi].
\end{aligned}\]
As a result, dominated convergence in \eqref{perioddelta} together with $\delta=1-2a$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{endpointsTa}
\lim_{a\to\frac12^-}T_a=\pi,
\qquad
\lim_{a\to0^+}T_a=2,
\end{equation}
where the strict monotonicity also provides
the desired bijection for $T_a$. Finally, using \eqref{exactidentity} and \eqref{periodderivative} we directly obtain 
\[\begin{aligned}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}} I_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}&=(1-2a)T_a+a(1-a)\frac{{\mathrm{d}} T_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}>0,\\
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} a}\bigl(2T_aI_a\bigr)&=
2(1-2a)T_a^2+4a(1-a)T_a\frac{{\mathrm{d}} T_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}>0. 
\end{aligned}\] 
Likewise, the endpoint limits for $T_a$ in \eqref{endpointsTa} yield
\begin{equation} \label{limitsinproof}
\lim_{a\to0^+}I_a=0,
\qquad
\lim_{a\to\frac12^-}I_a=\frac{\pi}{4}, \qquad \lim_{a\to0^+}2T_aI_a=0,
\qquad
\lim_{a\to\frac12^-}2T_aI_a=\frac{\pi^2}{2},
\end{equation}
while the remaining two bijections for $I_a$ and $2T_aI_a$ follow from the established strict monotonicity.

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{volumemonotonicity}
For every \(a\in(0,\frac12)\), the volume and the surface area of one period in \eqref{periodgeometry} satisfy the exact relation
\begin{equation}\label{areaidentity}
A_a=3V_a+\pi I_a.
\end{equation}
Moreover, both \(V_a\) and \(A_a\) are strictly decreasing functions of \(a\), and their endpoint limits are
\begin{equation}\label{volumearealimits}
\lim_{a\to0^+}V_a=\frac{4\pi}{3},\qquad
\lim_{a\to\frac12^-}V_a=\frac{\pi^2}{4},\qquad
\lim_{a\to0^+}A_a=4\pi,\qquad
\lim_{a\to\frac12^-}A_a=\pi^2.
\end{equation}
In particular, \(a\mapsto V_a\) is a bijection onto $\left(\frac{\pi^2}{4},\frac{4\pi}{3}\right),$ while 
$a\mapsto\frac{2T_aI_a}{V_a}$ and $a\mapsto\frac{A_a}{V_a}$ are strictly increasing, with endpoint limits
\begin{equation}\label{quotientlimits}
\lim_{a\to0^+}\frac{2T_aI_a}{V_a}=0,\qquad
\lim_{a\to\frac12^-}\frac{2T_aI_a}{V_a}=2,\qquad
\lim_{a\to0^+}\frac{A_a}{V_a}=3,\qquad
\lim_{a\to\frac12^-}\frac{A_a}{V_a}=4.
\end{equation}
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
As a first step, we recall that $b=1-a,\,c=ab$ and use \eqref{profileequation} and \eqref{conservedquantity} to obtain the three identities
\begin{equation}\label{threeuseful}
\sqrt{1+(f_a')^2}=\frac{f_a}{f_a^2+c},
\qquad (f_a')^2=\frac{f_a^2}{(f_a^2+c)^2}-1, \qquad f_a''=\frac{f_a(c-f_a^2)}{(f_a^2+c)^3}.
\end{equation}
Next, using \eqref{threeuseful} we compute
\begin{equation}\label{areaexactderivative}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} s}\left(f_a(f_a^2+c)f_a'\right)=2f_a\sqrt{1+(f_a')^2}-3f_a^2-c,
\end{equation}
so that integrating \eqref{areaexactderivative} over
$[-\frac{T_a}{2},\frac{T_a}{2}]$ and multiplying by $\pi$ gives \eqref{areaidentity}, where we have used that $f_a'\left(-\frac{T_a}{2}\right)=f_a'\left(\frac{T_a}{2}\right)=0$ together with \eqref{periodgeometry} and \eqref{exactidentity}.

To proceed, setting again $\delta= b-a=1-2a\in(0,1)$, we use the arclength coordinate of \eqref{arclengthdefinition} in combination with \eqref{rhodefinition}, \eqref{arclengthderivatives} and Proposition \ref{arclengthrepresentation} to write  
\begin{equation} \label{transformedvolumeint}
V_a=\pi\int_0^{T_a}f_a(s)^2\,{\mathrm{d}} s=\pi\int_0^\pi\rho_a(\sigma)^2
\frac{{\mathrm{d}} s_a}{{\mathrm{d}}\sigma}(\sigma)\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma,
\end{equation}
which takes the equivalent form
\begin{equation}\label{volumedelta}
V_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}=\frac{\pi}{4}\int_0^\pi
\bigl(1+\delta\cos(2\sigma)\bigr)
\sqrt{1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)}
\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma.
\end{equation}
For $\delta$ in any compact subinterval of $(0,1)$, the integrand
in \eqref{volumedelta} and its derivative with respect to $\delta$
are uniformly bounded for $\sigma \in[0,\pi]$, hence differentiation under the
integral sign gives
\begin{equation} \label{derivdeltavolume}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{4}{\pi}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\delta}V_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}&=\int_0^\pi
\bigg[\cos(2\sigma)\sqrt{1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)}
+\frac{\bigl(1+\delta\cos(2\sigma)\bigr)
\bigl(\delta+\cos(2\sigma)\bigr)}
{\sqrt{1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)}}\bigg]{\mathrm{d}}\sigma\\
&=\delta\int_0^\pi\frac{(1+\delta^2)\bigl(1+\cos^2(2\sigma)\bigr)+4\delta\cos(2\sigma)}{\bigl(1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)\bigr)^{\frac32}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma>0,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where we integrated by parts and used that 
\[(1+\delta^2)\bigl(1+\cos^2(2\sigma)\bigr)
+4\delta\cos(2\sigma)=(1+\delta^2)\Big(\cos(2\sigma)+\frac{2\delta}{1+\delta^2}
\Big)^2+\frac{(1-\delta^2)^2}{1+\delta^2}>0.\]
As a consequence, one finds 
\begin{equation} \label{monotonicityvolumeinproof}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}} V_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}=-2\Big.\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\delta}V_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}
\Big|_{\delta=1-2a}<0.
\end{equation}
To prove \eqref{volumearealimits}, for notational convenience we first denote the integrand in \eqref{volumedelta} by
\[
G_\delta(\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop:}=\bigl(1+\delta\cos(2\sigma)\bigr)\sqrt{1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)},
\]
and observe that for every \(\delta\in(0,1)\) and \(\sigma\in[0,\pi]\) it holds 
\[
0<G_\delta(\sigma)\leq4,\qquad
\lim_{\delta\to0^+}G_\delta(\sigma)=1,\qquad
\lim_{\delta\to1^-}G_\delta(\sigma)=4|\cos\sigma|^3.
\]
As such, dominated convergence in \eqref{volumedelta} establishes the limits
\begin{equation}\label{volumelimitsinproof}
\lim_{a\to\frac{1}{2}^-}V_a=\frac{\pi^2}{4},
\qquad
\lim_{a\to0^+}V_a=\frac{4\pi}{3},
\end{equation}
while the continuity and strict monotonicity of $V_a$ also
show that $V_a:(0,\frac{1}{2})\to
(\frac{\pi^2}{4},\frac{4\pi}{3})$ is a bijection. In addition, \eqref{areaidentity} gives $\frac{A_a}{V_a}=3+\pi\frac{I_a}{V_a}$, which is strictly increasing since $I_a$ is positive and strictly increasing, whereas $V_a$ is positive and strictly
decreasing.

Employing the same change of variables $s=s_a(\sigma)$ as in
\eqref{transformedvolumeint} to the area formula in
\eqref{periodgeometry}, by \eqref{arclengthderivatives} and
Proposition \ref{arclengthrepresentation} we obtain
\[
A_a=2\pi\int_0^\pi\rho_a(\sigma)\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma,
\]
while using \eqref{rhoexplicit} and $\delta=1-2a$ we further write
\[
A_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}=\pi\int_0^\pi\sqrt{1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)}
\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma.
\]
Similarly to \eqref{derivdeltavolume}, differentiation under the
integral sign followed by integration by parts yields
\[
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\delta}A_{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}=\pi\int_0^\pi
\frac{\delta+\cos(2\sigma)}
{\sqrt{1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)}}
\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma=\pi\delta\int_0^\pi
\frac{\bigl(\delta+\cos(2\sigma)\bigr)^2}
{\bigl(1+\delta^2+2\delta\cos(2\sigma)\bigr)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma>0,
\]
while combining \eqref{areaidentity} and \eqref{volumelimitsinproof} with the
endpoint limits for $I_a$ in Proposition
\ref{coefficientmonotonicity} we infer that
\begin{equation} \label{limitAainproof}
\lim_{a\to0^+}A_a=4\pi,\qquad \lim_{a\to\frac12^-}A_a=\pi^2.
\end{equation}
Finally, using \eqref{monotonicityvolumeinproof} and Proposition  \ref{coefficientmonotonicity} once more we deduce that 
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} a}
\Big(\frac{2T_aI_a}{V_a}\Big)&=\frac{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} a}\bigl(2T_aI_a\bigr)V_a
-2T_aI_a \frac{{\mathrm{d}} V_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}}{V_a^2}>0, \\
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}} a}\Big(\frac{I_a}{V_a}\Big)&=\frac{\frac{{\mathrm{d}} I_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}V_a-I_a\frac{{\mathrm{d}} V_a}{{\mathrm{d}} a}}{V_a^2}>0,
\end{aligned}
\]
while the limits in \eqref{quotientlimits} follow from a combination of \eqref{limitsinproof}, \eqref{volumelimitsinproof} and \eqref{limitAainproof}.

We take $K=[a_-,a_+]\subset(0,\frac{1}{2})$ with
$a_-<a_0<a_+$, and consider the domains $\Omega_{n,a}$
provided by Corollary \ref{uniformconstruction} for
$n\geq n_K$ and $a\in K$. Following the prescribed-mass
argument in \cite{dPMZ}*{Proof of Theorem 1}, we first choose $n_m$ and then adjust the neck parameter.

More specifically, we set $\zeta(x)\mathrel{\mathop:}=\frac{x}{\log x}$ for $x>e$, define $\mathcal M_0\mathrel{\mathop:}=\mathcal M(a_0)>0$, and select
\begin{equation}\label{integerpart}
n_m=\Big\lfloor
\frac{m}{\mathcal M_0}
\left(\log m+\log\log m-\log\mathcal M_0\right)
\Big\rfloor,
\end{equation}
where $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ denotes the integer part function. Due to the estimates
\[
n_m=\frac{m\log m}{\mathcal M_0}(1+o(1)), \qquad
\log n_m=\log m+\log\log m-\log\mathcal M_0+o(1),
\]
one can verify that
\begin{equation}\label{targetlimit}
n_m\to\infty, \qquad \frac{m}{\zeta(n_m)}
=\frac{m\log n_m}{n_m}
\rightarrow\mathcal M_0 \quad \mbox{as}\quad m\to \infty,
\end{equation}
and therefore $n_m\geq n_K$ for all sufficiently large $m$.

The uniform mass asymptotics \eqref{massreduction} and \eqref{uniformerror} then yield 
\[
\frac{m_{n_m}(a_\pm)}{\zeta(n_m)}=\mathcal M(a_\pm)\bigl(1+\varepsilon_{n_m}(a_\pm)\bigr)\rightarrow\mathcal M(a_\pm) \quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty,
\]
where \eqref{targetlimit} and the strict inequalities $\mathcal M(a_-)<\mathcal M_0<\mathcal M(a_+)$ further imply
\[
m_{n_m}(a_-)<m<m_{n_m}(a_+)
\]
for all sufficiently large $m$.

Next, the intermediate value theorem provides $a_m\in(a_-,a_+)$ satisfying $m_{n_m}(a_m)=m$, so that \eqref{massreduction}, \eqref{uniformerror} and \eqref{targetlimit} give
\[
\mathcal M(a_m)=\frac{m}{\zeta(n_m)\bigl(1+\varepsilon_{n_m}(a_m)\bigr)}\rightarrow\mathcal M_0=\mathcal M(a_0) \quad\text{as} \quad m\to\infty.
\]
Consequently, $a_m\to a_0$ as $m\to\infty$ by the continuity of $\mathcal M^{-1}$, while \eqref{nasymptotic} and \eqref{ninversion} follow from \eqref{targetlimit} and \eqref{integerpart}, respectively.
