<!-- customthm-1: body_md -->
{A}\label{thm:A}
Assume $G=P\bowtie N$ and $\pi\colon G\to U(H)$ is a projective unitary representation of~$G$ of Heisenberg type with respect to this factorization, with the corresponding phase factor~$\Phi$. Then the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the representation $\pi$ is irreducible;
  \item the matrix $(\Phi(p,n))_{p\in P,n\in N}$ is invertible;
  \item the matrix $(\Phi(p,n))_{p\in P,n\in N}$ is Hadamard.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, if these conditions are satisfied, then $\pi$ is determined uniquely, up to projective unitary equivalence (and even up to unitary equivalence if we in addition require $\pi(pn)=\pi(p)\pi(n)$), by~$\Phi$. We then call $\pi$ the Heisenberg representation of $G$ with phase~$\Phi$.
<!-- end customthm-1 -->
<!-- customthm-2: body_md -->
{B}\label{thm:B}
Assume $G=P\bowtie N$. Then, for any function $\Phi\colon P\times N\to{\mathbb C}^\times$ with $\Phi(e,e)=1$, the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the element $\Omega:=|G|^{-1/2}\sum_{p\in P,n\in N}\Phi(p,n^{-1})\,p\otimes n\in{\mathbb C} G\otimes{\mathbb C} G$ is a $2$-cocycle on $\widehat G$;
  \item the function $\Phi$ is the phase of an irreducible projective unitary representation of Heisenberg type, so that it satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:A}.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, if these conditions are satisfied, then the cocycle $\Omega$ is nondegenerate and unitary.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
The following identities hold:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dress1}
(p_1p_2)\lhd n=\big(p_1\lhd(p_2\rhd n)\big)(p_2\lhd n),\qquad p\rhd(n_1n_2)=(p\rhd n_1)\big((p\lhd n_1)\rhd n_2\big).
\end{equation}
In particular, the dressing transformations act trivially on the unit element and we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dress2}
(p^{-1}\lhd n)^{-1}=p\lhd(p^{-1}\rhd n),\qquad (p\rhd n^{-1})^{-1}=(p\lhd n^{-1})\rhd n.
\end{equation}

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:b1-cocycle}
We say that a function $\mathbb E\colon P\times N\to{\mathbb T}$ is a \emph{bi-$1$-cocycle} on $G=P\bowtie N$ if it satisfies the following identities:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:b1-cocycle1}
\mathbb E(p_1p_2,n)=\mathbb E(p_1,n)\mathbb E(p_2,p_1^{-1}\rhd n),\qquad \mathbb E(p,n_1n_2)=\mathbb E(p,n_1)\mathbb E((p^{-1}\lhd n_1)^{-1},n_2).
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:Mackey}
Assume $G=P\bowtie N$ and $c\in H^2(G;{\mathbb T})$ is a class with respect to which~$P$ and~$N$ are isotropic. Then $c$ can be represented by a cocycle $\sigma\in Z^2(G;{\mathbb T})$ such that $\sigma|_{P\times P}=1$ and $\sigma|_{N\times N}=1$. For any such $\sigma$, the function $\mathbb E\colon P\times N\to{\mathbb T}$ defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:b1-from-2}
\mathbb E(p,n):=\sigma(p^{-1}\rhd n, p^{-1}\lhd n)\sigma(p^{-1},n)^{-1}
\end{equation}
is a bi-$1$-cocycle and $c=[\sigma_\mathbb E]$, where $\sigma_\mathbb E$ is the $2$-cocycle given by~\eqref{eq:MackeyE}. All bi-$1$-cocycles $\mathbb E$ are obtained this way from appropriate $c$ and $\sigma$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Consider the central extension $1\to{\mathbb T}\to G_c\to G\to1$ corresponding to $c$. Fix a section $s\colon G\to G_c$. The assumption that $P$ and $N$ are isotropic implies that we can modify $s$ on $P$ and $N$ so that it becomes a homomorphism on these groups. Then the corresponding cocycle $\sigma(g,h):=s(g)s(h)s(gh)^{-1}$ has the properties $\sigma|_{P\times P}=1$ and $\sigma|_{N\times N}=1$.

Assume now that we are given a cocycle $\sigma$ representing the class $c$ such that $\sigma|_{P\times P}=1$ and $\sigma|_{N\times N}=1$. We can find a section $s\colon G\to G_c$ defining it. Then $s|_P$ and $s|_N$ are homomorphisms. Applying $s$ to the identity $pn=(p\rhd n)(p\lhd n)$ we get
$$
\sigma(p,n)^{-1}s(p)s(n)=\sigma(p\rhd n,p\lhd n)^{-1}s(p\rhd n)s(p\lhd n),
$$
which shows that $\mathbb E$ is determined by the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exchange}
\mathbb E(p,n)s(p^{-1})s(n)=s(p^{-1}\rhd n)s(p^{-1}\lhd n).
\end{equation}
Applying this separately to $p=p_1p_2$ and $n=n_1n_2$ we get the bi-$1$-cocycle identities.

Next, define a new section $t\colon G\to G_c$ by $t(pn):=s(p)s(n)$. Then
\begin{align}
t(p_1n_1)t(p_2n_2)&=s(p_1)\big(s(p_2^{-1})s(n_1^{-1})\big)^{-1}s(n_2)\notag\\
&=\mathbb E(p_2,n_1^{-1})s(p_1)s\big((p_2^{-1}\lhd n_1^{-1})^{-1}\big)s\big((p_2^{-1}\rhd n_1^{-1})^{-1}\big)s(n_2)\notag\\
&=\mathbb E(p_2,n_1^{-1})t(p_1n_1p_2n_2).\label{eq:cocycle-computation}
\end{align}
Thus, the cocycle defined by the section $t$ is $\sigma_\mathbb E$, so $c=[\sigma_\mathbb E]$.

To prove the last part of the proposition it suffices to show that the construction~\eqref{eq:b1-from-2}, being applied to $\sigma_\mathbb E$, gives back $\mathbb E$, that is, it defines a left inverse to the map
$$
\{\text{bi-$1$-cocycles}\}\to\{\text{$2$-cocycles that are identically $1$ on $P$ and $N$}\},\quad \mathbb E\mapsto\sigma_\mathbb E.
$$
In order to show this, we need to check that for any bi-$1$-cocycle $\mathbb E$ we have the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flip}
\mathbb E(p^{-1}\lhd n,(p^{-1}\rhd n)^{-1})=\mathbb E(p,n).
\end{equation}
Replacing $p$ by $(p\lhd n^{-1})^{-1}$ and using~\eqref{eq:dress2}, we equivalently need to check that
$$
\mathbb E(p,p\rhd n^{-1})=\mathbb E(p^{-1}\blacktriangleleft n^{-1},n).
$$
This identity is true, since by the cocycle relations both sides are equal to $\mathbb E(p^{-1},n^{-1})^{-1}$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:cobound}
Given two bi-$1$-cocycles $\mathbb E_1$ and $\mathbb E_2$, we have $[\sigma_{\mathbb E_1}]=[\sigma_{\mathbb E_2}]$ in $H^2(G;{\mathbb T})$ if and only if there exist homomorphisms
$\chi_P\colon P\to{\mathbb T}$ and $\chi_N\colon N\to{\mathbb T}$ such that
$$
\mathbb E_1(p,n)=\mathbb E_2(p,n)\frac{\chi_P(p^{-1})}{\chi_P(p^{-1}\lhd n)}\frac{\chi_N(n)}{\chi_N(p^{-1}\rhd n)}.
$$
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Consider the class $c:=[\sigma_{\mathbb E_1}]$, the corresponding extension $1\to{\mathbb T}\to G_c\to G\to1$ and a section $t\colon G\to G_c$ defining $\sigma_{\mathbb E_1}$. We then have to understand what kind of bi-$1$-cocycles we can get by modifying $t$ in such a way that the corresponding $2$-cocycle is identically $1$ on~$P$ and~$N$, that is, the new section $s$ must still be a homomorphism on $P$ and $N$. From~\eqref{eq:exchange} we see, however, that for the construction of a bi-$1$-cocycle we only need to know the restrictions of~$s$ to~$P$ and~$N$. Therefore the only meaningful choice for $s$ that we can make is to multiply~$t|_P$ and~$t|_N$ by some characters $\chi_P$ and $\chi_N$. This gives the result.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:Kac}
The bi-$1$-cocycles form a group under multiplication. It is isomorphic (or equal, depending on the conventions) to the first Kac cohomology group $H^1_{\mathrm{Kac}}(P, N;{\mathbb T})$, see~\cites{Kac,MR2115374}; we are indebted to Arthur Massar for this remark. Kac's cohomology fits into a long exact sequence, and the above considerations reprove exactness of the following part of it:
\[
H^1(P;{\mathbb T})\oplus H^1(N;{\mathbb T})\to H^1_{\mathrm{Kac}}(P, N;{\mathbb T})\to H^2(G;{\mathbb T})\to H^2(P;{\mathbb T})\oplus H^2(N;{\mathbb T}).\tag*{$\diamond$}
\]
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:roots}
Any ${\mathbb C}^\times$-valued bi-$1$-cocycle $\mathbb E$ takes values in the group $\mu_m$ of roots of unity of order $m:=|G|\operatorname{lcm}(|P|,|N|)$.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
As is well-known, any cohomology class in $H^2(G;{\mathbb C}^\times)$ has order dividing $|G|$, see~\cite{MR672956}*{Corollary III.10.2}. Therefore $[\sigma_{\mathbb E^{|G|}}]=[\sigma_\mathbb E^{|G|}]=0$ in~$H^2(G;{\mathbb C}^\times)$. By Corollary~\ref{cor:cobound}, or rather by its proof for ${\mathbb C}^\times$ instead of ${\mathbb T}$, applied to $\mathbb E^{|G|}$ and the trivial bi-$1$-cocycle, it follows that there exist homomorphisms $\chi_P\colon P\to{\mathbb C}^\times$ and $\chi_N\colon N\to{\mathbb C}^\times$ such that
$$
\mathbb E(p,n)^{|G|}=\frac{\chi_P(p^{-1})}{\chi_P(p^{-1}\lhd n)}\frac{\chi_N(n)}{\chi_N(p^{-1}\rhd n)}.
$$
Since $\chi_P$ and $\chi_N$ take values in the groups $\mu_{|P|}$ and $\mu_{|N|}$, we get the result.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:dual-bi1}
Assume $G=P\bowtie N$ and $\mathbb E\colon P\times N\to{\mathbb C}^\times$ is a function. Consider the element
\begin{equation*}
\label{eq:dual-crossed-term}
\Omega:=\sum_{p\in P,n\in N}\mathbb E(p^{-1},n^{-1})\,p\otimes n\in{\mathbb C} G\otimes{\mathbb C} G.
\end{equation*}
Then $\Omega$ satisfies the dual cocycle identity~\eqref{eq:dual-2-cocycle} if and only if $\mathbb E(e,e)^{-1}\mathbb E$ is a bi-$1$-cocycle.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Since~\eqref{eq:dual-2-cocycle} is stable under multiplication of $\Omega$ by a scalar, we may assume that $\mathbb E(e,e)=1$. Since $\mathbb E$ is a bi-$1$-cocycle if and only if $\bar\mathbb E$ is, we can also replace $\mathbb E$ by $\bar\mathbb E$ in the proof, so that the adjoint element $\Omega^*$ becomes equal to $\sum_{p,n}\mathbb E(p,n)\,p\otimes n$. We need to show that $\mathbb E$ is a bi-$1$-cocycle if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual-2-cocycle2}
(\Delta\otimes\iota)(\Omega^*)(\Omega^*\otimes 1)=(\iota\otimes\Delta)(\Omega^*)(1\otimes\Omega^*),
\end{equation}
that is,
$$
\sum_{p,p',n,n'} \mathbb E(p,n)\mathbb E(p^{-1}p',n')\,p'\otimes pn'\otimes n=
\sum_{p,p',n,n'} \mathbb E(p',n')\mathbb E(p,n'^{-1}n)\,p'\otimes n'p\otimes n.
$$
If we write $n'p$ as $\tilde p\tilde n$, then $n'=\tilde p\rhd\tilde n$ and $p=\tilde p\lhd\tilde n$. Hence the right hand side of the above equation can be written as
$$
\sum_{p,p',n,n'} \mathbb E(p',p\rhd n' )\mathbb E(p\lhd n',(p\rhd n')^{-1}n\big)\, p'\otimes pn'\otimes n.
$$
Therefore $\Omega^*$ satisfies~\eqref{eq:dual-2-cocycle2} if and only if $\mathbb E$ satisfies
$$
\mathbb E(p,n)\mathbb E(p^{-1}p',n')=\mathbb E(p',p\rhd n' )\mathbb E(p\lhd n',(p\rhd n')^{-1}n\big),
$$
or equivalently, in a more symmetric form,
\begin{equation}
\label{ECond}
\mathbb E(p,n'n)\mathbb E(p',p^{-1} \rhd n')=\mathbb E(pp',n')\mathbb E((p^{-1}\lhd n')^{-1},n),
\end{equation}
where we have used that $p\lhd(p^{-1}\rhd n')=(p^{-1}\lhd n')^{-1}$ by~\eqref{eq:dress2}.

Now, if $\mathbb E$ is a bi-$1$-cocycle, then the cocycle relations~\eqref{eq:b1-cocycle1} imply that both sides of~\eqref{ECond} equal
$$
\mathbb E(p,n')\mathbb E((p^{-1}\lhd n')^{-1},n)\mathbb E(p',p^{-1} \rhd n').
$$
Conversely, assume~\eqref{ECond} is satisfied.
Evaluating it at  $p'=e$ and $n'=e$ gives
$\mathbb E(p,e)=1$, since $\mathbb E(e,e)=1$ by assumption, and evaluating at $p=e$ and $n=e$ gives
$\mathbb E(e,n')=1$.
The two bi-$1$-cocycle relations follow then by evaluating \eqref{ECond} at  $n=e$ for the first one and at  $p'=e$ for the second.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:Nondeg}
For any $\sigma\in Z^2(G;{\mathbb T})$, the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the cocycle $\sigma$ is nondegenerate;
  \item there is an irreducible unitary $\sigma$-representation $\pi$ of dimension $|G|^{1/2}$;
  \item the smallest nonzero dimension for which there exists a unitary $\sigma$-representation is $|G|^{1/2}$.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, if these conditions are satisfied, then any unitary $\sigma$-representation is unitarily equivalent to a multiple of~$\pi$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
Both $\pi^P$ and $\pi^N$ are unitary $\sigma_\mathbb E$-representations.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
If a function $f$ on $P$ is viewed as an operator of multiplication by $f$ on $\ell^2(P)$, then $V_nf=f(\cdot\blacktriangleleft n)V_n$. From this and the cocycle relations one sees immediately that $\pi^P|_N$ is a homomorphism. In a similar way one sees that $\pi^N|_P$ is a homomorphism. It follows that in order to prove the lemma we only need to check the identity
$$
\mathbb E(p,n)\pi(p^{-1})\pi(n)=\pi(p^{-1}\rhd n)\pi(p^{-1}\lhd n)
$$
for $\pi=\pi^P$ and $\pi=\pi^N$, since the property $\pi(pn)=\pi(p)\pi(n)$ and  the same computation as in~\eqref{eq:cocycle-computation} show then that $\pi$ is a $\sigma_\mathbb E$-representation.

Consider first the representation $\pi^P$. We have
$$
\mathbb E(p,n)\pi^P(p^{-1})\pi^P(n)=\mathbb E(p,n)\lambda^P_{p^{-1}}\overline{\mathbb E(\cdot,n)}V_n=\mathbb E(p,n)\overline{\mathbb E(p\,\cdot, n)}\lambda^P_{p^{-1}}V_n,
$$
$$
\pi^P(p^{-1}\rhd n)\pi^P(p^{-1}\lhd n)=\overline{\mathbb E(\cdot, p^{-1}\rhd n)}V_{p^{-1}\rhd n}\lambda^P_{p^{-1}\lhd n}.
$$
Since $\lambda^P_{p^{-1}}V_n=V_{p^{-1}\rhd n}\lambda^P_{p^{-1}\lhd n}$ by~\eqref{eq:exchange-op}, we thus need to show that
$$
\mathbb E(p,n)\overline{\mathbb E(pp', n)}=\overline{\mathbb E(p', p^{-1}\rhd n)}.
$$
This is just the first cocycle identity in~\eqref{eq:b1-cocycle1}.

Consider now the representation $\pi^N$. In this case we need to check the identity
$$
\mathbb E(p,n)\mathbb E(p^{-1}, n')=\mathbb E(p^{-1}\lhd n, (p^{-1}\rhd n)^{-1}n').
$$
The right hand side, by the second cocycle identity in~\eqref{eq:b1-cocycle1}, equals
$$
\mathbb E(p^{-1}\lhd n, (p^{-1}\rhd n)^{-1})\mathbb E\Big(\big((p^{-1}\lhd n)^{-1}\lhd (p^{-1}\rhd n)^{-1}\big)^{-1},n'\Big).
$$
In this product, the first factor equals $\mathbb E(p,n)$ by~\eqref{eq:flip}. Since $(p^{-1}\lhd n)^{-1}=p\lhd(p^{-1}\rhd n)$ by~\eqref{eq:dress2}, the first argument in the second factor is $p^{-1}$, so this factor equals $\mathbb E(p^{-1},n')$, as needed.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:Fourier1}
We have ${\mathcal F}\pi^N(g)=\pi^P(g){\mathcal F}$ for all $g\in G$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
It suffices to check this on the elements of $P$ and $N$. Consider first $g=p\in P$. In this case it is more convenient to check that $\lambda^P_{p^{-1}}{\mathcal F}\pi^N(p)={\mathcal F}$. We compute:
\begin{align*}
(\lambda^P_{p^{-1}}{\mathcal F}\pi^N(p)f)(p') & =\sum_n\overline{\mathbb E(pp',n)}\mathbb E(p,n)f(p^{-1}\rhd n) \\
   & =\sum_n\overline{\mathbb E(p',p^{-1}\rhd n)}f(p^{-1}\rhd n)=({\mathcal F} f)(p').
\end{align*}
For $g=n\in N$, the verification of the identity ${\mathcal F}\lambda^N_n=\pi^P(n){\mathcal F}$ is equally straightforward and we omit it.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:Fourier2}
We have $K\lambda^G_g=(\pi^P(g)\otimes\bar\pi^P(g))K$ for all $g\in G$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We will check this separately on the elements of $P$ and $N$. Consider first $g=p\in P$. By definition and identity~\eqref{eq:mult-implementation}, we have
$$
(\pi^P(p)\otimes\bar\pi^P(p))K=(\lambda^P_p\otimes\lambda^P_p)W_P(1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F})T=W_P(1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F})(\lambda^P_p\otimes1)T,
$$
which proves what we need, as we obviously have $(\lambda^P_p\otimes1)T=T\lambda^G_p$.

Consider now $g=n\in N$. By definition,
\begin{align*}
  \big((\pi^P(n)\otimes\bar\pi^P(n))Kf\big)(p_1,p_2) & =\overline{\mathbb E(p_1,n)}\mathbb E(p_2,n)\big(W_P(1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F})Tf\big)(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,p_2\blacktriangleleft n) \\
  & =\overline{\mathbb E(p_1,n)}\mathbb E(p_2,n)\big((1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F})Tf\big)\big(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,(p_1\blacktriangleleft n)^{-1}(p_2\blacktriangleleft n)\big).
\end{align*}
By \eqref{eq:dress1} and \eqref{eq:dress2}, we have $(p_1^{-1}p_2)\blacktriangleleft(p_1^{-1}\rhd n)=(p_1\blacktriangleleft n)^{-1}(p_2\blacktriangleleft n)$. Therefore we can write the above expression as
$$
\overline{\mathbb E(p_1,n)}\mathbb E(p_2,n)\big((1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F})Tf\big)\big(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,(p_1^{-1}p_2)\blacktriangleleft(p_1^{-1}\rhd n)\big),
$$
which, in turn, can be written as
$$
\overline{\mathbb E(p_1,n)}\big((1\otimes \mathbb E(\cdot,n)\lambda^P_{p_1}V_{p_1^{-1}\rhd n}\bar{\mathcal F})Tf\big)(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,p_2).
$$
Since $V_{p_1^{-1}\rhd n}=\overline{\mathbb E(\cdot,p_1^{-1}\rhd n)}\bar\pi^P(p_1^{-1}\rhd n)$ by definition and $\bar\pi^P(p_1^{-1}\rhd n)\bar{\mathcal F}=\bar{\mathcal F}\lambda^N_{p_1^{-1}\rhd n}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:Fourier1},  the above expression equals
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K1}
\overline{\mathbb E(p_1,n)}\big((1\otimes\mathbb E(\cdot,n)\lambda^P_{p_1}\overline{\mathbb E(\cdot,p_1^{-1}\rhd n)}\bar{\mathcal F}\lambda^N_{p_1^{-1}\rhd n})Tf\big)(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,p_2).
\end{equation}
Next, we have
$$
\lambda^P_{p_1}\overline{\mathbb E(\cdot,p_1^{-1}\rhd n)}=\overline{\mathbb E(p_1^{-1}\cdot,p_1^{-1}\rhd n)}\lambda^P_{p_1}=\overline{\mathbb E(p_1^{-1},p_1^{-1}\rhd n)\mathbb E(\cdot,n)}\lambda^P_{p_1},
$$
and since $\mathbb E(p_1^{-1},p_1^{-1}\rhd n)=\overline{\mathbb E(p_1,n)}$, we see that~\eqref{eq:K1} equals
$$
\big((1\otimes\lambda^P_{p_1}\bar{\mathcal F}\lambda^N_{p_1^{-1}\rhd n})Tf\big)(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,p_2)=\big((1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F}\lambda^N_{p_1^{-1}\rhd n})Tf\big)(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,p_1^{-1}p_2).
$$
To summarize, we have shown that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K2}
\big((\pi^P(n)\otimes\bar\pi^P(n))Kf\big)(p_1,p_2)=\big((1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F}\lambda^N_{p_1^{-1}\rhd n})Tf\big)(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,p_1^{-1}p_2).
\end{equation}

On the other hand, we have
$$
(K\lambda^G_nf)(p_1,p_2)=\big((1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F})T\lambda^G_nf\big)(p_1,p_1^{-1}p_2).
$$
Comparing this with~\eqref{eq:K2} we see that in order to finish the proof it suffices to show that
$$
\big((1\otimes\lambda^N_{p_1^{-1}\rhd n})Tf\big)(p_1\blacktriangleleft n,n')=(T\lambda^G_nf)(p_1,n')
$$
for all $n'\in N$, that is,
$$
(p_1\blacktriangleleft n)(p_1^{-1}\rhd n)^{-1}n'=n^{-1}p_1n',
$$
which is indeed true by the definition of the dressing actions.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Assume $G=P\bowtie N$ and $\mathbb E\colon P\times N\to{\mathbb T}$ is a bi-$1$-cocycle. Then the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the cocycle $\sigma_\mathbb E\in Z^2(G;{\mathbb T})$ defined by~\eqref{eq:MackeyE} is nondegenerate;
  \item the matrix $(\mathbb E(p,n))_{p\in P,n\in N}$ is invertible;
  \item the matrix $(\mathbb E(p,n))_{p\in P,n\in N}$ is Hadamard.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Obviously, (3) implies (2), so we only need to prove the implications (1)$\Rightarrow$(3) and (2)$\Rightarrow$(1).

\smallskip

(1)$\Rightarrow$(3) Since $\pi^P$ and $\pi^N$ are unitary $\sigma_\mathbb E$-representations of dimension~$|P|$ and~$|N|$, resp., and $|P|\,|N|=|G|$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:Nondeg} it follows that $|P|=|N|=|G|^{1/2}$, both representations are irreducible and we have a unitary equivalence $\pi^P\sim\pi^N$. Then ${\mathcal F}$, being a nonzero intertwiner between irreducible unitary $\sigma_\mathbb E$-representations by Lemma~\ref{lem:Fourier1}, must be a scalar multiple of a unitary operator by Schur's lemma. Thus, the matrix $(\mathbb E(p,n))_{p,n}$ is Hadamard.

\smallskip

(2)$\Rightarrow$(1) Since the invertibility of $(\mathbb E(p,n))_{p,n}$ already implies that $|P|=|N|=|G|^{1/2}$, by Lem\-ma~\ref{lem:Nondeg} it suffices to show that the $\sigma_\mathbb E$-representation $\pi^P$ is irreducible. Consider the representation $\operatorname{Ad}\pi^P$ of $G$ on $B(\ell^2(P))$. Then irreducibility of $\pi^P$ is equivalent to the claim that the space of invariant vectors for $\operatorname{Ad}\pi^P$ is one-dimensional. We have a standard equivalence $\pi^P\otimes\bar\pi^P\sim\operatorname{Ad}\pi^P$, explicitly given by mapping $\xi\otimes\zeta\in\ell^2(P)\otimes\ell^2(P)$ into the rank-one operator $(\cdot,J\zeta)\xi\in B(\ell^2(P))$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:Fourier2} and the assumption of invertibility of ${\mathcal F}$ we then conclude that $\operatorname{Ad}\pi^P\sim\lambda^G$. Since the space of vectors fixed by the operators of the regular representation is one-dimensional, this gives the result.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
Another, and even shorter, proof of the implication (2)$\Rightarrow$(1) can be obtained as follows. Observe first that the space $\operatorname{Hom}_G(\pi^N,\pi^P)$ is one-dimensional and spanned by~${\mathcal F}$ for any bi-$1$-cocycle $\mathbb E$, no nondegeneracy assumptions are needed. Indeed, assuming that $S\colon\ell^2(N)\to\ell^2(P)$ intertwines~$\pi^N$ and~$\pi^P$, consider the vector $S\delta_e$. Since $\delta_e$ is fixed by $\pi^N(P)$, this vector is fixed by $\lambda^P(P)$, hence it is a scalar multiple $c\,\mathds{1}$ of the constant function $1$ on $P$. Then
$$
S\delta_n=S\lambda^N_n\delta_e=\pi^P(n)S\delta_e=c\,\pi^P(n)\mathds{1}=c\,\overline{\mathbb E(\cdot,n)}=c\,{\mathcal F}\delta_n,
$$
hence $S=c\,{\mathcal F}$. Now, if ${\mathcal F}$ is invertible, then we conclude that $\pi^N$ and $\pi^P$ are equivalent and $\operatorname{End}_G(\pi^P)$ is one-dimensional, hence $\pi^P$ is irreducible, proving the desired implication. The advantage of the proof using the operator $K$ that we gave is that it generalizes to infinite-dimensional representations. This operator will also allow us to compute dual cocycles associated with $\sigma_\mathbb E$.  \nopagebreak\mbox{\ }\hfill$\diamond$

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:cocycle-Hadamard}
Assume $G=P\bowtie N$ and $\sigma\in Z^2(G;{\mathbb T})$ is a cocycle such that $\sigma|_{P\times P}=1$ and $\sigma|_{N\times N}=1$. Then $\sigma$ is nondegenerate if and only if the matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma-matrix}
\big(\sigma(p\rhd n, p\lhd n)\overline{\sigma(p,n)}\big)_{p\in P,n\in N}
\end{equation}
is invertible, if and only if it is Hadamard.
<!-- end corollary-3 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
This follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:main} and Proposition~\ref{prop:Mackey}.
<!-- end proof-9 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
The matrix~\eqref{eq:sigma-matrix} depends on the choice of $\sigma$, while nondegeneracy depends only on the cohomology class $[\sigma]$. Therefore invertibility and the Hadamard property also depend only on the cohomology class. At the same time, when the matrix is non-invertible, its rank depends on the cocycle, not just on its cohomology class. In order to see this, assume $N$ is normal and abelian, so that $\rhd\colon P\times N\to N$ is an action by automorphisms and $G=P\ltimes N$. Given a character $\chi\in \widehat N$, consider the function $f(pn):=\chi(n)$ and the coboundary $\sigma:=\partial f$. Then
$$
\sigma(p_1n_1,p_2n_2)=\chi(n_1)\overline{\chi(p_2^{-1}\rhd n_1)},\qquad \sigma(p\rhd n, p\lhd n)\overline{\sigma(p,n)}=\chi(p\rhd n)\overline{\chi(n)}.
$$
We see that $\sigma$ has trivial restrictions to $P$ and $N$, as required, but the rank of the matrix~\eqref{eq:sigma-matrix} equals the number of elements in the $P$-orbit of~$\chi$ in~$\widehat N$ and so in general depends on the choice of~$\chi$.\nopagebreak\mbox{\ }\hfill$\diamond$

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:dual-computation}
Assume $G=P\bowtie N$ and $\mathbb E\colon P\times N\to{\mathbb T}$ is a bi-$1$-cocycle satisfying the equivalent conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. Then the corresponding unitary dual $2$-cocycle defined by~\eqref{eq:star} is given by
$$
\Omega=|G|^{-1/2}\sum_{p\in P,n\in N}\overline{\mathbb E(p^{-1},n^{-1})}\,n\otimes p.
$$
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Recalling that $K=W_P(1\otimes\bar{\mathcal F})T$ and observing that $W_P=\sum_p\delta_p\otimes\lambda^P_p$, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op-prime}
\operatorname{Op}(\delta_{pn})=|G|^{-1/2}(\cdot,J\lambda^P_p\bar{\mathcal F}\delta_n)\delta_p\in B(\ell^2(P)).
\end{equation}
As before, write $\mathds{1}\in\ell^2(P)$ for the constant function $1$. Then $\bar{\mathcal F}\delta_e=\mathds{1}$ and we get
$$
\operatorname{Op}(\delta_{p})=|G|^{-1/2}(\cdot,\mathds{1})\delta_p.
$$
It follows then that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op-prime-product}
\operatorname{Op}(\delta_{pn})\operatorname{Op}(\delta_e)=|G|^{-1}(\delta_e,J\lambda^P_p\bar{\mathcal F}\delta_n)(\cdot,\mathds{1})\delta_p=|G|^{-1/2}\mathbb E(p^{-1},n)\operatorname{Op}(\delta_p).
\end{equation}
Therefore the new product $\star$ on $C(G)$ satisfies
$\delta_{pn}\star \delta_e=|G|^{-1/2}\mathbb E(p^{-1},n)\delta_p$. Since the product is left $G$-equivariant, we then get
$$
\delta_{hpn}\star \delta_h=|G|^{-1/2}\mathbb E(p^{-1},n)\delta_{hp}
$$
for all $h\in G$, $p\in P$ and $n\in N$. In other words, for all $g,h\in G$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:star2}
\delta_{g}\star \delta_h=|G|^{-1/2}\mathbb E(p^{-1},n)\delta_{hp},\quad\text{where}\quad pn=h^{-1}g.
\end{equation}

From the defining relation~\eqref{eq:star} we deduce then that
$$
\Omega^*=\sum_{g,h\in G}(\delta_g\star\delta_h)(e)\,g\otimes h=|G|^{-1/2}\sum_{p\in P,n\in N}\mathbb E(p,n)\,n\otimes p.
$$
By taking the adjoints we get the result.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:dual}
Assume $G=P\bowtie N$ and $\mathbb E\colon P\times N\to{\mathbb T}$ is a bi-$1$-cocycle. Then the element
$$
\Omega:=|G|^{-1/2}\sum_{p\in P,n\in N}\overline{\mathbb E(p^{-1},n^{-1})}\,n\otimes p\in {\mathbb C} G\otimes{\mathbb C} G
$$
is invertible if and only if $\mathbb E$  satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
The ``if'' part follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-computation}. For the opposite implication, assume $\Omega$ is invertible, so that $\Omega$ is a dual $2$-cocycle by Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-bi1} and the fact that the flip map on ${\mathbb C} G\otimes{\mathbb C} G$ preserves the set of dual $2$-cocycles. We want to conclude that the matrix $(\mathbb E(p,n))_{p,n}$ is invertible. By passing to the pair $(N,P)$ if necessary we may assume that $|N|\le|P|$.

Define a new product $\star$ on $C(G)$ by~\eqref{eq:star}. Since it is left $G$-equivariant, it is completely specified by the values $(\delta_g\star\delta_h)(e)$. By the computations in the second part of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-computation} it follows that this product is given by~\eqref{eq:star2}. Consider also the linear map $\operatorname{Op}\colon C(G)\to B(\ell^2(P))$ defined by~\eqref{eq:op-prime}. The computations in the first part of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-computation} did not use any nondegeneracy assumptions, so by~\eqref{eq:op-prime-product} we have
$$
\operatorname{Op}(\delta_{pn})\operatorname{Op}(\delta_e)=|G|^{-1/2}\mathbb E(p^{-1},n)\operatorname{Op}(\delta_p)=\operatorname{Op}(\delta_{pn}\star\delta_e).
$$
Since $\operatorname{Op}$ intertwines $\lambda^G$ with $\operatorname{Ad}\pi^P$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:Fourier2},  by applying $\operatorname{Ad}\pi^P(h)$ we conclude that
$$
\operatorname{Op}(\delta_{g})\operatorname{Op}(\delta_h)=\operatorname{Op}(\delta_g\star\delta_h)
$$
for all $g,h\in G$, that is, $\operatorname{Op}$ is a representation of the algebra $(C(G),\star)$.

By \cite{MR1264314}*{Proposition~7}, the invertibility of $\Omega^*$ implies that the algebra $(C(G),\star)$ is semisimple. It follows that its image under $\operatorname{Op}$ is semisimple as well. From~\eqref{eq:op-prime} and the fact that $\lambda^P_p{\mathcal F}={\mathcal F}\pi^N(p)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:Fourier1} we see that $\operatorname{Op}(C(G))$ is spanned by the operators of the form $(\cdot,\zeta)\xi$ with $\xi\in\ell^2(P)$ and $\zeta\in\operatorname{Im}{\mathcal F}$. Thus, it consists of all operators on $\ell^2(P)$ that vanish on $(\operatorname{Im}{\mathcal F})^\perp$. The operators $S$ such that $(\operatorname{Im}{\mathcal F})^\perp\subset\ker S$ and $\operatorname{Im}S\subset (\operatorname{Im}{\mathcal F})^\perp$ form an ideal with zero product in this algebra, so $\operatorname{Op}(C(G))$ is semisimple only when $(\operatorname{Im}{\mathcal F})^\perp=0$, that is, when ${\mathcal F}$ is surjective. Since $|N|\le|P|$ by assumption, this means that $(\mathbb E(p,n))_{p,n}$ must be invertible.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
Let $\mathfrak g$ be a finite-dimensional nilpotent Lie algebra over a finite field ${\mathbb F}_q$ of characteristic $p$. Assume its nilpotency class satisfies $c(\mathfrak g)<p-1$ and let $G$ be the corresponding finite nilpotent group. For a cocycle $\omega\in Z^2(\mathfrak g;{\mathbb F}_q)$ and a nontrivial character $\chi\colon{\mathbb F}_q\to{\mathbb T}$, consider the cocycle $\chi\circ l_\omega\in Z^2(G;{\mathbb T})$, where $l_\omega$ is given by~\eqref{eq:L-cocycle2}. Then the cocycle $\chi\circ l_\omega$ is nondegenerate if and only if the form $\omega$ is symplectic.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
Consider the element $f\in\mathfrak g_\omega^*$ defined by $f(a,x):=a$. By a version of Kirillov's orbit method we have a representation of $G_\omega$ corresponding to the character $\chi\circ f\colon\mathfrak g_\omega\to{\mathbb T}$. The precise result sufficient for our purposes is given in \cite{MR2403615}*{Theorem~2.6}, according to which there is an irreducible unitary representation $\pi$ of $G_\omega$ with character
$$
\chi_\pi(\exp(a,x))=\frac{1}{|\mathcal O|^{1/2}}\sum_{\tilde\chi\in\mathcal O}\tilde\chi(a,x),
$$
where $\mathcal O$ is the orbit of $\chi\circ f$ in the Pontryagin dual $\widehat\mathfrak g_\omega:=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak g_\omega,{\mathbb T})$ under the action of~$G_\omega$ on~$\widehat\mathfrak g_\omega$ induced by the adjoint action on $\mathfrak g_\omega$. In particular, $\dim\pi=|\mathcal O|^{1/2}$.

Since ${\mathbb F}_q\oplus0\subset\mathfrak g_\omega$ is central, it is clear that $\tilde\chi(a,0)=\chi(a)$ for all $\tilde\chi\in\mathcal O$ and $a\in{\mathbb F}_q$. Therefore if we view ${\mathbb F}_q$ as a subgroup of $G_\omega$ by identifying $a$ with $\exp(a,0)$, then $\chi_\pi(a)=|\mathcal O|^{1/2}\chi(a)$, and hence $\pi(a)=\chi(a)1$. This shows that if we view $\pi$ as a projective representation of $G$, then the corresponding cocycle is $\chi\circ l_\omega$. It follows that this cocycle is nondegenerate if and only if $\dim\pi=|G|^{1/2}$, that is, if and only if $|\mathcal O|=|G|$. Since $|G_\omega|=q|G|$, the last condition is equivalent to saying that the stabilizer of $\chi\circ f$ in $G_\omega$ has order~$q$.
                                                                             

Since the composition of $\chi$ with any nonzero element of $\mathfrak g_\omega^*$ gives a nontrivial character of~$\mathfrak g_\omega$, the stabilizer of $\chi\circ f$ coincides with the stabilizer of $f$ under the coadjoint action. By~\eqref{eq:ad}, the latter stabilizer is $\exp({\mathbb F}_q\oplus\operatorname{rad}(\omega))$, so it has order~$q$ if and only if $\operatorname{rad}(\omega)=0$.

<!-- corollary-5: body_md -->
\label{cor:Lagrangian}
In the setting of the theorem, assume we are given a symplectic cocycle $\omega\in Z^2(\mathfrak g;{\mathbb F}_q)$. Assume $\mathfrak p,\mathfrak n\subset\mathfrak g$ are complementary Lagrangian subalgebras with respect to~$\omega$, and consider the corresponding subgroups $P,N\subset G$. Then $G=P\bowtie N$ and the matrix
$$
\big(\chi\big(l_\omega(p\rhd n, p\lhd n)-l_\omega(p,n)\big)\big)_{p\in P,n\in N}
$$
is Hadamard.
<!-- end corollary-5 -->

Since $\mathfrak p\cap\mathfrak n=0$, we have $P\cap N=\{e\}$, and as $|P|\,|N|=|G|$, it follows that $G=P\bowtie N$. Consider the cocycle $\sigma:=\chi\circ l_\omega$ on $G$. Let $q\geq 7$ be a prime, and consider the following eight-dimensional Lie algebra~$\mathfrak g$ over~$\mathbb F_q$ with basis
$a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4,b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4$. The nonzero brackets, up to skew-symmetry, are
\begin{align*}
[a_1,a_4]&=[a_1,b_4]=a_2,\\
[b_1,a_4]&=[b_1,b_4]=b_2,\\
[a_1,a_2]&=a_3,\\
[b_1,b_2]&=b_3,\\
[a_1,b_2]&=[b_1,a_2]=a_3+b_3,\\
[a_1,b_3]&=[b_1,a_3]=a_4-b_4.
\end{align*}
This is a finite field version of the complexification of a real Lie algebra from \cite{MR4868109}*{Theorem~5.1(ii)}. The lower central series of $\mathfrak g$ is
$$
\mathfrak g\supset\operatorname{span}\{a_2,b_2,a_3,b_3,a_4-b_4\}\supset
\operatorname{span}\{a_3,b_3,a_4-b_4\}\supset\mathbb F_q(a_4-b_4)\supset0.
$$
Therefore $c(\mathfrak g)=4$. Writing $a_i^*,b_i^*$ for the dual basis vectors, define
$$
\omega:
=
a_1^*\wedge b_1^*
-a_2^*\wedge b_3^*
+a_3^*\wedge b_2^*
-a_4^*\wedge b_4^*.
$$
This is a symplectic $2$-cocycle. The Lie subalgebras
$$
\mathfrak p:=\operatorname{span}
\{a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4\}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\mathfrak n:=\operatorname{span}
\{b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4\}
$$
are complementary Lagrangian subalgebras.
                                                              
Thus, we are in a position to apply Corollary~\ref{cor:Lagrangian}.

In order to do computations it will be convenient to slightly modify the exponential coordinates. Observe that if we put
$$
\mathfrak p_0:=\operatorname{span}\{a_2,a_3,a_4\},
\qquad
\mathfrak n_0:=\operatorname{span}\{b_2,b_3,b_4\},
$$
then $\mathfrak a:=\mathfrak p_0\oplus\mathfrak n_0$ is an abelian ideal of $\mathfrak g$.
In view of this and the relation $[a_1,b_1]=0$ we parameterize~$P$ and~$N$~by
\begin{align*}
p(x,r,s,t)&:=\exp(r a_2+s a_3+t a_4)\exp(xa_1),\\
n(y,R,S,T)&:=\exp(yb_1)\exp(Rb_2+Sb_3+Tb_4).
\end{align*}
In these coordinates, the group laws are
$$
(x,r,s,t)(x',r',s',t')\\
=
\Big(
x+x',
r+r'+xt',
s+s'+xr'+\frac{x^2t'}2,
t+t'
\Big)
$$
on $P$, and
$$
(y,R,S,T)(y',R',S',T')
=
\Big(
y+y',
R+R'-y'T,
S+S'-y'R+\frac{(y')^2T}{2},
T+T'
\Big)
$$
on $N$. To compute the dressing actions, put
$$
U_p:=r a_2+s a_3+t a_4,\qquad
V_n:=Rb_2+Sb_3+Tb_4,
$$
and consider the operators
\[
D_p:=x\operatorname{ad}_{a_1}|_{\mathfrak a},\qquad D_n:=y\operatorname{ad}_{b_1}|_{\mathfrak a}.
\]
Since $\mathfrak a$ is abelian and $[a_1,b_1]=0$, we have
\[
pn=
\exp(yb_1)
\exp\bigl(e^{-D_n}U_p+e^{D_p}V_n\bigr)
\exp(xa_1).
\]
The two terms in the middle exponent are
\begin{align*}
e^{-D_n}U_p
={}&r a_2+(s-yr)a_3
+\Big(t-ys+\frac{y^2r}{2}\Big)a_4\\
&-yt\,b_2
+\Big(-yr+\frac{y^2t}{2}\Big)b_3
+\Big(ys-\frac{y^2r}{2}\Big)b_4,
\\[1ex]
e^{D_p}V_n
={}&xT\,a_2
+\Big(xR+\frac{x^2T}{2}\Big)a_3
+\Big(xS+\frac{x^2R}{2}\Big)a_4\\
&+R b_2+(S+xR)b_3
+\Big(T-xS-\frac{x^2R}{2}\Big)b_4.
\end{align*}
Splitting their sum as $U'_{pn}+V'_{pn}$, with $U'_{pn}\in\mathfrak p_0$ and $V'_{pn}\in\mathfrak n_0$, gives
\begin{align*}
p\rhd n
&=
\Big(
y,\,
R-yt,\,
S+xR-yr+\frac{y^2t}{2},
T-xS-\frac{x^2R}{2}+ys-\frac{y^2r}{2}
\Big),\\
p\lhd n
&=
\Big(
x,\,
r+xT,\,
s-yr+xR+\frac{x^2T}{2},
t-ys+\frac{y^2r}{2}+xS+\frac{x^2R}{2}
\Big).
\end{align*}

We next compute the phase
$$
\Psi(p,n):=l_\omega(p\rhd n, p\lhd n)-l_\omega(p,n)\in{\mathbb F}_q.
$$
Note that if we denote by $z$ the central element $(1,0)\in\mathfrak g_\omega$, then, in view of~\eqref{eq:exchange} and the facts that $\mathfrak p$ and $\mathfrak n$ are Lie subalgebras of $\mathfrak g_\omega$, so that $P$ and $N$ can be viewed as subgroups of $G_\omega$, the phase is determined by the following relation in $G_\omega$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase}
\exp(\Psi(p,n)z)pn=(p\rhd n)(p\lhd n).
\end{equation}
The $\operatorname{BCH}$-formula, together with the facts that the central extension of $\mathfrak a$ is two-step nilpotent and $[a_1,b_1]_{\mathfrak g_\omega}=z$, gives then that
$$
\Psi(p,n)
=-xy-\frac12\omega\big(e^{-D_n}U_p,e^{D_p}V_n\big)+\frac12\omega(V'_{pn},U'_{pn}).
$$
The final expression is
\begin{align*}
\Psi(x,r,s,t;y,R,S,T)
=&-xy+r(S+xR)-(s-yr)R\\
&+\Big(t-ys+\frac{y^2r}{2}\Big)
\Big(T-xS-\frac{x^2R}{2}\Big)\\
&-\frac12\Big(
yr^2-2yst+y^2(rt+s^2)
-y^3rs+\frac{y^4r^2}{4}
\Big)\\
&-\frac12\Big(
xR^2-2xST+x^2(S^2-RT)
+x^3RS+\frac{x^4R^2}{4}
\Big).
\end{align*}
Therefore the conclusion is that the following $q^4\times q^4$ matrix is Hadamard for all primes $q\ge7$:
$$
\Big(
e^{\frac{2\pi i}{q}\Psi(x,r,s,t;y,R,S,T)}\Big)_{(x,r,s,t),(y,R,S,T)\in\mathbb F_q^4}.
$$
A direct computation shows that the same is in fact true for all odd primes.

\smallskip

We claim that, for $q\ge5$, the matrix $(e^{\frac{2\pi i}{q}\Psi(u,v)})_{u,v\in\mathbb F_q^4}$
is neither equivalent to a Fourier matrix nor of Di\c{t}\u{a} type. Recall that a complex Hadamard matrix is of \emph{Di\c{t}\u{a} type}
if it is equivalent, by permutations and multiplication of rows
and columns by scalars of modulus one, to a block matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dita}
D=\begin{pmatrix}
a_{11}B_1 & \cdots & a_{1k}B_k\\
\vdots & & \vdots\\
a_{k1}B_1 & \cdots & a_{kk}B_k
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $A=(a_{ij})$ is a complex Hadamard matrix of size $k>1$
and $B_1,\ldots,B_k$ are complex Hadamard matrices of a common
size $m>1$. This construction generalizes the tensor product $A\otimes B$,
obtained when all $B_j$ coincide.

In order to prove our claim we introduce two invariants similar to~\cite{MR2372303}*{Definition~3.1}. Given an Hadamard matrix $H=(H_{ij})_{i,j}$, denote by $m_1(H)$ the maximal number of distinct ordered pairs $(i,i')$ ($i\ne i'$) such that the row quotients $(H_{ij}/H_{i'j})_j$ are equal up to phase factors. Denote by $m_2(H)$ the maximal number of disjoint ordered pairs $(i,i')$ ($i\ne i'$) with the same property.

Let us compute these invariants for our matrix $H:=(e^{\frac{2\pi i}{q}\Psi(u,v)})_{u,v\in\mathbb F_q^4}$. Since $H_{u,0}=1$, proportional row quotients of $H$ are in fact equal. Comparing coefficients in $\Psi(u,v)-\Psi(u',v)$ it is not too difficult to see that this difference determines $(u,u')$ uniquely unless~$u$ and~$u'$ have the same first three coordinates, namely, one distinguishes successively the cases (a) $x\ne x'$, (b)~$x=x'$ but $r\ne r'$, and finally (c) $(x,r)=(x',r')$ but $s\ne s'$.
Coefficient comparison is valid, since the degree in each column variable is less than $q\ge5$. The remaining quotients to consider~are
$$
 \frac{H_{(x,r,s,t+d),v}}{H_{(x,r,s,t),v}}
 =
 \exp\!\Big(\frac{2\pi i d}{q}
 \Big(T-xS-\frac{x^2R}{2}+ys-\frac{y^2r}{2}\Big)\Big),
 \qquad d\ne0.
$$
Such a row quotient determines $(x,r,s,d)$, so the same quotient occurs exactly $q$ times, with all corresponding pairs of rows contained in the same set
of $q$ rows. It follows that
$$
m_1(H)=q,\qquad m_2(H)=\frac{q-1}{2}.
$$

On the other hand, for the Fourier matrix $F:=(\chi(x))_{x\in B,\chi\in\widehat B}$ of any finite abelian group $B$ we have $m_1(F)=|B|$, while
for any Di\c{t}\u{a}-type matrix~\eqref{eq:Dita} of size $km$ we have $m_2(D)\ge m$, since for any fixed $1\le i<i'\le k$ we have $m$ disjoint row pairs $((i,\alpha),(i',\alpha))$, $1\le\alpha\le m$, with the same entrywise quotient. Therefore if our matrix was equivalent to a Fourier matrix or to a matrix of Di\c{t}\u{a} type, we would have $m_1(H)=q^4$ or $m_2(H)\ge q$, resp. This proves our claim. In fact, the claim remains true for $q=3$, but this case requires a separate argument, which we omit.

\smallskip


Let us finally say a few words about $\mathfrak g$ and $G$, assuming that $q\ge7$. This example is designed to satisfy a couple of special requirements.

Consider the $2$-cocycle $\sigma:=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{q}l_\omega}$. Recall that an isotropic (with respect to the nondegenerate class $[\sigma]$)  subgroup $H\subset G$ is called \emph{Lagrangian} if $|H|=|G|^{1/2}$. By \cite{MR3210715}*{Lemma~2.2}, a normal isotropic subgroup is necessarily abelian. We claim that our group $G$ does not have any normal Lagrangian subgroups. In fact, the order of any normal isotropic subgroup of $G$ is at most $q^3$. Indeed, if $H$ is such a subgroup, then $H=\exp\mathfrak h$ for some abelian ideal $\mathfrak h\subset\mathfrak g$. Then $\exp\colon\mathfrak h\to H$ is an isomorphism and $\exp^*(l_\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\omega$ on $\mathfrak h$. Since $\operatorname{res}^G_H[\sigma]=0$ by assumption, it follows that the ideal $\mathfrak h\subset\mathfrak g$ is isotropic with respect to $\omega$. Observe next that we must have $\mathfrak h\subset\mathfrak a$. In order to see this, consider an element
$$
X=ua_1+vb_1+r\in\mathfrak h,\quad\text{with}\quad r\in\mathfrak a.
$$
Then
$$
0=[X,[X,a_4]] =u(u+2v)a_3+v(v+2u)b_3.
$$
As $q\ne 3$, the equations $u(u+2v)=v(v+2u)=0$ force $u=v=0$. Thus, $\mathfrak h\subset\mathfrak a$. Since the form~$\omega|_{\mathfrak a}$ is nondegenerate, we conclude that $\dim \mathfrak h\le\frac{1}{2}\dim\mathfrak a=3$, hence $|H|\le q^3$, proving our claim. By~\cite{MR2534251}*{Theorem~A} it follows that the nondegenerate class $[\sigma]$ cannot be obtained by the bijective $1$-cocycle method. Note also that \cite{MR3210715}*{Theorem~1.9} (see also~\cite{MR3499032}*{Theorem~8.4}) implies that eight is the minimal dimension where one can hope that there are no Lagrangian ideals, so dimension-wise our $\mathfrak g$ is the smallest possible nilpotent Lie algebra with this property.

The second special property is that the Lagrangian subalgebras $\mathfrak p$ and $\mathfrak n$ have trivial intersections with the center $\mathfrak z(\mathfrak g)={\mathbb F}_q(a_4-b_4)$. Thus neither chosen Lagrangian subalgebra contains a nonzero central ideal along which one could perform symplectic reduction. \nopagebreak\mbox{\ }\hfill$\diamond$
