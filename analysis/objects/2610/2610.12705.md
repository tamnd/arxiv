<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}

There exists a polynomial matrix
\[
\mathcal P\in\operatorname{SL}_2\bigl(\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\ldots,X_N]\bigr)
\]
for some positive integer $N$ such that for every global function field $K$ and every nonempty finite
set of places $S$, the ring $R=\mathcal{O}_{K,S}$ satisfies
\[
\mathcal P(R^{N})=\operatorname{SL}_2(R).
\]
In particular, $\operatorname{SL}_2(R)$ is polynomially parametrized.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:cancel}
For $R=\mathcal{O}_{K,S}$ as above, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cancel}
(1+ab,bc)\sim(1+ab,c).
\end{equation}
The polynomial families realizing this equivalence can be chosen
independently of $K$ and $S$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Both rows are primitive simultaneously. Put $x=1+ab$. If $x$ is zero
or a unit, each primitive row in question has a unit entry, so the
elementary observation above applies. Thus assume that $x$ is nonzero and
nonunit. This, in particular, implies that $a$ is nonzero.

Two changes may be made simultaneously on both sides of
\eqref{eq:cancel}. First, replace $c$ by $c+xd$: the second coordinates
change by $xbd$ and $xd$, respectively. Second, replace $a$ by $a+ce$:
the first coordinate changes by $bce$, an elementary change using the
second coordinate on either side. These use a fixed number of elementary
operations.

We contend that there exists an element $r \in R$ such that $a \equiv -r^2 \pmod{cR}$. 

Suppose first that $\operatorname{char}K=2$. Replacing $c$ by a prime
generator in its residue class modulo $xR$, we see that the residue field $R/cR$
is finite and hence perfect. Consider the Frobenius map $F : R/cR \to R/cR$ that sends each element $u$ to $u^2$. Since $R$ is of characteristic $2$, we see that $F(u) = u^2 = v^2 = F(v)$ implies that $(u - v)^2 = 0$, and hence $u = v$ in the field $R/cR$. Thus $F$ is injective. Since $R/cR$ is finite, $F$ is also surjective, and thus $F$ is an isomorphism. Therefore there exists an element $r \in R$ such that $-a \equiv F(\bar r) \equiv r^2 \pmod{cR}$, and thus $a \equiv - r^2 \pmod{cR}$.

 





 Thus there is $r\in R$ with
$a\equiv-r^2\pmod{cR}$.

Now suppose that $\operatorname{char}K\ne2$. Since $a\ne 0$, there are only finitely many prime divisors of $a$. We first replace $c$ by a
prime generator in its residue class modulo $xR$ that is relatively prime to the prime
divisors of $a$. Then $aR + cR = R$, and thus one can replace $a$ by a prime generator
in its residue class modulo $cR$. Denote the resulting elements by the same symbols $a,c$, respectively, and put $x=1+ab$ again. If the new $x$ is a unit, then we can repeat the same arguments at the beginning of the proof. Otherwise, we observe that the quadratic extension
\[
L=K(\sqrt{-a})
\]
is ramified at the prime $aR$, since $aR$ is prime and the valuation of $-a$ at $aR$
is one (see \cite[Proposition 10.3]{Rosen}). On the other hand, $aR$ is prime to $xR$, and thus the ray class field 
$H_{xR}/K$ is unramified at $aR$. Therefore $L\cap H_{xR}=K$.
The prime-selection observation in Section \ref{sec-selecting-prime-generators} gives a prime generator $\pi$ in $R$ such that
\[
\pi\equiv c\pmod{xR},\qquad \pi R\text{ splits completely in }L,
\]
with $\pi R\ne aR$. Replace $c$ by $\pi$, and rename $\pi$ to be $c$ again. The prime $cR$ splits in $L$ if and only if $-a$ is a
square modulo $cR$. Thus $a\equiv-r^2\pmod{cR}$ for some $r\in R$.

In either characteristic, write $a+r^2=ch$ for some element $h \in R$. Then
\begin{align*}
(1+ab,bc)
&\sim(1-r^2b,bc)\\
&\sim\bigl(1-r^2b,bc-(1-r^2b)bc\bigr)\\
&=\bigl(1-r^2b,(rb)^2c\bigr)\\
&\sim(1-r^2b,c)\\
&\sim(1+ab,c).
\end{align*}
The penultimate equivalence uses \eqref{eq:square} with $u=-r$ and
$v=rb$, and the last equivalence follows from $a + r^2 = ch$ and the following elementary operation
\[
(1 - r^2b, c)E_{21}(bh) = (1 - r^2b + bch, c) = (1 + ab, c).
\]

 Every operation uses a fixed polynomial family. The chosen
primes and square roots affect parameter values only.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:power}

Let $M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{SL}_2(R)$, and let
$(a_n,b_n)$ be the first row of $M^n$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:power}
(a_n,b_n)\sim(a^n,b),\qquad n\ge0.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Define $Q_{-1}(T) = -1$, $Q_0(T)=0$, $Q_1(T)=1$, and
$Q_{j+1}(T)=TQ_j(T)-Q_{j-1}(T)$. Set $u_j=Q_j(\operatorname{tr} M)$ for each $j \ge - 1$. By induction on $j$, we see that
\begin{align}
\label{eq:eq1-in-power-lem}
Q_{j + 1}(T)Q_{j - 1}(T) = Q_j(T)^2 - 1.
\end{align}
for $j \ge 0$. 




By (\ref{eq:eq1-in-power-lem}), there are polynomials $V_n,W_n\in\mathbb{Z}[T]$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factor}
Q_n=V_nW_n,\qquad V_n\mid Q_{n-1}-1,\qquad
W_n\mid Q_{n-1}+1.
\end{equation}
Explicit choices are
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
n&V_n&W_n\\ \hline
2k+1&Q_{k+1}-Q_k&Q_{k+1}+Q_k\\
2k\ (k\ge1)&Q_{k+1}-Q_{k-1}&Q_k.
\end{array}
\]
These identities follow by induction from the recurrence. In particular,
they do not require unique factorization in $R$ and remain valid in
characteristic two.

By the Cayley--Hamilton theorem, and by induction on $n$, we have 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eq2-power}
M^n=u_nM-u_{n-1}I\qquad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}


Set $v=V_n(\operatorname{tr} M)$ and $w=W_n(\operatorname{tr} M)$. Then it follows from (\ref{eq:factor}) and (\ref{eq:eq2-power}) that
\[
b_n=bu_n = bvw,\qquad a_n\equiv-1\pmod{vR},\qquad
a_n\equiv1\pmod{wR}.
\]
 Since for arbitrary elements $\alpha, \beta \in R$, 
\begin{align*}
(\alpha, \beta)(E_{12}(1)E_{21}(-1)E_{12}(1))^2 = (-\alpha, -\beta),
\end{align*}
and thus
\[
(\alpha, \beta) \sim (-\alpha, -\beta).
\]

Hence applying Lemma~\ref{lem:cancel} to cancel $w$, then changing signs and
applying it again to cancel $v$, we obtain 
\[
(a_n,b_n)\sim(a_n,bv)\sim(-a_n,-bv)
\sim(-a_n,-b)\sim(a_n,b).
\]
Modulo $bR$, the matrix $M$ is lower triangular, so $a_n\equiv a^n$
modulo $bR$. One more elementary change proves \eqref{eq:power}.
The case $n=0$ is immediate.

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $A\in\operatorname{SL}_2(R)$. By Trost's first lemma, a modification of $A$, denoted by
$A'$, using elementary matrices  has first row $(a^m,b)$ with $bR$ prime. If $a$ is a unit,
the row can be transformed into $(1, 0)$ by a product of a uniformly bounded number of elementary matrices. The case $a=0$
would force $b$ to be a unit since $aR + bR = R$, and so cannot occur with
$bR$ being prime. Thus assume $a$ is a nonzero nonunit.

Choose $c$ by Trost's second lemma and define
\[
d=\frac{1+bc}{a}\in R,\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{SL}_2(R).
\]
If $c$ is a unit, apply Lemma~\ref{lem:power} to the transpose $B^T$ with exponent
$m$. Its comparison row $(a^m,c)$ has a unit entry, so $(B^T)^m$
and hence $B^m$ are polynomially bounded. The final comparison below
then completes this case. Also $c=0$ is impossible since
$bc\equiv-1\pmod{aR}$ and $aR$ is proper.

Assume therefore that $c$ is a nonzero nonunit. Since
$\gcd(\varepsilon(b),\varepsilon(c))=m$, there are positive integers $s,t$ such that $\varepsilon(b)$ divides $t$, $\varepsilon(c)$ divides $s$, and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exponents}
t-s=m.
\end{equation}





The determinant equation ensures that $a$ is invertible modulo both
$bR$ and $cR$. Consequently
\[
a^t\equiv1\pmod{bR},\qquad a^s\equiv1\pmod{cR}.
\]
A row of the form $(1+zh,z)$ reduces to $(1,0)$ in two elementary
operations. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:power} to $B$ and $B^T$ shows
that $B^t$ and $(B^T)^s$ belong to fixed polynomially bounded families.
Taking transposes and inverses, the same is true of $B^{-s}$.
Therefore
\[
B^m=B^tB^{-s}
\]
belongs to a fixed polynomially bounded set in $\operatorname{SL}_2(R)$.

Finally, Lemma~\ref{lem:power} with exponent $m$ identifies the first
row of $B^m$, up to bounded equivalence, with $(a^m,b)$, the first
row of $A'$. This implies that $A'$ belongs to a fixed polynomially bounded set. Since $A'$ is obtained from $A$, using up to three elementary operations by Trost's first lemma, $A$ also belongs to a fixed polynomially bounded set in $\operatorname{SL}_2(R)$. Since $A$ is arbitrary in $\operatorname{SL}_2(R)$, $\operatorname{SL}_2(R)$ is polynomially parametrized.

All polynomial matrices used have integer coefficients and are drawn
from a fixed finite collection of universal families, their inverses
and transposes, and fixed products. The arithmetic choices, including
$m,s,t$, affect witness values only. The finitely many branches can
be included in one product of families containing the identity.
Thus a single polynomial matrix works for every $K,S$, as asserted.
