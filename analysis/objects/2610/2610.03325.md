\label{subsec:scale_anchor}
        The physics losses chosen in two cases are invariant under positive rescaling of the density. Indeed, for any constant $c>0$,
        \[
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{W}}(cu)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{W}}(u),
        \]
        thanks to $D\nabla(cu)-\bm b(cu)=c(D\nabla u-\bm b u)$. This scale-invariant construction prevents the physics loss from being artificially reduced by simply decreasing the overall amplitude of $u$, thereby removing the dependence of the physics loss on the global scale of \(u\). We emphasize that this does not enforce the probability normalization $\int_\Omega u_\theta\,\mathrm dx=1$; the scale invariance applies only to the physics loss, while the scale of \(u_\theta\) is determined by the MC data pretraining.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{subsec:8d_importance}
		In high dimensions, uniform sampling places many physics points in low-density regions. We therefore use a mixture proposal of a local truncated Gaussian and a uniform distribution to improve sampling efficiency. Define \(q\) as the proposal density for sampling physics points, then we decompose
		\begin{equation}
		q(x)=a q_G(x)+(1-a)q_U(x),
		\qquad q_U(x)=|\Omega|^{-1},
		\end{equation}
        where \(q_G\) denotes the truncated Gaussian density, \(q_U\) is the uniform density over \(\Omega\), and \(a\in[0,1]\) is the mixture weight.  Specifically, its standard deviation along the \(i\)-th coordinate is set as
\[
\sigma_{q,i}=c_q\sqrt{\frac{D}{\lambda_i}},
\]
where $\lambda_i$ characterizes the drift strength near the linear equilibrium point and $c_q$ controls the proposal width. In the high-dimensional implementation, we set $a=0.70$ and $c_q=1.20$.  

We emphasize that since the physics points are sampled from the nonuniform proposal density \(q\), importance weights are introduced to correct the spatial integrals. For \(z_j\sim q\), define
\(w_j=\frac{1}{|\Omega|q(z_j)}.\)
Then, for an integrable function \(f\),
\[	
\int_\Omega f(x)\,\mathrm dx
\approx
\frac{|\Omega|}{N_p}
\sum_{j=1}^{N_p}w_jf(z_j).
\]
The importance correction is applied to all spatial integrals in the physics losses, reducing the variance associated with finite sampling. The detailed procedure is given in the following algorithm.

	\begin{algorithm}[H]
		\caption{High-dimensional importance sampling for physics points}
		\label{alg:importance}
		\footnotesize
		\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Require Point count $N$, box domain $\Omega=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[l_i,h_i]$, diffusion $D$, linear scales $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=1}^{d}$, Gaussian fraction $a$, scale factor $c_q$, seed
		\Ensure Weighted physics points $\{(x_j,w_j)\}_{j=1}^{N}$
		\State Set $\sigma_{q,i}=c_q\sqrt{D/\lambda_i}$ and split $N$ into truncated-Gaussian and uniform proposal counts
		\State Generate the truncated-Gaussian component by mapping scrambled Sobol points through the coordinatewise truncated-Gaussian inverse CDF
		\State Generate the uniform component by scrambled Sobol sampling over $\Omega$ and merge the two point sets
		\For{each generated point $x_j$}
		    \State Evaluate $q_G(x_j)$ and the mixture density $q(x_j)=a q_G(x_j)+(1-a)/|\Omega|$
		    \State Set the integration weight $w_j=1/(|\Omega|q(x_j))$
		\EndFor
		\State Apply a deterministic shuffle so that mini-batches mix the two proposal components
		\State \Return $\{(x_j,w_j)\}_{j=1}^{N}$
		\end{algorithmic}
		\end{algorithm}

\label{thm:spectral}
Assume $\lambda_{\rm W},\lambda_{\rm MC}>0$,
$\mathbb E[e_h]=0$, and
$\operatorname{Cov}(e_h)=\Sigma_h$ with
$0<\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_h)<\infty$.
The unique minimizer of \eqref{app:quadratic-model} is
\begin{equation}\label{app:filter}
 \bar z_h=B_he_h,
 \qquad
 B_h
 =
 \lambda_{\rm MC}
 \bigl(\lambda_{\rm MC}I+\lambda_{\rm W}K_h\bigr)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Its mean-square error satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{app:general-ratio}
 \frac{\mathbb E\|\bar z_h\|_2^2}
      {\mathbb E\|e_h\|_2^2}
 =
 \frac{\operatorname{tr}
       (B_h\Sigma_hB_h^\mathsf T)}
      {\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_h)}
 \leq 1.
\end{equation}
If $\Sigma_h=\zeta_h^2I$ with $\zeta_h>0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{app:isotropic-ratio}
 \frac{\mathbb E\|\bar z_h\|_2^2}
      {\mathbb E\|e_h\|_2^2}
 =
 \frac1{m_h}\sum_{k=1}^{m_h}
 \left(
 \frac{\lambda_{\rm MC}}
      {\lambda_{\rm MC}+\lambda_{\rm W}\mu_{h,k}}
 \right)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{h,1},\ldots,\mu_{h,m_h}$ are the eigenvalues
of $K_h$. Error components corresponding to positive
eigenvalues are reduced, whereas those corresponding
to zero eigenvalues remain unchanged.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The stationary equation for \eqref{app:quadratic-model} is
\[
 \bigl(\lambda_{\rm MC}I+\lambda_{\rm W}K_h\bigr)z
 =
 \lambda_{\rm MC}e_h.
\]
The matrix on the left is positive definite,
so $F_h$ is strictly convex and its unique minimizer
is given by \eqref{app:filter}.

Since $\mathbb E[e_h]=0$, we have
$\mathbb E[\bar z_h]=0$ and
$\operatorname{Cov}(\bar z_h)
=B_h\Sigma_hB_h^\mathsf T$.
Taking traces gives the identity in
\eqref{app:general-ratio}.
Moreover, the eigenvalues of $B_h$ are
$\lambda_{\rm MC}/
(\lambda_{\rm MC}+\lambda_{\rm W}\mu_{h,k})$,
which belong to $(0,1]$. Hence
$\|B_he_h\|_2\leq\|e_h\|_2$, proving the inequality.

If $\Sigma_h=\zeta_h^2I$, then
$\mathbb E\|\bar z_h\|_2^2
=\zeta_h^2\operatorname{tr}(B_h^2)$ and
$\mathbb E\|e_h\|_2^2=m_h\zeta_h^2$.
Summing the squared eigenvalues of $B_h$ gives
\eqref{app:isotropic-ratio}.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
Since $W_h(u_h^*)$ is positive definite,
$\ker K_h=\ker R_h$. If the MC error satisfies
$R_he_h=0$, then \eqref{app:filter} gives
$\bar z_h=e_h$. Thus, adding the weak loss does
not reduce such errors.

In particular, $\Phi_h(cu_h^*)=0$ for every $c>0$,
so the weak loss cannot determine the multiplicative
constant $c$. This constant is determined by the
MC data-fitting term. If
$\ker R_h=\operatorname{span}\{u_h^*\}$,
all errors satisfying $R_he_h=0$ are scalar
multiples of $u_h^*$.

When $\Sigma_h=\zeta_h^2I$, the mean-square error
ratio tends to zero precisely when
\[
  \frac1{m_h}\sum_{k=1}^{m_h}
  \left(
    \frac{\lambda_{\rm MC}}
    {\lambda_{\rm MC}+\lambda_{\rm W}\mu_{h,k}}
  \right)^2
  \longrightarrow0.
\]
Each zero eigenvalue contributes $1/m_h$ to this
sum. Therefore, $\dim(\ker R_h)/m_h\to0$ is
a necessary condition.
