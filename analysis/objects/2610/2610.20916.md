<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:dichotomy}
Let $X$ be a real L\'evy process of infinite variation. Exactly one
of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $J_X(I)<\infty$ for every bounded interval $I$;
\item $J_X(I)=\infty$ for every proper interval $I$.
\end{enumerate}
Equivalently, $X$ is either abrupt or strongly eroded.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:counterexample}
There exist abrupt symmetric pure-jump L\'evy processes for which $\mathfrak{s}_1(0)=\infty$, $\mathfrak{s}_1(r)<\infty$ for $r\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ and $\mathfrak{s}_1\in L^1_\mathrm{loc}(\mathbb{R})$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:cantor-exceptional-slopes}
There exists an abrupt pure-jump L\'evy process of infinite variation such that
\[
E=\{r\in\mathbb{R}:\mathfrak{s}_1(r)=\infty\}
\]
is a compact perfect set of Lebesgue measure zero. In particular, $E$ is uncountable but $\mathfrak{s}_1\in L^1_\mathrm{loc}(\mathbb{R})$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:counterexample}
\textbf{(a)} Theorems~\ref{thm:counterexample} and
\ref{thm:cantor-exceptional-slopes} show two different levels of failure of Vigon's conjecture. Theorem~\ref{thm:counterexample} gives the particularly simple symmetric situation in which the exceptional set is the singleton $\{0\}$, whereas Theorem~\ref{thm:cantor-exceptional-slopes} shows that the exceptional set may instead be uncountable and perfect. Thus, pointwise finiteness of $\mathfrak{s}_1$ can depend on the drift in a substantially more complicated way than local integrability alone suggests.\\
\textbf{(b)} The constructions are not canonical. Their proofs use quantitative separation between successive jump scales, together with corresponding balance conditions on the masses and, in the asymmetric construction, on the drift parameters. These conditions leave considerable freedom in the choice of the defining sequences. Thus, the particular parameters used in Theorems~\ref{thm:counterexample} and~\ref{thm:cantor-exceptional-slopes} are chosen for convenience rather than being intrinsic to the phenomenon. \\
\textbf{(c)} The counterexamples may also be chosen with L\'evy measures having $C^\infty$ densities on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$. This can be achieved by replacing the atoms by suitably chosen smooth bumps, in the spirit of the construction in Section~\ref{subsec:full-index-region}. In the interest of brevity, the details are omitted in the present work.\\
\textbf{(d)} Theorem~\ref{thm:counterexample} has a particularly striking interpretation in terms of point hitting and local times. Since $\mathfrak{s}_1(r)<\infty$ if and only if $X^{(r)}=(X_t-rt)_{t\geqslant0}$ hits points, the process $X$ itself does not hit points and has no local time field, whereas every non-zero linear drift perturbation $X^{(r)}$ hits points and admits a local time field; see the discussion following~\cite[Conj.~1.10]{MR4828847} and the references therein.

More generally, for the process in Theorem~\ref{thm:cantor-exceptional-slopes}, the drift perturbation $X^{(r)}$ fails to hit points precisely for $r\in E$, while it hits points for every $r\notin E$. Hence the existence of a local time field may vary over an uncountable set of deterministic slopes.

This has a natural interpretation in terms of local times along curves: the local time of $X^{(r)}$ at level $a$ is the local time of $X$ along the affine line $t\mapsto a+rt$. Thus, even within the class of straight lines, existence of local times may depend delicately on the slope. This connects the examples with the study of local times along curves. Bass and Burdzy~\cite{MR4901629} establish continuity and uniform boundedness of Brownian local times over bounded H\"older classes with exponent greater than $1/2$, while Holden and Sheffield~\cite{MR4055190} obtain analogous occupation-density estimates for certain Gaussian fields under Lipschitz perturbations. Our examples show that for infinite-variation L\'evy processes even the existence of a local time field need not be stable under deterministic linear perturbations.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:hulls}
Let $X$ be a real L\'evy process of infinite variation. Exactly one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}, leftmargin=2em]
\item $X$ is abrupt, $\mathrm{d} C_T'$ is purely atomic and its atoms are isolated in $(0,T)$ a.s.
\item $X$ is strongly eroded, $\mathrm{d} C_T'$ is diffuse, singular continuous and $C_T$ is continuously differentiable a.s.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, the dichotomy is independent of $T\in(0,\infty)$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
By Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy}, it suffices to show that abrupt L\'evy processes satisfy (i) and strongly eroded processes satisfy (ii). The convex function $C_T$ admits a right-continuous right-derivative $C_T'$ on $(0,T)$. Denote its range (image) by $S_T\subset \mathbb{R}$ and let $\mathcal{L}(S_T)\subset\mathbb{R}$ denote its set of finite limit points. According to~\cite[Thms~1.1 and~1.2]{MR4828847}, abrupt L\'evy processes satisfy $\mathcal{L}(S_T)=\emptyset$ and $\mathrm{d} C_T'$ puts positive mass at all jump times of $C_T'$. If there existed some accumulation $t\in(0,T)$ of jump times $t_n$ of $C_T'$, since $C_T'$ is locally bounded, then $C_T'(t_n)$ would have a convergent subsequence, contradicting $\mathcal{L}(S_T)=\emptyset$. Thus, $\mathrm{d} C_T'$ is purely atomic, and its atoms are isolated. (Note that the atoms do accumulate at $0$ and $T$.) 

Again by~\cite[Thms~1.1 and~1.2]{MR4828847}, for a strongly eroded L\'evy processes, $\mathcal{L}(S_T)=\mathbb{R}$ and $C'_T$ is continuous. Thus, $\mathrm{d} C'_T$ is diffuse. Moreover, the stick-breaking representation in~\cite[Thm~3.1]{MR4448688} shows that the sum of the lengths of maximal intervals of constancy of $C'_T$ (where $C_T'$ is differentiable with $C_T''=0$) equals $T$, making $\mathrm{d} C'_T$ a singular continuous measure.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:smoothed}
Set $C_0=5$, $c_0=1/32$ and $c=1/385$. Then, for every $u>0$ and $v\in[u,2u]$, we have
\begin{align}
\label{eq:HD-derivative}
\max\{|H'(u)|,|D'(u)|\}
&\leqslant u^{-1}H(u),\\
\label{eq:dyadic-HD}
\tfrac{1}{2} H(u)
\leqslant H(v)\leqslant2H(u)
\quad &\text{and}\quad 
|D(v)-D(u)| \leqslant 2(\log 2)H(u),\\
\label{eq:smoothed-approximation}
\sup_{|z|\leqslant1}|\psi(uz)-iuzD(u)|
&\leqslant C_0uH(u),\\
\min\bigg\{\int_{-1}^1 Q(uz)\mathrm{d} z,
\int_1^2 Q(uz)\mathrm{d} z\bigg\}
&\geqslant c_0uH(u)\qquad\text{and}
\label{eq:smoothed-average}\\
cu\bigl(H(u)+|D(u)|\bigr)
\leqslant P(u)
&\coloneqq\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant u}|\psi(v)|
\leqslant u\bigl(|D(u)|+C_0H(u)\big).
\label{eq:P-comparison}
\end{align}
Moreover, the process with characteristic exponent as in~\eqref{eq:compact-exponent} has infinite variation if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variation-H}
 \int_1^\infty H(u)\frac{\mathrm{d} u}{u}=\infty.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Since $\int_{(-1,1)}x^2 \nu(\mathrm{d} x)<\infty$, differentiation under the integrals in~\eqref{eq:HD} is justified on compact subintervals of $(0,\infty)$. First, note that $f:u\mapsto\sigma^2 u$ satisfies $f'(u)=u^{-1}f(u)$. Second, 
\[
f:(x,u)\mapsto \frac{ux^2}{1+u^2x^2}
\quad\text{satisfies}\quad
\bigg|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} u}f(x,u)\bigg| = \bigg|\frac{x^2 (1-u^2x^2)}{(1+u^2x^2)^2}\bigg|
\leqslant u^{-1}f(u,x).
\]
Altogether, $|H'(u)| \leqslant u^{-1}H(u)$ for $u>0$, concluding the first part of~\eqref{eq:HD-derivative}. Next, since $2|ux|\leqslant1+u^2x^2$, then $2u^2|x|^3/(1+u^2x^2)^2 \leqslant ux^2/(1+u^2x^2)$ and hence $|uD'(u)|\leqslant H(u)$ for $u>0$, proving~\eqref{eq:HD-derivative}.

Using~\eqref{eq:HD-derivative}, we find that
\[
\left|\log \frac{H(v)}{H(u)}\right|
=
\left|\int_u^v \frac{H'(t)}{H(t)}\,\mathrm{d} t\right|
\leqslant
\int_u^v\frac{\mathrm{d} t}{t}
=
\log(v/u)
\leqslant\log2,
\] 
which gives the first half of~\eqref{eq:dyadic-HD}. Similarly,~\eqref{eq:HD-derivative} gives $|D'(t)|\leqslant H(t)/t$ and, since $H(t)\leqslant2H(u)$ for every $t\in[u,v]\subset[u,2u]$, the second half of~\eqref{eq:HD-derivative} follows:
\[
|D(v)-D(u)|
\leqslant\int_u^v |D'(t)| \mathrm{d} t \leqslant
\int_u^v\frac{H(t)}{t}\mathrm{d} t \leqslant
2H(u)\int_u^v\frac{\mathrm{d} t}{t} =
2H(u)\log(v/u) \leqslant
2(\log2)H(u).
\]

By definition of $D$, it follows directly, that 
\begin{equation*}
\psi(uz)-iuzD(u)= -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2u^2z^2+ \int_{(-1,1)} \left(e^{iuzx}-1-\frac{iuzx}{1+u^2x^2}\right) \nu(\mathrm{d} x).
\end{equation*} 
Hence, since the Gaussian remainder is numerically at most $\sigma^2u^2/2$, it remains to bound the integrand inside the integral in the expression above. For $|z|\leqslant1$ and $y\in\mathbb{R}$, it follows that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elementary-Taylor}
\left|e^{izy}-1-\frac{izy}{1+y^2}\right|
\leqslant5\frac{y^2}{1+y^2}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, for $|y|\leqslant1$, subtracting and adding $izy$, and applying the triangle inequality, implies that
\begin{equation*}
\left|e^{izy}-1-\frac{izy}{1+y^2}\right| 
\leqslant |e^{izy}-1-izy|+|zy|\left|1-\frac{1}{1+y^2}\right| 
\leqslant \frac{y^2}{2}+\frac{|y|^3}{1+y^2}
\leqslant \frac{3y^2}{2}.
\end{equation*}
For $|y|>1$, the triangle inequality implies directly, that $|e^{izy}-1-izy/(1+y^2)| \leqslant 2+|y|/(1+y^2)\leqslant 5/2$, and since $y^2/(1+y^2) \geqslant 1/2$ for all $|y|>1$, we can conclude~\eqref{eq:elementary-Taylor}. Substituting $y=ux$ in~\eqref{eq:compact-exponent} and applying~\eqref{eq:elementary-Taylor}, proves~\eqref{eq:smoothed-approximation}.

Put $\operatorname{sinc} y=\sin(y)/y$ for $y\ne0$, with $\operatorname{sinc}0=1$. Note, that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sinc-bound}
1-|\operatorname{sinc} y|\geqslant\frac18\frac{y^2}{1+y^2}, \quad \text{for all }y \in \mathbb{R}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $|y|\leqslant2$, then $\operatorname{sinc} y\geqslant0$ and $1-|\operatorname{sinc} y|=1-\operatorname{sinc} y$. Hence, the alternating Taylor bound gives $1-\operatorname{sinc} y
\geqslant y^2/6- y^4/120
\geqslant 2y^2/15
\geqslant (1/8)y^2/(1+y^2)$. If $|y|\geqslant2$, then $|\operatorname{sinc} y|
=|\sin y|/|y|
\leqslant |y|^{-1}
\leqslant1/2$, and therefore $1-|\operatorname{sinc} y|
\geqslant 1/2
\geqslant(1/8)y^2/(1+y^2)$. Hence,~\eqref{eq:sinc-bound} implies for all $y \in \mathbb{R}$, that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ineq_cos_sinc}
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^1(1-\cos(zy))\mathrm{d} z
&=2(1-\operatorname{sinc} y)
\geqslant\frac14\frac{y^2}{1+y^2},\quad \text{and}\\
\int_1^2(1-\cos(zy))\mathrm{d} z
&=1-\cos(3y/2)\operatorname{sinc}(y/2)
\geqslant\frac1{32}\frac{y^2}{1+y^2}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Since $Q(u)=\sigma^2u^2/2+\int_{(-1,1)} (1-\cos(ux))\nu(\mathrm{d} x)$, Tonelli's theorem, together with~\eqref{eq:ineq_cos_sinc} proves~\eqref{eq:smoothed-average} for the jump part, where the Gaussian average can be calculated directly to be $\sigma^2u^2/3$ and $7\sigma^2u^2/6$, so the same constant $c_0$ works for both parts, concluding both estimates in~\eqref{eq:smoothed-average}.

For the lower bound in~\eqref{eq:P-comparison}, the first average in~\eqref{eq:smoothed-average} gives  $c_0uH(u)
\leqslant \int_{-1}^1Q(zu)\mathrm{d} z
\leqslant 2P(u)$, while~\eqref{eq:smoothed-approximation} at $z=1$ gives $u|D(u)|\leqslant P(u)+C_0uH(u)$. Thus,
\[
u(|D(u)|+H(u))
=u|D(u)|-C_0uH(u)
+(1+C_0)uH(u)
\leqslant P(u) + 2\frac{1+C_0}{c_0}P(u)
=\frac{2+2C_0+c_0}{c_0}P(u),
\]
implying the lower bound in~\eqref{eq:P-comparison}. The upper bound in~\eqref{eq:P-comparison} follows by taking the supremum in~\eqref{eq:smoothed-approximation}.

If $\sigma=0$, another application of Tonelli gives
\[
 \int_1^\infty H(u)\frac{\mathrm{d} u}{u}
 =\int_{(-1,1)}|x|
       \left(\frac\pi2-\arctan|x|\right)\nu(\mathrm{d} x),
\] where we know that $(\pi/2-\arctan|x|) \in [\pi/4,\pi/2]$ for all $|x|<1$. If $\sigma>0$, the Gaussian contribution to the integral is infinite. The usual finite-variation criterion, i.e. that the process has finite variation if and only if $\sigma=0$ and $\int_{(-1,1)}|x|\nu(\mathrm{d} x)<\infty$, therefore proves~\eqref{eq:variation-H}; see~\cite{MR1406564,Sato1999}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:energy-escape}
If $X$ has infinite variation and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-energy}
 \int_1^\infty\frac{H(u)}{1+H(u)^2+D(u)^2}
                      \frac{\mathrm{d} u}{u}<\infty,
\end{equation}
then $H(u)+|D(u)|\to\infty$ and $P(u)/u\to\infty$ as $u \to \infty$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
For $s\geqslant0$, define locally within this proof $R(s)=(H(e^s)^2+D(e^s)^2)^{1/2}$. Since \(H,D\in C^1((0,\infty))\) and \(H(e^s)>0\), the vector \((H(e^s),D(e^s))\) never vanishes. Hence, since  \(R(s)=\sqrt{H(e^s)^2+D(e^s)^2}\) is continuously differentiable on \([0,\infty)\). Inequality~\eqref{eq:HD-derivative}, implies that, for all $s \geqslant 0$, 
\[
|R'(s)|=
\left|\frac{
H(e^s)\,e^sH'(e^s)
+
D(e^s)\,e^sD'(e^s)
}{
\sqrt{H(e^s)^2+D(e^s)^2}}\right|
\leqslant\sqrt2\,H(e^s),\quad \text{and}\quad 
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d} }{\mathrm{d} s}\arctan R(s)\right|
\leqslant\frac{\sqrt2 H(e^s)}{1+R(s)^2}.
\]
Condition~\eqref{eq:finite-energy} makes $\varpi:s \mapsto \arctan R(s)$ be of finite total variation on $[0,\infty)$ since $\int_0^\infty |\varpi'(s)| \mathrm{d} s<\infty$. In particular, $|\varpi(t)-\varpi(s)| \leqslant \int_s^t |\varpi'(r)| \mathrm{d} r$ for \(t>s\), and the right-hand side tends to \(0\) as \(s\to\infty\), uniformly in \(t\geqslant s\). Thus, $\varpi$ admits a limit in $[0,\pi/2]$ at infinity. A limit strictly less than $\pi/2$ would make $R$ eventually bounded, and therefore~\eqref{eq:finite-energy} would imply $\int_0^\infty H(e^s)\mathrm{d} s<\infty$, contradicting~\eqref{eq:variation-H}. Thus, $R(s)\to\infty$. The first conclusion $H(u)+|D(u)| \to \infty$ as $u \to \infty$ follows directly, where $P(u)/u\to \infty$ as $u \to \infty$ follows from~\eqref{eq:P-comparison}, concluding the proof.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:interval-escape}
Suppose $X$ has infinite variation and $\int_1^\infty\Phi_I(u)u^{-1}\mathrm{d} u<\infty$ for an interval $I$. Then, $P(u)/u\to\infty$. For every $K>0$, the first exit radius $\rho_K(u)=\inf\{v>0:|\psi(v)|=Ku\}$ is finite for all sufficiently large $u$ and satisfies $\rho_K(u)=\mathrm{o}(u)$ for $u \to \infty$ and $P(\rho_K(u))=Ku$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
For $u\geqslant1$ and $v\in[u,2u]$, applying~\eqref{eq:smoothed-approximation} yields $|\psi(v)-ivD(v)|\leqslant C_0vH(v)$. Since $\psi(v)-ivD(v) = -Q(v)+i(B(v)-vD(v))$, we obtain $Q(v)/v\leqslant C_0H(v)$ and $|b(v)-D(v)|\leqslant C_0H(v)$. Together with~\eqref{eq:dyadic-HD}, this yields, uniformly for
$v\in[u,2u]$,
\[
a(v)
=
\frac{1+Q(v)}v
\leqslant 1+2C_0H(u), \quad \text{and}\quad 
|b(v)|
\leqslant
|D(u)|+2(\log2)H(u)+2C_0H(u).
\]
Hence, there exists a constant $C_I<\infty$ such that, uniformly for $r\in I$ and $v\in[u,2u]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dyadic-Ab-bound}
 a(v)^2+(r-b(v))^2
 \leqslant
 C_I\bigl(1+H(u)^2+D(u)^2\bigr).
\end{equation}

Since $a(v)\geqslant Q(v)/v$, it follows by~\eqref{eq:dyadic-Ab-bound}, that
\[
\Phi_I(v)
=
\int_I\frac{a(v)}
{a(v)^2+(r-b(v))^2}\,\mathrm{d} r
\geqslant
\frac{|I|\,Q(v)}
{v\,C_I\bigl(1+H(u)^2+D(u)^2\bigr)}.
\]
With the change of variables $v=uz$,~\eqref{eq:smoothed-average} gives 
\[
\int_u^{2u}Q(v)\frac{\mathrm{d} v}{v^2}
\geqslant \frac{1}{4u^2}\int_u^{2u}Q(v)\mathrm{d} v
=\frac{1}{4u}\int_1^2Q(uz)\,\mathrm{d} z
\geqslant \frac{c_0}{4}H(u),
\]
and hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dyadic-Phi-lower}
\int_u^{2u}\Phi_I(v)\frac{\mathrm{d} v}{v}
\geqslant
\frac{|I|c_0H(u)}
{4C_I\bigl(1+H(u)^2+D(u)^2\bigr)}.
\end{equation}

Integrating~\eqref{eq:dyadic-Phi-lower} with respect to $u^{-1}\mathrm{d} u$ and applying Tonelli's theorem yields
\begin{equation*}
    \int_1^\infty
\frac{H(u)}{1+H(u)^2+D(u)^2}\frac{\mathrm{d} u}{u}
\leqslant
\frac{4C_I}{|I|c_0}
\int_1^\infty\int_u^{2u}
\Phi_I(v)\frac{\mathrm{d} v}{v}\frac{\mathrm{d} u}{u} \leqslant
\frac{4C_I\log2}{|I|c_0}
\int_1^\infty\Phi_I(v)\frac{\mathrm{d} v}{v}
<\infty.
\end{equation*}
Thus,~\eqref{eq:finite-energy} holds, and Lemma~\ref{lem:energy-escape} implies $P(u)/u \to \infty$ as $u \to \infty$.

Fix now $K>0$ and $\theta>0$. Since $P(u)/u\to\infty$ as $u \to \infty$, it follows that $P(\theta u)>Ku$ for all sufficiently large $u$. Hence, there exists $w$ with $|w|\leqslant\theta u$ and $|\psi(w)|>Ku$. Since $\psi(0)=0$, $|\psi(-v)|=|\psi(v)|$, and $\psi$ is continuous, the intermediate value theorem gives some $v\in(0,|w|]$ such that $|\psi(v)|=Ku$. Thus $\rho_K(u)$ is finite and $\rho_K(u)\leqslant\theta u$ for all sufficiently large $u$. Since $\theta>0$ is arbitrary, $\rho_K(u)=\mathrm{o}(u)$ as $u \to \infty$. Finally, by the definition of the first exit radius and continuity, we have $|\psi(v)|<Ku$ for all $0\leqslant v<\rho_K(u)$ and $|\psi(\rho_K(u))|=Ku$. Therefore, using $|\psi(-v)|=|\psi(v)|$, we may conclude $P(\rho_K(u))=Ku$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:increment}
For all $u,v \in \mathbb{R}$, we have
\begin{gather}
\label{eq:increment}
|\psi(u+v)-\psi(u)-\psi(v)|
\leqslant2\sqrt{Q(u)Q(v)}
\quad\text{and}\\
\label{eq:Q-increment}
\bigl(\sqrt{Q(v)}-\sqrt{Q(u)}\bigr)^2
\leqslant Q(u+v)
\leqslant\bigl(\sqrt{Q(v)}+\sqrt{Q(u)}\bigr)^2.
\end{gather}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Using the L\'evy--Khintchine representation, the drift terms cancel and
\begin{equation*}
    \psi(u+v)-\psi(u)-\psi(v) =
-\sigma^2uv
+
\int_{(-1,1)}
(e^{iux}-1)(e^{ivx}-1)\nu(\mathrm{d} x).
\end{equation*}
Hence, by the triangle inequality and applying Cauchy--Schwarz twice, it follows for all $u,v \in \mathbb{R}$, that
\begin{align*}
\left|\psi(u+v)-\psi(u)-\psi(v)\right|
&\leqslant
\sigma^2|uv|
+
\bigg(\int_{(-1,1)}|e^{iux}-1|^2\nu(\mathrm{d} x)\bigg)^{1/2}
\bigg(\int_{(-1,1)}|e^{ivx}-1|^2\nu(\mathrm{d} x)\bigg)^{1/2}\\
&\leqslant
\bigg(
\sigma^2u^2+
\int_{(-1,1)}|e^{iux}-1|^2\nu(\mathrm{d} x)
\bigg)^{1/2}
\bigg(
\sigma^2v^2+
\int_{(-1,1)}|e^{ivx}-1|^2\nu(\mathrm{d} x)
\bigg)^{1/2}.
\end{align*}
Since $|e^{iy}-1|^2=2(1-\cos y)$, and $Q(u) =-\Re\psi(u) =\sigma^2u^2/2 +\int_{(-1,1)}(1-\cos(ux))\nu(\mathrm{d} x)$, we have $\sigma^2u^2 +\int_{(-1,1)}|e^{iux}-1|^2\nu(\mathrm{d} x) =2Q(u)$ and similarly for $v$. Thus,~\eqref{eq:increment} follows.

Since $\Re\psi(w)=-Q(w)$,~\eqref{eq:increment} gives $|Q(u+v)-Q(u)-Q(v)| \leqslant 2\sqrt{Q(u)Q(v)}$. Thus, 
\[
                                       
Q(u)+Q(v)-2\sqrt{Q(u)Q(v)}
\leqslant
Q(u+v)
\leqslant
Q(u)+Q(v)+2\sqrt{Q(u)Q(v)}.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:local-average}
Suppose $P(u)/u\to\infty$. For each interval $I$ and $M>0$, there exist $c>0$ and $U<\infty$ such that, whenever $u\geqslant U$ satisfies $|\psi(u)|\leqslant Mu$, there exists some $r\in(0, 1/10]$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-average}
 \frac1{ru}\int_{(1-r)u}^{(1+r)u}\Phi_I(w)\,\mathrm{d} w\geqslant c.
\end{equation}

Write $I=(a,b)$, $L=b-a$, $R=\max\{1,|a|,|b|\}$ and $K=4(M+R+1)$. Fix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta-epsilon}
 \delta=\min\left\{1,\frac{K}{4C_0},\frac{L}{256C_0}\right\},
 \quad \text{and}\quad 
 \varepsilon=\min\left\{1,\frac{c_0\delta}{16},
                            \frac{L}{256}\right\}.
\end{equation}
All constants below are independent of the frequency $u$. We split the proof of~\eqref{eq:local-average} into two parts, according to the size of $Q(u)/u$.

\textbf{Part 1.} Suppose $Q(u)/u\geqslant\varepsilon$. For $u\geqslant1$, it follows by assumptions that $a(u)=(1+Q(u))/u\in[\varepsilon,M+1]$ and $|b(u)|=|B(u)|/u\leqslant M$, so
\[
 \Phi_I(u)\geqslant
 \frac{L\varepsilon}{(M+1)^2+(R+M)^2}\eqqcolon c_1>0.
\] The constant $c_1$ depends only on $I$, $M$ and $\varepsilon$, and in particular is independent of $u$. Since $w\mapsto\Phi_I(w)$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$, for each such $u$ there exists $\eta_u>0$ such that $\Phi_I(w)\geqslant c_1/2$ whenever $|w-u|<2\eta_u$. Set  $r=\min\{\eta_u/u,1/10\}>0$ note that $\Phi_I(w)\geqslant c_1/2$ for $w\in J_u\coloneqq ((1-r)u,(1+r)u)$, and hence $\int_{J_u}\Phi_I(w)\,\mathrm{d} w\geqslant c_1ru$. Thus,~\eqref{eq:local-average} holds in the regime $Q(u)/u\geqslant\varepsilon$, with a constant independent of $u$.

\textbf{Part 2.}
It remains to consider the case $Q(u)/u<\varepsilon$.
Set
\[
 \chi_u(z)=\frac{\psi(\rho_K(u) z)}u,\quad
 q_u(z)=-\Re\chi_u(z),\quad
 T(u)=\frac{\rho_K(u) H(\rho_K(u))}u,\quad \text{and}\quad 
 d(u)=\frac{\rho_K(u) D(\rho_K(u))}u.
\]
Since $P(t)/t\to\infty$, the same argument as in Lemma~\ref{lem:interval-escape} shows that, for all sufficiently large $u$, the first exit radius is finite and $\rho_K(u)=o(u)$, where $P(\rho_K(u))=Ku$. In particular, $\rho_K(u)>0$, and hence $|\chi_u(z)|\leqslant K$ for all $|z|\leqslant 1$ and $|\chi_u(1)|=K$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:smoothed} with frequency $\rho_K(u)$, gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chi-estimates}
 \sup_{|z|\leqslant1}|\chi_u(z)-idz|\leqslant C_0T(u),
 \quad\text{and}\quad 
 \int_{-1}^1q_u(z)\,\mathrm{d} z\geqslant c_0T(u).
\end{equation}
Moreover, Lemma~\ref{lem:increment}, applied to $u$ and $\rho_K(u) z$, yields a $\widetilde E_u(z)$, such that
\[
 \psi(u+\rho_K(u) z)=\psi(u)+\psi(\rho_K(u) z)+\widetilde E_u(z),
 \quad \text{where} \quad 
 |\widetilde E_u(z)|
 \leqslant2\sqrt{Q(u)Q(\rho_K(u) z)}.
\]
Since $Q(\rho_K(u) z)=u q_u(z)$ and $Q(u)/u<\varepsilon$, division by $u$ implies the existence of a $E_u(z)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:translated-chi}
 \frac{\psi(u+\rho_K(u) z)}u
 =
 \frac{\psi(u)}u+\chi_u(z)+E_u(z),
 \quad \text{where}\quad 
 |E_u(z)|\leqslant2\sqrt{\varepsilon q_u(z)}.
\end{equation}

Since $\rho_K(u)/u\to0$, we may take a constant $U>0$, and increase it so that, for all $u\geqslant U$ under consideration, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:large-u-average}
 u^{-1}\leqslant \frac{L}{256},
 \quad \text{and}\quad 
 \theta(u)\coloneqq\frac{\rho_K(u)}{u}
 \leqslant\min\left\{\frac1{10},\frac{L}{256R}\right\}.
\end{equation}
We use throughout the remainder of the proof $J_u=(u-\rho_K(u),u+\rho_K(u))$ and $r=\rho_K(u)/u$. By~\eqref{eq:large-u-average}, $r\leqslant 1/10$ and hence $J_u\subset(0,\infty)$. To prove~\eqref{eq:local-average}, it remains only to prove a uniform positive lower bound for the average of $\Phi_I$ over $J_u$. We will split in cases according to the size of $T(u)$.

\emph{Case 1.} Assume $T(u)\geqslant\delta$. Since $q_u(z)\geqslant0$, we have that $q_u(z)\leqslant|\chi_u(z)|\leqslant K$, and $\int_{-1}^1q_u(z)\,\mathrm{d} z\geqslant c_0 T(u)\geqslant c_0\delta$, where the set $ V=\{z\in[-1,1]: q_u(z)\geqslant c_0\delta/4\}$ has uniformly positive measure. Indeed,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:V-case1-size}
c_0\delta
\leqslant \int_{-1}^1q_u(z)\,\mathrm{d} z
\leqslant K|V|+\frac{c_0\delta}{4}(2-|V|)
\leqslant K|V|+\frac{c_0\delta}{2},
\quad\text{implying}\quad
|V|\geqslant\frac{c_0\delta}{2K}>0.
\end{equation}

For $z\in V$, the lower bound in~\eqref{eq:Q-increment} together with the bound $\varepsilon\leqslant c_0\delta/16$, gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bound_Q_rho_delta}
\frac{Q(u+\rho_K(u) z)}u
\geqslant
\big(\sqrt{q_u(z)}-\sqrt{Q(u)/u}\big)^2
\geqslant
\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{c_0\delta}{4}}-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\bigg)^2
\geqslant\frac{c_0\delta}{16}.
\end{equation}

We next obtain uniform upper bounds for $a(v)$ and $b(v)$ on the whole interval $J_u$. For $|z|\leqslant1$, the upper bound in~\eqref{eq:Q-increment}, together with $q_u(z)\leqslant K$, gives $Q(u+\rho_K(u) z)/u \leqslant(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\sqrt K)^2$. Since $(u+\rho_K(u) z)/u=1+\theta(u) z\in[0.9,1.1]$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A_up_unif_bound}
    a(u+\rho_K(u) z)
 \leqslant
 \frac{1+(\sqrt\varepsilon+\sqrt K)^2}{0.9}.
\end{equation}
Similarly,~\eqref{eq:translated-chi}, $|\psi(u)|/u\leqslant M$, $|\chi_u(z)|\leqslant K$, and $q_u(z)\leqslant K$ imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:b_up_unif_bound}
    |b(u+\rho_K(u) z)|
 \leqslant
 \frac{M+K+2\sqrt{\varepsilon K}}{0.9}.
\end{equation}
On the set $V$, the lower bound for $Q$ in~\eqref{eq:bound_Q_rho_delta} also gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A_low_unif_bound}
     a(u+\rho_K(u) z)
 \geqslant
 \frac{Q(u+\rho_K(u) z)}{u+\rho_K(u) z}
 \geqslant\frac{c_0\delta}{20}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, there exists a constant $c_1>0$, depending only on $I,M,K,\delta$ and $c_0,C_0$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi-case1-lower}
 \Phi_I(u+\rho_K(u) z)\geqslant c_1,
 \quad \text{for all } z\in V.
\end{equation}
Indeed, on $V$ the numerator $a(u+\rho_K(u) z)$ is uniformly bounded below as in~\eqref{eq:A_low_unif_bound}, whereas~\eqref{eq:A_up_unif_bound} and~\eqref{eq:b_up_unif_bound} implies that both $a(u+\rho_K(u) z)$ and $|b(u+\rho_K(u) z)|$ are uniformly bounded above.

Changing variables $w=u+\rho_K(u) z$ and using~\eqref{eq:V-case1-size}--\eqref{eq:Phi-case1-lower}, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac1{|J_u|}\int_{J_u}\Phi_I(w)\,\mathrm{d} w
 &=
 \frac12\int_{-1}^1\Phi_I(u+\rho_K(u) z)\,\mathrm{d} z \geqslant
 \frac{c_1|V|}{2}
 \geqslant
 \frac{c_1c_0\delta}{4K}>0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus,~\eqref{eq:local-average} holds uniformly in this case.

\emph{Case 2.} Assume $T(u)<\delta$. The first estimate in~\eqref{eq:chi-estimates} now shows that $\chi_u$ is uniformly close to the purely imaginary linear function $z\mapsto idz$. Since $|\chi_u(1)|=K$, it follows that $||d(u)|-K| \leqslant|\chi_u(1)-id(u)| \leqslant C_0\delta$. By the choice $\delta\leqslant K/(4C_0)$, we may in particular use the coarser bounds $K/2\leqslant|d(u)|\leqslant 3K/2$. Moreover, because $idz$ is purely imaginary,
\[
 0\leqslant q_u(z)=-\Re\chi_u(z)
 \leqslant|\chi_u(z)-idz|
 \leqslant C_0\delta,
 \quad \text{for all } |z|\leqslant1.
\]

Put $b_0=B(u)/u$, so that $|b_0|\leqslant M$, and define
\[
 V= \{z \in \mathbb{R}:
 b_0+d(u)z\in
 [a+ L/4,b- L/4 ]\}.
\]
This is an interval of length $|V|=L/(2|d(u)|) \geqslant L/(3K)$. Note that $V \subset [-1,1]$, since if $z\in V$, then $|b_0+dz|\leqslant R$, and hence $|z| \leqslant (M+R)/|d(u)| \leqslant2(M+R)/K<1$ by the definition $K=4(M+R+1)$.

\[
 |b(w)-(b_0+d(u)z)|
 \leqslant
 \frac{C_0\delta
       +2\sqrt{\varepsilon C_0\delta}
       +\theta(u) R}
      {1-\theta(u)} \leqslant \frac{L}{8}.
\]
Furthermore, the upper bound in~\eqref{eq:Q-increment} and $q_u(z)\leqslant C_0\delta$, together with~\eqref{eq:delta-epsilon} and~\eqref{eq:large-u-average}, yields
\[
 a(w)
 \leqslant
 \frac{u^{-1}
       +(\sqrt\varepsilon+\sqrt{C_0\delta})^2}
      {1-\theta(u)} \leqslant \frac{L}{8}.
\]
Since $b_0+d(u)z\in[a+L/4,b-L/4]$, it follows altogether that $b(w)-a\geqslant L/8$, $ b-b(w)\geqslant L/8$, and $ a(w)\leqslant L/8$. Using the explicit form of the integral in~\eqref{eq:Phi}, yields that 
\[
\Phi_I(w)
=\arctan\bigg(\frac{b-b(w)}{a(w)}\bigg)
+\arctan\bigg(\frac{b(w)-a}{a(w)}\bigg),
\]
and therefore each arctangent is at least $\arctan(1)=\pi/4$. Hence, $\Phi_I(u+\rho_K(u) z)\geqslant \pi/2$ for all $z \in V$. Changing variables $w=u+\rho_K(u)z$, then yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac1{|J_u|}\int_{J_u}\Phi_I(w)\,\mathrm{d} w
 &=
 \frac12\int_{-1}^1\Phi_I(u+\rho_K(u) z)\,\mathrm{d} z 
 \geqslant
 \frac{\pi}{4}|V|
 \geqslant
 \frac{\pi L}{12K}>0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus,~\eqref{eq:local-average} also holds uniformly in Case~2.

\label{lem:covering}
Let $f:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be measurable with $\int_0^\infty f(u)u^{-1}\mathrm{d} u<\infty$. Fix $c>0$ and $\delta\in(0,1)$. If for every $u$ in a Borel set $E$, there exists $r\in(0,\delta/5]$ with $\int_{(1-r)u}^{(1+r)u}f(w)\,\mathrm{d} w\geqslant cru$, then $ \int_Eu^{-1}\mathrm{d} u<\infty$.

Fix $n\geqslant 2$ and consider the family $F_n=\{J_u:\ u\in E\cap[1/n,n]\}$, where $J_u=(u-ru,u+ru)$. Since each interval $J_u$ has radius at most $u\delta/5\leqslant n\delta/5$, the radii of the intervals in $F_n$ are uniformly bounded. By the $5r$-covering theorem~\cite[Thm~2.1]{MR1333890}, there exists a finite or countable pairwise disjoint subfamily $((1-r_k)u_k,(1+r_k)u_k)_{k \in \mathcal K}$ of $F_n$ with $\mathcal K \subset \mathbb{N}$, such that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fivefold-cover}
E\cap[1/n,n]\subset \bigcup_{k \in \mathcal K} ((1-5r_k)u_k,(1+5r_k)u_k).
\end{equation}

Let $J_{k}=((1-r_k)u_k,(1+r_k)u_k)$. Every $w\in ((1-5r_k)u_k,(1+5r_k)u_k)$ satisfies $w\geqslant (1-\delta)u_k$, so
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fivefold-weight}
\int_{(1-5r_k)u_k}^{(1+5r_k)u_k}\frac{\mathrm{d} w}{w}
\leqslant\frac{10}{1-\delta}r_k.
\end{equation}
Since $w\leqslant (1+r_k)u_k\leqslant (1+\delta/5)u_k$ for $w\in J_k$, the assumption $\int_{J_k} f(w)\,\mathrm{d} w\geqslant cr_ku_k$ implies that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:J-weight}
\int_{J_k} f(w)\frac{\mathrm{d} w}{w}
\geqslant
\frac{1}{(1+\delta/5)u_k}\int_{J_k}f(w)\,\mathrm{d} w
\geqslant
\frac{cr_k}{1+\delta/5}.
\end{equation}
Combining~\eqref{eq:fivefold-weight} and~\eqref{eq:J-weight} gives $\int_{(1-5r_k)u_k}^{(1+5r_k)u_k} w^{-1}\mathrm{d} w \leqslant C\int_{J_k} f(w)w^{-1}\mathrm{d} w$ for $C\coloneqq(10+2\delta)/(c(1-\delta))$.

Using~\eqref{eq:fivefold-cover} and the pairwise disjointness of the intervals $J_{k}$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{E\cap[1/n,n]}\frac{\mathrm{d} w}{w}
\leqslant
\sum_{k \in \mathcal K}\int_{(1-5r_k)u_k}^{(1+5r_k)u_k}\frac{\mathrm{d} w}{w} 
\leqslant
C\sum_{k \in \mathcal K}\int_{J_{k}}f(u)\frac{\mathrm{d} u}{u} \leqslant
C\int_0^\infty f(u)\frac{\mathrm{d} u}{u}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The right-hand side is finite and independent of $n$. Since $E\cap[1/n,n]\uparrow E$ as $n \to \infty$, the monotone convergence theorem yields $\int_E u^{-1}\mathrm{d} u\leqslant C\int_0^\infty f(u) u^{-1}\mathrm{d} u <\infty$, which concludes the proof.

\Re\frac{1}{1+iur-\psi(u)}
=\frac{1+Q(u)}
{|1+iur-\psi(u)|^2},
\]
it follows that, uniformly in $|r|\leqslant R$, 
\[
\frac{4}{9}\frac{1+Q(u)}{|\psi(u)|^2}
\leqslant
\Re\frac{1}{1+iur-\psi(u)}
\leqslant
4\frac{1+Q(u)}{|\psi(u)|^2}.
\]

\leqslant

Thus, the set $E \coloneqq \{u \geqslant 1 : |r - b(u)| < \delta\}$ intersects $\tilde I_n$ for only finitely many $n$, and is contained in the union of the intervals $J_n \coloneqq [2^{-n} a_n^{-1}, 2^n a_n^{-1}]$ together with the finitely many intervals $\tilde I_n$ that it does intersect. Since $\mathfrak{z}_0 < \infty$, Lemma~\ref{lem:z1}(ii) will imply that $\mathfrak{s}_1(r) < \infty$ as soon as we show that $\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathfrak{z}(J_n)<\infty$, which would complete the proof. In fact, it suffices to show that $\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathfrak{z}(J_n^l)$ for $l\in\{1,2\}$, where $J_n^1\coloneqq[2^{-n} a_n^{-1}, a_n^{-1}]$ and $J_n^2\coloneqq[a_n^{-1}, 2^n a_n^{-1}]$.

Let us bound $\mathfrak{z}(J_n^1)$. For $u\in J_n^1=[2^{-n} a_n^{-1}, a_n^{-1}]$, the bound $1-\cos z\geqslant z^2/4$ for $|z|\leqslant 1$ yields
\[
1+Q(u)
\geqslant
\lambda_{n}\bigl(1-\cos(a_{n}u)\bigr)
\geqslant \frac{1}{4}\lambda_{n}a_{n}^2u^2 
=\frac{1}{4} v_{n}u^2
\geqslant \frac{1}{4} 4^{-n} \lambda_n.
\]
Thus, we have the summable upper bound
\[
\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathfrak{z}(J_n^1)
=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\int_{J_n^1} \frac{\mathrm{d} u}{1+Q(u)} 
\leqslant \sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{4\cdot 4^n}{\lambda_n a_n} 
= \sum_{n\geqslant 1}4\cdot 2^n e^{-(3/2)4^n}
<\infty.
\]

For $u\in J_n^2=[a_n^{-1}, 2^n a_n^{-1}]$, we have $u\leqslant 2^na_n^{-1}\leqslant a_{n+1}^{-1}$. The bound $1-\cos z\geqslant z^2/4$ for $|z|\leqslant 1$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:step5-Q-lower}
1+Q(u) 
\geqslant \lambda_n(1-\cos(a_nu))
+\lambda_{n+1}(1-\cos(a_{n+1}u))
\geqslant \lambda_n(1-\cos(a_nu))+\frac{1}{4}v_{n+1}u^2,
\quad u\in J_n^2.
\end{equation}
Partition the interval $[a_n^{-1}, 2^n a_n^{-1}]$ into $[ja_n^{-1},(j+1)a_n^{-1}]$ for $j\in\{1,\ldots,2^n\}$. For $u\in [ja_n^{-1},(j+1)a_n^{-1}]$, 
\[
\frac{1}{4} v_{n+1} u^2 
\geqslant \frac{1}{4} j^2 v_{n+1}a_n^{-2} 
\eqqcolon q_j.
\]
Using~\eqref{eq:step5-Q-lower} and applying Lemma~\ref{lem:periodic} yields:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:step5-single-period}
\int_{ja_n^{-1}}^{(j+1)a_n^{-1}} \frac{\mathrm{d} u}{1 + Q(u)} 
\leqslant \int_{ja_n^{-1}}^{ja_n^{-1}+2\pi a_n^{-1}} \frac{\mathrm{d} u}{q_j + \lambda_n \bigl(1 - \cos(a_n u)\bigr)} 
=\frac{2\pi a_n^{-1}}{\sqrt{q_j (q_j + 2\lambda_n)}}.
\end{equation}
Since $q_j + 2\lambda_n \geqslant 2\lambda_n$, we get $\sqrt{q_j (q_j + 2\lambda_n)} \geqslant \sqrt{2 \lambda_n q_j}$, so the upper bound in~\eqref{eq:step5-single-period} satisfies
\[
\frac{2\pi a_n^{-1}}{\sqrt{q_j (q_j + 2\lambda_n)}} 
\leqslant \frac{2\sqrt{2}\pi a_n^{-1}}{\sqrt{j^2\lambda_nv_{n+1}a_n^{-2}}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\pi}{j \sqrt{\lambda_n v_{n+1}}} 
= \frac{2\sqrt{2}\pi}{j 2^n}
\leqslant \frac{9}{j 2^n}.
\]
Combining this bound with~\eqref{eq:step5-single-period} and summing over $1\leqslant j\leqslant 2^n$ and $n\geqslant 1$, we obtain 
\[
\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathfrak{z}(J_n^2)
=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\int_{J_n^2} \frac{\mathrm{d} u}{1 + Q(u)}
\leqslant
\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sum_{j=1}^{2^n} \int_{ja_n^{-1}}^{(j+1)a_n^{-1}} \!\!\!\frac{\mathrm{d} u}{1 + Q(u)}
\leqslant \sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sum_{j=1}^{2^n} \frac{9}{j 2^n} 
\leqslant 9\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1+n\log 2}{2^n}<\infty.\qedhere
\]

The necessity of $\beta_-\leqslant1\leqslant\beta_+$ for strongly eroded processes is established in~\cite[Prop.~1.6]{MR4828847}. It remains to show that the entire region can be attained. Fix $0\leqslant b_-\leqslant 1\leqslant b_+\leqslant 2$. 

\textbf{Part 1:} We start by constructing a smooth base process with indices $\beta_-=0$ and $\beta_+=b_+$. Define the sequences
\[
z_n = 2^{-(n!)^2},
\quad
a_n = \frac{nb_++3}{n+2}\in(1,2),
\quad
\ell_n = z_n^{-a_n},
\quad\text{and}\quad
h_n = \ell_n z_n^2 = z_n^{2-a_n}.
\]
Note that $a_n \to b_+\in[1,2]$ and $2-a_n \to 2-b_+\in[0,1]$ as $n \to \infty$.

Let $\varphi \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ be an even, non-negative function supported in $[-1,1]$ with $\int_\mathbb{R} \varphi(u)\mathrm{d} u = 1$. Fix $\rho > 0$ and set $\kappa \coloneqq \int_\mathbb{R} (1+y/4)^2 \varphi(y)\mathrm{d} y$. Define the symmetric $C^\infty$ density on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:full-region-base-density}
\upsilon_+(x) = \rho e^{-x^2} + \sum_{n\geqslant1} \frac{4\ell_n}{z_n} \left( \varphi\left(\frac{4(x-z_n)}{z_n}\right) + \varphi\left(\frac{4(x+z_n)}{z_n}\right) \right) > 0.
\end{equation}
The background $\rho e^{-x^2}$ ensures $\upsilon_+$ is everywhere strictly positive, but only adds finite mass, so by~\cite[Prop.~1.5]{MR4828847} it affects neither strong erosion nor the indices $\beta_\pm$ nor the $C^\infty$ property of the transition laws. To simplify the estimates, we treat $\upsilon_+$ with $\rho=0$.

\textit{Valid L\'evy measure.} 
The bumps are centred at $\pm z_n$ and have radius $z_n/4$ (making their supports disjoint, as $5z_{n+1}/4< 3z_{n}/4$), total mass $2\ell_n$, and second moment $2\kappa h_n$. By definition of $a_n$, it follows that $2-a_n=(n(2-b_+)+1)/(n+2)$, implying that 
\begin{equation*}
(n+1)^2(2-a_{n+1})-(2-a_n) =
\frac{(n+1)^2}{n+3}-\frac{1}{n+2} +(2-b_+)\left(
\frac{(n+1)^3}{n+3}-\frac{n}{n+2}
\right) \geqslant
\frac{(n+1)^2}{n+3}-\frac{1}{n+2}
\geqslant 1,
\end{equation*} for all $n \geqslant 2$, and similarly, since $1<a_{n+1}<2$, we get that
\begin{equation*} 
(n+1)^2 a_{n+1} - a_n \geqslant (n+1)^2 - 2 \geqslant 2, \quad \text{for all }n \geqslant 1.
\end{equation*}
Next, since $z_{n+1} = z_n^{(n+1)^2}$, and $z_n \leqslant 1/4$, it follows that $h_{n+1}/h_n \leqslant z_n \leqslant 1/4$ and $\ell_{n-1}/\ell_n \leqslant z_{n-1}^2 \leqslant 1/16$ for $n \geqslant 2$. A geometric comparison thereby yields
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:full-region-geom-sums}
h_{n+1} \leqslant \sum_{k>n} h_k \leqslant 2h_{n+1},
\quad\text{and}\quad
\sum_{k<n} \ell_k \leqslant 2\ell_{n-1},
\quad\text{for all } n\geqslant 2.
\end{equation}
In particular, $\sum_{n\geqslant 1} h_n < \infty$, making $\upsilon_+$ a valid L\'evy density with finite second moment.

\textit{Strong erosion.}
Let $X$ have density $\upsilon_+$, zero Gaussian coefficient, and zero drift parameter. Set $r_n = 2z_n$ and $t_n = 2\kappa h_n$. The cutoff $(-r_n, r_n)$ contains precisely the bumps with $k \geqslant n$ and excludes all bumps with $k < n$. Symmetry gives $d(r_n) = 0$. We verify the three limits in~\eqref{gen:cutoff-conditions} as $n \to \infty$ sequentially:
\begin{gather*}
    \frac{r_n}{t_n} = \kappa^{-1} z_n^{a_n-1} \leqslant \kappa^{-1} 2^{-(n!)^2/(n+2)} \to 0, \quad 
0 \leqslant \frac{V(r_n)}{t_n} - 1 = \frac{\sum_{k>n}h_k}{h_n} \leqslant 2\frac{h_{n+1}}{h_n} \leqslant 2z_n \to 0,\\
t_n \overline{\nu}(r_n) \leqslant 8\kappa h_n \ell_{n-1} = 8\kappa z_{n-1}^{n^2(2-a_n) - a_{n-1}} \leqslant 8\kappa 2^{-\frac{n^2-2n-4}{n+2}((n-1)!)^2} \to 0.
\end{gather*}
Proposition~\ref{prop:gen-Gaussian} implies that $X$ is strongly eroded with $\mathfrak{s}_{1} \equiv \infty$. 

\textit{Activity indices.} Bounding $|x|^p$ on $[3z_n/4, 5z_n/4]$ shows that
\[
\kappa h_n\left(5z_n/4\right)^{p-2}
\leqslant \int_{3z_n/4}^{5z_n/4} x^p \upsilon_+(x)\mathrm{d} x
\leqslant \kappa h_n\left(3z_n/4\right)^{p-2}.
\]
Hence, summing over $n$, we obtain that
\[
\int_{(-1,1)} |x|^p \upsilon_+(x)\mathrm{d} x < \infty 
\quad \text{if and only if}\quad  \sum_{n\geqslant 1} h_n z_n^{p-2} = \sum_{n\geqslant 1} z_n^{p-a_n} < \infty.
\]
The series $\sum_{n\geqslant 1} z_n^{p-a_n}$ converges for $p > b_+$ and diverges for $p < b_+$, so $\beta_+(X) = b_+$. For the lower index, at $y_n = 3 z_n/4$ the cutoff excludes all bumps with $k \leqslant n$, giving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:V_X-V}
y_n^{p-2} V(y_n) \leqslant 4\kappa y_n^{p-2} h_{n+1} = 4\kappa (3/4)^{p-2} z_n^{(n+1)^2(2-a_{n+1}) + p - 2} \to 0, \qquad \text{for any}\enskip p > 0.
\end{equation}
Thus, $\liminf_{y\downarrow0} y^{p-2}V(y) = 0$ for all $p > 0$, proving that $\beta_-(X) = 0$.

\textit{Smooth transition densities.} For $z_n^{-1} \leqslant |u| \leqslant z_{n+1}^{-1}$, the $n$-th bump pair yields $Q(u) \geqslant 2(1-q)\ell_n$ with $q \coloneqq \sup_{|u|\geqslant 1/4}|\widehat{\varphi}(u)| < 1$. Because $a_n > 1$, we have $\ell_n = z_n^{-a_n} \geqslant z_n^{-1}$. Since $\log(z_{n+1}^{-1}) = (n+1)^2(n!)^2\log 2$, it follows that
\[
\frac{Q(u)}{\log|u|} 
\geqslant \frac{2(1-q) 2^{(n!)^2}}{(n+1)^2(n!)^2\log 2}\to \infty,
\quad\text{as}\enskip |u| \to \infty.
\]
Thus, $\int_\mathbb{R} |u|^j e^{-tQ(u)}\mathrm{d} u < \infty$ for all $t > 0$ and $j \geqslant 0$, ensuring bounded $C^\infty$ transition densities with bounded derivatives of all orders.

\textbf{Part 2:} We will now alter the process to have the correct lower index. If $b_-=0$, the process $X$ satisfies all required properties. Assume instead $b_- \in (0,1]$. Let $\chi \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ be even with $0 \leqslant \chi \leqslant 1$, $\chi = 1$ on $[-e^{-3},e^{-3}]$, and $\chi = 0$ outside $(-e^{-2},e^{-2})$. Define
\[
\upsilon_-(x) \coloneqq
\begin{cases}
\chi(x)|x|^{-1-b_-}, &b_- \in (0,1),\\
\chi(x)(|x|^2\log^2|x|)^{-1}, &b_-=1,
\end{cases}
\qquad\text{with}\quad \int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\upsilon_-(x)\mathrm{d} x<\infty.
\]
The truncated variance $W(r) \coloneqq \int_{(-r,r)} x^2 \upsilon_-(x)\mathrm{d} x$ satisfies, for $r \in (0, e^{-3})$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:V_Y-W}
W(r) = \frac{2}{2-b_-} r^{2-b_-} \quad \text{for }b_-\in(0,1),
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{r}{\log^2(2/r)} \leqslant W(r) \leqslant \frac{2r}{\log^2(1/r)} \quad \text{for }b_- = 1.
\end{equation}
Consequently, as $r\downarrow 0$, $r^{p-2} W(r) \to \infty $ for every $p \in (0,b_-)$ and $r^{p-2} W(r) \to 0$ for every $p > b_-$.

Let $Y$ be an independent symmetric L\'evy process with density $\upsilon_-$, and set $Z = X + Y$, with density $\upsilon_+ + \upsilon_-$. Because $Y$ has finite variation, $Z$ is strongly eroded by~\cite[Prop.~1.5]{MR4828847}. Small-jump moments add, so $\beta_+(Z) = \max\{b_+, b_-\} = b_+$. For the lower index, $r^{p-2}V_Z(r) \geqslant r^{p-2}W(r) \to \infty$ for $p < b_-$, while along $y_n = 3z_n/4$, we have as $n \to \infty$,
\[
y_n^{p-2} V_Z(y_n) = y_n^{p-2} V(y_n) + y_n^{p-2} W(y_n) \to 0, \quad\text{for all } p > b_-,
\]
by~\eqref{eq:V_X-V} and~\eqref{eq:V_Y-W}. Thus $\beta_-(Z) = b_-$. Finally, the non-negative real exponent of $Y$ preserves the super-logarithmic Fourier growth and hence the smooth transition densities.
