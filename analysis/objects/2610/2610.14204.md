<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{reidemeisterabelian}
Let $\varphi$ be an endomorphism of an abelian group $G$. Then
\begin{itemize}
    \item[1)] $R(\varphi)$ equals the cardinality of the quotient group $G/\ensuremath{\textnormal{im}}(\varphi-\ensuremath{\textnormal{id}})$.
    \item[2)] In particular, if $G$ is finitely generated abelian and $\varphi=- \ensuremath{\textnormal{id}}$, then $R(\varphi)$ is finite.
\end{itemize}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
In fact, given $g,h \in G$, we have
\begin{eqnarray*}
g \sim_\varphi h 	&\Longleftrightarrow & \exists z \in G: z + g - \varphi(z)= h\\
					&\Longleftrightarrow & \exists z \in G:g - h=(\varphi-\ensuremath{\textnormal{id}})(z)\\
					&\Longleftrightarrow & g - h \in \ensuremath{\textnormal{im}}(\varphi-\ensuremath{\textnormal{id}}).		
\end{eqnarray*} So, $R(\varphi)$ is clearly the cardinality of $G/\ensuremath{\textnormal{im}}(\varphi-\ensuremath{\textnormal{id}})$ and item 1) is proved. Suppose now that $G$ is finitely generated and $\varphi=-\ensuremath{\textnormal{id}}$. By item 1), $R(\varphi)$ equals the cardinality of $G/2G$, which is clearly a finitely generated torsion abelian group and hence finite. This shows item 2) and completes the proof.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{atleast2}
The $R_\infty$-nilpotency degree of any finitely generated group is at least 2.
<!-- end cor-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
If $G$ is finitely generated, then $G/\gamma_2(G)$ is a finitely generated abelian group. Since $\varphi=-\ensuremath{\textnormal{id}}$ is clearly an automorphism of any abelian group, it follows immediatly from item 2) of Lemma \ref{reidemeisterabelian} that $G/\gamma_2(G)$ does not have property $R_\infty$. Hence, the $R_\infty$-nilpotency degree of $G$ is at least 2.
<!-- end proof-2 -->

  1 \ar[r] & A \ar[r]_-{i} & B \ar[r]_-{p} & C \ar[r] & 1
}
\]
where the rows are short exact sequences of groups and the vertical arrows are group endomorphisms. Then
\begin{itemize}
\item[1)] If $R(\overline{\varphi})=\infty$, then $R(\varphi)=\infty$;
\item[2)] If (the image of) $A$ is central in $B$, then $R(\varphi)\leq R(\varphi')R(\overline{\varphi})$. In particular, if both $R(\varphi')$ and $R(\overline{\varphi})$ are finite, then $R(\varphi)$ is finite.
\end{itemize}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Denote by $[b]_\varphi$ and $[c]_{\overline{\varphi}}$ the twisted conjugacy classes of any $b \in B$ and $c \in C$, respectively. Let $\mathcal{R}(\varphi)$ and $\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi})$ denote the sets of twisted conjugacy classes of $\varphi$ and $\overline{\varphi}$, respectively. There is a well-defined map $\hat{p}\colon \mathcal{R}(\varphi) \to \mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi})$ with $\hat{p}([b]_\varphi)=[p(b)]_{\overline{\varphi}}$. Indeed, for $b,b' \in B$, we have \begin{eqnarray*}
[b]_\varphi=[b']_\varphi 	&\Rightarrow & \exists z \in B:zb\varphi(z)^{-1}=b'\\
&\Rightarrow & p(z)p(b)p(\varphi(z))^{-1}=p(b')\\
&\Rightarrow & p(z)p(b)\overline{\varphi}(p(z))^{-1}=p(b')\\
&\Rightarrow & [p(b)]_{\overline{\varphi}}=[p(b')]_{\overline{\varphi}}.
\end{eqnarray*} Since $p$ is surjective, it is clear that $\hat{p}$ is also surjective, which implies $R(\varphi) \geq R(\overline{\varphi})$. Therefore, $R(\overline{\varphi})=\infty$ implies $R(\varphi)=\infty$ and item 1) is proved. For the proof of item 2), see \cite[Lemma 2.1]{Senden25}.

<!-- cor-2: body_md -->
\label{characteristic}
If $H$ is characteristic in $G$ and $G/H$ has $R_\infty$, then $G$ has $R_\infty$. In particular, if a group $G$ is such that $G/\gamma_n(G)$ has $R_\infty$ for some $n \geq 2$, then $G/\gamma_m(G)$ has $R_\infty$ for any $m\geq n$.
<!-- end cor-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
If $H$ is characteristic in $G$ and $\varphi$ is an automorphism of $G$, there is a well-defined commutative diagram as the one in Lemma \ref{cavelemma}, with $A=H$, $B=G$, $C=G/H$ and $\varphi'$, $\overline{\varphi}$ being the naturally induced automorphisms on $A$ and $C$, respectively. Since $G/H$ has $R_\infty$, we have $R(\overline{\varphi})=\infty$. Hence, $R(\varphi)=\infty$ by item 1) of Lemma \ref{cavelemma}, which shows the first claim. For the last claim, recall that  $\gamma_{n}(G)/\gamma_{n+1}(G)$ is characteristic in $G/\gamma_{n+1}(G)$, with quotient
\[

\]By what we have just shown, if $G/\gamma_{n}(G)$ has $R_\infty$, then $G/\gamma_{n+1}(G)$ has $R_\infty$ as well. By performing an easy induction, one can show that $G/\gamma_m(G)$ has $R_\infty$ for any $m\geq n$.

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{commut3}
Let $k,m,n \geq 1$ be integers and let $x,y,z \in G$ be elements of a group $G$ such that $x \in \gamma_k(G)$, $y \in \gamma_m(G)$ and $z \in \gamma_n(G)$. Then
\begin{itemize}
\item[a)] $xy=yx\  \pmod{ \gamma_{k+m}(G)}$;
\item[b)] $[x,yz]=[x,y][x,z]\  \pmod{\gamma_{k+m+n}(G)}$;
\item[c)] $[xy,z]=[x,z][y,z]\pmod{\gamma_{k+m+n}(G)}$.
\end{itemize}

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{torfinita}
Let $G$ be finitely generated. If $\gamma_n/\gamma_{n+1}$ is finite for some $n \geq 1$, then $\gamma_k/\gamma_m$ is finite for all $n \leq k \leq m$.

First, we claim that $\gamma_k/\gamma_{k+1}$ is finite for any $k \geq n$. By assumption, it holds for $k=n$. Suppose it holds for some $k\geq n$. It follows from Proposition \ref{commutmks} that $\gamma_{k+1}/\gamma_{k+2}$ is generated by all the elements $[z,x]\gamma_{k+2}$, where $z\gamma_{k+1}$ is a generator of $\gamma_k/\gamma_{k+1}$ and $x\gamma_2$ a generator of $G/\gamma_2$. so all generators of $\gamma_{k+1}/\gamma_{k+2}$ are of finite order. Since $\gamma_{k+1}/\gamma_{k+2}$ is finitely generated and abelian (Proposition \ref{commutmks}), it is a finite group, hence the claim holds for $k+1$. Therefore, the induction step is complete and the claim is true for all $k \geq n$.

Finally, given $n \leq k \leq m$, by the previous claim and Lagrange's Theorem we obtain
\[

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{powers}
Let $G$ be a GBS group represented by a labeled tree $\Gamma$. Then for any vertices $v,w \in \Gamma$, there exist non-zero integers $\alpha,\beta$ such that $x_v^\alpha=x_w^\beta$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We proceed by induction on the geodesic distance $n\geq 0$ between $v$ and $w$ in $\Gamma$. The case $n=0$ is obvious. If $n=1$, then $v$ and $w$ are adjacent and the lemma is true by the presentation of $G$. Suppose the lemma is true for some $n \geq 1$ and let us show it for $n+1$. Let $\gamma$ be a geodesic in $\Gamma$ of length $n+1$ from $v$ to $w$. Let $u \in \gamma$ be the vertex adjacent to $w$. Then the distance between $v$ and $u$ is $n$. By induction hypothesis, we have $x_v^k=x_u^l$, for non-zero integers $k,l$. By the presentation of $G$, we have $x_u^p=x_w^q$, for non-zero integers $p,q$.                                                                             
Then, for the non-zero integers $\alpha=pk$ and $\beta=ql$ we have
\[
x_v^\alpha=x_v^{pk}=x_u^{pl}=x_w^{ql}=x_w^{\beta},
\] as desired. This completes the proof of the lemma.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{centralsubgroup}
Let $G$ be a GBS group represented by a labeled tree $\Gamma$. Then there exists an infinite cyclic subgroup $C \leq Z(G)$ such that, for every vertex $v$ of $\Gamma$, some positive power of the vertex generator $x_v$ belongs to $C$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Choose a vertex $v_0$ of $\Gamma$, and write $x_0 = x_{v_0}$. By Lemma \ref{powers}, for every vertex $v$ of $\Gamma$ there exist non-zero integers $\alpha_v$, $\beta_v$ such that $x_0^{\alpha_v}=x_v^{\beta_v}$.

\[
z \coloneq x_0^L = (x_0^{\alpha_v})^{L/\alpha_v} = (x_v^{\beta_v})^{L/\alpha_v}
= x_v^{\beta_v L /\alpha_v}
\]
for every vertex $v$. Set $C = \langle z\rangle$. Since the vertex group $\langle x_0 \rangle$ embeds in $G$ and is infinite cyclic, $C$ is infinite cyclic. Moreover, $z$ commutes with every vertex generator, because $z$ lies in every vertex group. Since $\Gamma$ is a tree, there are no stable letters in the presentation of $G$, thus $G$ is generated by its vertex generators. Hence $z \in Z(G)$, and therefore $C \leq Z(G)$. Moreover, 
$x_v^{\beta_v L/\alpha_v} \in C$ for any vertex generator $x_v$, by the above.

\label{rank0}

their images generate $\overline{G}/\overline{H}$. Thus $\overline{G}/\overline{H}$ is a finitely generated nilpotent group generated by elements of finite order, so it is finite.

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              

Now, we consider the automorphism
$$\varphi\colon G \to G$$
such that $\varphi(w)=w^{-1}$ for every vertex letter $w$, and let $\overline{\varphi}\colon \overline{G}\to \overline{G}$ be the induced automorphism in $\overline{G}$. It is clear that $\overline{H}$ is invariant by $\overline{\varphi}$, thus we have the following commutative diagram:

\[
\xymatrix{
  1 \ar[r] & \overline{H} \ar[d]_-{\overline{\varphi}|_{\overline{H}}} \ar[r]^-{} & \overline{G} \ar[d]_-{\overline{\varphi}} \ar[r]^-{} & \overline{G}/\overline{H} \ar[d]^-{\overline{\overline{\varphi}}} \ar[r] & 1 \\

}
\]

Furthermore, since $\overline{H}$ is cyclic and $\overline{\varphi}|_{\overline{H}}$ acts as multiplication by $-1$ on $\overline{H}$, it follows from item 2) of Lemma \ref{reidemeisterabelian} that $R(\overline{\varphi}|_{\overline{H}})< \infty$. Since $n$ is arbitrary, the $R_{\infty}$-nilpotency degree of $G$ is infinite.

\label{normalformlemma}
For every $g \in G/\gamma_3(G)$, there are unique integers $k_i$, $1 \leq i \leq 6$, such that $k_1,k_5 \in [0,a)$, $k_2,k_6 \in [0,|c-d|)$, $k_4 \in [0,n)$ and

<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
We follow the language and procedure on Sims's work \cite[Sec.~9.5, 9.6]{Sims}. Existence of the normal form above follows easily from the existence of normal form for elements in $\overline{F}$ and the successive use of the \emph{power relations} of $G/\gamma_3(G)$, which are $x^a=y^b$, $y^{c-d}=w^c$, $u^n=1$, $v^a=w^b$ and $w^{c-d}=1$, in this exact order, in an algorithm called \emph{collection}. By Chapter 9 of \cite{Sims}, showing uniqueness is equivalent to showing that the relative order of $x,y,u,v$ and $w$ is, respectively, $a,|c-d|,n,a$ and $|c-d|$, and that the relative order of $t$ is infinite. The latter claim follows easily, for example, from the fact that $t$ has infinite order in the abelian group $G/\gamma_2(G)$, which is a quotient of $G/\gamma_3(G)$, by the third Isomorphism Theorem. To determine the other relative orders, we first briefly describe an explicit procedure, based on \cite{Sims}, to compute relative orders in the quotient group $\overline{F}/N$ of $\overline{F}$. The procedure below works, in fact, for any finitely generated free nilpotent group and any normal subgroup. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Denote the standard list of generators of $\overline{F}$ by $(f_1,\ldots,f_n)$ (the ordering is relevant). In our case, $(f_1,f_2,f_3,f_4,f_5,f_6)=(x,y,t,u,v,w)$;
    \item Find a finite list $U=(u_1,\ldots,u_s)$ of elements of $N$ such that $N=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_s\rangle$. In our case, this involves computing the conjugates of the form $f_imf_i^{-1}$ and $f_i^{-1}mf_i$ for each normal generator $m$ of $N$ and each generator $f_i$ of $\overline{F}$, in order to determine which elements need to be added to the list of normal generators to obtain such a list $U$.
    \item For each $1 \leq i \leq s$, write $u_i=f_1^{k_{i1}}\cdots f_n^{k_{in}}$, where $k_{ij} \in \mathbb{Z}$, $1 \leq j \leq n$. Consider the $s \times n$ matrix $A=(k_{ij})$, called the \emph{exponent matrix} associated to $U$ in Sims's terminology.
    \item Perform the \emph{row reduction} process to $A$. The three allowed row operations are: interchanging two rows, multiplying a row by $-1$ and adding an integral multiple of one row to another one. After suitable row operations and deleting all zero rows (if any), we obtain an $r \times n$ matrix $\tilde{A}=(\alpha_{ij})$, for some $1 \leq r \leq \min\{s,n\}$, with no zero rows and with the following property: if the first nonzero entry of the $i$-th row is $\alpha_{ij_i}$ (these are called the \emph{corner entries} of $\tilde{A}$), then $\alpha_{ij_i}>0$ and $j_1<j_2<\cdots j_r$. In other words, corner entries are positive and appear in columns progressively further to the right.
    \item Then, by Sims's work \cite{Sims}, especially Sections 9.5 and 9.6, the relative orders of the image of the generators $f_{j_i}$ in the quotient group $\overline{F}/N$ are exactly the respective corner entries $\alpha_{ij_i}$, while the relative orders of the images of the other generators $f_j$ (if any) are infinite.
    
\end{enumerate}

In our particular case, we claim that $N=\langle U \rangle$ for $U=\{ x^ay^{-b},y^{c-d}w^{-c}, u^n,v^aw^{-b},w^{c-d} \}$. \begin{align*}

\end{align*} which shows the claim.

Recall that we proved in Section \ref{normalformsection} that for $u=[x,y]\gamma_3(G)$, $u^a=u^b=u^{d-c}=1$. Since $n=\gcd(a,b,c-d)=1$ in our case, we conclude that $u=1$ in $\overline{G}$. We claim that $\tau(\overline{G})=\left< x,y,v,w \right>$. In fact, since $u=1$, the subgroup $\left<x,y,v,w\right>$ is abelian and each generator has finite order, thus $\left<x,y,v,w\right> \subset \tau(\overline{G})$. Following the notations obtained in Section 4, if 
\[ x^{k_1}y^{k_2}t^{k_3}v^{k_5}w^{k_6}\in \tau(\overline{G}),\]
then $(x^{k_1}y^{k_2}t^{k_3}v^{k_5}w^{k_6})^l=1$ for some $l\geq 1$. By projecting naturally this equation on $G/\gamma_2=\langle x,y \rangle \oplus \langle t\rangle$ we obtain
\[x^{lk_1}y^{lk_2}t^{lk_3}=1.\]
Since $t$ has infinite order in $G/\gamma_2$ and $l \geq 1$ we obtain $k_3=0$.

Therefore, $\tau(\overline{G})=\left< x,y,v,w \right>$, and it is a finite nilpotent (in fact, abelian) group. Thus, by Lemmas \ref{lema1} and \ref{lema2}, $\tau(\overline{G})$ is the direct product of its Sylow subgroups, which are normal and characteristic. Because $\varphi(t)=s$ is of infinite order, it follows that $k_3\neq 0$ implies $x^{k_1}y^{k_2}t^{k_3}v^{k_5}w^{k_6} \not\in \ker(\varphi)$. Hence, $\ker(\varphi) \subset \tau(\overline{G})$ and it is also a finite abelian group. In particular, $\ker(\varphi)=\ker(\varphi)\cap \tau(\overline{G})=\ker(\varphi_\tau)$, where $\varphi_\tau\colon \tau(\overline{G}) \to \tau(\overline{BS})$ is the restricted torsion homomorphism. We claim that $\varphi_{\tau}$ is an epimorphism. Indeed, by following arguments similar to those used to prove that $\tau(\overline{G})=\left< x,y,v,w\right>$, we show that $\tau(\overline{BS})=\left<z,r\right>$. Since $\gcd(a',b')=1$, there exists $\alpha$, $\beta\in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $\alpha a'+\beta b'=1$ Consequently, $\varphi_{\tau}(x^{\beta}y^{\alpha})=z$ and $\varphi_{\tau}(v^{\beta}w^{\alpha})=r$, which implies that $\varphi_{\tau}$ is an epimorphism.

Now we show that $\ker(\varphi)$ is characteristic in $\overline{G}$. Since elements of both groups $\overline{G}$ and $\overline{BS}$ have unique normal forms, it follows that $|\tau(\overline{G})|=a^2|c-d|^2$ and 
$|\tau(\overline{BS})|=|c'-d'|^2$. By using that $|c-d|=m|c'-d'|$, Lagrange's Theorem and the isomorphism $\tau(\overline{BS})\simeq \tau(\overline{G})/\ker(\varphi)$, we obtain
\[
a^2m^2|c'-d'|^2=a^2|c-d|^2=|\tau(\overline{G})|=|\tau(\overline{BS})||\ker(\varphi)|=|c'-d'|^2|\ker(\varphi)|,
\] so $|\ker(\varphi)|=a^2m^2$. Since $m$ is a Hall divisor of $|c-d|$, $m^2$ is clearly a Hall divisor of $|c-d|^2$. By Lemma \ref{lemanumerodesylow}, since $P(a)\cap P(|c-d|) \subset P(m)$ by hypothesis, $a^2m^2=|\ker(\varphi)|$ is a Hall divisor of $a^2|c-d|^2=|\tau(\overline{G})|$. By basic Sylow theory, $\ker(\varphi)$ must be a product of Sylow subgroups of $\tau(\overline{G})$ (the ones related to the prime numbers involved in $a^2m^2$). Since these subgroups are all characteristic, $\ker(\varphi)$ is characteristic in $\tau(\overline{G})$, so it must also be characteristic in $\overline{G}$, since $\tau(\overline{G})$ is characteristic in $\overline{G}$.

Finally, in both cases of the statement, Theorem \ref{DaciDekimpe} ensures that the group $\overline{BS}$ has $R_\infty$. Thus, by Corollary \ref{characteristic}, $\overline{G}$ has $R_\infty$, which implies the $R_{\infty}$-nilpotency degree of $G$ is $2$, as desired.

\label{potenciascomutador}
    Let $G=\textnormal{loll}(a,b,c,d)$ be a lollipop GBS group. Then, for every $n\in \mathbb{Z}$, we have $[t,x^{nac}]=[t,x^{ac}]^n$.

    \begin{proof}
    We use the following group identity: $[x,yz]=[x,y][x,z][z,x,y]$, which holds for any elements $x,y,z$ of any group $G$. For $n=0,1$, the result is clear. Suppose that $n\geq 2$ and that the lemma holds for $n-1$. Using the defining relations of $G$, we obtain
    \begin{align*}
    [t,x^{nac}] & =[t,x^{(n-1)ac}x^{ac}] \\
    & =[t,x^{(n-1)ac}][t,x^{ac}][x^{ac},t,x^{(n-1)ac}] \\
    & =[t,x^{ac}]^{n-1}[t,x^{ac}][x^{-a(d-c)},x^{(n-1)ac}] \\
    & =[t,x^{ac}]^n.
    \end{align*} Thus, the lemma is true for any $n\geq 0$. For $n=-1$, the lemma also holds:
    \[[t,x^{-ac}]=x^{-ac}[x^{ac},t]x^{ac}=x^{-ac}x^{-a(d-c)}x^{ac}=x^{-a(d-c)}=[t,x^{ac}]^{-1}.\]
Suppose that $n\leq -2$ and that the lemma holds for $n+1$. Following a similar computation as in the previous case, we obtain
    \begin{align*}
    [t,x^{nac}] & =[t,x^{(n+1)ac}x^{-ac}] \\
    & =[t,x^{(n+1)ac}][t,x^{-ac}][x^{-ac},t,x^{(n+1)ac}] \\
    & =[t,x^{ac}]^{n+1}[t,x^{ac}]^{-1}[x^{a(d-c)},x^{(n+1)ac}] \\
    & =[t,x^{ac}]^n.
    \end{align*} Thus, the lemma holds for any $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
    \end{proof}

\label{isomorfismodosquocientes}
Let $G=\textnormal{loll}(a,b,c,d)$ be a $2$-generated lollipop GBS group. Thus, for every $n\geq 2$,
    \[ \dfrac{G}{\gamma_n(G)}\simeq \dfrac{BS(ac,ad)}{\gamma_n(BS(ac,ad))}.\]

\begin{proof}
We will prove that both nilpotent quotients mentioned above share a common presentation and are therefore isomorphic. Recall that we have the following presentation:
    \[ BS(ac,ad)=\langle x,t \: | \: tx^{ac}t^{-1}=x^{ad}\rangle.\]
    Therefore, by Theorem \ref{quociente}, 
    \begin{equation} \label{presbsquoc}
        \dfrac{BS(ac,ad)}{\gamma_n(BS(ac,ad))}=\langle x,t \: | \: tx^{ac}t^{-1}=x^{ad}, [w_1,\dots ,w_n],\: w_i \in F(x,t)  \rangle.
    \end{equation}
For the group $G$, we have
    \[ \dfrac{G}{\gamma_n(G)}=\langle x,y,t \: | \: x^a=y^b, \: ty^ct^{-1}=y^d, [z_1,\dots ,z_n],\: z_i \in F(x,y,t)\rangle,\] where $F(x,t)$ and $F(x,y,t)$ denote the associated free groups.

Note that a similar argument as in Lemma \ref{auxiliar}, applied to the relation $ty^ct^{-1} = y^d$,
gives $y^{(d-c)^k} \in \gamma_{k+1}(G)$ for all $k \geq 0$. Since $G$ is $2$-generated, our earlier analysis shows that $\gcd(b,c,d)=1$ and that, for any prime $p$, if $p|b$ then $p|c$ or $p|d$. From this it follows that $\gcd(b,d-c)=1$ and, therefore, $\gcd(b,(d-c)^{n-1})=1$. Thus, there are $\alpha, \theta\in \mathbb{Z}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{equation2gen}
    \alpha b+\theta (d-c)^{n-1}=1.
\end{equation} Hence,
\[
  y=y^{\alpha b}y^{\theta (d-c)^{n-1}}=x^{\alpha a}y^{\theta (d-c)^{n-1}}=x^{\alpha a} \pmod{\gamma_n(G)}.
\]
This way, by performing the Tietze transformation \cite[p.~89]{LyndonSchupp} of replacing $y$ by $x^{a\alpha}$, the relations in the presentation of $G/\gamma_n(G)$ become
\[
  x^a=y^b \Longleftrightarrow x^a=x^{\alpha ab} \Longleftrightarrow x^{a(1-\alpha b)}=1 \Longleftrightarrow x^{a\theta(d-c)^{n-1}}=1,
\]
\[
 ty^ct^{-1}=y^d \Longleftrightarrow y^{d-c}=[t,y^c] \Longleftrightarrow x^{\alpha a(d-c)}=[t,x^{\alpha ac}],
\]
and the relations $[z_1,\dots ,z_n]$ become all relations $[z_1,\dots ,z_n]$, with $z_i$ words in letters $x,t$. Therefore
    \[ \dfrac{G}{\gamma_n(G)}=\langle x,t \: | \: x^{a\theta(d-c)^{n-1}}=1, \: x^{\alpha a(d-c)}=[t,x^{\alpha ac}], \: [z_1,\dots ,z_n], \: z_i\in F(x,t)\rangle. \]
    Now, by Equation \ref{equation2gen} we clearly have
\[
a(d-c)=a(d-c)(\alpha b+\theta (d-c)^{n-1})= \alpha a(d-c) b+a\theta (d-c)^{n}.
\]
Therefore, using Lemma \ref{potenciascomutador}, Equation \ref{equation2gen} and the relations on $G/\gamma_n(G)$, it follows that
    \begin{align*}
        x^{a(d-c)} & =x^{\alpha a(d-c) b}x^{a\theta (d-c)^{n}} \\
                & =x^{\alpha a(d-c) b} \\
                   & =[t,x^{\alpha ac}]^b \\
                   & =[t,x^{ac}]^{\alpha b} \\
                   & =[t,x^{ac}]^{1-\theta (d-c)^{n-1}} \\
                   & =[t,x^{ac}][t,x^{ac}]^{-\theta(d-c)^{n-1}} \\
                   & =[t,x^{ac}][t,x^{ac(d-c)^{n-2}}]^{-\theta(d-c)}\\
                   & =[t,x^{ac}],
    \end{align*}
    since $x^{a(d-c)^{n-2}}=y^{b(d-c)^{n-2}}\in \gamma_{n-1}(G)$. 
    So, we have the relation $tx^{ac}t^{-1}=x^{ad}$ in $G/\gamma_n(G)$. Now we will prove that the group given by the presentation
    \[ \langle x,t \: | \: tx^{ac}t^{-1}=x^{ad}, \: [z_1,\dots ,z_n], \: z_i\in F(x,t)\rangle\]
   is exactly $G/\gamma_n(G)$. In fact, since $tx^{ac}t^{-1}=x^{ad}$, following the same arguments in the proof of Lemma \ref{potenciascomutador}, we have that $[t,x^{nac}]=[t,x^{ac}]^n$. Thus,
    \[ x^{\alpha a(d-c)}=(x^{a(d-c)})^{\alpha}=[t,x^{ac}]^{\alpha}=[t,x^{\alpha ac}].\]
    Denoting $y=x^{\alpha a}$, we have $y^{d-c}=[t,y^c]$. For the relation $x^a=y^b$, it is sufficient to prove that $x^{a\theta (d-c)^{n-1}}$=1, and this is true by Lemma \ref{auxiliar}. Therefore, using Tietze transformations \cite[p.~89]{LyndonSchupp}, we can rewrite the presentation of $G/\gamma_n(G)$ as:
    \[ \dfrac{G}{\gamma_n(G)}=\langle x,t \: | \: tx^{ac}t^{-1}=x^{ad}, \: [z_1,\dots ,z_n], \: z_i\in F(x,t)\rangle, \]
   which is precisely the presentation in Equation \ref{presbsquoc}. Therefore, we have the desired isomorphism.
    \end{proof}

<!-- theorem-10: body_md -->
\label{2geradosfinal}
   Let $G=\textnormal{loll}(a,b,c,d)$ be a $2$-generated lollipop GBS group. Then $G$ and $BS(ac,ad)$ have the same $R_{\infty}$-nilpotency degree $r$. Specifically, let $p$ denote the largest integer such that $2^p$ divides $2\frac{c}{\gcd(c,d)}+2$. We have:

\begin{itemize}
    \item If $d<0$ and $d \neq -c$, then $r=2$.
    \item If $d=c$ or $d=-c$, then $r=\infty$.
    \item If $d-c=\gcd(c,d)$, then $r=\infty$.
    \item If $d-c=2\gcd(c,d)$ and $ac,ad$ are not both even, then $r=2$.
    \item If $d-c=2\gcd(c,d)$ and $ac,ad$ are both even, then $2\leq r\leq p+2$.
    \item If $d-c\geq 3\gcd(c,d)$, then $r=2$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem \ref{isomorfismodosquocientes}, $G$ and $BS(ac,ad)$ must have the same $R_{\infty}$-nilpotency degree $r$.
In the first case, we have $ad<0$ (since $a>0$) and $ad\neq -ac$. Thus, by Theorem \ref{DaciDekimpe}, the $R_{\infty}$-nilpotency degree of $BS(ac,ad)$ is $2$. In the second case, we have either $ad=ac$ or $ad=-ac$. Thus, by Theorem \ref{DaciDekimpe}, the $R_{\infty}$-nilpotency degree of $BS(ac,ad)$ is $r=\infty$. For the other cases, it is enough to note that, if $d-c=k\gcd(c,d)$, then $ad-ac=ka\gcd(c,d)=k\gcd(ac,ad)$. Thus, the $R_{\infty}$-nilpotency degree of $G$ (and of $BS(ac,ad)$) is given either by Theorem \ref{DaciDekimpe} or by Theorem \ref{refinement} (in the fourth case).
\end{proof}
