\label{prop:local-work-representation}
{A linear second-jet density $\pi_s$ is pointwise objective and vanishes for infinitesimal rigid virtual displacements if and only if there exist unique material vectors $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{n}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{h}}\in\mathbb R^3$, whose dependence on the configuration and material point is suppressed, such that}
\begin{align}
	\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}^*=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}},
	\quad
	\boldsymbol{\mathsf{n}}^*=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{n}},
	\quad 
	\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}^*=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}},
\quad 
	\boldsymbol{\mathsf{h}}^*=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{h}},
	\notag
\end{align}
and
\begin{align}
	\pi_s[\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{R}]
	\bigl(j_s^2(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\psi})\bigr)
	=
	\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
	+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{n}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}
	+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}\cdot\partial_s\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
	+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{h}}\cdot\partial_s\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}.
	\label{eq:objective-linear-work-representation}
\end{align}
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Fix a material point $s$ and a configuration, and suppress their appearance in the notation. Let $\mathcal J_s^2\cong(\mathbb R^3)^6$ denote the second-jet space at $s$, and define the linear map
\begin{align}
	T_s:\mathcal J_s^2\rightarrow(\mathbb R^3)^4,
	\quad 
	T_s\bigl(j_s^2(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\psi})\bigr)
	:=
	\bigl(
	\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}},
	\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}},
	\partial_s\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}},
	\partial_s\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}
	\bigr).
	\notag
\end{align}
In terms of the second jet,
\begin{gather}
	\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
	=\boldsymbol{R}^T\partial_s\boldsymbol{\psi},
\quad 
	\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}
	=\boldsymbol{R}^T
	\bigl(\partial_s\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{\psi}\bigr),
	\notag\\
	\partial_s\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
	=\boldsymbol{R}^T
	\bigl(
	\partial_s^2\boldsymbol{\psi}
	-\boldsymbol{u}\times\partial_s\boldsymbol{\psi}
	\bigr),
	\quad 
	\partial_s\delta\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}
	=\boldsymbol{R}^T\bigl[
	\partial_s^2\boldsymbol{\varphi}
	+\partial_s\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{\psi}
	+\boldsymbol{v}\times\partial_s\boldsymbol{\psi}
	-\boldsymbol{u}\times
	\bigl(
	\partial_s\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{\psi}
	\bigr)
	\bigr].
	\label{eq:virtual-strain-jet-map}
\end{gather}
The map $T_s$ is onto. Indeed, for arbitrary material vectors
$\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta}$, prescribe a second jet at $s$ by
\begin{gather}
	\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{0},
	\quad 
	\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{0},
	\notag\\
	\partial_s\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\alpha},
	\quad 
	\partial_s\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\beta},
	\notag\\
	\partial_s^2\boldsymbol{\psi}
	=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\alpha},
	\quad 
	\partial_s^2\boldsymbol{\varphi}
	=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\delta}
	-\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
	+\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\beta}.
	\notag
\end{gather}
Substitution gives
\begin{align}
	T_s\bigl(j_s^2(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\psi})\bigr)
	=
	(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta}),
	\notag
\end{align}
which proves surjectivity.

Suppose that a difference jet
$j_s^2(\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi},\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi})$ belongs to $\ker T_s$. Equation \eqref{eq:virtual-strain-jet-map} then gives
\begin{gather}
	\partial_s\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}
	=\boldsymbol{0},
	\quad 
	\partial_s\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}
	=\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}\times\boldsymbol{v},
	\notag\\
	\partial_s^2\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}
	=\boldsymbol{0},
	\quad 
	\partial_s^2\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}
	=\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}\times\partial_s\boldsymbol{v}.
	\notag
\end{gather}
Set
\begin{align}
	\boldsymbol{d}
	:=\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi},
	\quad
	\boldsymbol{c}_0
	:=
	\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}
	-\boldsymbol{d}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{o}).
	\notag
\end{align}
The value and first two derivatives of
\begin{align}
	\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\mathrm{rig}}
	=
	\boldsymbol{c}_0+\boldsymbol{d}\times(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{o}),
	\quad 
	\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\mathrm{rig}}
	=\boldsymbol{d}
	\notag
\end{align}
agree with the difference jet at $s$. Conversely, every rigid second jet is mapped to zero by $T_s$. Therefore,
\begin{align}
	\ker T_s
	=
	\left\{
	j_s^2(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\mathrm{rig}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\mathrm{rig}})
	\mid \boldsymbol{c}_0,\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb R^3
	\right\}.
	\notag
\end{align}

{Insensitivity to infinitesimal rigid virtual displacements} implies $\ker T_s\subseteq\ker\pi_s$; hence any two second jets having the same image under $T_s$ have the same value under $\pi_s$, and we may define the unique linear functional $\widehat\pi_s:(\mathbb R^3)^4\to\mathbb R$ by
\[
\widehat\pi_s(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta}):=\pi_s(J),
\]
where $J$ is any second jet satisfying $T_sJ=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta})$.
Every linear functional on $(\mathbb R^3)^4$ has a unique representation
\begin{align}
	\widehat\pi_s
	(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta})
	=
	\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}
	+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}
	+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}
	+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{h}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}.
	\notag
\end{align}
{This gives \eqref{eq:objective-linear-work-representation}. The four arguments of $\widehat\pi_s$ are variations of material strain vectors and are unchanged by a change of observer.} Pointwise objectivity and uniqueness therefore imply
$\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}^*=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}$, $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{n}}^*=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{n}}$, $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}^*=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}$, and $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{h}}^*=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{h}}$.

Conversely, if the coefficient vectors are observer-invariant, the representation \eqref{eq:objective-linear-work-representation} is pointwise objective. All four variations of material strain vectors vanish on a rigid second jet, so the density also vanishes for infinitesimal rigid virtual displacements.

\label{lem:contact-regularity}
{Suppose that \eqref{eq:pvw} holds for every smooth virtual displacement,} with $(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{R})\in W^{2,p}$ and
$\boldsymbol{m},\allowbreak\boldsymbol{n},\allowbreak\boldsymbol{g},\allowbreak\boldsymbol{h},
\allowbreak\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}},\allowbreak\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in L^{p'}([0,L];\mathbb R^3)$.
Then the distributions $\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{C}$ defined in \eqref{eq:force-couple-resultants} and the fields $\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g}$ belong to $W^{1,p'}(I;\mathbb R^3)$. In particular, they have continuous representatives on $[0,L]$.
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The configuration regularity implies that $\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}$ are bounded and belong to $W^{1,p}$. Testing \eqref{eq:pvw} with $(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{0})$, where $\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in C_c^\infty((0,L);\mathbb R^3)$, gives
\[
\int_0^L\left[
\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\partial_s^2\boldsymbol{\varphi}+
(\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{h})\cdot\partial_s\boldsymbol{\varphi}
\right]\,ds
=\int_0^L\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\,ds.
\]
Thus $\partial_s\boldsymbol{F}=-\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}$ distributionally. A distribution on an interval whose derivative belongs to $L^{p'}$ is an absolutely continuous primitive of that derivative plus a constant, so $\boldsymbol{F}\in W^{1,p'}$. Moreover,
\[
\partial_s\boldsymbol{h}=\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{F}\in L^{p'},
\]
which proves $\boldsymbol{h}\in W^{1,p'}$.

Next take $\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{0}$ and $\boldsymbol{\psi}\in C_c^\infty((0,L);\mathbb R^3)$ to parameterize the {virtual displacement}. The regularity just obtained permits integration by parts in the terms involving $\boldsymbol{h}$ and reduces this identity to
\[
\int_I\left[
\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\partial_s^2\boldsymbol{\psi}+
(\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{g})\cdot\partial_s\boldsymbol{\psi}
\right]\,ds
=\int_I(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}+\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{F})\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}\,ds.
\]
Consequently, $\partial_s\boldsymbol{C}=-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}-\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{F}\in L^{p'}$, so $\boldsymbol{C}\in W^{1,p'}$. Finally,
\[
\partial_s\boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{C}\in L^{p'},
\]
and hence $\boldsymbol{g}\in W^{1,p'}$.

	Let $X$ and $Y$ be Banach spaces over $\mathbb K=\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. Let $B\in X^*$ and let $A:X\rightarrow Y$ be a bounded surjective linear operator. If $Bz=0$ for every $z\in\ker A$, then there exists a unique $\Lambda\in Y^*$ such that

	\end{align}

The case of $\mathcal G = \mathcal G_0$ is an immediate consequence of the First Fundamental Theorem of classical invariant theory (Theorem 11.2.1 in \cite{Procesi2007} or \cite{Weyl1946}).  For transverse isotropy, set $\mathcal G = \mathcal G_1$, and observe that {$\mathcal P_0 = \mathcal E_1 \cup \mathcal F_1$}. We can therefore write $P$ as the average \begin{align}
		\label{eq:average}
		P  = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(Q(\mathcal E_1, \mathcal F_1) + Q(\mathcal E_1, -\mathcal F_1)\bigr). 
	\end{align} But the right hand side contains no monomials of odd total degree in the elements of $\mathcal F$, which proves the representation for $\mathcal P_1$. The representation for $\mathcal P_2$ is proved in the same way as for $\mathcal P_1$.

Moreover, the basis map separates $\mathcal G$-orbits \cite{Schwarz1975}: if $\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}) = \rho(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}})$, then $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}$ belong to the same orbit of $\mathcal G$. \label{thm:eigenvalue_simplicity}
	Suppose that $\alpha \in C^1([0,1])$ and $\beta \in C^2([0,1])$. Furthermore, suppose that $\alpha(s) \geq 0$ and $\beta(s) > 0$ for all $s \in [0,1]$. Then the eigenvalues of \eqref{eq:linearized_BVP} are simple.
<!-- end prop-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Fix an eigenvalue $\lambda =\lambda_i$ of \eqref{eq:linearized_BVP}, and let $E =  E_i$ denote the corresponding eigenspace. Suppose for contradiction that $\dim(E) > 1$, and let $u_1, u_2 \in E$ be linearly independent. For $u \in E$, we see upon integrating that
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:I_geq_0_1_integral}
	(\beta u'')' - \alpha u' = [(\beta u'')' - \alpha u']_{s=0} + \int_0^s \lambda u(t) dt.
\end{align}
We integrate once more to obtain 
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:I_geq_0_2_integral}
	\beta u'' &= [\beta u'']_{s=0} + \int_0^s [\alpha u'](\xi) + [(\beta u'')' - \alpha u']_{s=0} d\xi + \int_0^s (s-t) (\lambda u(t))dt  \\
		  &= [\beta u'']_{s=0} + s[(\beta u'')' - \alpha u']_{s=0} + \int_0^s [\alpha u'](\xi) d\xi + \int_0^s (s-t)\lambda u(t) dt
\end{align}
where we have used Fubini's theorem to switch the order of integration.  

Set $w := c_1u_1 + c_2u_2$ for $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb R$ to be chosen presently. Then $w \in  E$, and in particular \[w(0) =w'(0) = w''(1) = [\alpha w' - (\beta w'')']_{s=1}= 0. \] Furthermore, we can choose $c_1$ and $c_2$ such that $c_1^2 +c_2^2 > 0$, $w''(0) = 0$ and 
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:w_boundary_inequality}
	[ (\beta w'')' -\alpha w']_{s=0} \geq 0. 	
\end{align}

Since we assumed $u_1$ and $u_2$ linearly independent, $w$ cannot be identically zero. By the uniqueness of the associated initial value problem for \eqref{eq:linearized_BVP}, the inequality \eqref{eq:w_boundary_inequality} must be strict.  

We claim that $w, w'$ and $w''$ are all strictly positive on $(0,1]$. Indeed, in the present case, we have $[(\beta w'')' - \alpha w']_{s=0} = (\beta w''')_{s=0}$, so that 
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:w_reinforcement_inequality}
	\beta w'' = s[\beta w''']_{s=0} + \int_0^s [\alpha w'](\xi) d\xi + \int_0^s (s-t)(\lambda w(t))dt
\end{align}
and $(\beta w''')_{s=0} > 0$. Since $\beta $ is positive, we must have  $w'''(0) >0$. Since $w(0) = w'(0) = w''(0) =0$, $w$ and $w'$ must be positive on an interval $(0, \epsilon)$ for some  $\epsilon>0$. If $w'$ were to vanish on (0,1], relabel $\epsilon$  as the first point at which this occurs. By \eqref{eq:w_reinforcement_inequality}, $w''(s) > 0$ on $(0, \epsilon]$, so that $w'$ is increasing and $w'(s) > 0$ on $(0, \epsilon]$, contradicting the definition of $\epsilon$. 

Thus, $w'$, and therefore $w$, are positive on $(0, 1]$. The identity \eqref{eq:w_reinforcement_inequality} implies that  $w''$ is positive on $(0, 1]$. However, this contradicts the fact that  $w''(1) =0$. Thus $E$ is one-dimensional.

<!-- prop-7: body_md -->
\label{prop:fredholm_alternative}
	The operator $\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{J} : \mathcal X \to \mathcal X$ has a bounded inverse.
<!-- end prop-7 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We claim that the equation $\boldsymbol{J} \boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{w}$ has no non-trivial solutions, so the result follows from the Fredholm alternative since $\boldsymbol{J}$ is compact \cite{Brezis2011}. Indeed, suppose $\boldsymbol{w}$ solves $\boldsymbol{J} \boldsymbol{w}= \boldsymbol{w}$. Then we must have $w_0 \in C^3[0,1]$ and 
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:w_0_third_order}
	\begin{cases}
	(\beta w_0'')' - \alpha w_0' = 0 \\
	w_0(0) = w_0'(0) = w_0''(1) = 0. 
	\end{cases} 
\end{align}
Setting $v = w_0'$ we find that, via integration by parts and the fact that $v(0) = v'(1) =0$,
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:v_iBP}
	\int_0^1 \beta (v')^2 ds  + \int_0^1 \alpha v^2 ds= 0.	
\end{align}
Since $\beta >0$ and $\alpha \geq 0$, we must have $v' = 0$ on $[0,1]$. From the boundary condition $v(0) =0, w_0(0)= 0$, we obtain $w_0 =0$ on $[0,1]$. This implies that $\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{0}$.

<!-- prop-8: body_md -->
\label{prop:algebraic_multiplicity_one}
	Under the same assumptions on $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as in Proposition \ref{thm:eigenvalue_simplicity}, $m(\lambda, \boldsymbol{L}) = 1$ for all $\lambda \in \mathcal E$.
<!-- end prop-8 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Fix $\lambda \in \mathcal E$. Recall that $\dim E(\lambda) = 1$, where $E(\lambda)$ denotes the eigenspace associated to $\lambda$ in \eqref{eq:linearized_BVP}. The integral formulation \eqref{eq:linear_integral_system} implies \begin{align}
		\label{eq:dim_f_F}
		\dim \ker(\boldsymbol{I} - \lambda \boldsymbol{L}) &= \dim \ker((\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{J})^{-1}( \boldsymbol{I} -\boldsymbol{J} - \lambda \boldsymbol{N}))\\
						 &= \dim \ker (\boldsymbol{I} -\boldsymbol{J} -\lambda \boldsymbol{N}) =  1. 
	\end{align} Suppose there exists $\boldsymbol{u} \in \ker((\boldsymbol{I} - \lambda \boldsymbol{L})^2)$ but $\boldsymbol{u} \notin \ker (\boldsymbol{I} - \lambda \boldsymbol{L})$. Setting $\boldsymbol{w} := (\boldsymbol{I} - \lambda \boldsymbol{L}) \boldsymbol{u} \neq \boldsymbol{0}$, we see that $\boldsymbol{w} \in \ker (\boldsymbol{I} -\lambda \boldsymbol{L})$, i.e. $\boldsymbol{0} = (\boldsymbol{I} - \lambda \boldsymbol{L})\boldsymbol{w},$
	which implies that we also have 
	\begin{align}
	0 =  (\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{J})( \boldsymbol{I} - \lambda \boldsymbol{L})\boldsymbol{w} = (\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{J} - \lambda \boldsymbol{N})\boldsymbol{w}. 
	\end{align}
	Setting $\boldsymbol{w} = (w_0, w_1, w_2, w_3)$, we see that $w_0$ solves \eqref{eq:linearized_BVP}. Observe also that $(\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{J})\boldsymbol{w} = (\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{J} -\lambda \boldsymbol{N})\boldsymbol{u}$, which implies 
	\begin{align}
		\label{eq:w-u0-relationship}
		(0, 0, 0, w_3) = \left( u_0 - \int_0^s u_1 dt, u_1 - \int_0^s \frac{u_2}{\beta}dt, u_2 - \int_1^s (u_3 + \alpha u_1)dt, u_3 - \lambda \int_1^s u_0 dt \right).
	\end{align}
	Since $w_3 = \lambda \int_1^s w_0 dt$, we use \eqref{eq:w-u0-relationship} to see that $u_0$ solves
	\begin{align}
		\label{eq:w_u_differential_relationship}
		\begin{cases} (\beta u_0'')'' - ( \alpha u_0')' -\lambda u_0 = \lambda w_0\\
				u_0(0) = u_0'(0) = u_0''(1) = [\alpha u_0' - (\beta u_0'')']_{s=1} = 0
		\end{cases}. 
	\end{align}
	Pairing \eqref{eq:w_u_differential_relationship} against $w_0$ and integration by parts yields 
	\begin{align}
		\label{eq:w_u_contradiction}
		\int_0^1 \lambda w_0^2 ds &= \int_{0}^1 (\beta u_0'')'' w_0 - (\alpha u_0')'w_0 - \lambda u_0 w_0 ds\\
					  &= \int_{0}^1 (\beta w_0'')'' u_0 - (\alpha w_0')'u_0 - \lambda w_0 u_0 ds = 0.  
	\end{align}
	This integration by parts is possible because $u_0$ and $w_0$ satisfy the same boundary conditions. Since $\lambda >0$, we must have that $w_0 \equiv 0$ on $[0,1]$, which implies that $\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{0}$. This contradicts the fact that $\boldsymbol{w} \neq \boldsymbol{0}$. Thus \[\ker((\boldsymbol{I} - \lambda \boldsymbol{L})^2)  = \ker((\boldsymbol{I} - \lambda \boldsymbol{L})), \] and this implies via induction that $m(\lambda, \boldsymbol{L}) = 1$.

<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:global_bif_theorem}
	The set of bifurcation points from the trivial branch of $\boldsymbol{f}$ is exactly $\{(\lambda, \boldsymbol{0}), \lambda \in \mathcal E\}$. For $\lambda \in \mathcal E$, $(\lambda, \boldsymbol{0})$ belongs to a maximal connected subset $\mathcal C(\lambda) \subset \mathcal S$ which satisfies at least one of the following:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\mathcal C(\lambda)$ is unbounded in $\mathbb R \times \mathcal X$. 
		\item $\mathcal C(\lambda)$ contains another bifurcation point $(\mu, \boldsymbol{0})$. 
	\end{enumerate}
	Furthermore, near $(\lambda, \boldsymbol{0})$, $\mathcal C(\lambda)$ is locally an analytic curve.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Each $\lambda \in \mathcal E$ has algebraic multiplicity $1$, so Theorem 1.3 of \cite{Rabinowitz1971} implies that $(\lambda, \boldsymbol{0})$ is a bifurcation point of $\boldsymbol{F}$, and therefore of $\boldsymbol{f}$. Theorem 1.3 also provides the global alternative for $\mathcal C(\lambda)$. If $\lambda \not\in \mathcal E$, the implicit-function theorem implies that $(\lambda, \boldsymbol{0})$ cannot be a bifurcation point of $\boldsymbol{f}$ (see Theorem 5.4.1 in \cite{antman05}). Observe that $\boldsymbol{h}: \mathbb R \times \mathcal X \to \mathcal X$ is analytic, which implies that $\boldsymbol{g}$ is analytic as well. The remark following Theorem 1.19 of \cite{Rabinowitz1973} and Theorem 1.7 of \cite{crandallrabinowitz71} then yield the local analyticity of $\mathcal C(\lambda)$.
