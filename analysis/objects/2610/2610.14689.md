<!-- mainthm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:brauer}
Let $P$ be a nontrivial finite $2$-group. Then $P$ is a Sylow
$2$-subgroup of a finite group whose involutions form one conjugacy class
if and only if at least one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $P$ is homocyclic;
\item $P$ is generalized quaternion;
\item $P$ is a Suzuki $2$-group;
\item $P$ is isomorphic to a Sylow $2$-subgroup of one of the non-abelian
simple groups in Table~\ref{tab:simple-list}.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:oddcore}
Let $G$ be a finite group, and set $N=\mathrm{O}_{2'}(G)$. Then $G$ has
exactly one conjugacy class of involutions if and only if $G/N$ does.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Set $\overline{G}=G/N$, and let $\overline{x}\in\overline{G}$ be an
involution. The preimage of $\langle\overline{x}\rangle$ in $G$ is an
extension of the odd-order group $N$ by a group of order $2$. By the
Schur--Zassenhaus theorem, $N$ has a complement of order $2$ in this
preimage. Thus every involution of $\overline{G}$ lifts to an involution
of $G$. It follows that if $\mathcal{I}(G)$ is a single $G$-conjugacy
class, then $\mathcal{I}(\overline{G})$ is a single
$\overline{G}$-conjugacy class.

Conversely, suppose that $\mathcal{I}(\overline{G})$ is a single
$\overline{G}$-conjugacy class, and let $x,y\in\mathcal{I}(G)$.
After conjugating $x$ by an element of $G$, we may assume that
$\overline{x}=\overline{y}$. Then
\[
N\langle x\rangle=N\langle y\rangle,
\]
and $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle y\rangle$ are complements to $N$ in
this group. The conjugacy part of the Schur--Zassenhaus theorem shows
that $x$ and $y$ are $N$-conjugate. Hence $\mathcal{I}(G)$ is a single
$G$-conjugacy class.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:monolithic}
Let $G$ be a finite group with $\mathrm{O}_{2'}(G)=1$. 
Suppose that
$\mathcal{I}(G)$ is a single $G$-conjugacy class. Then $G$ has a unique
minimal normal subgroup $N=\operatorname{soc}(G)$, and one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $N\cong \mathsf{C}_2^n$ and $\mathcal{I}(G)=N^\#$. In particular,
$G$ acts transitively on $N^\#$;
\item $G$ is almost simple with socle $N$, and
$\mathcal{I}(G)=\mathcal{I}(N)$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $N$ be a minimal normal subgroup of $G$. Since
$\mathrm{O}_{2'}(G)=1$, the group $N$ has even order and hence contains
an involution. As $\mathcal{I}(G)$ is a single $G$-conjugacy class and
$N$ is normal in $G$, we have $\mathcal{I}(G)\subseteq N^\#$ and thus
$\mathcal{I}(G)=\mathcal{I}(N)$. Any two minimal normal subgroups
therefore contain all involutions of $G$. Since distinct minimal normal
subgroups intersect trivially, $N$ is unique. Hence $N=\operatorname{soc}(G)$.

If $N$ is solvable, then $N\cong\mathsf{C}_2^n$, so
$\mathcal{I}(G)=N^\#$ and (i) holds. Suppose that $N$ is nonsolvable.
Then $N=S_1\times\cdots\times S_t$, where the $S_i$ are isomorphic
non-abelian simple groups. If $t>1$, choose involutions $a\in S_1$ and
$b\in S_2$. Conjugation by $G$ permutes the factors $S_i$, whereas $a$
and $ab$ have nonidentity components in different numbers of factors.
They therefore cannot be $G$-conjugate. Thus $t=1$, and $N$ is
non-abelian simple. Consequently, $G$ is almost simple with socle $N$,
as in (ii).

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:fusion-gap}
Let $G \in \mathbf{OC}_2$, and let $P \in \mathrm{Syl}_2(G)$. 
Suppose that $E:=\mathrm{soc}(G)$ is solvable and $\mathrm{O}_{2'}(G)=1$.
 The following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is transitive on $\mathcal{I}(P)$;
\item $E\leq \mathrm{Z}(P)$;
\item $P\leq \mathrm{C}_{G}(E)$.
\end{enumerate}
If these conditions hold, then $\mathrm{N}_{G}(P)$ acts transitively on
$\mathcal{I}(P)$ by conjugation.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:monolithic}, we have $\mathcal{I}(P)=E^\#$.
Suppose that (i) holds, and choose an involution in $\mathrm{Z}(P)$.
Since $\mathrm{Z}(P)$ is characteristic in $P$, transitivity of
$\mathrm{Aut}(P)$ on $\mathcal{I}(P)$ implies that every involution of
$P$ lies in $\mathrm{Z}(P)$. Thus (ii) holds. Conditions (ii) and (iii)
both assert that $P$ centralizes $E$, so they are equivalent.

Assume (ii), and let $x,y\in E^\#$. Choose $g\in G$ such that $x^g=y$.
Both $P^g$ and $P$ are Sylow $2$-subgroups of $\mathrm{C}_G(y)$.
By Sylow conjugacy, there exists $c\in\mathrm{C}_G(y)$ such that
\[
(P^g)^c=P.
\]
Then $gc\in\mathrm{N}_{G}(P)$ and $x^{gc}=y$. 
So $\mathrm{N}_{G}(P)$ is
transitive on $\mathcal{I}(P)$, proving the final assertion and (i).

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:normalizer}
Let $G$ be a finite group, and let $N \unlhd G$ be a normal subgroup admitting a normal Sylow $2$-subgroup $A$. 
Let $E = \Omega_1(A)$, and suppose that $N \leq \mathrm{C}_G(E)$ and $\mathcal{I}(G) \subseteq E$. 
Set $H = G/N$. 
If $X \leq H$ has odd order and $\mathrm{C}_E(X) = 1$, then $\mathrm{N}_H(X)$ has odd order.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $L\leq G$ be the preimage of $X$, so that $L/N=X$.
Since $A$ is a $2$-group and $|L/A|=|X||N/A|$ is odd, the
Schur--Zassenhaus theorem gives $L=A\rtimes Y$ for a Hall
$2'$-subgroup $Y$ of $L$. Moreover, all Hall $2'$-subgroups of $L$
are conjugate.
We first show that $\mathrm{C}_A(Y)=1$. Otherwise,
$\mathrm{C}_A(Y)$ contains an involution $a$. Since $E=\Omega_1(A)$,
we have $a\in E$, and hence
$a\in\mathrm{C}_E(Y)=\mathrm{C}_E(X)=1$, a contradiction.

Applying the Frattini argument to $L\unlhd\mathrm{N}_G(L)$ and its
Hall $2'$-subgroup $Y$, we obtain
\[
\mathrm{N}_G(L)
 =L\,\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{N}_G(L)}(Y)
 =A\bigl(\mathrm{N}_G(Y)\cap\mathrm{N}_G(L)\bigr)
 \leq N\,\mathrm{N}_G(Y).
\]
Conversely, both $N$ and $\mathrm{N}_G(Y)$ normalize $NY=L$, so
$N\mathrm{N}_{G}(Y)\leq \mathrm{N}_{G}(L)$. Therefore
\[
\mathrm{N}_H(X)=\mathrm{N}_{G/N}(L/N)= \mathrm{N}_{G}(L)/N
 =\mathrm{N}_G(Y)N/N\cong \mathrm{N}_{G}(Y)/\mathrm{N}_{N}(Y).
\]
Moreover,
$\mathrm{N}_G(Y)\cap A
 =\mathrm{N}_A(Y)
 =\mathrm{C}_A(Y)
 =1$.
Thus $\mathrm{N}_G(Y)\cap E=1$. Since every involution of $G$ lies in
$E$, the group $\mathrm{N}_G(Y)$ contains no involutions and hence has
odd order. Its quotient $\mathrm{N}_{H}(X)$ therefore also has odd order.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:hering}
Suppose that $G\leq \mathrm{GL}_n(2)$ acts transitively on the nonzero vectors of $V=\mathbb{F}_2^n$. Then one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $G\leq \Gamma\mathrm{L}_1(2^n)$ is solvable;
\item 
$G^{(\infty)}$ is transitive on the nonzero vectors of $V$, and
\[
(G^{(\infty)},2^{n})\in \{ (\mathrm{SL}_{m}(q),q^{m}), (\mathrm{Sp}_{2m}(q),q^{2m}),(G_2(q),q^{6}) \}.
\]
Moreover, $V$ can be viewed as the natural $\mathbb{F}_{q}G^{(\infty)}$-module;
\item
$$(G,n)\in
\left\{
(\mathsf{A}_6,4),\,
(\mathrm{Sp}_4(2),4),\,
(\mathsf{A}_7,4),\,
(\mathrm{PSU}_3(3),6),\,
(G_2(2),6)
\right\}.$$
In these cases, the action of $G$ on $V$ is obtained from the corresponding natural low-dimensional modules as follows. For $G=\mathrm{Sp}_4(2)$, the module $V$ is its natural $4$-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_2$-module; for $G=\mathsf{A}_6\cong \mathrm{Sp}_4(2)'$, it is the restriction of this module to $\mathsf{A}_6$. 
For $G=\mathsf{A}_7$, it is the restriction of the natural $4$-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_2$-module of $\mathrm{SL}_4(2)\cong \mathsf{A}_8$. 
For $G=G_2(2)$, the module $V$ is its natural $6$-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_2$-module, while for $G=\mathrm{PSU}_3(3)\cong G_2(2)'$, it is the restriction of this $6$-dimensional module.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:linear}
Let $V=\mathbb F_2^{ n}$ be such that $n\ge2$. 
Suppose that $H\le\operatorname{GL}(V)$ is transitive on the nonzero vectors of $V$. 
If
\begin{equation}\label{eq:restriction}
 |\mathrm{N}_{H}(X)|\text{ is odd whenever }X\le H\text{ has odd order and }\mathrm{C}_{V}(X)=0,
\end{equation}
then either $|H|$ is odd, or $n=3$ and $H=\operatorname{GL}_3(2)$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
By Theorem~\ref{thm:hering}, either
$H\leq\Gamma\mathrm{L}_1(2^n)$, or $H$ is non-solvable, in which case 
either 
$$(H^{(\infty)},2^n)
\in
\{
(\mathrm{SL}_m(q),q^m),
(\mathrm{Sp}_{2m}(q),q^{2m}),
(G_2(q),q^6)\},$$
or
$$(H,n)\in \{ (\mathsf{A}_6,4),
(\operatorname{Sp}_4(2),4),
(\mathsf{A}_7,4),
(\operatorname{PSU}_3(3),6),
(G_2(2),6) \}.$$
We consider these cases in turn. 
To exclude a case, it suffices to find a subgroup $L\leq H$ and an odd-order
subgroup $X\leq L$,
such that $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X)=0$ and an involution in
$\mathrm{N}_{L}(X)$.






\medskip\noindent\emph{The semilinear case.}
Suppose that $H\le\Gamma \mathrm{L}_1(2^n)$. 
Set $X=H\cap \mathrm{GL}_{1}(2^{n})$. If $X=1$, then $|H|\le n$, whereas transitivity gives $2^n-1\mid |H|$, which is impossible for $n\geq2$.
Hence $X\ne 1$. 
Every nonidentity scalar multiplication is fixed-point-free on $V^\#$,
so $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X)=0$. 
Since $X\unlhd H$, condition~\eqref{eq:restriction} implies that $|H|$ is odd.






\medskip\noindent\emph{The special linear case.}
Suppose that $H^{(\infty)}=\mathrm{SL}_m(q)$.
By Theorem \ref{thm:hering}, we may regard $V$ as the natural $\mathbb{F}_qH^{(\infty)}$-module.
Let $\mathbb{X}$ be the subgroup of $\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}$ of order $q+1$,
and set $t=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ and $s=m-2t$.
Then $s\in \{ 0,1 \}$.
We identify the natural module $V$ with $\mathbb{F}_4 \oplus \mathbb{F}_{2^{m-2}}$ when $t>s=1$ and $q = 2$,
and with 
$\mathbb{F}_{q^2}^{t-1} \oplus \mathbb{F}_{q^2} \oplus \mathbb{F}_q^s$ whenever either $s=0$ or $q > 2$.







Suppose that $s=1$ and $q=2$. 
If
$H^{(\infty)}=\mathrm{SL}_3(2)$, then $(H,n)=(\mathrm{GL}_3(2),3)$ because 
$\mathrm{SL}_3(2)=\mathrm{GL}_3(2)$.
For $t>1$, we have $V=\mathbb{F}_4 \oplus \mathbb{F}_{2^{m-2}}$ with $m>3$ odd.
Let 
\[
X=\{d_{\lambda,\mu}:\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_4^\times,
\ \mu\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m-2}}^\times\}~\text{with}~(x,y)^{d_{\lambda,\mu}}=(\lambda x,\mu y).
\]
Then $X$ has odd order, and $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X)=0$. 
Note that $\det(d_{\lambda,\mu})=\lambda^{3}\mu^{2^{m-2}-1}=1$,
and so $X\leq\mathrm{SL}_m(2)$.
The involution $\sigma$ given by
$(x,y)^\sigma=(x^2,y)$ normalizes $X$, again contradicting
condition~\eqref{eq:restriction}.



Now suppose that $s=0$.
Then $V=(\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})^{ t}$.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:x_2}
  X_{2t}=\{d_\lambda:\lambda\in \mathbb{X}\}~\text{with}~(x_1,\dots,x_{t})^{d_\lambda}=(\lambda x_1,\dots, \lambda x_{t}),
\end{equation}
for $(x_1,\dots, x_{t})\in V$.
For each $\lambda\in \mathbb{X}$,
since $\lambda^{q+1}=1$, $d_\lambda$ has determinant $1$. 
Hence $X_{2t}\leq H^{(\infty)}$ and $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X_{2t})=0$.
The map
\[
(x_1,\dots,x_t)^\sigma=(x_1^q,\dots,x_t^q)
\]
defines an involution $\sigma\in H^{(\infty)}$, and
$(d_\lambda)^\sigma=d_{\lambda^q}$. Thus
$\sigma\in\mathrm{N}_{H^{(\infty)}}(X_{2t})$, contrary to
condition~\eqref{eq:restriction}.




It remains to consider $s=1$ and $q>2$. 
Then $V=(\mathbb F_{q^2})^{ t-1}\oplus \mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\oplus \mathbb{F}_q$.
Define 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:x_3}
X_{2t+1}=\{d_{\lambda,\mu}:\lambda\in \mathbb{X}, \mu\in\mathbb F_{q^2}^{\times}\}~\text{with}~(x_1,\dots,x_{t-1},x_{t},z)^{d_{\lambda,\mu}}=(\lambda x_1,\dots, \lambda x_{t-1},\mu x_{t}, \mu^{-(q+1)}z),
\end{equation}
for $(x_1,\dots, x_{t},z)\in V$.
It is straightforward to check that $X_{2t+1}\leq H^{(\infty)}$ and $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X_{2t+1})=0$.
Define $\sigma\in H^{(\infty)}$ by 
$$(x_1,\dots, x_{t},z)^{\sigma}=(x_1^{q},\dots, x_{t}^{q},z^{q}).$$
Then $\sigma$ is an involution and $(d_{\lambda,\mu})^{\sigma}=d_{\lambda^q,\mu^{q}}$.
Hence $\sigma\in\mathrm{N}_{H^{(\infty)}}(X_{2t+1})$, again contradicting
condition~\eqref{eq:restriction}.




\medskip\noindent\emph{The symplectic case.}
Suppose that $H^{(\infty)}=\operatorname{Sp}_{2m}(q)$. 
By Theorem \ref{thm:hering}, we regard $V$ as the natural module of $H^{(\infty)}$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$.
Decompose $V$ as an orthogonal sum
of $m$ hyperbolic planes, each identified with $\mathbb{F}_{q^2}$. The
subgroup fixing every summand contains $\mathrm{Sp}_2(q)^m$, acting
naturally on the respective planes.
Since $\mathrm{Sp}_2(q)=\mathrm{SL}_2(q)$,
we take $X=(X_2)^{m}$, where $X_2$ is defined in \eqref{eq:x_2} with $t=1$.
Then $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X)=0$, 
\[
 \mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{SL}_2(q)}(X_2)^{m}= \mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{Sp}_2(q)^{m}}(X)\leq \mathrm{N}_{H}(X).
\]
The special linear case shows that $2\mid |\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{SL}_2(q)}(X_2)|$,
and hence $2 \mid |\mathrm{N}_{H}(X)|$.
This excludes the symplectic case, including
$(H,n)=(\mathrm{Sp}_4(2),4)$.





\medskip\noindent\emph{The $G_2$ case.} Suppose that $H^{(\infty)}=G_2(q)$.
Then $q>2$.
By Theorem \ref{thm:hering}, we may view $V$ as the natural $H^{(\infty)}$-module over $\mathbb{F}_q$.
The preceding discussion provides a subgroup $L\leq H^{(\infty)}$ isomorphic to $\mathrm{SL}_3(q)$
such that, as an $L$-module, 
\[
V=W\oplus W^*,
\] 
where $W$ is the natural $L$-module and $W^*$ is its dual.
Identifying $L$ with $\mathrm{SL}_3(q)$,
we set $X=X_3$, where $X_3$ is defined in
\eqref{eq:x_3} with $t=1$. 
Then $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X)=\mathrm{C}_{W}(X)\oplus\mathrm{C}_{W^*}(X)=0$.
The special linear case gives $2\mid |\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{SL}_3(q)}(X_3)|$,
and therefore $2\mid |\mathrm{N}_{H^{(\infty)}}(X)|$, 
which contradicts~\eqref{eq:restriction}.




\medskip\noindent\emph{The four-dimensional exceptions.}
Suppose that $(H,n)\in \{ (\mathsf{A}_6,4),(\mathsf{A}_7,4) \}$.
Let $X$ be a subgroup of $H$ of order $5$.
Since the multiplicative order of $2$ modulo $5$ is $4$, 
the restriction to $X$ of a faithful
$4$-dimensional representation over $\mathbb F_2$ has no trivial constituent.
Thus $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X)=0$.
A $5$-cycle is inverted by a double transposition
in the corresponding alternating group, so $\mathrm{N}_{H}(X)$ has even order.
This contradicts condition~\eqref{eq:restriction}.





\medskip\noindent\emph{The six-dimensional exceptions.}
It remains to consider $(H,n)\in \{ (\mathrm{PSU}_{3}(3),6),(G_2(2),6) \}$.
Since $G_2(2)'\cong \mathrm{PSU}_{3}(3)$ is non-abelian simple,
we have $(H^{(\infty)},n)=(\mathrm{PSU}_{3}(3),6)$.
By the $\mathbb{ATLAS}$ \cite{Atlas}, the group $H^{(\infty)}$ has a maximal subgroup $M\cong \mathsf{ES}(3_+^{3}) \rtimes \mathsf{C}_{8}$.
Let $X$ be a Sylow $3$-subgroup of $M$.
Then $\mathrm{N}_{H^{(\infty)}}(X)$ has even order, and $V$ is a faithful $\mathbb{F}_2 X$-module.
Since $\mathbb{F}_2$ is not a splitting field for $X$ but $\mathbb{F}_4$ is, all faithful irreducible $\mathbb{F}_2X$-modules have dimension $6$.
 Hence $V$ is a faithful irreducible $\mathbb{F}_2X$-module, 
 and therefore $\mathrm{C}_{V}(X)=0$.
This is the final contradiction.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:solvable}
Let $G\in \mathbf{OC}_2$. 
If $E:=\mathrm{soc}(G)$ is solvable and $\mathrm{O}_{2'}(G)=1$,
then $P\in \mathrm{Syl}_{2}(G)$ is homocyclic, generalized quaternion, or a Suzuki $2$-group. 
Conversely, every nontrivial group in
one of these three families occurs as a Sylow $2$-subgroup of a finite
group $G$ satisfying these hypotheses.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:monolithic}, $E = \mathrm{soc}(G) = \mathcal{I}(G) \cup \{1\} \cong \mathsf{C}_2^n$. If $n = 1$, then every Sylow $2$-subgroup of $G$ has a unique involution and is therefore cyclic or generalized quaternion. 
 We may thus assume that $n\geq2$.

Let $P \in \mathrm{Syl}_2(G)$, and set $C = \mathrm{C}_G(E)$, $S = P \cap C$, $N = \mathrm{N}_G(S)$.
Since $C \unlhd G$, we have $S \in \mathrm{Syl}_2(C)$, $S \unlhd P$, and $P \leq N$. 
The Frattini argument then gives $G = CN$.
 Moreover, $E \leq S$, and $C$ acts trivially on $E$. 
 Hence $N$ acts transitively on $E^\#$. Since every involution of $G$ lies in $E^\#$, it follows that $\mathcal{I}(N)=\mathcal{I}(S) = E^\#$ and $\Omega_1(S) = E$.





Set $D=N\cap C=\mathrm{C}_{N}(E)$ and $\mathcal{H}=N/D$. Then
$\mathcal{H}\leq\operatorname{GL}(E)$ acts transitively on $E^\#$. We verify
condition~\eqref{eq:restriction} of Lemma~\ref{lem:linear}. Let
$X\leq\mathcal{H}$ have odd order and satisfy $\mathrm{C}_{E}(X)=1$. The group
$D$ has the normal Sylow $2$-subgroup $S$, with $\Omega_1(S)=E$;
moreover, $D=\mathrm{C}_{N}(E)$ and $\mathcal{I}(N)\subseteq E$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:normalizer}, applied to the pair $(N,D)$, therefore shows
that $\mathrm{N}_{\mathcal{H}}(X)$ has odd order.




Lemma~\ref{lem:linear} now implies that either $\mathcal{H}$ has odd order, or $n = 3$ and $\mathcal{H} \cong \mathrm{GL}_3(2)$. 
In the latter case,
\[
\operatorname{Aut}_G(E)=G/C\cong N/D=\mathcal{H}\cong\operatorname{GL}_3(2),
\]
contrary to Lemma~\ref{lem:exclude}.
Thus $|G : C|=|N:D|=|\mathcal{H}|$ is odd. 
Since $$P/S = P/(P \cap C) \cong PC/C \leq G/C$$ 
is a $2$-group, we have $P = S$.
Consequently, $N = \mathrm{N}_G(P)$ acts transitively on $\mathcal{I}(P) = E^\#$. 
By Lemma \ref{lem:fusion-gap},
$\mathrm{Aut}(P)$ is transitive on the involutions of $P$.
It follows from
\cite[Theorem~1.1]{LZ} that $P$ is homocyclic, generalized quaternion,
or a Suzuki $2$-group.
Since $n \geq 2$, the group $P$ has at least three involutions and hence is not generalized quaternion. 
Together with the case $n=1$, this proves the forward implication.

 


\medskip

For the converse, if $P$ is generalized quaternion, we simply take $G=P$. 
Now
suppose that $P$ is homocyclic or a Suzuki $2$-group, and set
$E=\Omega_1(P)$. Then
$E=\mathcal{I}(P)\cup\{1\}$ is an elementary abelian 2-group. 
In the Suzuki case,
this follows because $\mathrm{Z}(P)$ contains an involution and is
characteristic in $P$, so transitivity forces every involution of $P$ to
be central.

We claim that $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ contains an odd-order cyclic subgroup $B$ acting
transitively on $E^\#$. Suppose first that
$P\cong(\mathsf{C}_{2^a})^n$. 
Restriction of automorphisms yields an epimorphism
\[
\operatorname{Aut}(P)\longrightarrow \mathrm{Aut}(\Omega_1(P))
\]
whose kernel is a $2$-group. The preimage of a cyclic subgroup of
$ \mathrm{Aut}(\Omega_1(P))\cong\operatorname{GL}_n(2)$ of order $2^n-1$ therefore has, by the Schur--Zassenhaus
theorem, a cyclic complement $B$ of order $2^n-1$. Its induced action on
$E^\#$ is regular. If $P$ is a Suzuki $2$-group, take the odd-order
cyclic subgroup $B\leq\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ provided by the definition.



Set $G=P\rtimes B$. Since $G/P$ has odd order, every involution of $G$
lies in $P$. Thus $\mathcal{I}(G)=E^\#$, which is a single
$G$-conjugacy class.

It remains to identify the socle and the odd core. Let
$1\neq M\unlhd G$. If $M\cap P\neq1$, then the $2$-group $M\cap P$
contains an involution. Hence $M\cap E\neq1$, and the transitivity of $B$
on $E^\#$ gives $E\leq M$. If $M\cap P=1$, then
$[M,P]\leq M\cap P=1$, so $M\leq\mathrm{C}_{G}(P)$. Since $B$ has odd order
and $\operatorname{Inn}(P)$ is a $2$-group, no nonidentity element of $B$ induces an
inner automorphism of $P$. Therefore $\mathrm{C}_{G}(P)=\mathrm{Z}(P)\leq P$,
contrary to $M\cap P=1$. Thus every nontrivial normal subgroup of $G$
contains $E$. It follows that $E$ is the unique minimal normal subgroup,
$\operatorname{soc}(G)=E$, and $\mathrm{O}_{2'}(G)=1$. This completes the proof.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:even-index-reduction}
  Let $A$ be an almost simple group with socle $S$.
  Suppose that $A\in \mathbf{OC}_2$.
  If $|A:S|$ is even,
  then $S\cong \mathrm{PSL}_{2}(p^{f})$ for an odd prime $p$ and an even integer
$f$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:socle-inheritance} and \ref{lem:simple-list}, the group $S$ occurs in
Table~\ref{tab:simple-list}. Suppose first that
$S\cong\mathsf{A}_n$ with $n\geq7$. Since $|A:S|$ is even, we have
$A\cong\mathsf{S}_n$, which contains involutions outside
$\mathsf{A}_n$. This contradicts Lemma~\ref{lem:monolithic}(ii), so no
such alternating group occurs.

For each sporadic group $S$ in Table~\ref{tab:simple-list}, the outer
automorphism group has order at most $2$. When it has order $2$, 
the involution of $\mathrm{Out}(S)$ lifts to an involution
of $\mathrm{Aut}(S)$, as recorded in the $\mathbb{ATLAS}$
\cite{Atlas}. Lemma~\ref{lem:monolithic}(ii) therefore excludes every
sporadic group in the table.

Since $\mathsf{A}_5\cong\mathrm{PSL}_2(5)$ and
$\mathsf{A}_6\cong\mathrm{PSL}_2(9)$, it remains to consider the groups
of Lie type in Table~\ref{tab:simple-list}. By
\cite[Theorem~2.5.12]{GLS},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aut}
  \operatorname{Aut}(S)=G\rtimes\langle\phi\rangle\Gamma,
\end{equation}
where $G:=\operatorname{Inndiag}(S)$, $\phi$ is a field automorphism and $\Gamma$ is generated by graph
automorphisms.

First suppose that $S=\mathrm{PSL}_4^{\epsilon}( q)$, where
$\epsilon\in\{\pm\}$ and $4\parallel q-\epsilon1$. By
\cite[Theorems~1.12 and~2.9]{LMM}, the group $\operatorname{Aut}(S)$ splits over $S$,
and hence so does $A$. Since $2\mid|A:S|$, the group $A$ contains an
involution outside $S$, contrary to Lemma~\ref{lem:monolithic}(ii).

Now suppose that $|G:S|$ is odd. 
Choose an involution
$xS\in A/S$. 
Note that 
$$\operatorname{gcd}(|\langle x\rangle G/G|,|G/S|)=\operatorname{gcd}(2,|G/S|)=1,$$
and so an application of the Schur-Zassenhaus theorem to $\langle x\rangle G/S$ forces $\langle x\rangle G/S=G/S  \rtimes \langle xS\rangle$.
As $x\notin G$, by \eqref{eq:aut}, 
$\langle x\rangle G$ splits over $G$,
so that there exists an involution $y\in \langle x\rangle G$ 
such that $y^{g}\in y^gS=xS \subseteq A$ for some $g\in G$.
Thus $A$ again contains an involution
outside $S$, contradicting Lemma~\ref{lem:monolithic}(ii). Consequently,
$|G:S|$ is even. 
Inspection of the remaining groups of Table \ref{tab:simple-list} in the
$\mathbb{ATLAS}$ \cite{Atlas} yields
\[
S\cong\mathrm{PSL}_2(p^f)
\]
with $p$ odd and $p^f>4$. Finally, \cite[Theorem~1.12]{LMM} shows that
$\operatorname{Aut}(S)$ splits over $S$ when $f$ is odd. The same argument would then
produce an involution in $A\setminus S$. Hence $f$ is even.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:A1-even}
Let $A$ be an almost simple group with socle $S$.
Suppose that $2\mid|A:S|$. Then $A\in\mathbf{OC}_2$ if and only if
there are an odd prime $p$, an
integer $a\geq 1$, and an odd divisor $d$ of $a$ such that
\[
 S\cong\mathrm{PSL}_2(p^{2a}),\qquad
 A\cong\operatorname{PGL}_2^*(p^{2a})\rtimes\langle\phi^{2a/d}\rangle,
\]
where $\phi$ is the field automorphism induced by $t\mapsto t^p$ on
$\mathbb{F}_{p^{2a}}$. In this case, every $P\in\operatorname{Syl}_2(A)$ satisfies
$P\cong\operatorname{\mathsf{SD}}_{2(p^{2a}-1)_2}$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Assume first that $A \in \mathbf{OC}_2$. By Lemma \ref{lem:monolithic}(ii)
and Proposition~\ref{prop:even-index-reduction}, we have $S \cong \mathrm{PSL}_2(p^{2a})$ for some odd prime $p$ such that $\mathcal{I}(S)=\mathcal{I}(A)$.
Write $\mathrm{Aut}(S) = G \rtimes \langle \phi \rangle$ where $G : = \mathrm{Inndiag}(S)$, and identify $G$ with $\mathrm{PGL}_2(p^{2a})$.
Then $|G:S|=2$.
Since $G$ splits over $S$, it forces $G\cap A = S$.
Let $\langle \sigma\rangle$ be the Sylow 2-subgroup of $GA\cap\langle \phi\rangle(\cong GA/G\cong A/S)$.
We claim that $o(\sigma)=2$.
In fact, otherwise $o(\sigma)=2^{k}>2$;
since $\sigma S=gS \cdot aS$ for some $g\in G$ and $a\in A$ with $o(gS)\leq 2$,
and $GA/S$ is an abelian group,
it follows that $\sigma^{2^{k-1}}S=a^{2^{k-1}}S\in A/S$, whence $\sigma^{2^{k-1}}$ is an involution in $A\setminus S$; this contradicts $\mathcal{I}(S)=\mathcal{I}(A)$.
As $|A:S|=|AG:G|$, 
we conclude that 
$$|A:S|_2=|AG:G|_2=o(\sigma)=2.$$




Choose $P\in\operatorname{Syl}_2(A)$, and set $Q=P\cap S$. Then
$Q\in\operatorname{Syl}_2(S)$. By \cite[Chapter~2, Theorem~8.3]{Gorenstein}, the
Sylow $2$-subgroups of $\mathrm{SL}_2(p^{2a})$ are generalized
quaternion. Since
$\mathrm{Z}(\mathrm{SL}_2(p^{2a}))\cong\mathsf{C}_2$, it follows that
$Q\cong\mathsf{D}_{2^b}$, where $2^b=|S|_2$.
Since every involution of $A$ lies in $S$, every element of
$P\setminus Q$ has order greater than $2$, so that
\[
|\mathcal{I}(P)|=|\mathcal{I}(Q)|=2^{b-1}+1\equiv1\pmod4.
\]
By \cite[Theorem~4.9]{Isaacs}, $P'$ has index $4$ in $P$. Therefore,
by \cite[Chapter~5, Theorem~4.5]{Gorenstein},
\[
P\in\{\mathsf{D}_{2^{b+1}},\mathsf{Q}_{2^{b+1}},
\mathsf{SD}_{2^{b+1}}\}.
\]
The group $P$ has the maximal subgroup
$Q\cong\mathsf{D}_{2^b}$ and no involution in $P\setminus Q$.
Consequently, $P\cong\mathsf{SD}_{2^{b+1}}$.

The group $PS$ is isomorphic to neither $\mathrm{PGL}_2(p^{2a})$ nor
$S\rtimes\langle\phi^a\rangle$, since each of those groups contains an
involution outside $S$. Hence
$PS=\mathrm{PGL}_2^*(p^{2a})$. Moreover,
\[
A/S=PS/S\times\langle\phi^{2a/d}S\rangle
\]
for some odd divisor $d$ of $a$. Since
$PS\cap\langle\phi^{2a/d}\rangle
\leq S\cap\langle\phi^{2a/d}\rangle=1$,
we obtain
$A=PS\rtimes\langle\phi^{2a/d}\rangle$.

\medskip
Conversely, suppose that $A$ and $S$ have the stated properties. The calculation preceding this theorem shows that every $P\in\operatorname{Syl}_2(A)$ satisfies
$P\cong\mathsf{SD}_{2(p^{2a}-1)_2}$.
Set $Q=P\cap S$. Then $Q\in\operatorname{Syl}_2(S)$ and
$Q\cong\mathsf{D}_{(p^{2a}-1)_2}$. 
Every $2$-element of $A\setminus S$
lies in $P^g\setminus Q^g$ for some $g\in A$. 
The semidihedral
structure of $P$ shows that no such element is an involution. Finally,
$S\in\mathbf{OC}_2$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:simple-list}, and hence
$A\in\mathbf{OC}_2$.

  Let $q$ be a power of an odd prime $p$, and let $\epsilon\in \{ \pm \}$.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:almost-sylow}
A finite $2$-group $P$ occurs as a Sylow $2$-subgroup of an almost simple
group in $\mathbf{OC}_2$ if and only if $P$ is isomorphic to a Sylow
$2$-subgroup of a simple group in Table~\ref{tab:simple-list}.

<!-- proof-16: body_md -->

$|A:S|$ is odd, so $P\in\operatorname{Syl}_2(S)$ and $S$ occurs in
Table~\ref{tab:simple-list}. If case (ii) holds, then
Theorem~\ref{thm:A1-even} and Lemma~\ref{lem:mixed-redundant} show that
$P$ is isomorphic to a Sylow $2$-subgroup of either
$\mathrm{PSL}_3(p^a)$ or $\mathrm{PSU}_3(p^a)$, both of which occur in
Table~\ref{tab:simple-list}.

Conversely, if $P$ is isomorphic to a Sylow $2$-subgroup of a simple
group $S$ in Table~\ref{tab:simple-list}, then
Lemma~\ref{lem:simple-list} gives $S\in\mathbf{OC}_2$, proving
the result.

$N=\mathrm{O}_{2'}(G)$ and $\overline G=G/N$.
