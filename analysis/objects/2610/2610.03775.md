<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:block}
For \(1\leq j\leq\binom{N+s}{s}\), there is a unique
\(q\in\{0,\ldots,N\}\) satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block}
 f(N,s,q)<j\leq f(N,s,q+1).
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For \(q=0,\ldots,N\), the successive difference is
\[
 f(N,s,q+1)-f(N,s,q)=\binom{N-q+s-1}{s-1}>0.
\]
Thus \(f(N,s,\cdot)\) is strictly increasing. Since
\(f(N,s,0)=0\) and \(f(N,s,N+1)=\binom{N+s}{s}\), the intervals
\((f(N,s,q),f(N,s,q+1)]\) for \(q=0,\ldots,N\) are disjoint and their union
is \((0,\binom{N+s}{s}]\). Hence every integer
\(j\in\{1,\ldots,\binom{N+s}{s}\}\) lies in exactly one of these intervals,
which proves existence and uniqueness of \(q\).
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:local}
Let \(j\) be an integer with \(1\leq j\leq\binom{N+s}{s}\), and let \(q\) be
the unique integer from Proposition~\ref{prop:block}. Define
\[
 j'=j-f(N,s,q).
\]
Then
\[
 1\leq j'\leq\binom{N-q+s-1}{s-1}.
\]
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
From \eqref{eq:block}, we have
\(0<j-f(N,s,q)\leq f(N,s,q+1)-f(N,s,q)\). Using the difference formula from
the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:block}, this becomes
\[
 0<j'\leq\binom{N-q+s-1}{s-1}.
\]
Since \(j'\) is an integer, the lower bound is \(1\).
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:local-index}
The integer \(j'\) is the position of \(j\) within the block of monomials
whose exponent in the last variable equals \(q\). After fixing this exponent,
the remaining monomials in \(s-1\) variables of total degree at most \(N-q\)
are listed in the same order. There are \(\binom{N-q+s-1}{s-1}\) such
monomials. The first one corresponds to \(j'=1\), the second to \(j'=2\), and
so on. This is the index used in the recursive step of the
algorithm.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:two}
Let \(1\leq j\leq\binom{n+2}{2}\), and let \(q(j)\) be the unique integer in
\(\{0,\ldots,n\}\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qdef}
 f(n,2,q(j))<j\leq f(n,2,q(j)+1).
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ml}
 \ell(j)=q(j)+1,\qquad m(j)=j-f(n,2,q(j)).
\end{equation}
Then \(1\leq m(j),\ell(j)\leq n+1\), the \(j\)-th monomial in the
total-degree order defined above is \(x^{m(j)-1}y^{\ell(j)-1}\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bivfact}
 (V^{(2)})_{i,j}=(V_x)_{i,m(j)}(V_y)_{i,\ell(j)}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We prove the claims one by one.

First, recall from Proposition~\ref{prop:block} that for \(N=n\) and \(s=2\)
the function \(f(n,2,\cdot)\) is strictly increasing and satisfies
\[
f(n,2,0)=0,\qquad
f(n,2,n+1)=\binom{n+2}{2}.
\]
Hence there is a unique integer \(q(j)\in\{0,\ldots,n\}\) such that
\[
f(n,2,q(j))<j\leq f(n,2,q(j)+1).
\]

By definition, \(f(n,2,q)\) counts the monomials \(x^a y^b\) with
\(a+b\leq n\) and \(b<q\). Therefore the columns whose monomials have
\(y\)-exponent exactly \(q\) occupy the global indices \(j\):
\[
f(n,2,q)+1,\quad f(n,2,q)+2,\quad\ldots,\quad f(n,2,q+1).
\]
Since \(j\) lies in this interval, the \(j\)-th monomial has \(y\)-exponent
equal to \(q(j)\).

Next, the local position of \(j\) inside this block is
\[
j-f(n,2,q(j)).
\]
Because indices start at \(1\), the first column of the block corresponds to
\(x\)-exponent \(0\), the second to \(x\)-exponent \(1\), and so on. Thus
\[
m(j):=j-f(n,2,q(j))
\]
is precisely the column index in \(V_x\) associated with the \(x\)-part of the
monomial. Consequently, the \(x\)-exponent is \(m(j)-1\).

For the \(y\)-part, the exponent is \(q(j)\), so the corresponding column
index in \(V_y\) is one plus that exponent:
\[
\ell(j):=q(j)+1.
\]
Hence the \(y\)-exponent is \(\ell(j)-1\).

The bounds follow immediately: since \(q(j)\in\{0,\ldots,n\}\), we have
\[
1\leq \ell(j)=q(j)+1\leq n+1.
\]
Moreover, because \(j\) belongs to the block of \(y\)-exponent \(q(j)\),
\[
1\leq j-f(n,2,q(j))\leq f(n,2,q(j)+1)-f(n,2,q(j))=n-q(j)+1\leq n+1,
\]
so \(1\leq m(j)\leq n+1\). Therefore the \(j\)-th monomial is
\[
x^{m(j)-1}y^{\ell(j)-1}.
\]

Finally, for a data point \((x_i,y_i)\), the value of this monomial is
\[
x_i^{m(j)-1}y_i^{\ell(j)-1}.
\]
By the definition of the univariate Vandermonde matrices,
\[
(V_x)_{i,m(j)}=x_i^{m(j)-1},\qquad
(V_y)_{i,\ell(j)}=y_i^{\ell(j)-1}.
\]
Multiplying these two entries gives
\[
(V^{(2)})_{i,j}
=
x_i^{m(j)-1}y_i^{\ell(j)-1}
=
(V_x)_{i,m(j)}\,(V_y)_{i,\ell(j)}.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:bivfact}.
<!-- end proof-3 -->

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:general}
For every \(s\geq1\), \(N\geq0\), and
\(1\leq j\leq\binom{N+s}{s}\), \(\Phi_s(N,j)\) is the exponent vector of the
\(j\)-th monomial of \(\mathcal{M}_{N,s}\) in the prescribed order.  If
\(\Phi_d(n,j)=(a_1(j),\ldots,a_d(j))\) and
\(m_k(j)=a_k(j)+1\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-factor}
 (V^{(d)})_{i,j}=\prod_{k=1}^d (V_{x_k})_{i,m_k(j)}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We prove the statement by induction on \(s\), universally in the degree bound
\(N\). That is, for each \(s\geq 1\) we prove that \(\Phi_s(N,j)\) is correct
for every \(N\geq 0\) and every valid index \(j\).

The cases \(s=1\) and \(s=2\) are \eqref{eq:phi1} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:two}, respectively. We therefore assume \(s\geq 3\) and that
the statement holds for \(s-1\) variables for every degree bound \(N\geq 0\).

Let \(N\geq 0\) and \(1\leq j\leq\binom{N+s}{s}\). By
Proposition~\ref{prop:block}, there exist a unique
\(a_s\in\{0,\ldots,N\}\), a reduced degree \(N'=N-a_s\), and a local index
\(j'=j-f(N,s,a_s)\) such that
\[
1\leq j'\leq\binom{N'+s-1}{s-1}.
\]
Thus \(j'\) is a valid index for the problem of \(s-1\) variables with total
degree at most \(N'\). By the induction hypothesis,
\[
\Phi_{s-1}(N',j')=(a_1,\ldots,a_{s-1})
\]
gives the correctly ordered exponent vector for that reduced problem.

It remains to show that \(\Phi_s(N,j)=(a_1,\ldots,a_{s-1},a_s)\) is the exponent
vector of the \(j\)-th monomial in \(\mathcal{M}_{N,s}\). By
Proposition~\ref{prop:block}, the columns whose last exponent equals \(a_s\)
occupy the global indices
\[
f(N,s,a_s)+1,\quad f(N,s,a_s)+2,\quad\ldots,\quad f(N,s,a_s+1).
\]
Since \(j\) lies in this interval and \(j'=j-f(N,s,a_s)\), the integer \(j'\)
is exactly the local position of \(j\) inside the \(a_s\)-block. The ordering
inside this block is, by definition, the same as the ordering of monomials in
\(s-1\) variables with total degree at most \(N'=N-a_s\). Therefore, by the
induction hypothesis, the \(j'\)-th monomial of that reduced problem is
\[
x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_{s-1}^{a_{s-1}}.
\]
Appending \(a_s\) gives the monomial
\[
x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_{s-1}^{a_{s-1}}x_s^{a_s}.
\]
Its local position within the \(a_s\)-block is \(j'\), and the block itself
starts at global offset \(f(N,s,a_s)\). Hence its global position is
\[
f(N,s,a_s)+j'=f(N,s,a_s)+\bigl(j-f(N,s,a_s)\bigr)=j.
\]
Thus \(\Phi_s(N,j)=(a_1,\ldots,a_s)\) is precisely the exponent vector of the
\(j\)-th monomial in \(\mathcal{M}_{N,s}\).

Finally, for any data point \(\boldsymbol{x}_i\), the value of this monomial is
\[
\prod_{k=1}^s x_{ik}^{a_k}.
\]
By the definition of the univariate Vandermonde matrices,
\[
(V_{x_k})_{i,m_k}=x_{ik}^{m_k-1}=x_{ik}^{a_k},
\]
where \(m_k=a_k+1\). Therefore
\[
(V^{(d)})_{i,j}=\prod_{k=1}^s (V_{x_k})_{i,m_k(j)},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:general-factor}.
<!-- end proof-4 -->

    \]

    \(f(1,2,0)=0<2\le f(1,2,1)=2\), we get \(a_2=0\).
    Then \(j\leftarrow 2-0=2\), \(N\leftarrow 1-0=1\).
    \par\hangindent 40\p@ \(s=1\): \(N=1\), \(j=2\). By the base case
    \(\Phi_1(N,j)=(j-1)\), we get \(a_1=1\).
\end{itemize}

Hence \(\Phi_3(2,8)=(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(1,0,1)\), corresponding to
\(x_1^1x_2^0x_3^1=x_1x_3\), which is the eighth monomial in the list of
Example~\ref{ex:order-3d}. The univariate column indices are
\((m_1,m_2,m_3)=(a_1+1,a_2+1,a_3+1)=(2,1,2)\).
