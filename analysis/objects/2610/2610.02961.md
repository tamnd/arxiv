<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{A} {\rm \cite{c2022}} \\
Let $K$ be a number field and $\mathcal{O}_K$ the ring of integers in $K$. For every $n>1$ the conjecture {\rm JC}$(K,n)$: Polynomial
mappings $F=(F_1,F_2,\ldots,F_n)\in K[X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n]^n$ with $JF:=\det\,DF\equiv 1$ {\rm (Keller mappings)} are invertible and hence
have an inverse in $K[X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n]^n$, is equivalent to the following statement: \\
{\rm DJC}$(K,n)$: For every Keller mapping $F\in\mathcal{O}_K[X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n]^n$ with \\ $F(0,0,\ldots,0)=(0,0,\dots,0)$ the affine
curve
$$
F_1(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n)=F_2(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n)=\ldots=F_n(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n)\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(\mathcal{C}_F)
$$
always has non-zero points in $\mathcal{O}_K^n$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{B} {\rm \cite{c2022}} \\
If the Jacobian Conjecture over $\mathbb{C}$ is {\bf not} true, then for some $n\gg 1$ there exists a Keller map 
$F\in\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n]^n$ of the so-called quadratic-cubic linear form over $\mathbb{Z}$,
$$
F_i(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n)=X_i+\langle a_i,X\rangle^{d_i},\,\,\,\,\,\,X=(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n),
$$
$$
a_i=(a_{i1},a_{i2},\ldots,a_{in})\in\mathbb{Z}^n,\,d_i\in\{2,3\}\,\,\,i=1,2,\ldots,n,
$$
such that the affine curve $(\mathcal{C}_F)$ has {\bf no} non-zero points in $\mathbb{Z}^n$.

\label{thm-c2005} {\rm \cite{c2005}}
Let $F=(P,Q):\,\mathbb{C}^2\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^2$ be a polynomial map with integer coefficients, $F=(P,Q)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]^2$, and

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem1}
van den Dries showed that if $F\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]^2$ is a counter-example to the Jacobian Conjecture ($JF\equiv 1$), then $F$ maps 
$\mathbb{Z}[i]^2$ into a narrow neighborhood of its exceptional value set $A_F$. Namely,
$$
{\rm dist}\left(F(p),A_F\right)=\inf_{q\in A_F}|F(p)-q|\le 1,\,\,\,\,\,\forall\,p\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^n.
$$
The exceptional value set $A_F$ of $F$ is the smallest subset $A_F\subseteq\mathbb{C}^n$ such that the restriction 
$F:\,\mathbb{C}^n-F^{-1}(A_F)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^n-A_F$ is a un-branched covering.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop-c2005} {\rm \cite{c2005}}
Let $F=(P,Q)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]^2$ with Jacobian $JF\equiv 1$. If $F$ is not invertible, then $\hat{d}(F(p),A_F)\le 1$, 
$\forall\,p\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^2$. Here if $q=(a,b)\in\mathbb{C}^2$ and $V\subseteq\mathbb{C}^2$ we define:
$$
\hat{d}(q,V):=\max\,\left(\min\left\{|a-u||(u,b)\in V\right\},\min\left\{|b-v||(a,v)\in V\right\}\right).
$$

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm-c2023} {\rm \cite{c2023}}
Suppose $F\in\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n]^n$ and $JF\equiv 1$. If $F$ is {\bf not} invertible, then \\
{\rm (i)} For generic lines $l$ in $\mathbb{Z}^n$, the cardinalities $|F^{-1}(l)\cap\mathbb{Z}^n|$ are uniformly bounded. \\
{\rm (ii)} $N(F(\mathbb{Z}^n),B)=O(B^{n-1})$ as $B\rightarrow+\infty$, where the implied constants (in the $O(\cdot)$)
depend on $F$.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem3} 
Sometimes the definition of asymptotic value uses instead of smooth paths that tend to infinity sequences $(x_n,y_n)\in\mathbb{C}^2$ that
tend to infinity, such that their $F$-image, $\{F(x_n,y_n)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ tend to $(a,b)$. Do we get the same notion of asymptotic values
when we use asymptotic paths versus when using sequences $\{(x_n,y_n)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$? \underline{\bf NO!} Let $F(z)=e^z$. Then the only
asymptotic value of $F(z)$ using smooth paths is $0=0+i0$. Any path $x(t)+iy(t)$, $t>0$, where $x(t)^2+y(t)^2\rightarrow\infty$ and where
$x(t)<0\,\,\,\forall\,t>0$ while $|y(t)|$ is a bounded function is an asymptotic path of $F(z)$. On the other hand the total set of
sequential asymptotic values of $F(z)$ is huge. It equals $\mathbb{C}$. For let $z_0\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$. Then $z_0=R_0e^{i\theta_0}$
for some $R_>0$. We take the asymptotic sequence $a_n=\left\{\log\,R_0+i(\theta_0+2\pi\cdot i\cdot n\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$. For the 
sequential asymptotic value $z_0$ we take $a_n=-n$, say.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{A(F)}
If $F$ is a two dimensional polynomial mapping, and if $F$ satisfies {\bf the $Y$-degree condition}:
$\deg F=\deg_Y P=\deg_Y Q$, $(F=(P,Q)$) then $\forall\,(a,b)\in A(F)$ there exists a rational
mapping: $R(X,Y)=(X^{-\alpha},X^{\beta}Y+X^{-\alpha}\phi(X))$, ($\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^+,\,\beta\in
\mathbb{Z}^+\cup\{0\},\,\phi(X)\in\mathbb{C}[X],\,\deg\phi<\alpha+\beta$ and the gcd of the powers of $X$ that
effectively appear in $X^{\alpha+\beta}Y+\phi(X)$ is $1$) with the following two properties: \\
(i) $\exists\,G_R\in\mathbb{C}[X,Y]^2$ such that $G_R=F\circ R$ off ${\rm sing}(R)=\{X=0\}$. (The
mapping $G_R$ is called {\bf the $R$-dual of $F$}) \\
(ii) $\exists\,Y_0\in\mathbb{C}$ such that $(a,b)=G_R(0,Y_0)$. \\
Moreover, the set of all those rational mappings $R$ can be chosen to be a finite set.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{no-need-for-etale}
We did not use the Jacobian condition in our proof of Theorem \ref{A(F)}. The theorem is valid for polynomial mappings that are not
necessarily Keller. The proof shows the following point: the asymptotic paths we constructed in Theorem \ref{A(F)} are algebraic
in the coefficients of $F$. We only used rational transformations and zeros of $A_0(V)$'s. All the powers in our construction are integers,
i.e. no non-integral fractional powers are in our formulas. Soon we will be interested in the number of points of the lattice
$\mathbb{Z}^2$ on those asymptotic paths. The above implies that there will not be apriori cases in which this diophantine problem
is trivial.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{R_0(F)}
Let $F\in\mathbb{C}[X,Y]^2$ be a Keller mapping which is not a $\mathbb{C}^2$-automorphism. Then there exists a non-empty set $R_0(F)$ of
rational non-polynomial mappings $R\in\mathbb{C}(X,Y)^2-\mathbb{C}[X,Y]^2$ that satisfies the following: \\
{\rm (i)} The cardinality of $R_0(F)$ equals the number of the irreducible components of the $F$-asymptotic variety $A(F)$. \\
{\rm (ii)} $\forall\,R\in R_0(F)$, the mapping $G_R=F\circ R$ has a polynomial extension to $\mathbb{C}^2$. \\
{\rm (iii)} $\forall\,R\in R_0(F)$, the set $\{G_R(a,b)|\,(a,b)\in {\rm sing}(R)\}$ is an irreducible component of $A(F)$. \\
{\rm (iv)} We have the representation $A(F)=\bigcup_{R\in R_0(F)}\{G_R(a,b)|\,(a,b)\in {\rm sing}(R)\}$.

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
\label{non-empty-R_0(F)}

\'etale but not a $\mathbb{C}^2$-automorphism. Furthermore, each component of $A(F)$ is a polynomial plane curve (i.e. it has exactly

<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{fiber-theorem} {\rm(Fiber Theorem for Polynomial Maps, \cite{a1994}, page 32)} \\
Let $K$ be a commutative integral domain of characteristic zero and suppose that $F:=(F_1,F_2,\ldots,F_n)$ is a locally invertible system 
of polynomials in $K[X]$, $X:=(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n)$. Then the geometric degree $\dim_{K(F)}K(X)$ is finite. Moreover, for any point
$b\in K^n$, $\dim_{K(F)}K(X)$ majorizes the cardinality of the fiber $F^{-1}(b)$.

<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{invertible and locally-invertible systems} {\rm (\cite{a1994}, page 29)} \\
An element $F=(F_1,\ldots,F_n)$ of $K[[X]]^n$ is called an invertible system of formal series of $K[[X]]$or more briefly an invertible
element of $K[[X]]^n$ if $F(0)=0$ and there exits $G$ in $K[[X]]^n$ called the formal inverse of $F$, such that $G(0)=0$, $F(G)=G(F)=X$.
Now, let $F=(F_1,\ldots,F_n)\in K[X]^n$. For $a\in K^n$ we call the reduced Taylor polynomial of $F$ at the point $a$ the polynomial
$F_a=F_a(X)=F(X+a)-F(a)$. We say that $F$ is locally invertible system of polynomials of $K[X]$ or more briefly a locally invertible 
element of $K[X]^n$ if the reduced Taylor polynomial of $F$ at each point of $K^n$ is an invertible system of formal series of $K[[X]]$.
We say that $F$ is an invertible system of $K[X]$ or more briefly an invertible element of $K[X]^n$ if there exists $G\in K[X]^n$, called
"the inverse of $F$", such that $F(G)=G(F)=X$.

\label{generalization}
Let $F\in(\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\ldots,X_n])^n$ be \'etale and not injective. If $A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots$ is an infinite sequence of different points

<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
\label{alternative-proof}
We can give an alternative proof for Theorem \ref{generalization} by reducing it to the case of Theorem 
\ref{no-asymptotic-values-along-lattice-points}. We define,
$$
G(X_1,\ldots,X_n)=N^d\cdot F\left(\left(\frac{X_1}{N},\ldots,\frac{X_n}{X_N}\right)\right)\,\,\,\,\,\,\,{\rm where}\,\,d=\deg\,F.
$$
Then $G\in(\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\ldots,X_n])^n$ and for any sequence $A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/N)^n$ we have,
$$
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}||F(A_m)||_2=N^{-d}\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}||G(N\cdot A_m)||_2,
$$
where $NA_1,NA_2,NA_3,\ldots$ is a sequence in $\mathbb{Z}^n$. Thus Theorem \ref{no-asymptotic-values-along-lattice-points}
applies to the data $G$ and $NA_1,NA_2,NA_3,\ldots$ and we get $\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}||G(A_m)||_2=\infty$.
<!-- end remark-6 -->
<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
\label{interpretation}
Theorem \ref{no-asymptotic-values-along-lattice-points} and Theorem \ref{generalization} say that if $F\in(\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\ldots,X_n])^n$
is \'etale and not injective then any asymptotic path $\{(\alpha_1(t),\ldots,\alpha_n(t))|\,0<t\}$ of $F(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, i.e.
$F(\alpha_1(t),\ldots,\alpha_n(t))\rightarrow_{t\rightarrow\infty}(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n)$, 
where $|\alpha_1(t)|^2+\ldots+|\alpha_n(t)|^2\rightarrow_{t\rightarrow\infty}\infty$, contains only finitely many lattice points
$\mathbb{Z}^n$. This means that each such an asymptotic path misses all the points of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ except for finitely many of them.
We recall that by Theorem \ref{A(F)} all the coordinates $\alpha_1(t),\ldots,\alpha_n(t)$ are algebraic in the coefficients of 
$F(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$. So the system $\alpha_j(t)=m_j$, $1\le j\le n$ of algebraic equations in the unknown $t\in\mathbb{C}$ have only finitely many integral solutions, i.e. as diophantine system. Only finitely many $n$-tuples $(m_1,\ldots,m_n)\in\mathbb{Z}^n$ give
solvable diophantine equations.

<!-- theorem-11: body_md -->
\label{X,Y-finite} {\rm (\cite{p1996}, page 107 and \cite{p1998}, page 11)}\\
Let $f:\,\mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^2$ be a polynomial map that satisfies the Jacobian condition: $\forall\,(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^2$,
$\det\,J(f)(X,Y)\ne 0$. Then also the $k$-normalized form of $f$, $F_k$, satisfies this Jacobian condition and $f$ is a global diffeomorphism
if and only if $F_k$ has no $X$-finite or $Y$-finite asymptotic values.

<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\label{X,Y finite and weak,strong etale}
An asymptotic path $\sigma(t)=(X(t),Y(t))$, $0\le t<\infty$, of a polynomial $P(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}[X,Y]$ or of a polynomial map $(P,Q)$
is called $X$-finite if $\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty} |Y(t)|=\infty$ while $-\infty<\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty} X(t)<\infty$. The corresponding
asymptotic value, $\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty} P(X(t),Y(t))$ or  $\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty} f(X(t),Y(t))$ ($f(X,Y)=(P(X,Y),Q(X,Y))$)
is called an $X$-finite asymptotic value. \\
$f:\,\mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^2$ is weakly \'etale if $J(F)(X,Y)>0$ and it is strongly \'etale if there is a constant $\epsilon>0$
such that $J(F)(X,Y)\ge\epsilon$.

<!-- theorem-12: body_md -->
\label{thm2} {\rm (\'etale mappings and Theta functions)}
Let $f:\,\mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^2$, $f(X,Y)=(P(X,Y),Q(X,Y))\in\mathbb{R}[X,Y]^2$. Let $k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ be a natural number
that satisfies $\deg_X P<2k+1$. We define the $k$-transformation by $T_k:\,\mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^2$, $T_k(U,V)=(U,(1+U^{2k})V)$.
We define the $k$-normalized form of $f$ by $F_k=f\circ T_k$. {\rm See the beginning of section \ref{sec3}}. Then: \\ \\
{\rm (1)} $f$ satisfies the Jacobian condition in the weak form: $J(f)(X,Y)\ne 0$, if and only if $F_k$ satisfies that condition, i.e.
$J(F_k)(X,Y)\ne 0$.
\\ \\
{\rm (2)} $f$ satisfies the Jacobian condition in the strong form: $\exists\,\epsilon>0$ such that $J(f)(X,Y)\ge\epsilon$, if and only if
$F_k$ satisfies that condition with the same $\epsilon>0$, i.e. $J(F_k)(X,Y)\ge\epsilon$.
\\ \\
{\rm (3)} $f$ is an injection $\mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow f(\mathbb{R}^2)$, if and only if $F_k$ is an injection $\mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow
F_k(\mathbb{R}^2)=f(\mathbb{R}^2)$.
\\ \\
{\rm (4)} The complex Euler type infinite product $\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}(1+U^{2j})$, $U\in\mathbb{C}$, converges uniformly on compact
subsets of of the unit disc $\mathbb{D}=\{U\in\mathbb{C}|\,|U|<1\}$, and hence defines an analytic function of $U$ in $\mathbb{D}$.
\\ \\
{\rm (5)} The set of all the zeros of each of the factors $1+U^{2j}$, $j\ge k$, is the countable and dense (in the strong topology) subset
of the unit circle $\mathbb{T}=\{U\in\mathbb{C}|\,|U|=1\}$ given by:
$$
\left\{\exp\left(\left(\frac{1+2N}{2j}\right)\pi\cdot i\right):\,N\in\mathbb{Z},\,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\,j\ge k\right\}.
$$
{\rm (6)} The unit circle $\mathbb{T}$ is a natural boundary for $\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}(1+U^{2j})$, $U\in\mathbb{D}$.
\\ \\
{\rm (7)} The following sequence of complex polynomial maps:
$$
\left\{(f\circ T_k\circ T_{k+1}\circ\ldots\circ T_n)\right\}_{n=k}^{\infty}=
\left\{f\left(U,\left(\prod_{j=k}^{n}(1+U^{2j})\right)\cdot V\right)\right\}_{n=k}^{\infty},
$$
$(U,V)\in\mathbb{D}^2$, converges uniformly on compact subsets of $\mathbb{D}^2$.
\\ \\
{\rm (8)} The set $\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{D}$ is a natural boundary of the holomorphic mapping,
$$
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f\left(U,\left(\prod_{j=k}^{n}(1+U^{2j})\right)\cdot V\right),\,\,\,\,\,(U,V)\in\mathbb{D}^2.
$$
Analytic extension in the $U$ variable of the infinite product is the obstacle.
\\ \\
{\rm (9)} The holomorphic mapping defined by the limit of the sequence in $\mathbb{D}^2$ (in {\rm (7)} above) has the following three
representations in $\mathbb{D}^2$ below. \\
We denote the univariate polynomial in $U$, of degree $2(k-1)$
$$
S_{2(k-1)}(U)=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(1+U^{2j}),\,\,\,\,\,\,\,U\in\mathbb{D}.
$$
Then the first representation is:
$$
f\left(U,V\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_d(j)U^{2j}/S_{2(k-1)}(U)\right).
$$
The second representation is:
$$
f\left(U,V\cdot\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^n U^{n(3n-1)}/\left\{\left(1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^n U^{2n^2}\right)(S_{2(k-1)}(U))\right\}\right),
$$
and the third representation is:
$$
f\left(U,V\cdot\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n(2n+1)U^{n(n+1)}\right\}^{1/3}/
\left\{\left(1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^n U^{2n^2}\right)(S_{2(k-1)}(U))\right\}\right).
$$
\\ \\
{\rm (10)} The holomorphic mapping in {\rm (9)}, restricted to the square $(U,V)\in (-1,1)^2$ satisfies the same Jacobian condition
(weak or strong) as the polynomial mapping $f(U,V)$. Also, it is an injection $(-1,1)\rightarrow f((-1,1))$ if and only if $f$
(the original polynomial mapping) is such an injection.
