\label{def:hyperbolic_admissibility}
Let \(\Omega_\sharp\) be either domain in \eqref{eq:Omega_sharp_alternatives}. A pair \((f,c)\) is \emph{hyperbolically admissible} relative to \(\Omega_\sharp\) if the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sharp truncation and positivity.}
There exist \(f_{\mathrm{in}}\in L^2(\Omega_\sharp)\), \(c_{\mathrm{in}}\in L^\infty(\Omega_\sharp)\), and \(0<c_-\le c_+<\infty\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_hyperbolic_common_truncation}
f=f_{\mathrm{in}}\chi_{\Omega_\sharp},\quad
c=1+(c_{\mathrm{in}}-1)\chi_{\Omega_\sharp},\quad
c_-\le c_{\mathrm{in}}\le c_+,\quad \text{a.e. in }\Omega_\sharp.
\end{equation}
Thus \(f\) and \(c-1\) share the same sharp cutoff at \(\partial\Omega_\sharp\),
\begin{equation*}
\operatorname*{ess\,supp}f\cup\operatorname*{ess\,supp}(c-1)
\subset\overline{\Omega}_\sharp,
\end{equation*}
and jumps across \(\partial\Omega_\sharp\) are allowed. Equivalently,
\(q\in L^\infty(\mathbb R^n)\), \(0<q_-\le q\le q_+<\infty\), and
\(q=1\) on \(\mathbb R^n\setminus\overline{\Omega_\sharp}\).

\item \textbf{Transverse quotient structure.}
If \(\Omega_\sharp=\Omega_{\theta,\mu,h}\), then there exists
\(F\in L^1(D)\cap L^2(D)\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_hyperbolic_quotient_structure_translated}
\frac{f_{\mathrm{in}}(x)}{c_{\mathrm{in}}^2(x)}=F(y),
\quad\text{a.e. }\Omega_{\theta,\mu,h}.
\end{equation}
Hence \(f/c^2=F(y)\chi_{\Omega_{\theta,\mu,h}}\).

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:hyperbolic_forward_wellposedness}
Let \(A_q\) be the nonnegative self-adjoint operator in
\(L^2(\mathbb R^n,q\,dx)\) associated with the closed form
\begin{equation*}
\mathfrak a(v,w)=\int_{\mathbb R^n}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\overline w\,dx,
\quad\mathcal D(\mathfrak a)=H^1(\mathbb R^n).
\end{equation*}
Since \(q\) is bounded above and below, the weighted \(L^2\)-norm is
equivalent to the usual one, so \(\mathfrak a\) is densely defined, closed,
and nonnegative. Hence the representation theorem defines \(A_q\).

We claim that $\mathcal D(A_q)=H^2(\mathbb R^n)$, $A_qv=-q^{-1}\Delta v$.
Indeed, \(v\in\mathcal D(A_q)\) if and only if some \(g\in L^2(\mathbb R^n)\)
satisfies
\begin{equation*}
\int_{\mathbb R^n}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\overline w\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R^n}qg\overline w\,dx, \quad\forall\,w\in H^1(\mathbb R^n).
\end{equation*}
This means \(-\Delta v=qg\) distributionally. Since \(qg\in L^2(\mathbb R^n)\),
the Fourier multiplier estimate gives \(v\in H^2(\mathbb R^n)\). Conversely,
if \(v\in H^2(\mathbb R^n)\), then \(g=-q^{-1}\Delta v\in L^2(\mathbb R^n)\)
and the identity holds. The bounds on \(q\), together with
\(\|v\|_{H^2}\le C(\|\Delta v\|_{L^2}+\|v\|_{L^2})\), make the norm
equivalent to the \(H^2\)-norm.

For \(f\in L^2\), the spectral theorem gives the mild solution
\begin{equation*}
u(t)=\cos(tA_q^{1/2})f.
\end{equation*}
Moreover,
\begin{equation*}
U_\tau=\tau(\tau^2+A_q)^{-1}f\in H^2(\mathbb R^n),
\end{equation*}
which proves \eqref{eq:main_hyperbolic_laplace_equation}. Hence the Dirichlet
and weak Neumann traces in \eqref{eq:main_hyperbolic_laplace_data} lie in the
stated boundary spaces. This remains true even though the time-domain solution
is generated by a discontinuous \(L^2\) initial state. If classical
time-domain traces exist, their Laplace transforms agree with this definition.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:why_laplace_data}
The use of \(U_\tau\), and of \(U_\lambda\) in the parabolic problem below,
is dictated by the regularity of the forward solution. Under the present
assumptions, a mild solution is only known to lie in
\(C([0,\infty);L^2(\mathbb R^n))\), which does not define the Dirichlet or
Neumann trace of \(u(t,\cdot)\) on \(\partial\Omega_\sharp\). In contrast, the
resolvent formulas place every Laplace transform in \(H^2(\mathbb R^n)\), so
its elliptic Cauchy traces are well defined. Thus the Laplace-domain family is
a rigorous representation of the passive observation at the regularity allowed
by the sharp cutoff.

If higher regularity is imposed, the measurement can be stated directly in the
time domain. For example, if the solution is integrable with values in
\(H^2(\mathbb R^n)\) after multiplication by the relevant exponential weight,
then the boundedness of the trace maps and the Bochner integral give
\begin{equation*}
\gamma_DU_\tau=\int_0^\infty e^{-\tau t}\gamma_Du(t)\,dt,
\quad \gamma_NU_\tau=\int_0^\infty e^{-\tau t}\gamma_Nu(t)\,dt.
\end{equation*}
The same holds with \(e^{-\lambda t}\) for the heat equation. Hence equality
of the full time-domain Cauchy traces implies equality of the Laplace-domain
data. This is an exact-data statement; it does not provide stability for
inversion of the Laplace transform.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:dirichlet_only_observation}
Only the Dirichlet component of \eqref{eq:main_hyperbolic_laplace_data} is needed. For \(\tau>0\), \eqref{eq:main_hyperbolic_common_truncation} gives \(f=0\) and \(q=1\) in \(\mathbb R^n\setminus\overline{\Omega}_\sharp\), hence
\begin{equation*}
(-\Delta+\tau^2)U_\tau=0,\quad\text{in }\mathbb R^n\setminus\overline{\Omega}_\sharp.
\end{equation*}
Uniqueness follows from an \(H^1\) energy argument. Suppose
\(Z\in H^1(\mathbb R^n\setminus\overline{\Omega}_\sharp)\) solves
\((-\Delta+\tau^2)Z=0\) and has zero Dirichlet trace. Then
\(Z\in H_0^1(\mathbb R^n\setminus\overline{\Omega}_\sharp)\). Testing the weak
equation with \(\overline Z\) and taking real parts gives
\begin{equation*}
\int_{\mathbb R^n\setminus\overline{\Omega}_\sharp}
\bigl(|\nabla Z|^2+\tau^2|Z|^2\bigr)\,dx=0,
\end{equation*}
so \(Z=0\). The same holds on each connected component of the complement.
Thus the exterior \(H^1\) solution is determined by its Dirichlet trace, and
the exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann map determines its weak Neumann trace. On
the inner boundary, the exterior unit normal is the negative of the unit
normal for \(\Omega_\sharp\), fixing \(\gamma_NU_\tau\) according to
\eqref{eq:main_hyperbolic_laplace_data}.

If the time-domain Dirichlet trace exists with the weighted integrability of
Remark~\ref{rem:why_laplace_data}, it determines \(\gamma_DU_\tau\) for every
\(\tau>0\), and the exterior argument then determines the full Laplace-domain
Cauchy data. At the present \(L^2\) regularity, the Laplace-domain Dirichlet
family is taken as the observation. We do not claim unconditional equivalence
with a time-domain trace or stability of inverse Laplace transformation; see
\cite{knox2020determining}.

\label{def:parabolic_admissibility}
A pair \((f,\kappa)\) is \emph{parabolically admissible} relative to
\(\Omega_\sharp\) if the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sharp truncation and positivity.}
There exist \(f_{\mathrm{in}}\in L^2(\Omega_\sharp)\) and
\(\kappa_{\mathrm{in}}\in L^\infty(\Omega_\sharp)\) with
\(0<\kappa_-\le\kappa_{\mathrm{in}}\le\kappa_+<\infty\) such that
\begin{equation*}
f=f_{\mathrm{in}}\chi_{\Omega_\sharp},\quad \kappa=1+(\kappa_{\mathrm{in}}-1)\chi_{\Omega_\sharp}.
\end{equation*}
Thus \(f\) and \(\kappa-1\) are supported in
\(\overline{\Omega}_\sharp\) and may jump at
\(\partial\Omega_\sharp\). Equivalently, \(q\in L^\infty\)
satisfies \(0<q_-\le q\le q_+<\infty\) and
\(q=1\) outside \(\overline{\Omega}_\sharp\).

\item \textbf{Transverse quotient structure.}
If \(\Omega_\sharp=\Omega_{\theta,\mu,h}\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_parabolic_quotient_structure_translated}
\frac{f_{\mathrm{in}}(x)}{\kappa_{\mathrm{in}}(x)}=F(y),
\quad F\in L^1(D)\cap L^2(D), \quad \mbox{a.e. }\Omega_{\theta,\mu,h}.
\end{equation}
Hence $f/\kappa=F(y)\chi_{\Omega_{\theta,\mu,h}}$.

If \(\Omega_\sharp=\Omega_{\mathcal B}\), then there exist transverse
polynomials \(F_j\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_parabolic_quotient_structure_broken}
\frac{f_{\mathrm{in}}(x)}{\kappa_{\mathrm{in}}(x)}
=\sum_{j=1}^N F_j(Y(x))\chi_{P_j}(x),
\quad\text{a.e. }\Omega_{\mathcal B}.
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation*}
f/\kappa=\sum_{j=1}^N F_j(Y(x))\chi_{P_j}(x), \quad\text{in }\mathbb R^n.
\end{equation*}

\item \textbf{Mild solution, local boundedness and decay.}

\begin{equation*}
\sup_{t\ge0}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}<\infty,
\quad \|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}\to 0,
\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.
\end{equation*}
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:H_entire_TR}
The function \(H\) in \eqref{eq:H_def_TR} is entire on \(\mathbb C^m\).
Moreover, for every multi-index \(\gamma\in\mathbb N_0^m\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H_derivative_formula_TR}
\partial_z^\gamma H(z)=\int_D y^\gamma g_0(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy,
\quad z\in\mathbb C^m,
\end{equation}
where \(y^\gamma=y_1^{\gamma_1}\cdots y_m^{\gamma_m}\).
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Fix a compact set \(K\Subset\mathbb C^m\). Since \(D\) is bounded, choose
\(R_D>0\) with \(|y|\le R_D\) for \(y\in D\), and set
\(M_K:=\sup_{z\in K}|z|<\infty\). For \(z\in K\), \(y\in D\),
\begin{equation*}
|e^{z\cdot y}|=e^{\operatorname{Re}(z\cdot y)}\le e^{|z||y|}\le e^{M_KR_D}.
\end{equation*}
Hence \(|g_0(y)e^{z\cdot y}|\le e^{M_KR_D}|g_0(y)|\), which is integrable on
\(D\). Thus \(H\) is well defined and locally bounded.

To justify differentiation under the integral sign, fix \(z\in\mathbb C^m\)
and a coordinate vector \(e_j\). For small \(h\),
\begin{equation*}
\frac{e^{(z+he_j)\cdot y}-e^{z\cdot y}}{h}=y_j e^{z\cdot y}\int_0^1 e^{thy_j}\,dt.
\end{equation*}
For \(z,z+he_j\in K\) and \(|h|\le1\), this is bounded by $R_De^{(M_K+1)R_D}|g_0(y)|$,
which is integrable. Dominated convergence gives
\begin{equation*}
\partial_{z_j}H(z)=\int_D y_jg_0(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy.
\end{equation*}
Repeating the argument yields
\eqref{eq:H_derivative_formula_TR}.

Finally, for fixed \(z_0\in\mathbb C^m\),
\begin{equation*}
e^{z\cdot y}=e^{z_0\cdot y}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb N_0^m}
\frac{y^\gamma(z-z_0)^\gamma}{\gamma!}.
\end{equation*}
On a small ball about \(z_0\), the series is dominated by \(C|g_0(y)|\).
Termwise integration gives a convergent power series with coefficients
\(\partial_z^\gamma H(z_0)/\gamma!\). Hence \(H\) is holomorphic at every
point of \(\mathbb C^m\), and therefore entire.

It is \emph{totally real at \(p\in M\)} if
\begin{equation*}
T_pM\cap iT_pM=\{0\}.
\end{equation*}
If this holds at every \(p\in M\), then \(M\) is \emph{totally real}. Since

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:MU_totally_real_TR}
The set \(M_U\) defined in \eqref{eq:MU_def_TR} is an embedded real-analytic,
maximally totally real submanifold of \(\mathbb C^{n-1}\).
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We show that \(\Phi\) is a real-analytic embedding and that \(M_U\) is totally real.

Since \(U\Subset\mathbb R^{n-1}\setminus\{0\}\), the map \(\eta\mapsto|\eta|\) is
real analytic, so \(\Phi\) is real analytic. Moreover,
\begin{equation*}
\operatorname{Re}\Phi(\eta)=|\eta|\mu,
\quad \operatorname{Im}\Phi(\eta)=-\eta.
\end{equation*}
Thus \(\eta=-\operatorname{Im}z\) for \(z\in M_U\), so \(\Phi\) is injective
with real-analytic inverse \(z\mapsto-\operatorname{Im}z\).

For \(a\in\mathbb R^{n-1}\),
\begin{equation*}
d\Phi_\eta(a)=\mu\frac{\eta\cdot a}{|\eta|}-ia.
\end{equation*}
If \(d\Phi_\eta(a)=0\), then taking imaginary parts gives \(a=0\). Hence
\(\Phi\) is an immersion, and therefore an embedding. Thus \(M_U\) is an
\(m\)-dimensional real-analytic submanifold of \(\mathbb C^{n-1}\).

It remains to prove total reality. Fix \(\eta\in U\), set
\(e=\eta/|\eta|\), and suppose
\begin{equation*}
v\in T_{\Phi(\eta)}M_U\cap iT_{\Phi(\eta)}M_U.
\end{equation*}
Then for some \(a,b\in\mathbb R^{n-1}\),
\begin{equation*}
v=\mu(e\cdot a)-ia,\quad v=i\{\mu(e\cdot b)-ib\}=b+i\mu(e\cdot b).
\end{equation*}
Comparing real and imaginary parts,
\begin{equation*}
b=\mu(e\cdot a),\quad -a=\mu(e\cdot b).
\end{equation*}
Substituting the first identity into the second gives
\begin{equation*}
a=-\mu(e\cdot\mu)(e\cdot a).
\end{equation*}
Taking the scalar product with \(e\),
\begin{equation*}
\bigl(1+(e\cdot\mu)^2\bigr)(e\cdot a)=0.
\end{equation*}
Since the coefficient is positive, \(e\cdot a=0\), hence \(b=0\) and
\(a=0\). Therefore \(v=0\), so
\begin{equation*}
T_{\Phi(\eta)}M_U\cap iT_{\Phi(\eta)}M_U=\{0\}.
\end{equation*}
Thus \(M_U\) is maximally totally real.

\label{lem:local_graph_TR}
Let \(M\subset\mathbb C^{n-1}\) be a real-analytic maximally totally real
submanifold and \(p\in M\). After a translation and a complex-linear change
of coordinates, \(M\) is locally the graph
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local_graph_form}
M=\{x+i\varphi(x):x\in V\subset\mathbb R^m\},
\end{equation}
where \(V\) is a neighborhood of \(0\) and \(\varphi:V\to\mathbb R^{n-1}\) is
real analytic with \(\varphi(0)=0\) and \(D\varphi(0)=0\).
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
After translating, assume \(p=0\), and set \(P:=T_0M\). Since \(M\) is
maximally totally real at \(0\),
\begin{equation*}
P\cap iP=\{0\},
\end{equation*}
so \(\mathbb C^{n-1}=P\oplus iP\) as real vector spaces. Choose a real basis
\(v_1,\dots,v_{n-1}\) of \(P\). These vectors are complex-linearly independent:
if \(\sum_j(a_j+ib_j)v_j=0\) with \(a_j,b_j\in\mathbb R\), then
\(\sum_j a_jv_j=-i\sum_j b_jv_j\in P\cap iP\), so both sums vanish and hence
\(a_j=b_j=0\). Thus an invertible complex-linear map sends \(P\) to

parametrization of \(M\) with \(\gamma(0)=0\) and
\(d\gamma_0(\mathbb R^{n-1})=\mathbb R^{n-1}\). Write
\begin{equation*}
\gamma(t)=\operatorname{Re}\gamma(t)+i\operatorname{Im}\gamma(t).
\end{equation*}
Since \(d\gamma_0\) maps onto \(\mathbb R^{n-1}\) and the imaginary part of
\(T_0M\) is zero, the differential of \(t\mapsto\operatorname{Re}\gamma(t)\)
at \(0\) is invertible. By the real-analytic inverse function theorem,
\begin{equation*}
t\mapsto x:=\operatorname{Re}\gamma(t),
\end{equation*}
is a real-analytic diffeomorphism from a neighborhood of \(0\) onto some
neighborhood \(V\subset\mathbb R^{n-1}\). Reparametrizing \(M\) by \(x\),
\begin{equation*}
\gamma(t(x))=x+i\varphi(x),\quad \varphi(x):=\operatorname{Im}\gamma(t(x)).
\end{equation*}
Then \(\varphi\) is real analytic, \(\varphi(0)=0\), and
\(D\varphi(0)=0\) because \(T_0M=\mathbb R^{n-1}\). This gives
\eqref{eq:local_graph_form}.

\label{lem:holomorphic_uniqueness_TR}
Let \(O\subset\mathbb C^{n-1}\) be connected and open, and let \(F\) be
holomorphic in \(O\). If \(F\) vanishes on a nonempty relatively open subset
of a real-analytic maximally totally real submanifold \(M\subset O\), then

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Choose \(p\in M\) such that \(F\) vanishes on a relatively open neighborhood of
\(p\) in \(M\). By Lemma~\ref{lem:local_graph_TR}, after a translation and a
complex-linear change of coordinates, assume \(p=0\) and, near \(0\),
\begin{equation*}
M=\{x+i\varphi(x):x\in V\subset\mathbb R^{n-1}\},
\end{equation*}
with \(\varphi\) real analytic, \(\varphi(0)=0\), \(D\varphi(0)=0\), and
\begin{equation*}
F(x+i\varphi(x))=0,\quad x\in V.
\end{equation*}
Since \(\varphi\) is real analytic, it has a holomorphic extension
\(\varphi_{\mathbb C}\) to a complex neighborhood \(V_{\mathbb C}\) of \(0\).
Define
\begin{equation*}
\Psi(z):=z+i\varphi_{\mathbb C}(z).
\end{equation*}
Then \(D\Psi(0)=I\), so by the holomorphic inverse function theorem \(\Psi\) is
biholomorphic from a neighborhood of \(0\) onto a neighborhood of \(0\), after
shrinking \(V_{\mathbb C}\). Set
\begin{equation*}
G(z):=F(\Psi(z)).
\end{equation*}
Then \(G\) is holomorphic near \(0\), and \(G(x)=0\) for real \(x\in V\).

Choose a product box \(I_1\times\cdots\times I_{n-1}\Subset V\) of open intervals
containing \(0\). We show \(G\equiv0\) in a polydisc around \(0\). Fix real
\((x_2,\cdots,x_{n-1})\in I_2\times\cdots\times I_{n-1}\). The holomorphic function
\(z_1\mapsto G(z_1,x_2,\cdots,x_{n-1})\) vanishes on \(I_1\), so it vanishes
identically in a complex disk about \(0\). Thus \(G(z_1,x_2,\cdots,x_{n-1})=0\) for
complex \(z_1\) near \(0\) and real \((x_2,\cdots,x_{n-1})\) in the box. Fixing such
\(z_1\) and real \((x_3,\cdots,x_{n-1})\), the same one-variable argument applied to
\(z_2\mapsto G(z_1,z_2,x_3,\cdots,x_{n-1})\) extends the vanishing in \(z_2\).
Repeating successively for \(z_3,\cdots,z_{n-1}\), we obtain \(G\equiv0\) in a
polydisc around \(0\).

Since \(\Psi\) is biholomorphic, \(F\equiv0\) in a neighborhood of \(p\). By the
identity theorem for holomorphic functions of several variables, the zero set
of \(F\) is all of the connected domain \(O\). Hence \(F\equiv0\) in \(O\).

Assume \eqref{eq:main_TR_identity_minus}. By the reduction in

\label{eq:H_identically_zero}
\end{equation}

Evaluating on the imaginary axis, for every \(\lambda\in\mathbb R^{n-1}\),
\begin{equation*}
0=H(-i\lambda)=\int_D g_0(y)e^{-i\lambda\cdot y}\,dy.
\end{equation*}
Let \(\widetilde g_0\) be the zero extension of \(g_0\) to \(\mathbb R^{n-1}\).
Since \(g_0\in L^1(D)\) and \(D\) is bounded, \(\widetilde g_0\in L^1(\mathbb R^{n-1})\).
Thus
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{\widetilde g_0}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb R^m}\widetilde g_0(y)e^{-i\lambda\cdot y}\,dy=0, \quad \lambda\in\mathbb R^{n-1}.
\end{equation*}
By injectivity of the Fourier transform on \(L^1(\mathbb R^{n-1})\),
\(\widetilde g_0=0\) a.e.; hence \(g_0=0\) a.e. in \(D\). By the structural
assumption \eqref{eq:g_codimension_one_structure_TR},
\begin{equation*}
g(x)=g_0(y)\chi_{\Omega_{\theta,\mu,E}}(x)=0, \quad  \mbox{a.e.~}\Omega_{\theta,\mu,E}.
\end{equation*}
For the sign-reversed identity, assume
\begin{equation*}
\int_{\Omega_{\theta,\mu,E}}g(x)e^{ix\cdot\zeta_\theta(\eta)}\,dx=0,
\quad \eta\in U.
\end{equation*}
Using (\ref{eq:phase_factorization_general}), Fubini's theorem gives
\begin{equation*}
B_E^-(|\eta|)
\int_D g_0(y)\exp\bigl[(-|\eta|\mu+i\eta)\cdot y\bigr]\,dy=0,
\end{equation*}
where $B_E^-(\rho):=\int_E e^{-\rho r}\,dr$. Since \(B_E^-(\rho)>0\) for \(\rho>0\),
$H(i\eta-|\eta|\mu)=0$, $\eta\in U$. But
\begin{equation*}
\Phi_+(\eta):=i\eta-|\eta|\mu=-\Phi(\eta),
\end{equation*}
and multiplication by \(-1\) preserves embeddedness and the maximally totally
real property. Hence \(\Phi_+(U)\) is again a real-analytic maximally totally
real submanifold. The same holomorphic uniqueness and Fourier transform
arguments yield \(H\equiv0\) and \(g=0\) a.e. This proves both assertions.

\label{lem:weighted_BB_vertex_expansion}
Let \(D\subset\mathbb R^{n-1}\) be a compact simple convex polytope with nonempty
interior, and let \(p\in\mathbb C[y_1,\ldots,y_{n-1}]\). For each vertex
\(v\in V(D)\), let \(u_{v,1},\ldots,u_{v,n-1}\) be the inward edge directions
generating the tangent cone
\begin{equation*}
C_v=\left\{v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_ku_{v,k}:s_k\ge0\right\}.
\end{equation*}
Then there exist rational functions \(R_{D,p,v}(z)\), \(v\in V(D)\), such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted_BB_expansion}
\int_D p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=\sum_{v\in V(D)}e^{z\cdot v}R_{D,p,v}(z),
\end{equation}
as a meromorphic identity in \(z\in\mathbb C^{n-1}\). If
\begin{equation*}
p\left(v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_ku_{v,k}\right)
=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb N_0^{n-1}}c_\alpha^{(v)}s^\alpha,
\end{equation*}
then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:explicit_R_Dpv}
R_{D,p,v}(z)=|\det(u_{v,1},\ldots,u_{v,n-1})|
\sum_\alpha c_\alpha^{(v)}\frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|+n-1}\alpha!}
{\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(z\cdot u_{v,k})^{\alpha_k+1}}.
\end{equation}
Moreover, if \(p\not\equiv0\), then
\begin{equation*}
R_{D,p,v}\not\equiv0, \quad \forall \,v\in V(D).
\end{equation*}

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
\noindent {Step 1.} Set
\begin{equation*}
H_{D,p}(z):=\int_D p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy,\quad z\in\mathbb C^{n-1}.
\end{equation*}
Since \(D\) is compact and \(p\) is polynomial, the integral converges
absolutely for every \(z\in\mathbb C^{n-1}\). Differentiation under the integral
sign gives
\begin{equation*}
\partial_z^\alpha H_{D,p}(z)=\int_D y^\alpha p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy.
\end{equation*}
Hence \(H_{D,p}\) is entire. The Brion--Barvinok expansion represents it as a
sum of meromorphic vertex terms whose poles cancel in the total sum.

\noindent {Step 2.}
Fix a vertex \(v\in V(D)\). Since \(D\) is simple, the tangent cone at \(v\) is generated by \(m\) linearly independent inward edge directions \(u_{v,1},\ldots,u_{v,n-1}\):
\begin{equation*}
C_v=\left\{v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_k u_{v,k}:s_k\ge0\right\}.
\end{equation*}
In the region $\operatorname{Re}(z\cdot u_{v,k})<0$, $k=1,\cdots,n-1$, the cone integral \(\int_{C_v}p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy\) converges absolutely. Using $
y=v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_k u_{v,k}$, $s_k\ge0$, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cone_integral_computation_clear}
\int_{C_v}p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=e^{z\cdot v}|\det(u_{v,1},\cdots,u_{v,m})| \int_{\mathbb R_+^{n-1}}p\left(v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_k u_{v,k}\right)e^{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_k z\cdot u_{v,k}}\,ds.
\end{equation}
Expand
\begin{equation*}
p\left(v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_k u_{v,k}\right)
=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb N_0^{n-1}}c_\alpha^{(v)}s^\alpha .
\end{equation*}
Substituting this into \eqref{eq:cone_integral_computation_clear} and using Fubini's theorem,
\begin{equation*}
\int_{C_v}p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=e^{z\cdot v}|\det(u_{v,1},\ldots,u_{v,n-1})|
\sum_\alpha c_\alpha^{(v)}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\int_0^\infty 
s_k^{\alpha_k}e^{s_k z\cdot u_{v,k}}\,ds_k .
\end{equation*}
It follows from 
\begin{equation*}
\int_0^\infty s^\ell e^{as}\,ds=\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}\ell!}{a^{\ell+1}},\quad \operatorname{Re}a<0, \quad  \ell \geq 1,
\end{equation*}
that in the convergence region,
\begin{equation*}
\int_{C_v}p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=e^{z\cdot v}R_{D,p,v}(z),
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{equation*}
R_{D,p,v}(z)=|\det(u_{v,1},\ldots,u_{v,n-1})|\sum_\alpha c_\alpha^{(v)}
\frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|+n-1}\alpha!} {\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(z\cdot u_{v,k})^{\alpha_k+1}} .
\end{equation*}
The right-hand side is rational in \(z\), so it gives the meromorphic continuation
\begin{equation*}
\int_{C_v}^{\mathrm{mer}}p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=e^{z\cdot v}R_{D,p,v}(z).
\end{equation*}

\noindent {Step 3.}
For a tangent cone \(C_v\), the integral $\int_{C_v} p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy$
converges absolutely when $\operatorname{Re}(z\cdot u_{v,k})<0$, $k=1,\cdots,n-1$.
Let \(\int_{C_v}^{\mathrm{mer}}\) denote its meromorphic continuation; in the
convergence region it agrees with the ordinary integral, and elsewhere it is
defined by the rational expression from Step 2.

Write $p(y)=\sum_\beta a_\beta y^\beta$ and $p(\partial_z):=\sum_\beta a_\beta \partial_z^\beta$. Barvinok's unweighted formula \cite[Theorem~2.6]{barvinok1993computing} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unweighted_BB_meromorphic_identity}
\int_D e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=\sum_{v\in V(D)}\int_{C_v}^{\mathrm{mer}} e^{z\cdot y}\,dy.
\end{equation}
Since \(D\) is compact, differentiating under the integral sign yields
\begin{equation*}
p(\partial_z)\int_D e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=\int_D p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy.
\end{equation*}
For fixed \(v\), the same holds in the convergence region, so
\begin{equation*}
p(\partial_z)\int_{C_v} e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=\int_{C_v} p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy.
\end{equation*}
Both sides continue meromorphically, hence
\begin{equation*}
p(\partial_z)\int_{C_v}^{\mathrm{mer}} e^{z\cdot y}\,dy
=\int_{C_v}^{\mathrm{mer}} p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy.
\end{equation*}
Applying \(p(\partial_z)\) to \eqref{eq:unweighted_BB_meromorphic_identity}
and using this termwise gives
\begin{equation*}
\int_D p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=\sum_{v\in V(D)}
\int_{C_v}^{\mathrm{mer}} p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy.
\end{equation*}
By Step 2,
\begin{equation*}
\int_D p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy=\sum_{v\in V(D)} e^{z\cdot v}R_{D,p,v}(z),
\end{equation*}
which is \eqref{eq:weighted_BB_expansion}.

The terms \(e^{z\cdot v}R_{D,p,v}(z)\) may have poles on \(z\cdot u_{v,k}=0\), but the full sum is entire, since it equals \(z\mapsto \int_D p(y)e^{z\cdot y}\,dy\). Thus the identity is meromorphic and all poles cancel in the sum.

\noindent {Step 4.}
It remains to prove that if \(p\not\equiv0\), then $R_{D,p,v}\not\equiv0$,
$\forall \,v\in V(D)$. Suppose \(R_{D,p,v}\equiv0\) for some vertex \(v\). Set
$X_k:=z\cdot u_{v,k}$, $k=1,\cdots,n-1$. Since \(u_{v,1},\cdots,u_{v,n-1}\) are linearly independent, \(X_1,\cdots,X_{n-1}\) are linear coordinates on \(\mathbb C^{n-1}\). Then
\begin{equation*}
0\equiv\sum_\alpha c_\alpha^{(v)} \frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|+n-1}\alpha!}
{X_1^{\alpha_1+1}\cdots X_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}+1}}.
\end{equation*}
Choose \(M\) larger than every component of every multi-index \(\alpha\) in the
finite sum, and multiply by \(X_1^{M+1}\cdots X_{n-1}^{M+1}\). This gives the
polynomial identity
\begin{equation*}
0\equiv\sum_\alpha c_\alpha^{(v)}(-1)^{|\alpha|+n-1}\alpha!
X_1^{M-\alpha_1}\cdots X_{n-1}^{M-\alpha_{n-1}}.
\end{equation*}
By linear independence of monomials, $c_\alpha^{(v)}=0$, $\forall \,\alpha$.
Hence
\begin{equation*}
p\left(v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_k u_{v,k}\right)\equiv 0,
\end{equation*}
as a polynomial in \(s\). Since $s\longmapsto v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_k u_{v,k}$ is an invertible affine change of coordinates, this implies \(p\equiv0\), contradicting the assumption. Therefore \(R_{D,p,v}\not\equiv0\) for every vertex \(v\).

\label{lem:homogeneous_polynomial_physical_ray}
Let \(P\) be a nonzero homogeneous polynomial on \(\mathbb C^{n-1}\), let
\(\gamma\in\mathbb R^{n-1}\), \(\sigma\in\{1,-1\}\), and let
\(\mathcal O\subset\mathbb S^{n-2}\) be nonempty and relatively open. Then
\(P(\gamma-i\sigma\omega)\) is not identically zero on \(\mathcal O\). Hence
\begin{equation*}
\{\omega\in\mathcal O:P(\gamma-i\sigma\omega)=0\},
\end{equation*}
has empty interior in \(\mathcal O\).
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Assume, to the contrary, that \(P(\gamma-i\sigma\omega)=0\) on a nonempty
relatively open subset \(\mathcal O_0\subset\mathcal O\). By homogeneity,
\begin{equation*}
P(|\xi|\gamma-i\sigma\xi)=0,
\end{equation*}
for all \(\xi=\rho\omega\), \(\rho>0\), \(\omega\in\mathcal O_0\), hence on an
open cone in \(\mathbb R^{n-1}\setminus\{0\}\).

The map
\begin{equation*}
\xi\longmapsto |\xi|\gamma-i\sigma\xi,
\end{equation*}
parametrizes a real-analytic maximally totally real \((n-1)\)-submanifold of
\(\mathbb C^{n-1}\). Indeed, it is injective since its imaginary part
\(-\sigma\xi\) determines \(\xi\). At \(\xi\) with \(e=\xi/|\xi|\), its
differential is $a\longmapsto \gamma(e\cdot a)-i\sigma a$. If this equals \(i(\gamma(e\cdot b)-i\sigma b)\) for some \(b\), then
\begin{equation*}
\sigma b=\gamma(e\cdot a),\quad -\sigma a=\gamma(e\cdot b).
\end{equation*}
Thus \(a=-\gamma(e\cdot\gamma)(e\cdot a)\), and taking the scalar product
with \(e\) gives
\begin{equation*}
\bigl(1+(e\cdot\gamma)^2\bigr)(e\cdot a)=0.
\end{equation*}
Hence \(e\cdot a=0\), so \(a=b=0\). Therefore the tangent space is totally
real, and the submanifold is a holomorphic uniqueness set.

Since \(P\) is entire and vanishes on a nonempty open piece of this
submanifold, \(P\equiv0\), contradicting the assumption. Hence
\(P(\gamma-i\sigma\omega)\not\equiv0\) on \(\mathcal O\), and its zero set
has empty interior in \(\mathcal O\).

\label{lem:vertex_coefficient_asymptotics}
Let \(D\), \(p\), \(v\), and \(R_{D,p,v}\) be as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:weighted_BB_vertex_expansion}, and assume \(p\not\equiv0\).
Write
\begin{equation*}
p\left(v+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} s_k u_{v,k}\right)=\sum_{\alpha} c_\alpha^{(v)} s^\alpha,
\end{equation*}
and set $\kappa_{p,v}:=\min\{|\alpha|:c_\alpha^{(v)}\ne0\}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R_vertex_asymptotic}
R_{D,p,v}(\rho\lambda)=\rho^{-(n-1)-\kappa_{p,v}}
R_{D,p,v}^{[\kappa]}(\lambda)+O(\rho^{-(n-1)-\kappa_{p,v}-1}),\quad \mbox{as~} \rho\to+\infty,
\end{equation}
uniformly on compact subsets of $\left\{\lambda:\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(\lambda\cdot u_{v,k})\ne0\right\}$. Here
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R_leading_homogeneous_rational}
R_{D,p,v}^{[\kappa]}(\lambda)
=|\det(u_{v,1},\ldots,u_{v,n-1})|\sum_{|\alpha|=\kappa_{p,v}}
c_\alpha^{(v)}\frac{(-1)^{\kappa_{p,v}+n-1}\alpha!}
{\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(\lambda\cdot u_{v,k})^{\alpha_k+1}},
\end{equation}
is a nonzero homogeneous rational function of degree
\(-(n-1)-\kappa_{p,v}\).
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Write \(\kappa=\kappa_{p,v}\).  The expansion
\eqref{eq:R_vertex_asymptotic} follows from
\eqref{eq:explicit_R_Dpv} by setting \(z=\rho\lambda\) and grouping terms
according to \(|\alpha|\).  The terms with \(|\alpha|=\kappa\) give the
leading order \(\rho^{-(n-1)-\kappa}\), while all remaining terms are
\(O(\rho^{-(n-1)-\kappa-1})\).

It remains to show that \(R_{D,p,v}^{[\kappa]}\not\equiv0\).  In the
coordinates \(X_k=\lambda\cdot u_{v,k}\), the expression
\eqref{eq:R_leading_homogeneous_rational} becomes
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{|\alpha|=\kappa}c_\alpha^{(v)} \frac{(-1)^{\kappa+n-1}\alpha!}
{X_1^{\alpha_1+1}\cdots X_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}+1}}.
\end{equation*}
If \(R_{D,p,v}^{[\kappa]} \equiv 0\), multiplying by
\(X_1^{\kappa+1}\cdots X_{n-1}^{\kappa+1}\) would give
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{|\alpha|=\kappa}
c_\alpha^{(v)}(-1)^{\kappa+n-1}\alpha!
\prod_{k=1}^{n-1} X_k^{\kappa-\alpha_k}=0.
\end{equation*}
Since \(\alpha_k\le\kappa\), these are distinct monomials.  Hence all
their coefficients vanish, so \(c_\alpha^{(v)}=0\) for every
\(|\alpha|=\kappa\), contradicting the definition of \(\kappa\).
Therefore \(R_{D,p,v}^{[\kappa]}\not\equiv0\).

Then there exists \(\omega\in\mathcal O\) such that,

\begin{equation*}
(\gamma_\ell-i\sigma_\ell\omega)\cdot u_{\ell,v_\ell,k}\ne0,
\quad k=1,\cdots,n-1, \quad R_{D_\ell,p_\ell,v_\ell}^{[\kappa]}
(\gamma_\ell-i\sigma_\ell\omega)\ne0,
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
\omega\cdot(v-w)\ne 0, \quad  \forall \,   v\ne w\in V(D_\ell).
\end{equation*}
Consequently, for this \(\omega\), each function $\rho\mapsto
R_{D_\ell,p_\ell,v_\ell}
\bigl(\rho(\gamma_\ell-i\sigma_\ell\omega)\bigr)$ 
has no poles for all sufficiently large \(\rho\) and grows polynomially in
\(\rho\). In fact, for each \(\ell\), there exist constants
\(c_\ell,C_\ell>0\) and \(\rho_\ell>0\) such that
\begin{equation*}
c_\ell\rho^{-(n-1)-\kappa_\ell} \le \left|R_{D_\ell,p_\ell,v_\ell}
\bigl(\rho(\gamma_\ell-i\sigma_\ell\omega)\bigr)\right|
\le C_\ell\rho^{-(n-1)-\kappa_\ell},\quad \rho\ge\rho_\ell,
\end{equation*}
where \(\kappa_\ell=\kappa_{p_\ell,v_\ell}\).

For each denominator factor, the map $\omega\mapsto(\gamma_\ell-i\sigma_\ell\omega)\cdot u_{\ell,v_\ell,k}$

Lemma~\ref{lem:homogeneous_polynomial_physical_ray}, its zero set has empty

\label{lem:dominant_oscillatory_laurent_separation}
Let \(\beta_1,\cdots,\beta_M\in\mathbb R\) be pairwise distinct, and let
\(r_1,\cdots,r_M\) be nonzero finite Laurent polynomials in \(\rho\). If
\(\delta>0\), then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:oscillatory_laurent_exponential_smallness}
\left|\sum_{k=1}^M e^{-i\rho\beta_k}r_k(\rho)\right|
\leq C e^{-\delta\rho}\rho^A,
\end{equation}
cannot hold for all sufficiently large \(\rho\).
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Let \(d\) be the largest power of \(\rho\) occurring in any \(r_k\), and let
\(c_k\) be the coefficient of \(\rho^d\) in \(r_k\), with \(c_k=0\) if that
power is absent. At least one \(c_k\) is nonzero. Dividing
\eqref{eq:oscillatory_laurent_exponential_smallness} by \(\rho^d\) gives
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{k=1}^M c_k e^{-i\rho\beta_k}=o(1), \quad\text{as }\rho\to+\infty,
\end{equation*}
since \(r_k(\rho)/\rho^d=c_k+o(1)\). On the other hand, the frequencies
\(\beta_k\) are pairwise distinct, so
\begin{equation*}
\lim_{T\to+\infty}\frac1T\int_0^T\left|\sum_{k=1}^M c_k e^{-i\rho\beta_k}
\right|^2\,d\rho=\sum_{k=1}^M |c_k|^2>0.
\end{equation*}
But the integrand tends to \(0\) and is bounded, so its Ces\`aro mean must also
tend to \(0\). This contradiction proves the claim.

Assume that the first equation of \eqref{eq:BB_minus_identity_main} holds. By
\eqref{eq:BB_reduced_minus}, the reduced identity is
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{e^{-ir_j\cdot\zeta_\theta(\eta)}
-e^{-ir_{j-1}\cdot\zeta_\theta(\eta)}}{\lambda_j(\eta)}H_j(-i\eta)=0.
\end{equation*}
By the choice of \(\mathcal C_U\), the set \(U\cap\mathcal C_U\) is a nonempty
open subset of \(\mathcal C_U\). Since the left-hand side is real analytic on
\(\theta^\perp\setminus\{0\}\) and vanishes on \(U\cap\mathcal C_U\), analytic
continuation on the connected set \(\mathcal C_U\) shows that it vanishes on all
of \(\mathcal C_U\).

For each \(j\), Lemma~\ref{lem:weighted_BB_vertex_expansion} gives
\begin{equation*}
H_j(z)=\sum_{v\in V(D)}e^{z\cdot v}R_{j,v}(z),\quad R_{j,v}:=R_{D,p_j,v}.
\end{equation*}
Along a ray \(\eta=\rho\omega\), set $a_j(\omega):=1-i\gamma_j\cdot\omega$.
Since \(\operatorname{Re}a_j(\omega)=1\), we have \(a_j(\omega)\ne0\).
Substituting the vertex expansion into the reduced identity gives, away from
the polar hyperplanes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BB_vertex_identity_minus}
\sum_{j=1}^N\sum_{v\in V(D)}\frac{e^{\rho U_j(v;\omega)}
-e^{\rho L_j(v;\omega)}}{\rho a_j(\omega)}R_{j,v}(-i\rho\omega)=0.
\end{equation}
This identity is well-defined along every ray for which the finitely many
restricted denominators are not identically zero; after excluding their
finitely many positive zeros in \(\rho\), it holds for all sufficiently large
\(\rho\). It follows from the entire reduced identity, the right-hand side
being only a meromorphic representation of it.

Let $J:=\{j:p_j\not\equiv 0\}$. We prove \(J=\varnothing\). 

Suppose \(J\ne\varnothing\), and set $j_*:=\max J$. The upper endpoint of this segment has height \(t_{j_*}\), while every other nonzero endpoint contribution has height at most \(t_{j_*-1}\). Thus the exponential gap is
\begin{equation*}
\delta:=t_{j_*}-t_{j_*-1}=h_{j_*}>0.
\end{equation*}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:finite_family_generic_ray} to the family
\begin{equation*}
(0,1,D,p_j,v),\quad j\in J,\quad v\in V(D),
\end{equation*}
with \(\mathcal O=\mathcal O_U\). We obtain \(\omega\in\mathcal O_U\) such that all relevant vertex coefficients are nonzero finite Laurent polynomials along
$\eta=\rho\omega$, $z=-i\rho\omega$, and such that the numbers \(\omega\cdot v\), \(v\in V(D)\), are pairwise distinct.

The upper endpoint contribution of the \(j_*\)-th segment in \eqref{eq:BB_vertex_identity_minus} is
\begin{equation*}
e^{\rho t_{j_*}}
\sum_{v\in V(D)}
e^{-i\rho\omega\cdot(b_{j_*}+v)}
\frac{R_{j_*,v}(-i\rho\omega)}
{\rho a_{j_*}(\omega)}.
\end{equation*}
Each Laurent polynomial here is nonzero, and the frequencies
\(\omega\cdot(b_{j_*}+v)\) are pairwise distinct. All remaining terms in
\eqref{eq:BB_vertex_identity_minus} are bounded by $C e^{\rho(t_{j_*}-\delta)}\rho^A$
for suitable \(A,C>0\). Dividing the identity by \(e^{\rho t_{j_*}}\), we contradict
Lemma~\ref{lem:dominant_oscillatory_laurent_separation}. Hence \(J=\varnothing\), and therefore $p_1=\cdots=p_N=0$. This proves \(G=0\) a.e. in \(\Omega_{\mathcal B}\) for the negative phase.

\begin{equation*}

\label{thm:polygonal_finite_dimensional_stability}

\end{equation*}

deterioration as the polynomial degree increases.

\label{lem:piecewise_polynomial_approximation}

\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}

\end{equation*}

\(E_{N,d}(F)\to0\).

Fix \(v\in\mathcal H(\Omega_\sharp)\). Recall the definition of \(W_\iota^T\) in (\ref{eq:WjT}); the Bochner integral lies in \(L^2(\Omega_\sharp)\).

For \(\iota=1,2\), choose \(f_{\iota,k}\in\mathcal D(A_{q_\iota})\) with
\begin{equation*}
f_{\iota,k}\longrightarrow f_\iota, \quad\text{in }L^2(\mathbb R^n),
\end{equation*}
and let {\(u_{\iota,k}\)} be the corresponding strong solution. Since {\(q_\iota\)}
is bounded above and below by positive constants, the weighted and
unweighted \(L^2\)-norms are equivalent.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Case 1. Wave equation.}
By the spectral theorem,
\begin{equation*}
{\sup_{0\le t\le T}\|u_{\iota,k}(t)-u_\iota(t)\|_{L^2(\mathbb R^n)}
\le \|f_{\iota,k}-f_\iota\|_{L^2(\mathbb R^n)}},
\end{equation*}
hence
\begin{equation*}
{\|W_{\iota,k}^T-W_\iota^T\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}
\le \frac{T^2}{2} \sup_{0\le t\le T} \|u_{\iota,k}(t)-u_\iota(t)\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}}
\longrightarrow 0.
\end{equation*}
For the strong solution, spatial testing and two integrations in time give
\begin{equation*}
{\int_0^T(T-t)\partial_t^2u_{\iota,k}(t)\,dt
=\bigl[(T-t)\partial_tu_{\iota,k}(t)\bigr]_{0}^{T}+\int_0^T\partial_tu_{\iota,k}(t)\,dt
=u_{\iota,k}(T)-f_{\iota,k}},
\end{equation*}
since {\(\partial_tu_{\iota,k}(0)=0\)}. Therefore
\begin{equation*}
{\Delta W_{\iota,k}^T=q_\iota\bigl(u_{\iota,k}(T)-f_{\iota,k}\bigr)},
\end{equation*}
and the contraction estimate at \(t=T\) yields
\begin{equation*}
{\|\Delta W_{\iota,k}^T-q_\iota(u_\iota(T)-f_\iota)\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}
\le \|q_\iota\|_{L^\infty(\Omega_\sharp)}\Big(\|u_{\iota,k}(T)-u_\iota(T)\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}
+\|f_{\iota,k}-f_\iota\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}\Big)}\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation*}

\noindent\emph{Case 2. Heat equation.}
The semigroup contraction gives the same uniform convergence of
{\(u_{\iota,k}\) to \(u_\iota\)} on \([0,T]\). By the definitions of {\(W_{\iota,k}^T\)}
and {\(W_\iota^T\)},
\begin{equation*}
{\|W_{\iota,k}^T-W_\iota^T\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}
\le T\sup_{0\le t\le T} \|u_{\iota,k}(t)-u_\iota(t)\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}} \longrightarrow0,
\end{equation*}
and a single integration in time gives
\begin{equation*}
{\Delta W_{\iota,k}^T =q_\iota\int_0^T\partial_tu_{\iota,k}(t)\,dt=q_\iota\bigl(u_{\iota,k}(T)-f_{\iota,k}\bigr)},
\end{equation*}
so the semigroup contraction and the norm equivalence imply
\begin{equation*}
{\Delta W_{\iota,k}^T\longrightarrow q_\iota\bigl(u_\iota(T)-f_\iota\bigr)},
\quad\text{in }L^2(\Omega_\sharp).
\end{equation*}

In either model, the convergence of {\(W_{\iota,k}^T\)} and its Laplacian
gives
\begin{equation*}
{W_\iota^T\in H_\Delta(\Omega_\sharp),\quad \Delta W_\iota^T=q_\iota\bigl(u_\iota(T)-f_\iota\bigr),
\quad W_{\iota,k}^T\longrightarrow W_\iota^T},\quad \text{in }H_\Delta(\Omega_\sharp).
\end{equation*}
Indeed, the distributional identity for the limit follows by testing
against \(C_c^\infty(\Omega_\sharp)\) and passing to the limit in both
\(L^2\)-convergences.

Define {\(\mathcal M_{\iota,k}^T:=\mathcal G_{W_{\iota,k}^T}\)}. The continuity
estimate from Subsection~\ref{subsec:main_finite_time} gives
\begin{equation*}
{\|\mathcal M_{\iota,k}^T-\mathcal M_\iota^T\|_{\mathfrak T(\Omega_\sharp)^*}
\le C_n\bigl(\|W_{\iota,k}^T-W_\iota^T\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}
+\|\Delta W_{\iota,k}^T-\Delta W_\iota^T\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}\bigr)}\longrightarrow0,
\end{equation*}
with \(C_n\) independent of {\(\iota\)}, \(k\), and \(T\).

The same estimates bound the extension. Fix a coefficient \(q\) and set
\begin{equation*}
q_-:=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{\mathbb R^n}q>0,
\quad b_T:=
\begin{cases}
T^2/2, & \text{wave equation},\\
T, & \text{heat equation}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}

\(\Delta W_f^T=q(u_f(T)-f)\) give
\begin{eqnarray*}
\|W_f^T\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}
&\le& b_T\,q_-^{-1/2}\|f\|_{L^2(\mathbb R^n)},\\
\|\Delta W_f^T\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}
&\le& 2\|q\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb R^n)}q_-^{-1/2}\|f\|_{L^2(\mathbb R^n)}.
\end{eqnarray*}
Consequently,
\begin{equation*}
\|\mathcal M_f^T\|_{\mathfrak T(\Omega_\sharp)^*}
\le C_n q_-^{-1/2}\bigl(b_T+2\|q\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb R^n)}\bigr)
\|f\|_{L^2(\mathbb R^n,q\,dx)}.
\end{equation*}
Thus, for each fixed \(T>0\), the map \(f\mapsto\mathcal M_f^T\) extends
uniquely and continuously from \(\mathcal D(A_q)\) to
\(L^2(\mathbb R^n,q\,dx)\).

For every \(k\), the strong solution lies in
{\(C([0,T];\mathcal D(A_{q_\iota}))\)}, and the graph norm on
{\(\mathcal D(A_{q_\iota})\)} is equivalent to the \(H^2(\mathbb R^n)\)-norm.

=\int_0^T a_T(t)\Big(\langle\gamma_N{u_{\iota,k}}(t),\gamma_D\phi\rangle_{-1/2,1/2}
-\langle\gamma_N\phi,\gamma_D{u_{\iota,k}}(t)\rangle_{-1/2,1/2}\Big)\,dt.
\end{eqnarray*}
Thus the weighted weak Cauchy traces of the strong solutions determine
{\(\mathcal M_{\iota,k}^T\)}, and the convergence in
\(\mathfrak T(\Omega_\sharp)^*\) shows that {\(\mathcal M_\iota^T\)} is their
continuous extension. The limit is independent of the approximating
sequence, since it equals {\(\mathcal G_{W_\iota^T}\)}. This conclusion does
not assign pointwise-in-time Cauchy traces to the mild solution.

Now take the harmonic test function \(v\). Since \(\Delta v=0\), the
definition of {\(\mathcal M_\iota^T\)} and the terminal identity give
\begin{equation*}
{\mathcal M_\iota^T([v])=\int_{\Omega_\sharp}q_\iota\bigl(u_\iota(T)-f_\iota\bigr)v\,dx.}
\end{equation*}
Subtracting the identities for {\(\iota=1\)} and {\(\iota=2\)}, and using the
definitions of \(G\), \(R_T\), and \(\mathfrak D_T\), we get
\begin{equation*}
\mathfrak D_T(v)=\int_{\Omega_\sharp}(R_T-G)v\,dx,
\end{equation*}
which is equivalent to \eqref{eq:finite_time_endpoint_identity}.

\begin{equation*}
\left|\int_{\Omega_\sharp}Gv\,dx\right|=\left|\int_{\Omega_\sharp}R_Tv\,dx\right|
\le \|R_T\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}\|v\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}
=\varepsilon_T\|v\|_{L^2(\Omega_\sharp)}.
\end{equation*}
This completes the proof.
