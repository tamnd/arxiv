<!-- convention-1: body_md -->
\par A nonzero real binary form $f \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$ is said to be \emph{real-rooted} if it splits completely into real linear factors.
<!-- end convention-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{theorem:struct-apid}
    \par Let $f \in \mathbb{K}[x, y]_d$. Then its apolar ideal $f^\bot$ is generated by two coprime forms $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ with
    $$
        \deg \psi_1 + \deg \psi_2 = d + 2.
    $$
    Moreover, if $\deg \psi_1 < \deg \psi_2$, then $\psi_1$ is uniquely determined up to scalar.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lemma:gen-aplem}
    \par Let $f \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$, and let $b_1 X - a_1 Y, \cdots, b_m X - a_m Y \in \mathbb{R}[X, Y]_1$ be pairwise non-proportional linear forms. Then the product 
    $$
        \prod_{k = 1}^{m} \left(b_k X - a_k Y\right)^{s_k} \quad \text{with} \quad s_1 + \cdots + s_m \le d
    $$
    belongs to $f^\bot$ if and only if there exist $g_k \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]_{s_k - 1}~(k = 1, \cdots, m)$ such that $f$ can be expressed as the sum
    $$
        f = \sum_{k = 1}^{m} g_k \cdot \left(a_k x + b_k y\right)^{d - s_k + 1}.
    $$
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{corollary:homcpn-apid}
    \par Let $f \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$. Then
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\operatorname{rank}_\mathbb{R} f \le s$ if and only if there exists a square-free real-rooted form in $(f^\bot)_s$.
        \item $\operatorname{\underline{rank}}_\mathbb{R} f \le s$ if and only if there exists a real-rooted (not necessarily square-free) form in $(f^\bot)_s$.
    \end{enumerate}
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
\par Part (1) is the usual real apolarity criterion, and we only prove part (2). Throughout the proof, closure is taken in the Euclidean topology on the coefficient space of $\mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$.

    \par \textsc{The ``only if'' part}. Suppose that $\operatorname{\underline{rank}}_\mathbb{R} f \le s$. Then there exists a sequence $\{f_n\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$ converging to $f$ such that $\operatorname{rank}_\mathbb{R} f_n \le s$ for every $n$. By part (1), after multiplying by several non-proportional real linear factors when necessary, we may choose a square-free real-rooted form $\varphi_n \in (f_n^\bot)_s$ for every $n$. Moreover, by rescaling $\varphi_n$, we may assume that their coefficient vectors lie on the real unit sphere
    $$
        \mathbb{S}^s(\mathbb{R}) := \left\{(x_0, \cdots, x_s) \in \mathbb{R}^{s + 1} : |x_0|^2 + \cdots + |x_s|^2 = 1\right\}.
    $$
    Since $\mathbb{S}^s(\mathbb{R})$ is compact in $\mathbb{R}^{s + 1}$, after passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that $\varphi_{n}$ converges to $\varphi$. Note that the restriction of the action $\circ$ on $\mathbb{R}[X, Y]_s \times \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$ is continuous. Thus, we have $\varphi \circ f = 0$, and it remains to show that $\varphi$ is a product of real linear forms.

    \par Indeed, assume to the contrary that $\varphi$ has an irreducible quadratic factor, and consider the dehomogenizations
    $$
        \tilde{\varphi}(x) = \varphi(x, 1) \quad \text{and} \quad
        \tilde{\varphi}_{n}(x) = \varphi_{n}(x, 1).
    $$
    Then, the sequence $\{\tilde{\varphi}_{n}\}$ converges to $\tilde{\varphi}$ and all $\tilde{\varphi}_{n}$ are real-rooted, while $\tilde{\varphi}(x)$ possesses at least one non-real root $z_0$. Now, consider the circle
    $$
        C_{\varepsilon} := \left\{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z - z_0| = \varepsilon\right\}.
    $$
    When $\varepsilon$ is sufficiently small, we may assume that $C_\varepsilon$ and its interior contain no roots of $\tilde{\varphi}$ except $z_0$, and that $C_\varepsilon$ does not intersect the real axis. Then, by Rouch\'e's Theorem \cite[Theorem 6.24]{Rod-2013}, for sufficiently large $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$, the polynomial $\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(x)$ has at least one zero inside $C_\varepsilon$. This implies that $\varphi_{n}(x, y)$ admits an irreducible quadratic factor, which is a contradiction.

    \par \textsc{The ``if'' part}. By Lemma \ref{lemma:gen-aplem}, we may suppose that $f$ admits a representation as follows:
    $$
        f = g_1 \cdot (a_1 x + b_1 y)^{d - s_1 + 1} + \cdots + g_m \cdot (a_m x + b_m y)^{d - s_m + 1},
    $$
    where $s = s_1 + \cdots + s_m$ and $a_k x + b_k y$ are pairwise non-proportional real linear forms. For each term $g_k \cdot (a_k x + b_k y)^{d - s_k + 1}$ in the representation above, after a suitable real linear change of coordinates, we may assume it has the form
    $$
        \gamma_k(u, v) \cdot u^{d - s_k + 1} = c_0 u^d + c_1 u^{d - 1} v + \cdots + c_{s_k - 1} u^{d - s_k + 1} v^{s_k - 1}.
    $$
    Expressing the higher-order derivatives in terms of finite differences, we see that for each $t \in \{1, \cdots, s_k\}$, we have
    $$
        \begin{aligned}
            u^{d - t + 1} v^{t - 1} &= \frac{\partial^{t - 1}}{\partial \varepsilon^{t - 1}} \left(\frac{(u + \varepsilon v)^d}{d! / (d - t + 1)!}\right) \bigg|_{\varepsilon = 0} \\ 
            &= \frac{(d - t + 1)!}{d!} \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{1}{\varepsilon^{t - 1}} \sum_{j = 0}^{t - 1} (-1)^{t - 1 - j} \binom{t - 1}{j} (u + j\varepsilon v)^d.
        \end{aligned}
    $$
    Thus each term $\gamma_k(u, v) \cdot u^{d - s_k + 1}$ is the limit of $\mathbb{R}$-linear combinations of the $s_k$ powers
    $$
        u^d, \quad (u + \varepsilon_n v)^d, \quad \cdots, \quad (u + (s_k - 1)\varepsilon_n v)^d,
    $$
    where $\varepsilon_n \to 0$ as $n \to +\infty$. Now, to complete the proof, we only need to ensure that all these $d$-th powers are pairwisely non-proportional, and for this it suffices to suitably adjust the rate at which the corresponding $\varepsilon_n$ tends to zero.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- observation-1: body_md -->
\label{observation:biharmform}
    \par After the complex change of variables $z = x + iy$ and $\bar{z} = x - iy$, any biharmonic binary form $f \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$ can be expressed as
    $$
        f = \binom{d}{0} a z^d + \binom{d}{0} \bar{a} \bar{z}^d + \binom{d}{1} b z^{d - 1} \bar{z} + \binom{d}{d - 1} \bar{b} z \bar{z}^{d - 1}, 
        \quad a, b \in \mathbb{C}.
    $$
<!-- end observation-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
\par Note that any real binary form can be expressed as
    $$
        f = \binom{d}{0} c_0 z^d + \binom{d}{1} c_1 z^{d - 1} \bar{z} + \cdots + \binom{d}{d} c_d \bar{z}^d,
    $$
    where $c_j$ and $c_{d - j}$ are conjugate (i.e. $c_j = \bar{c}_{d - j}$). Since $\Delta = 4 \partial_z \partial_{\bar{z}}$, the biharmonic condition $\Delta^2 f = 0$ forces the corresponding coefficients $c_2, \cdots, c_{d - 2}$ to vanish.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{proposition:possible-rk-brk}
    \par Let $f \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$ be a biharmonic form of degree $d \ge 4$. Then
    $$
        \operatorname{\underline{rank}}_\mathbb{R} f, \ \operatorname{rank}_\mathbb{R} f \in \{d - 2, d - 1, d\}.
    $$
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
\par From the discussion above, the proposition holds for $d = 4$ and $d = 5$. We now prove the case $d \ge 6$. By Corollary \ref{corollary:homcpn-apid}, it suffices to show that there exists no real-rooted form in $(f^\bot)_k$ with $k < d - 2$. Theorem \ref{theorem:struct-apid} allows us to assume that $f^\bot = \langle \psi_1, \psi_2\rangle$ with $\deg \psi_1 \le \deg \psi_2$.

    \par \textsc{Case 1. $\deg \psi_1 = \deg \psi_2$}. In this case, we must have $d = 6$. Otherwise, $(f^\bot)_4$ would vanish, which contradicts $\Delta^2 f = 0$. By Theorem \ref{theorem:struct-apid}, $\deg \psi_1 = 4$ and thus all homogeneous components of degree less than $4$ vanish.

    \par \textsc{Case 2. $\deg \psi_1 < \deg \psi_2$}. Since $d \ge 6$ and $\deg \psi_1 + \deg \psi_2 = d + 2$, we have $\deg \psi_2 > 4$, which implies that the degree-$4$ element $(X^2 + Y^2)^2 \in f^\bot$ must be divisible by $\psi_1$. Thus, we obtain $\deg \psi_1 \le 4$ and $\deg \psi_2 \ge d - 2$. Moreover, $\psi_1$ is divisible by the irreducible quadratic $X^2 + Y^2$, so every element of $f^\bot$ of degree smaller than $d - 2$ is a multiple of $\psi_1$ and therefore cannot be real-rooted. Consequently, in both cases no homogeneous component $(f^\bot)_k$ with $k < d - 2$ contains a real-rooted form, which proves the proposition.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{definition:regions}
    \par We define the following regions in the complex plane:
    $$
        \mathscr{E}_r := \left\{ \sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1} \omega_j - \prod_{j = 1}^{r + 1} \omega_j : |\omega_j| = 1 \right\}, \quad
        \mathscr{H}_r := \left\{ \sum_{j = 1}^{r} \omega_j : |\omega_j| = 1, \ \prod_{j = 1}^{r} \omega_j = 1 \right\}.
    $$
    Let $\mathscr{E}_r^{\mathrm{dist}} \subseteq \mathscr{E}_r$ and $\mathscr{H}_r^{\mathrm{dist}} \subseteq \mathscr{H}_r$ be the subsets obtained by requiring the $\omega_j$ to be pairwisely distinct.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\par The region $\mathscr{H}_r$ in Definition \ref{definition:regions} can be viewed as the set consisting of the traces of all $r \times r$ special unitary matrices, that is
    $$
        \mathscr{H}_r := \left\{\operatorname{trace}(M) : M \in \operatorname{SU}(r)\right\}.
    $$
    This is related to the region studied by Kaiser \cite{Kai-2006}.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- observation-2: body_md -->
\label{observation:region-inclusion}
    \par Fix $r \ge 2$. Then the four regions in Definition \ref{definition:regions} satisfy the inclusions:
    $$
        \mathscr{H}_r \subseteq \mathscr{E}_r
        \quad\text{and}\quad
        \mathscr{H}_r^\mathrm{dist} \subseteq \mathscr{E}_r^\mathrm{dist}.
    $$
<!-- end observation-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
\par For any $\omega_1, \cdots, \omega_r$ with each $|\omega_j| = 1$ and $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_r = 1$, choosing $\omega_{r + 1}$ on the unit circle different from each $\omega_j~(j = 1, \cdots, r)$, we have
    $$
        \sum_{j = 1}^{r} \omega_j = \sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1} \omega_j - \prod_{j = 1}^{r + 1} \omega_j.
    $$
    This proves the two inclusions.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{theorem:region-crit}
    \par Let $f \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$ be a nonzero biharmonic form, written in the form of Observation \ref{observation:biharmform}. If $b = 0$, then $\operatorname{\underline{rank}}_\mathbb{R} f = \operatorname{rank}_\mathbb{R} f = d$. Otherwise, by taking an arbitrary $r$-th root $\eta$ of $- b /\bar{b}$ and setting $T_f = - a / (b\eta)$, we obtain
    $$
        \operatorname{rank}_\mathbb{R} f = \begin{cases}
            d - 2, & T_f \in \mathscr{H}_r^{\mathrm{dist}}, \\
            d - 1, & T_f \in \mathscr{E}_r^\mathrm{dist} \setminus \mathscr{H}_r^\mathrm{dist},\\
            d, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
        \quad\text{and}\quad
        \operatorname{\underline{rank}}_\mathbb{R} f = \begin{cases}
            d - 2, & T_f \in \mathscr{H}_r, \\
            d - 1, & T_f \in \mathscr{E}_r \setminus \mathscr{H}_r, \\
            d, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
    $$
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
\par Recall that $\operatorname{rank}_\mathbb{R} f, \ \operatorname{\underline{rank}}_\mathbb{R} f \le d$. If $b = 0$, then $\Delta f = 0$ and every element of $f^\bot$ of degree less than $d$ must be divisible by $X^2 + Y^2$, which forces $\operatorname{rank}_\mathbb{R} f = \operatorname{\underline{rank}}_\mathbb{R} f = d$. Suppose now that $b \neq 0$. <!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lemma:interior-er}
    \par If $\omega \in \mathscr{M}_r \setminus \mathscr{X}_r$, then $\mu_r(\omega)$ is an interior point of $\mathscr{E}_r$ and there exists another $\omega' = (\omega'_1, \cdots, \omega'_{r + 1})$ with pairwise distinct entries such that $\mu_r(\omega) = \mu_r(\omega')$.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
\par We may suppose that $\omega = (e^{i \theta_1}, \cdots, e^{i \theta_{r + 1}})$ with each $\theta_j \in [0, 2\pi]$. Consider the map
    $$
        U_r(t_1, \cdots, t_{r + 1}) = \left(
            \operatorname{Re} \mu_r(e^{i t_1}, \cdots, e^{i t_{r + 1}}),
            \operatorname{Im} \mu_r(e^{i t_1}, \cdots, e^{i t_{r + 1}})
        \right).
    $$
    The derivative of $U_r$ at the point $t = \theta$ is
    $$
        \mathrm{D} U_r(\theta) = \left(\begin{array}{rcr}
            - \sin \theta_1 + \sin \left(\sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1}\theta_j\right) & \cdots & - \sin \theta_{r + 1} + \sin \left(\sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1}\theta_j\right) \\
            \cos \theta_1 - \cos \left(\sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1}\theta_j\right) & \cdots & \cos \theta_{r + 1} - \cos \left(\sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1}\theta_j\right) \\
        \end{array}\right).
    $$
    This matrix must be nonzero; otherwise each $e^{i\theta_j}$ would equal $e^{i(\theta_1 + \cdots + \theta_{r + 1})}$, so that all entries of $\omega$ coincide with some $p$ with $|p| = 1$. The identity $p = p^{r + 1}$ then gives $p^r = 1$, whence $\omega \in \mathscr{X}_r$, contrary to the hypothesis.
    
    \par We first treat the case in which $\mathrm{D} U_r(\theta)$ has full real rank $2$. In this case, the Implicit Function Theorem provides an open neighborhood of $U_r(\theta)$ contained in $U_r(\mathbb{R}^{r + 1}) \subseteq \mathscr{E}_r$. Furthermore, if $(j, k)$ is a pair of indices such that $\omega_j = \omega_k$, then the $j$-th and $k$-th columns of $\mathrm{D} U_r(\theta)$ coincide. Hence, $\mathbf{e}_j - \mathbf{e}_k \in \ker \mathrm{D} U_r(\theta)$ and 
    $$
        \mathscr{K}_{j, k} := \left\{v = (v_1, \cdots, v_{r + 1}) \in \ker \mathrm{D} U_r(\theta) : v_j = v_k\right\}
    $$
    is a proper subspace of $\ker \mathrm{D} U_r(\theta)$. Since a finite union of proper linear subspaces cannot cover $\ker \mathrm{D} U_r(\theta)$, we may choose $v \in \ker \mathrm{D} U_r(\theta)$ such that $v_j \neq v_k$ whenever $\omega_j = \omega_k$. Choose a curve $\delta(s)$ in the level set $\{t : U_r(t) = U_r(\theta)\}$ with $\delta(0) = \theta$ and tangent to $v$ at $s = 0$. Then, for any pair $(\omega_j, \omega_k)$ with $\omega_j = \omega_k$, we have 
    $$
        \delta_j(s) - \delta_k(s) = s(v_j - v_k) + o(s)
    $$
    with $v_j - v_k \neq 0$. Hence, for all sufficiently small $s \neq 0$, the numbers $\omega'_j = e^{i\delta_j(s)}$ are pairwise distinct, while $U_r(\delta(s)) = U_r(\theta)$, so that $\mu_r(\omega) = \mu_r(\omega')$.
    
    \par Now, we assume that $\operatorname{rank}_\mathbb{R} \mathrm{D} U_r(\theta) = 1$. Then, all the points $(\sin \theta_j, \cos \theta_j)$ lie on a common line $L$ passing through the point
    $$
        A = \left(\sin \left(\sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1} \theta_j\right), \cos \left(\sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1} \theta_j\right)\right).
    $$
    Since $A$ lies on the unit circle, let $B$ be the other point at which the line $L$ meets the unit circle, and denote by $n_A$ and $n_B$ the numbers of these points that coincide with $A$ and with $B$, respectively.

    \par We first claim that $2 \le n_A \le r$. Indeed, the condition $\omega \notin \mathscr{X}_r$ excludes the cases $n_A = 0$ and $n_A = r + 1$. For $n_A = 1$, we can assume without loss of generality that 
    $$
        \theta_1 = \cdots = \theta_r = \alpha
        \quad\text{and}\quad
        \theta_{r + 1} = \beta
    $$
    for some $\alpha, \beta \in [0, 2\pi]$ with $\alpha \neq \beta$. Then, comparing arguments with $\omega_{r + 1}$, we obtain $e^{i\beta} = e^{i(r\alpha + \beta)}$, which implies that $e^{ir\alpha} = 1$. However, the point $\omega$ obtained in this way necessarily belongs to $\mathscr{X}_r$, which yields a contradiction. Based on this claim, we may assume that $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = \xi$ such that $A = (\sin \xi, \cos \xi)$. By comparing the arguments with $\omega_1$, we obtain $e^{i(\theta_2 + \cdots + \theta_{r + 1})} = 1$ and
    $$
        \mu_r(\omega) =  e^{i\xi} + \sum_{j = 2}^{r + 1} \omega_j - e^{i\xi}  \prod_{j = 2}^{r + 1} \omega_j = \sum_{j = 2}^{r + 1} \omega_j,
    $$
    which shows that the value of $\mu_r(\omega)$ is independent of $\omega_1$. Therefore, if we replace $\theta_1$ by a value $\vartheta_1$ for which $(\sin \vartheta_1, \cos \vartheta_1)$ is neither $A$ nor $B$, then 
    $$
        \mathrm{D} U_r(\vartheta_1, \theta_2, \cdots, \theta_{r + 1})
    $$
    has real rank $2$ without changing $U_r(\theta)$, equivalently without changing $\mu_r(\theta)$. This reduces to the case treated at the beginning of the proof.

    $$
        V_r(t_1, \cdots, t_r) = \left(
            \sum_{j = 1}^{r} t_j, \sum_{j = 1}^{r} \cos t_j, \sum_{j = 1}^{r} \sin t_j
        \right).
    $$
    Its derivative at the point $t = \theta$ is
    $$
        \mathrm{D} V_r (\theta_1, \cdots, \theta_r) = \left(
            \begin{array}{cccc}
                1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\
                - \sin \theta_1 & - \sin \theta_2 & \cdots & -\sin \theta_r \\
                \cos \theta_1 & \cos \theta_2 & \cdots & \cos \theta_r
            \end{array}

    $$
        G(x, y) = \cos x + \cos y - 2 \cos \alpha.
    $$
    Note that $G(\alpha, \alpha) = 0$ and $G_y(\alpha, \alpha) = - \sin \alpha \neq 0$. Then the Implicit Function Theorem shows that for $x \neq \alpha$ sufficiently close to $\alpha$, there exists $y$ close to $\alpha$ such that $G(x, y) = G(\alpha, \alpha) = 0$ with $x, y \in (0, \pi)$ and $x \neq \pm y$. Consequently, we obtain four distinct values

        e^{i(\theta + x)}, \quad e^{i(\theta - x)}, \quad
        e^{i(\theta + y)}, \quad e^{i(\theta - y)},
    $$
    and their sum and product are
    $$
        2e^{i\theta}(\cos x + \cos y) = 4e^{i\theta} \cos \alpha
        \quad\text{and}\quad 
        e^{4i\theta},
    $$
    respectively, which coincide with those of two copies of $e^{i(\theta + \alpha)}$ and two copies of $e^{i(\theta - \alpha)}$. The problem is therefore reduced to the first case. We are done.

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
\par We prove the theorem only for the region $\mathscr{E}_r$; the proof for $\mathscr{H}_r$ is analogous. 

    \par \textsc{Proof of $\mathscr{E}_r = \Omega_r^+ \sqcup \Gamma_r$}. Since $\mathscr{E}_r$ is compact, it suffices to show that $\partial \mathscr{E}_r \subseteq \Gamma_r \subseteq \mathscr{E}_r$ and that $\mathscr{E}_r \cap \Omega_r^+$ is nonempty. Let $T \in \partial \mathscr{E}_r$ and write $T = \mu_r(\omega)$ for some $\omega \in \mathscr{M}_r$. Being compact, $\mathscr{E}_r$ is the disjoint union of its interior and its boundary. Hence, by Lemma \ref{lemma:interior-er}, the corresponding $\omega$ must lie in $\mathscr{X}_r$, so that $T = \mu_r(\omega)$ belongs to the epicycloid $\Gamma_r$ by Observation \ref{observation:cycloid}. Conversely, the parametrization of the epicycloid shows immediately that every point of $\Gamma_r$ lies in $\mathscr{E}_r$, which proves the inclusions $\partial \mathscr{E}_r \subseteq \Gamma_r \subseteq \mathscr{E}_r$. On the other hand, taking $\omega_1, \cdots, \omega_r$ to be the roots of $z^r - (-1)^{r + 1}$ and $\omega_{r + 1} = 1$, we have
    $$
        \sum_{j = 1}^{r + 1} \omega_{j} - \prod_{j = 1}^{r + 1} \omega_{j} = 1 - 1 = 0.
    $$
    Hence $\mathscr{E}_r \cap \Omega_r^+$ contains the origin.

    \par \textsc{Proof of $\mathscr{E}_r^\mathrm{dist} = \Omega_r^+$}. Lemma \ref{lemma:interior-er} immediately gives the inclusion $\mathscr{E}_r^\mathrm{dist} \subseteq \Omega_r^+$. Conversely, let $T \in \Omega_r^+$ and write $T = \mu_r(\omega)$. By Observation \ref{observation:cycloid}, $\omega$ cannot lie in $\mathscr{X}_r$, so Lemma \ref{lemma:interior-er} provides another $\omega'$ with pairwise distinct entries such that $T = \mu_r(\omega')$. Thus $T \in \mathscr{E}_r^\mathrm{dist}$.

\label{remark:crit-windnum}
    \par For $T \in \mathbb{C}$ off the corresponding boundary cycloid, membership in $\Omega^+$ or $\Omega^-$ can be characterized in terms of the zero distribution of the polynomials $P_{r, T}$ and $Q_{r, T}$. Indeed, the regions $\Omega^+$ and $\Omega^-$ are enclosed by the Jordan curves $\Gamma_r$ and $\Pi_r$, respectively, and the winding number shows that $T$ lies in $\Omega^+$ if and only if

        = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{|u|=1} \frac{P'_{r, T}(u)}{P_{r, T}(u)} \mathrm{d} u = 1,
    $$
    which is equivalent to $P_{r, T}$ having exactly one zero, counted with multiplicity, in the unit disk $\mathbb{D} := \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| < 1\}$; while $T$ lies in $\Omega^-$ if and only if
    $$
        \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\Pi_r} \frac{1}{z - T} \mathrm{d} z
        = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{|u|=1} \frac{Q'_{r, T}(u)}{Q_{r, T}(u)} \mathrm{d} u - (r - 1) = 1,
    $$
    which is equivalent to all zeros of $Q_{r, T}$ lying in $\mathbb{D}$.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lemma:resultant-crit}
    \par For every $T \in \mathbb{C}$, the two resultants $\mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T}, P^\#_{r, T})$ and $\mathrm{Res}(Q_{r, T}, Q^\#_{r, T})$ are real numbers. Moreover, for $r \ge 2$, we have
    $$
        (-1)^r \mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T}, P^\#_{r, T})\begin{cases}
            > 0, & T \in \Omega_r^+, \\
            = 0, & T \in \Gamma_r, \\
            < 0, & T \in \mathrm{E}_r^+
        \end{cases}
    $$
    In addition, if $r \ge 3$, then
    $$
        \mathrm{Res}(Q_{r, T}, Q^\#_{r, T})\begin{cases}
            > 0, & T \in \Omega_r^-, \\
            = 0, & T \in \Pi_r, \\
            < 0, & T \in \mathrm{E}_r^-.
        \end{cases}
    $$
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
\par By Lemma \ref{lemma:properties-crp}(2), $\mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T}, P^\#_{r, T})$ is real for all nonzero $T$ and $\mathrm{Res}(Q_{r, T}, Q^\#_{r, T})$ is real for all $T$. Since $\mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T}, P^\#_{r, T})$ depends continuously on $T$, passing to the limit shows that $\mathrm{Res}(P_{r, 0}, P^\#_{r, 0})$ is real as well.

    \par We claim that each resultant vanishes if and only if $T$ lies on the corresponding cycloid. Indeed, if $T \in \Gamma_r$, say $T = (r + 1)e^{i\theta} - e^{i(r + 1)\theta}$ with $\theta \in [0, 2\pi]$, then $e^{i\theta}$ is a common zero of $P_{r, T}$ and $P^\#_{r, T}$. Conversely, write a common zero as $\rho e^{i\theta}$ with $\rho > 0$. By Lemma \ref{lemma:properties-crp}, both $\rho e^{i\theta}$ and $\rho^{-1} e^{i\theta}$ are zeros of $P_{r, T}$; eliminating $T$ gives
    $$
        (r + 1)(\rho - \rho^{-1}) = (\rho^{r + 1} - \rho^{- (r + 1)}) e^{ir\theta}.
    $$
    If $\rho \neq 1$, taking absolute values and dividing by $|\rho - \rho^{-1}|$ yields
    $$
        \rho^{r} + \rho^{r - 2} + \cdots + \rho^{-r} = (r + 1),
    $$
    while the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality shows that equality can hold only when $\rho = 1$, a contradiction. Hence $\rho = 1$. For the hypocycloid $\Pi_r$, the same argument gives
    $$
        (r - 1) (\rho - \rho^{-1}) e^{ir\theta} = \rho^{r - 1} - \rho^{-(r - 1)},
    $$
    and the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality again forces $\rho = 1$ for $r \ge 3$.

    \par Finally, expanding these two resultants as polynomials in $T$ and $\bar{T}$, we obtain
    $$
        \begin{cases}
            \mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T}, P_{r, T}^\#) = (-1)^{r + 1} |T|^{2r + 2} + o(|T|^{2r + 2}), \\
            \mathrm{Res}(Q_{r, T}, Q_{r, T}^\#) = - |T|^{2r - 2} + o(|T|^{2r - 2}).
        \end{cases}
    $$
    Hence both $(-1)^r\mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T}, P_{r, T}^\#)$ and $\mathrm{Res}(Q_{r, T}, Q_{r, T}^\#)$ are negative when $|T|$ is sufficiently large. On the other hand, a direct computation gives
    $$
        (-1)^r \cdot \mathrm{Res}(P_{r, 0}, P_{r, 0}^\#) = (r^2 + 2r)^r > 0
    $$
    and, for $r \ge 3$,
    $$
        \mathrm{Res}(Q_{r, 0}, Q_{r, 0}^\#) = (r^2 - 2r)^r > 0.
    $$
    Since each resultant is a continuous function of $T$ that does not vanish on any connected component of the complement of the corresponding cycloid, its sign is constant on each such component.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{corollary:real-rooted-biharm}
    \par Let $f \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]_d$ be a nonzero biharmonic binary form of degree $d \ge 4$. Then, in the notation of Observation \ref{observation:biharmform}, $f$ is real-rooted if and only if one of the following conditions holds:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item[(i)] $b = 0$; 
        \item[(ii)] $b \neq 0$ and $(-1)^r \cdot \mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T_f}, P^\#_{r, T_f}) \le 0$.
    \end{enumerate}
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
\par Suppose first that $b = 0$. Since $f$ is nonzero, we have $a \neq 0$ and
    $$
        f(\cos \theta, \sin \theta) = 2 \operatorname{Re} (a e^{id\theta})
    $$
    If we treat this as a function of $\theta$, then it has exactly $d$ real projective zeros. Thus $f$ is a real-rooted form.
    
    \par Now suppose that $b \neq 0$. If $a = 0$, then $T_f$ is the origin and obviously lies in $\Omega_r^+$, so Lemma \ref{lemma:resultant-crit} gives $(-1)^r \cdot \mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T_f}, P^\#_{r, T_f}) > 0$. On the other hand, note that
    $$
        f(\cos \theta, \sin \theta) = 2d \operatorname{Re}(be^{ir\theta})
    $$
    has exactly $r = d - 2$ real projective zeros. Hence neither condition (ii) nor real-rootedness holds in this case. It remains to consider the case $ab \neq 0$. Define the polynomial
    $$
        H_f(u) = a u^d + d b u^{d - 1} + d \bar{b} u + \bar{a}.
    $$
    Note that $H_f(e^{2i\theta}) = e^{id\theta} f(\cos \theta, \sin \theta)$. Therefore, $f$ is real-rooted if and only if all the zeros of $H_f$ lie on the unit circle. Since $H^\#_f = H_f$, Cohn's theorem (see \cite[pp. 120--121]{Cohn-1922} or \cite[Theorem A]{Cho-2013}) shows that it is equivalent to that all the zeros of $H'_f$ lie in the closed unit disk $\bar{\mathbb{D}} = \left\{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| \le 1\right\}$. Choose $\eta$ such that $\eta^r = - b / \bar{b}$ and set $T_f = -a / (b\eta)$. A direct calculation gives 
    $$
        H'_f(u) = -db\eta u^{r + 1} P_{r, T_f}\left(\frac{1}{\eta u}\right).
    $$
    Since $|\eta| = 1$, all zeros of $H'_f$ lie in the closed unit disk $\bar{\mathbb{D}}$ if and only if $P_{r, T_f}$ has no zeros in the open unit disk $\mathbb{D}$. By Remark \ref{remark:crit-windnum}, the latter is equivalent to 
    $$
        T_f \in \Gamma_r \sqcup \mathrm{E}_r^+.
    $$
    This can be identified with $(-1)^r \cdot \mathrm{Res}(P_{r, T_f}, P^\#_{r, T_f}) \le 0$ by Lemma \ref{lemma:resultant-crit}.

\label{remark:blek-sinn}
    \par As observed in Section 2.2, no nonzero biharmonic form of degree $d \ge 4$ has real rank one. Hence the theorem of Blekherman and Sinn \cite[Theorem 2.2]{Blek-2016} applies and asserts that such a form has real rank $d$ if and only if it is real-rooted. This agrees with Corollary \ref{corollary:real-rooted-biharm}.
