<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Let $a>0$.  
We say that 
$\Omega\subset{\mathbb C}^n$ is a {\it horn domain of order} $a$ 
if $\Omega$ is a Reinhardt domain satisfying the 
following conditions 
\begin{enumerate}
\item 
There exists a positive number $\delta$ such that 
${\mathbb B}(\delta)\subset\Omega$. 
                                             
\item
There exist positive numbers $R$ and $\epsilon$ 
such that 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2.1}
\widehat{\Omega}\setminus\widehat{{\mathbb B}(R)}
=
\bigcup_{\rho\geq R} 
\rho\, U(\rho), 
\end{equation}
where $U(\rho)$ is an open connected subset of 
$S_{\epsilon}^+$ (see (\ref{eqn:1.1}))
and satisfies that there exist positive constants
$A_1$ and $A_2$
independent of $\rho$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2.2}
\frac{A_1}{\rho^{2a}}\leq 
d\sigma(U(\rho))\leq 
\frac{A_2}{\rho^{2a}}
\quad 
\mbox{for $\rho\geq R$}
\quad 
\left(\Leftrightarrow 
d\sigma(U(\rho))\approx \frac{1}{\rho^{2a}}
\right).
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Furthermore, we say that 
a horn domain $\Omega$ is {\it simple} 
if the sets $U(\rho)$ in (ii) satisfy
the following condition: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(iii)]
There exists a point $X^*\in S_{\epsilon}^+$ 
such that 
\begin{equation}
\bigcap_{\rho\geq R} U(\rho)=\{X^*\}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
(1) \;
The behavior of $d\sigma(U)$ in (\ref{eqn:2.2})
can be considered in much more generality.
However, for the purposes of the problems 
discussed below,
the most essential effect is captured 
by the expression (\ref{eqn:2.2}).
We will return to this point in the final section.

\smallskip
(2) \; 
In the above definitions, 
one may drop the assumption of connectedness of
$U(\rho)$ and $\widehat{\Omega}\cap\rho S^+$, 
and instead consider a more general situation, 
for instance,
a finite union of connected open sets.
However, 
under the condition that
$U(\rho)$ is contained in $S_{\epsilon}^{+}$ 
for some $\epsilon>0$,
such a generalization merely complicates 
the arguments
without yielding any essential strengthening 
of the main results.
For this reason, 
we do not pursue this direction in the present paper.

We emphasize that the condition 
$U(\rho)\subset S_{\epsilon}^{+}$ is crucial.
Without this assumption, 
none of the results in this paper hold
(see the forthcoming paper \cite{HKN26}). 

\smallskip
(3) \;
In the definition, 
the condition (ii) can be replaced by 
the following condition.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(ii')]
There exist positive numbers 
$R$ and $\epsilon$ such that 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2.3}
\widehat{\Omega}\cap
\{X\in{\mathbb R}_+^n: X_n\geq R\}=
\bigcup_{\rho\geq R} 
\rho V(\rho), 
\end{equation}
where $V(\rho)$ is an open and connected 
subset of $H^+_{\epsilon}$ (see (\ref{eqn:1.2}))
and satisfies that there exists 
positive numbers $A_1, A_2$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2.4}
\frac{A_1}{\rho^{2a}}\leq 
{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(V(\rho))\leq 
\frac{A_2}{\rho^{2a}}
\quad \mbox{for $\rho\geq R$}
\quad 
\left(\Leftrightarrow 
{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(V(\rho))\approx \frac{1}{\rho^{2a}}
\right).
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
Let $\alpha\in{\mathbb R}_{>0}^{n-1}$. 
If $\Omega\in{\mathcal H}_n(\alpha)$, then
$\Omega$  
is a simple horn domain of order 
$\langle\alpha\rangle+n-1$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
In order to prove the above lemma, 
it suffices to show that $D(\alpha)$ 
is a simple horn domain of order $\langle\alpha\rangle+n-1$.
                      
                 
                                                        
               

First, 
the existence of a positive number $\delta$ 
in (i) and 
the simplicity condition in (iii) are easily verified. 
 
Next, setting
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2.8}
V(\rho)=
\frac{1}{\rho}(\widehat{D(\alpha)}\cap \rho H^+), 
\end{equation}
we can obtain the equation in (\ref{eqn:2.3}), 
i.e.,  
$\widehat{D(\alpha)}\cap 
\{X\in{\mathbb R}_+^n:X_{n}\geq R\}=
\bigcup_{\rho\geq R}\rho V(\rho)$.
Therefore, in order to complete the proof of the lemma,
it suffices to show that
there exists $\epsilon>0$ such that
$V(\rho)\subset H_{\epsilon}^+$ 
for $\rho\geq 1$
and that
${\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(V(\rho))\approx 
\frac{1}{\rho^{2(\langle\alpha\rangle+n-1)}}$
for $\rho>1$.

From the definition of $D(\alpha)$ in 
(\ref{eqn:2.6}), 
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
&\quad
\widehat{D(\alpha)}\cap \rho H_+= 
\Biggl\{
(X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1},\rho) \in{\mathbb R}_+^n:  \biggr. \\
                        
& \left. \quad\quad\quad
\sqrt{\rho^2-\frac{1}{(\rho^2+1)^{\alpha_k}}} 
<X_k
<\sqrt{\rho^2+\frac{1}{(\rho^2+1)^{\alpha_k}}} 
\mbox{ for $k=1,\ldots, n-1$}
\right\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
Setting 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2.10}
\delta_k^{(\pm)}(\rho):=\frac{1}{\rho}
\sqrt{\rho^2\pm \frac{1}{(\rho^2+1)^{\alpha_k}}}-1, 
\end{equation}
respectively, we can see  
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2.11}
\begin{split}
&
V(\rho)=
\frac{1}{\rho}(\widehat{D(\alpha)}\cap \rho H_+)
= \\
&\{
(X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1},1)\in {\mathbb R}_+^n:
\delta_k^{(-)}(\rho)<X_k-1<\delta_k^{(+)}(\rho)
\mbox{ for $k=1,\ldots, n-1$}\}. 
\end{split}
\end{equation}
 
From the above expression of $V(\rho)$, 
there exists $\epsilon>0$ such that 
$V(\rho)\subset H_{\epsilon}^+$ for $\rho\geq 1$. 
                                                     
                 
                              
                                                    
                             
       
                                                            
                                    
               
Note that Taylor's formula implies 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2.12}
|\delta_k^{(\pm)}(\rho)|
\approx 
\frac{1}{\rho^{2\alpha_k+2}} 
\quad \mbox{ for $\rho\geq 1$.}
\end{equation}
From (\ref{eqn:2.11}), (\ref{eqn:2.12}),  
we see 
\begin{equation*}
{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(V(\rho))
\approx 
\prod_{k=1}^{n-1} |\delta_k^{(\pm)}(\rho)|
\approx 
\prod_{k=1}^{n-1} 
\frac{1}{\rho^{2\alpha_k+2}}
=\frac{1}{\rho^{2(\langle\alpha\rangle+n-1)}}
\; \mbox{ for $\rho\geq 1$,}
\end{equation*}
which completes the proof of the lemma.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
Let $\Omega\subset{\mathbb C}^n$ be a horn 
domain of order $a$. 
If $a>n$, then 
the Bergman space of $A^2(\Omega)$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:3.2}
A^2(\Omega)={\rm Span}\left\{z^p:
                           
p\in{\mathbb Z}_+^n, |p|<a-n
\right\}.
\end{equation}
More precisely, 
the dimension of $A^2(\Omega)$ 
can be expressed as 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:3.3}
\dim A^2(\Omega)=
\begin{cases} 
&0 \quad  \mbox{ if  $ 0<a\leq n$,} \\
&\displaystyle \binom{n+k}{n}  \quad
\mbox{if  
$n+k<a\leq n+k+1$ 
 $(k\in{\mathbb Z}_+)$}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Denoting
$\pi(z)=X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\in{\mathbb R}_+^n$ 
for $z\in{\mathbb C}^n$,  
                             
                                                    
we have
\begin{equation*}
              
\int_{\Omega\setminus {\mathbb B}(R)} 
|z_1|^{2p_1} \cdots |z_n|^{2p_n}dV(z)
=
(2\pi)^n 
\int_{\widehat{\Omega}\setminus
\widehat{{\mathbb B}(R)}} 
X_1^{2 p_1+1}\cdots X_n^{2 p_n+1} 
dV(X).
\end{equation*}
                                          
Let $\rho>0$ and 
$\xi=(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)\in S^+$.
When we express 
$X_j=\rho \, \xi_j$ for $j=1,\ldots,n$, 
$dV(X)=\rho^{n-1} d\sigma(\xi)\wedge d\rho$ holds.
Therefore, Fubini's theorem implies 
  
\begin{equation*}
\int_{\Omega\setminus {\mathbb B}(R)} 
|z_1|^{2p_1} \cdots |z_n|^{2p_n}dV(z)
=(2\pi)^n \int_1^{\infty} K(\rho) 
\rho^{2|p|+2n-1}d\rho, 
\end{equation*}
where 
$$
K(\rho)=\int_{U(\rho)} 
\xi_1^{2p_1+1}\cdots
\xi_n^{2p_n+1} d\sigma(\xi).
$$
Since $U(\rho)\subset S_{\varepsilon}^+$,
                                            
                                
$
\xi_1^{2p_1}
\cdots
\xi_n^{2p_n} \approx 1$ for $\xi\in U(\rho)$, 
which implies 
\begin{equation*}
K(\rho)\approx
d\sigma(U(\rho))
\approx \frac{1}{\rho^{2a}}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, 
we have 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:3.4}
\begin{split}
&\|z^{2p}\|_{\Omega} <\infty
\Longleftrightarrow 
\int_R^{\infty} 
\rho^{2|p|+2n-1-2a}d\rho <\infty \\
&\quad \Longleftrightarrow
2|p|+2n-1-2a<-1
\Longleftrightarrow
|p|<a-n,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
which implies 
                  
$A^2(\Omega)\supset 
{\rm Span}\{z^p:
                           
p\in{\mathbb Z}_+^n, |p|< a-n
\}$.
                

Conversely, the opposite inclusion can be 
established in a similar manner 
as in the proof of the main theorem 
in \cite{Wie84}.

We remark that 
since $|p|$ is nonnegative, 
the value of $a$ must be greater than 
$n$ from (\ref{eqn:3.1}).

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The pseudoconvexity of a domain 
has a significant influence on 
the dimension of its Bergman space. 
Wiegerinck \cite{Wie84} conjectured that 
property (\ref{eqn:2.1}) always holds 
for every pseudoconvex domain $\Omega$.
As far as we know, this conjecture remains 
unsolved at present, 
but it has already been affirmatively 
settled in many cases
(\cite{Zwo99, Zwo00, Eng07, Juc12, 
GHH17, PfZ17}).
In particular, 
it has been confirmed 
in the case of Reinhardt domains
(\cite{Zwo99, Zwo00, Eng07}). 
From these results,  
if the order is greater than $n$, 
then the respective horn domain
cannot be pseudoconvex.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
Let $\Omega\subset{\mathbb C}^n$ be a horn domain 
of order $a$.
If 
                                
$n+1<a\leq n+2$,
                
then 
the Bergman metric of $\Omega$ has constant 
holomorphic sectional curvature equal to $2$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
It follows from Theorem~3.1 that 
the condition 
$n+1<a\leq n+2$ is equivalent to 
$A^2(\Omega)={\rm Span}\{1,z_1,\ldots,z_n\}$. 
Therefore, 
the Bergman kernel of $\Omega$ can be written
as 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:4.1}
K_{\Omega}(z)=c_0+\sum_{k=1}^n c_k |z_k|^2
\quad \mbox{ for $z\in \Omega$}, 
\end{equation}
where 
$$
c_0={\rm Vol} (\Omega)^{-1}, 
\quad 
c_k=\|z_k\|_{\Omega}^{-2}=
\left(\int_{\Omega} |z_k|^2 dV(z)\right)^{-1}.$$ 
                                                      
Furthermore, we have
$$
K_{\Omega}(z)=
c_0\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^n \tilde{c}_k |z_k|^2\right)
\quad \mbox{ for $z\in \Omega$},
$$ 
where $\tilde{c}_k=c_k/c_0$ for
$k=1,\ldots,n$. 
Through
the scaling $(w_1,\ldots,w_n)=
(\sqrt{\tilde{c}_1}z_1,\ldots,
\sqrt{\tilde{c}_n}z_n)$,
the Bergman metric of $\Omega$ 
is isometric to the Fubini-Study metric
of ${\mathbb P}^n$ restricted to 
a domain in the cell ${\mathbb C}^n$ with 
$$
\omega_{FS} =
\partial\bar{\partial} \log 
\left(
1+\sum_{k=1}^n |w_k|^2
\right). 
$$
Therefore, the Bergman metric of $\Omega$ 
has a constant holomorphic 
sectional curvature equal 
to $2$.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
Let $\Omega_1, \Omega_2\subset{\mathbb C}^n$ be 
Reinhardt domains such that $0\in\Omega_j$ and
$A^2(\Omega_j)={\rm Span}\{1,z_1, \ldots,z_n\}$ for
$j=1,2$. 
If $\Omega_1$ and $\Omega_2$ are biholomorphic, 
then 
every biholomorphism $f:\Omega_1\to\Omega_2$ 
is linear.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
Let $\Omega_1, \Omega_2\subset{\mathbb C}^n$ be
simple horn domains 
that are biholomorphic to each other.   
If a biholomorphism $F : \Omega_1\to\Omega_2$ 
is linear, then
$F$ can be written in the form
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.1}
F(z) = 
\bigl(
C_1 z_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, C_n z_{\sigma(n)}
\bigr),
\end{equation}
where each $C_j$ is a nonzero complex number and 
$\sigma$ is a permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ 
(i.e., $\sigma \in S_n$).
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For $X=(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\in{\mathbb R}_{>0}^n$, 
we define
                                       
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
&L_{{\mathbb R}}(X):=
\{t X=(t X_1, \ldots, t X_n)\in{\mathbb R}_+^n: t\geq 0\}, \\
&L_{{\mathbb C}}(X):=
\{\zeta X=(\zeta X_1, \ldots, \zeta X_n) \in {\mathbb C}^n:
\zeta\in {\mathbb C}\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
For $\theta=(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_n)\in{\mathbb R}^n$, 
let $\sigma_{\theta}:{\mathbb C}^n\to{\mathbb C}^n$ be the map defined by 
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_{\theta}(z)=
(e^{i\theta_1} z_1,\ldots, e^{i\theta_n} z_n). 
\end{equation*}

For $j=1,2$, the domain
$\Omega_j$ satisfies the conditions 
(i), (ii) in Definition~2.1   
with $R=R_j$, $U(\rho)=U_j(\rho)$, 
respectively. 
Moreover, $\Omega_1$ satisfies 
the simplicity condition (iii),  that is, 
there exists a point $X^*\in S_{\epsilon}^+$ 
such that 
\begin{equation*}
\bigcap_{\rho\geq R_1} U_1(\rho)
=\{X^*\}=\{(X_1^*,\ldots,X_n^*)\}. 
\end{equation*}
                  
We then observe that 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.2}
L_{{\mathbb R}}(X^*)\subset \widehat{\Omega}_1, \quad
L_{{\mathbb C}}(X^*)\subset \Omega_1. 
\end{equation}
 
We write $w=F(z)$ in coordinates as 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.3}
w_j=\sum_{k=1}^n C_{jk} z_k \quad 
\mbox{ for } j=1,\ldots, n, 
\end{equation}
where each $C_{jk}$ is a complex number. 
                   
Let $Y^*=(Y_1^*,\ldots,Y_n^*)\in{\mathbb R}_{>0}^n$ 
be the point defined by 
\begin{equation*}
Y_j^*=\sum_{k=1}^n |C_{jk}| X_k^* 
\quad \mbox{for $j=1,\ldots,n$}.
\end{equation*}
The following lemma 
                                                  
plays a crucial role 
in the proof of the proposition. 
                                
                 
\begin{lemma}
There exist $\theta^*, \varphi^*\in [0,2\pi)^n$ 
such that 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.4}
(F\circ\sigma_{\theta^*})(X^*)=\sigma_{\varphi^*}(Y^*). 
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
                                  
\begin{proof}
From (\ref{eqn:5.3}), we have
\begin{equation*} 
(F\circ\sigma_{\theta})(X^*) =
\left(
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{1k} e^{i\theta_k} X_k^*, 
\ldots, 
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{nk} e^{i\theta_k} X_k^*
\right).
\end{equation*} 
Moreover, for each $j=1,\ldots, n$, 
we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\left|
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{jk} e^{i\theta_k} X_k^*
\right|
\leq 
\sum_{k=1}^n |C_{jk}| X_k^*=Y_j^*.
       
                              
\end{equation*}
Therefore, for any $\theta\in [0,2\pi)^n$ and
$j=1,\ldots,n$, 
there exist $\rho_j(\theta)\in(0,1]$ and 
$\varphi_j(\theta)\in[0,2\pi)$ such that 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.5}
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{jk} e^{i\theta_k} X_k^*=
\rho_j(\theta) e^{i \varphi_j(\theta)} Y_j^*.
\end{equation}
Since $\sigma_{\theta}(tX^*) 
\in\Omega_1$ for all 
$t>0$, $\theta\in[0,2\pi)^n$ 
from (\ref{eqn:5.2}), 
we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\bigl(
t\rho_1(\theta) e^{i\varphi_1(\theta)}Y_1^*, 
\ldots, 
t\rho_n(\theta) e^{i\varphi_n(\theta)}Y_n^*
\bigr)
\in \Omega_2 \quad 
\text{ for all } t>0, \theta\in[0,2\pi)^n.
\end{equation*}

For $j=1,\ldots, n$, define 
$\theta^j=(\theta^j_1,\ldots,\theta^j_n)
\in
[0,2\pi)^n$ 
by 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.6}
\theta^j_{k}:=
\begin{cases}
&\!\!\!\!\! -\arg(C_{jk}), 
\quad \text{ if } C_{jk}\neq 0, \\
& 0, \quad \text{ if } C_{jk}=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Then $\rho_j$ and $\varphi_j$ in (\ref{eqn:5.5})
satisfy 
$\rho_j(\theta^j)=1$ and $\varphi_j(\theta^j)=0$, 
that is,  
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.7}
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{jk} e^{i\theta^j_{k}} X_k^*
=
Y_j^* \quad 
\text{\; for } j=1,\ldots, n.
\end{equation}
For $l=1,\ldots,n$, set
\begin{equation*}
Y^l=(
\rho_1(\theta^l)Y_1^*,\ldots, 
\rho_n(\theta^l)Y_n^*).
\end{equation*}
Since $L_{{\mathbb R}}(Y^l)\subset \widehat{\Omega}_2$ 
for all $l$, 
the simplicity condition of $\Omega_2$ 
implies that 
$Y^l=a_{lk} Y^k$ with positive constants $a_{lk}$ 
for all $k, l$. 
Moreover, using $\rho_j(\theta^j)=1$ for all $j$, 
we conclude that 
$$
\rho_k(\theta^l)=1 \quad 
\text{ for all } k, l, 
$$
which is equivalent to 
$Y^1=\cdots=Y^n=Y^*$. 
Therefore, setting 
$\theta^*=\theta^1$ and 
$\varphi^*=(\varphi_1(\theta^1),\ldots,
\varphi_n(\theta^1))$ 
yields the assertion. 
   
\end{proof}
                           

Since
$\sigma_{\theta}(\zeta X^*)\in \Omega_1$ 
for all $\theta\in[0,2\pi)^n$, 
$\zeta\in{\mathbb C}$ from (\ref{eqn:5.2}), 
Lemma~5.3 implies that 
$$
\zeta Y^*
=\zeta (\sigma_{\varphi^*}^{-1}
\circ F \circ \sigma_{\theta^*})(X^*) =
\sigma_{\varphi^*}^{-1}
(F(\sigma_{\theta^*}(\zeta X^*)))
\in\Omega_2 \quad 
\mbox{ for all } \zeta\in{\mathbb C}. 
$$
                 
                                             
Consequently, 
              
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.8}
\bigcap_{\rho>R_2} U_2(\rho)=
\left\{\frac{Y^*}{|Y^*|}\right\}, 
\quad
L_{{\mathbb R}}(Y^*)\subset \widehat{\Omega}_2, 
\quad 
L_{{\mathbb C}}(Y^*)\subset {\Omega}_2. 
\end{equation}
   
We define the biholomorphism
$F^*:\Omega_1\to\Omega_2$ by 
\begin{equation*}
F^*:=
\sigma_{\varphi^*}\circ 
F \circ \sigma_{\theta^*}^{-1},
\end{equation*}
so that the following diagram commutes:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{tikzcd}
\Omega_1 \arrow[r,"F"] \arrow[d,"\sigma_{\theta^*}"'] 
& \Omega_2 \arrow[d,"\sigma_{\varphi^*}"] \\
\Omega_1 \arrow[r,"F^*"] 
& \Omega_2
\arrow[phantom, from=1-1, to=2-2, "\circlearrowright"]
\end{tikzcd}
\end{equation*}
It follows from (\ref{eqn:5.4}) in Lemma~5.3 
that  
                 
$Y^*=F^*(X^*)$, 
               
which implies that 
$w=F^*(z)$ can be written in coordinates as 
\begin{equation*}
w_j=\sum_{k=1}^n |C_{jk}| z_k \quad 
\mbox{\; for } j=1,\ldots, n.
\end{equation*}
We remark that 
$F^*$ can be extended to 
a biholomorphism from 
${\mathbb C}^n$ to ${\mathbb C}^n$ and 
that 
the restriction of $F^*$ 
to ${\mathbb R}^n$ is bijective from 
${\mathbb R}^n$ to ${\mathbb R}^n$. 

On the other hand, 
by considering similarly 
linear biholomorphisms from 
$\Omega_2$ to $\Omega_1$, 
we obtain analogous results to 
those of Lemma~5.3.
Let $G^*$ denote the inverse of $F^*$. 
Arguing as in the case of $F^*$, 
we can see 
that $z=G^*(w)$ can be written 
in coordinates as  
\begin{equation*}
z_j=\sum_{k=1}^n D_{jk} w_k 
\quad \mbox{ for $j=1,\ldots,n$},
\end{equation*}
where each $D_{jk}$ is a nonnegative number. 
The equation $F^*\circ G^*={\rm Id}$ implies that 
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{l=1}^n |C_{jl}| D_{lk} =\delta_{jk}, 
\end{equation*}
where $\delta_{jk}=1$ if $j=k$ or $=0$ if $j\neq k$. 
Therefore, 
for each $j$, there exists a unique $k$ such that 
$C_{jk}\neq 0$ and $C_{jl}=0$ for all $l\neq k$.
Since the $n\times n$ matrix $(|C_{jk}|)_{jk}$ 
is invertible, 
the correspondence $j\mapsto k$ defines 
a permutation $\sigma\in S_n$. 
  
Consequently, $F$ can be written in the form
\[
F(z)
=
\bigl(
C_1 z_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, C_n z_{\sigma(n)}
\bigr),
\]
where each $C_j \neq 0$ and
$\sigma \in S_n$.
This completes the proof of the proposition.

From (\ref{eqn:5.3}), we have
\begin{equation*} 
(F\circ\sigma_{\theta})(X^*) =
\left(
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{1k} e^{i\theta_k} X_k^*, 
\ldots, 
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{nk} e^{i\theta_k} X_k^*
\right).
\end{equation*} 
Moreover, for each $j=1,\ldots, n$, 
we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\left|
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{jk} e^{i\theta_k} X_k^*
\right|
\leq 
\sum_{k=1}^n |C_{jk}| X_k^*=Y_j^*.
       
                              
\end{equation*}
Therefore, for any $\theta\in [0,2\pi)^n$ and
$j=1,\ldots,n$, 
there exist $\rho_j(\theta)\in(0,1]$ and 
$\varphi_j(\theta)\in[0,2\pi)$ such that 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.5}
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{jk} e^{i\theta_k} X_k^*=
\rho_j(\theta) e^{i \varphi_j(\theta)} Y_j^*.
\end{equation}
Since $\sigma_{\theta}(tX^*) 
\in\Omega_1$ for all 
$t>0$, $\theta\in[0,2\pi)^n$ 
from (\ref{eqn:5.2}), 
we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\bigl(
t\rho_1(\theta) e^{i\varphi_1(\theta)}Y_1^*, 
\ldots, 
t\rho_n(\theta) e^{i\varphi_n(\theta)}Y_n^*
\bigr)
\in \Omega_2 \quad 
\text{ for all } t>0, \theta\in[0,2\pi)^n.
\end{equation*}

For $j=1,\ldots, n$, define 
$\theta^j=(\theta^j_1,\ldots,\theta^j_n)
\in
[0,2\pi)^n$ 
by 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.6}
\theta^j_{k}:=
\begin{cases}
&\!\!\!\!\! -\arg(C_{jk}), 
\quad \text{ if } C_{jk}\neq 0, \\
& 0, \quad \text{ if } C_{jk}=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Then $\rho_j$ and $\varphi_j$ in (\ref{eqn:5.5})
satisfy 
$\rho_j(\theta^j)=1$ and $\varphi_j(\theta^j)=0$, 
that is,  
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:5.7}
\sum_{k=1}^n C_{jk} e^{i\theta^j_{k}} X_k^*
=
Y_j^* \quad 
\text{\; for } j=1,\ldots, n.
\end{equation}
For $l=1,\ldots,n$, set
\begin{equation*}
Y^l=(
\rho_1(\theta^l)Y_1^*,\ldots, 
\rho_n(\theta^l)Y_n^*).
\end{equation*}
Since $L_{{\mathbb R}}(Y^l)\subset \widehat{\Omega}_2$

the simplicity condition of $\Omega_2$

\rho_k(\theta^l)=1 \quad 
\text{ for all } k, l, 
$$
which is equivalent to 
$Y^1=\cdots=Y^n=Y^*$. 
Therefore, setting 
$\theta^*=\theta^1$ and 
$\varphi^*=(\varphi_1(\theta^1),\ldots,
\varphi_n(\theta^1))$ 
yields the assertion.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
Let $n+1 < a_1, a_2 \leq n+2$. 
Let $\Omega_1, \Omega_2 \subset {\mathbb C}^n$ be 
simple horn domains of orders $a_1$, $a_2$, 
respectively.
If $\Omega_1$ and $\Omega_2$ are 
biholomorphic, then
every biholomorphism $F:\Omega_1\to\Omega_2$ 
can be written as in the form (\ref{eqn:5.1}) and 
$a_1 = a_2$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $F:\Omega_1\to\Omega_2$ 
be a biholomorphism. 
Applying Theorem~3.1 to Theorem~5.1, 
we see that $F$ is linear. 
Moreover, Proposition~5.2 implies that
$F$ can be written as in the form (\ref{eqn:5.1}).
Without loss of generality,
we may assume that the permutation 
in Proposition~5.2 satisfies 
$\sigma(j)=j$ for $j=1,\ldots, n$.
 

Let $c_j:=|C_j|$ for $j=1,\ldots,n$, where 
each $C_j$ is as in (\ref{eqn:5.1}).  
Define a map $\hat{F}: {\mathbb R}^n_+\to {\mathbb R}_+^n$ by
$$\hat{F}(X_1\ldots, X_n):=(c_1 X_1,\ldots, c_n X_n). $$
                                                
This induced map 
$\hat{F}:\widehat{\Omega}_1\to \widehat{\Omega}_2$ 
is bijective.   
 
For positive numbers $t$ and $s$,  set 
\begin{equation*}
V(t)=\frac{1}{t}(\widehat{\Omega}_1 \cap t H_+), 
\qquad
W(s)=\frac{1}{s}
(\widehat{\Omega}_2 \cap s H_+), 
\end{equation*}
where $H_+$ is as in (\ref{eqn:1.2}).
When $s=c_n t$, we have
$$
\hat{F}(t V(t))=s W(s).
$$
Therefore, 
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
&{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(W(s))=
{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}\left(\frac{1}{s}\hat{F}(tV(t))\right)=
{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}\left(\frac{1}{c_n t} t\hat{F}(V(t))\right) \\
&\quad\quad
=\frac{1}{c_n}{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(\hat{F}(V(t))) 
=\frac{1}{c_n}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1} c_j \cdot
{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(V(s/c_n)).
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
Since the order of $\Omega_1$ is $a_1$,   
the quantity ${\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(V(t))$ is estimated 
as in (\ref{eqn:2.4}) with $a=a_1$. 
The above equalities then imply  that, 
for sufficiently large $s$, 
\begin{equation*}
\frac{B_1}{s^{2a_1}}
\leq 
{\rm Vol}_{{\mathbb R}^{n-1}}(W(s))
\leq 
\frac{B_2}{s^{2a_1}}, 
\end{equation*}
where 
$$
B_j=A_j \cdot 
c_n^{2a_1-1} \prod_{k=1}^{n-1} c_k \;\;
\mbox{ for $j=1,2$.}
$$ 
Hence the order of $\Omega_2$ is also $a_1$. 
Thus, $a_1=a_2$.
