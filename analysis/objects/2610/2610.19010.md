\label{main_theorem}
	Let $\alpha, d \in \mathbb{N}$. For any $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_d \in \mathbb{C}$ the following inequality holds
	\begin{equation}\label{the_main}
		\| (a_j)_{j=1}^{d} \|_{\ell^1} \leqslant \sqrt{\binom{d+2\alpha}{2\alpha +1}\binom{2\alpha}{\alpha}^{-1}} \|((\Delta^\alpha a)_j)_{j=1}^{d+\alpha}\|_{\ell^2}.
	\end{equation}
	This estimate is sharp, with equality obtained when $(a_j)_{j=1}^d$ are samples of the discrete Landau polynomial over the set $\{1,...,d\}$  (see \eqref{the L polynomial}).

\item $a_{\mu} \ \neq 0$ and $a_{\mu} a_{\mu+1}<0$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lemma:DiscreteRolle}
    Suppose $(a_j)_{j \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is a sequence of real numbers that has generalised zeroes at indices $\mu, \nu \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $\mu < \nu$. Then the sequence $(\Delta a_j)_{j\in \mathbb{Z}}$ has a generalised zero for some $j \in \{\mu+1, \ldots, \nu\}$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Assume for a contradiction that $(\Delta a_j)_{j={\mu+1}}^{\nu}$ has no generalized zeroes. Without loss of generality, we may assume that $\Delta a_j > 0$ for all $j \in \{\mu +1, \ldots, \nu\}$ and that $\Delta a_{\nu+1} \geq 0$.  Since $a_{\mu}$ is a generalized zero, and $\Delta a_{\mu+1}>0$, this implies that $a_{\mu+1}> 0$. It follows that the sequence $(a_j)_{j={\mu+1}}^{\nu}$ is an increasing sequence of positive numbers. Since $\Delta a_{\nu+1}\geq 0$, we have $0 < a_{\nu}\leq a_{\nu+1}$, which contradicts the assumption that $a_{\nu}$ is a generalized zero.

We use the subscript $\alpha$ for $f_\alpha$, even though it is constructed from two polynomials of degree $\leq \alpha-1$, since, as can be seen in the next three lemmas, the zero structure of $f_\alpha-p$ mimics that of a polynomial of degree $\alpha$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{Discrete broken line lemma}
	For $\alpha\in \mathbb{N}$, let $p$ be a polynomial of degree at most $\alpha-1$.  If $g(n) = f_\alpha(n) - p(n)$ has at least $\alpha+1$ generalised zeroes, then either $p(n) = r_{\alpha-1}(n)$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ or $p(n) = 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The proof is by induction. First, let $\alpha=1$. In this case, $p$ is a constant function and
\begin{equation*}
    f_1(n) = \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
        1 & \quad n \geq 0 \\
        0 & \quad n \leq -1
    \end{aligned}\right..
\end{equation*}
In particular, for $g = f_1 - p$  to have at least $2$ distinct generalised zeroes, we must have either $ p(n) = 1$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ or $ p(n) = 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Next, let $\alpha \geq  2$ and suppose that $g = f_\alpha - p$ has at least  $\alpha+1$ distinct generalised zeroes. By Lemma \ref{lemma:DiscreteRolle}, we obtain that $\Delta g = \Delta f_{\alpha} - \Delta p$ has at least $\alpha$ distinct generalised zeroes. Since $\Delta p$ is a polynomial of degree at most $\alpha-2$, we may apply (\ref{Df_a}) and the induction assumption to conclude that $\Delta p$ is either identically equal to $r_{\alpha-2}$ or identically equal to $0$ on $\mathbb{Z}$.  

By \eqref{Delta r_alpha}, this implies that there exists a constant $C \in \mathbb{R}$ so that one of the following holds:
\begin{align*}
    (i)& \quad p(n) = r_{\alpha-1}(n) + C \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{Z}, \\[2mm]
    (ii)& \quad p(n) = C \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{Z}.
\end{align*}

If (ii) holds, it suffices to observe that, for $\alpha >  1$, the function $f_\alpha$ is strictly monotone  over  $n>0$. This implies that if $C \neq 0$, then $g = f_\alpha - p$ has at most one generalised zero. Hence, we must have $C=0$. If (i) holds with $C\neq 0$, then it follows immediately that $f_\alpha$ has no generalised zeroes for $n \geq -\alpha+1$, and, since $r_{\alpha-1}$ is strictly monotone for $n \leq -\alpha+1$, it has at most one generalised zero for $n \leq	 -\alpha$. Therefore $C=0$, which ends the proof.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lemma sign structure}
  For $\alpha\in \mathbb{N}$,  let $p$ be a polynomial of degree at most $\alpha-1$.  If $g(n) = f_\alpha(n) - p(n)$ has at least $\alpha$ generalised zeroes $k_1 < k_2 < \ldots < k_{\alpha}$, then
  \begin{equation*}
  	(-1)^{\alpha+\ell} g(n) \geq 0 \qquad \forall  n  \in \{k_\ell+1,..., k_{\ell+1}\}
  \end{equation*}
where $\ell\in \{0,1,\ldots, \alpha\}$ with 
 $\{k_0+1, \ldots, k_{1}\} = \{n : n \leq k_1\}$ and $\{k_\alpha+1, \ldots, k_{\alpha+1}\} = \{n: n > k_\alpha\}$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
If $g$ has at least $\alpha+1$ generalized zeroes, the conclusion follows from Lemma \ref{Discrete broken line lemma} since either $g = f_\alpha - 0 = f_\alpha$ or $$g = f_\alpha - r_{\alpha-1} =  \left\{ \begin{aligned} 0 & \quad \text{if }\ n \geq - \alpha+1 \\ -r_{\alpha-1} & \quad \text{if }\ n \leq -1 \end{aligned} \right.$$ In either case, the conclusion follows trivially. 

We now turn to the case when $g$ has exactly $\alpha$ zeroes $k_1 < \cdots < k_\alpha$.
 
The proof is by induction. First, let $\alpha=1$. In this case $p$ is a constant function and
\begin{equation*}
    f_1(n) = \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
        1 & \quad n \geq 0 \\
        0 & \quad n \leq -1
    \end{aligned}\right.
\end{equation*}
So, if $g = f_1 - p$ has \textit{exactly} one generalised zero, $k_1$, then we must have $p(n) = c$ with $0<c<1$ and $k_1=-1$. This implies the statement of the lemma.

Next, assume $\alpha \geq 2$ and suppose that $g = f_\alpha - p$ has exactly $\alpha$ distinct generalised zeroes. By Lemma \ref{lemma:DiscreteRolle}, we obtain that $\Delta g =   f_{\alpha-1} - \Delta p$ has at least $\alpha-1$ distinct generalised zeroes.

By Lemma \ref{Discrete broken line lemma}, it follows that $\Delta g$ cannot have more than $\alpha-1$ distinct generalised zeroes. Indeed, if this were to happen, then we would have that $\Delta p=0$ or  $\Delta p=r_{\alpha-2}$, which would imply that $g$ has either an infinite amount of zeroes or at most one generalized zero (cf., the last paragraph of the proof of Lemma \ref{Discrete broken line lemma}, above). In both cases this contradicts our assumption.

We may therefore conclude that $\Delta g$ has exactly $\alpha-1$ distinct generalised zeroes $\nu_1 < \cdots < \nu_{\alpha-1}$. Moreover, Lemma \ref{lemma:DiscreteRolle} implies that they interlace with the generalized zeroes of $g$, that is,
\begin{equation} \label{so many labels}
	k_\ell < \nu_\ell  \leq k_{\ell+1}\qquad \ell \in \{ 1, \ldots, \alpha-1\}.
\end{equation}


Fix  $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, \alpha\}$. By the induction hypothesis (and rewriting the interval), it follows that
  \begin{equation} \label{label label}
  	(-1)^{\alpha+\ell} \Delta g(n) \geq 0 \qquad \forall n  \in \{\nu_{\ell-1}+1,..., \nu_{\ell}\} ,
  \end{equation}
  where $\{\nu_0+1, \ldots, \nu_1\}=\{n : n \leq \nu_1\}$ and $\{\nu_{\alpha-1}+1, \ldots, \nu_{\alpha}\}=\{n : n > \nu_{\alpha-1}\}$.
   
Since $k_\alpha\geq \nu_{\alpha-1}$, we have that  (\ref{label label}) holds for $n=k_\alpha +1$. For $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, \alpha-1\}$, one can check that (\ref{so many labels}) implies (\ref{label label}) for $n=k_{\ell}+1$. Explicitly this means that
  \[
  (-1)^{\alpha+\ell} g(k_{\ell}+1)\geq (-1)^{\alpha+\ell} g(k_{\ell}) \qquad \forall \ell \in \{1, \ldots, \alpha\}.
  \]
  As each $k_{\ell}$ is a generalized zero for $g$ we may conclude that
   \[
  (-1)^{\alpha+\ell} g(k_{\ell}+1)\geq 0.
  \]
  Since $g$ has no sign changes over the interval $\{k_{\ell}+1,...,k_{\ell+1}\}$ the same conclusion holds for every $k$ in this interval. This completes the proof for  $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, \alpha\}$.

The case $\ell=0$ may be treated in a similar way: We note that since $k_1<\nu_1$ the function $g$ is monotone over  $\{n : n \leq k_1\}$ and its only generalized zero over this interval is at the point $k_1$. This implies that if $g$ decreases over this interval then it must be positive there, whereas if it increases then it must be negative. Applying the induction hypothesis we obtain the statement for the case  $\ell=0$.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{sum f_a}
Fix  $\alpha\in\mathbb{N}$. For $j\in\mathbb{Z}$ let $p_j$ be a polynomial of degree at most $\alpha-1$ and denote
\[
g_j(k)=f_{\alpha}(j-k)+p_j(k) \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.
\]
Then, for every $m\in\mathbb{N}$, the function $\psi_m$ defined by
\[
\psi_m(k) := \sum_{j=-m}^{m} g_j(k)
\]
is equal over the interval $\{-m,...,m\}$ to a polynomial of degree $\alpha$ and with leading coefficient $(-1)^{\alpha}/(\alpha!)$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Indeed, to see this first note that
\[
\psi_m(k) = \sum_{j=-m}^{m} \Big( f_\alpha(j-k)+ p_j(k) \Big) =\sum_{j=-m}^{m} f_\alpha(j-k)+P(k),
\]
where $P$ is a polynomial of degree at most $\alpha-1$. Next, observe that for $k\in\{-m,...,m\}$ we have
\[
\sum_{j=-m}^{m} f_\alpha(j-k)=\sum_{j=k}^{m} r_{\alpha-1}(j-k)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-k} r_{\alpha-1}(j).
\]
Applying the identity (\ref{sum r_a}), we find that over the interval $\{-m,...,m\}$ we have
\[
\psi_m(k) = r_{\alpha}(m-k)+P(k).
\]
The conclusion follows.

<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def poly realization}
Let $p$ be a real valued polynomial. We say that a matrix $A: \mathbb{C}^m \rightarrow \mathbb{C}^d$  \textit{realizes the polynomial} $p$ if
\vspace{5pt}
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] For every $j \in \{1, \ldots, d\}$ we have that   $\{A(j,k)\}_{k=1}^{m}$ follows the sign structure of $\{p(k)\}_{k=1}^{m}$ over $S=\{1,\ldots,m\}$.
\vspace{5pt}
\item[(ii)]  For every $1\leq k\leq m$ we have
\[
\sum_{j=1}^d A(j,k)=p(k).
\]
\end{itemize}

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{main thm}
Fix $d,\alpha\in\mathbb{N}$, and let $p$ be a real polynomial of degree $\alpha$. Assume that the leading coefficient of $p$ is $(-1)^{\alpha}/\alpha!$ and that the zeroes of $p$ are all real. Then there exists a left inverse of $\Delta_d^\alpha$ which realizes $p$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
We divide the proof into three steps. First we show that the claim holds in the case where the zeroes of $p$ are distinct integers, next we remove the requirement that these integers are distinct, and finally, in Step III, we consider the case of general real zeroes.

\vspace{5pt}

\underline{\textit{Step I:}}
Let
\begin{equation*}
	p(x)= \frac{1}{\alpha!} \prod_{n=1}^{\alpha}(k_n-x),
\end{equation*}
	 where $k_n$ are distinct integers. Denote by $\{\mathcal{L}_n\}_{n=1}^{\alpha}$   the Lagrange interpolation basis with respect to these points, that is,
	\begin{align*}
		\mathcal{L}_n(x)=\prod_{\substack{1\le \ell\le \alpha;\\ \ell\ne n}}\frac{k_\ell-x}{k_\ell-k_n}.
	\end{align*}
	For $j, k \in \mathbb{Z},$ define
	\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned} \label{the definition}
		g_j(k) &=f_\alpha(j-k) - \sum_{n=1}^\alpha \mathcal{L}_n(k)f_\alpha(j-k_n) \\[2mm]
		&:= f_\alpha(j-k)+s_j(k),
	\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
and observe that  $s_j(k)$ are polynomials of degree at most $\alpha-1$ in $k$.

Next, we claim that the functions $g_j$ satisfy the following properties:

\vspace{2pt}

\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] For every $j\in\mathbb{Z}$ the set $\{k_n\}_{n=1}^{\alpha}$ belongs to the zero set of $g_j$. In particular, this implies that each of the functions $g_j$ has at least $\alpha$ zeroes.


\vspace{0pt}

\item[(ii)] For every fixed $j\in\mathbb{Z}$, the sequence $\{g_j(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}$ follows the sign structure of $\{p(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}$.


\vspace{0pt}

\item[(iii)] For every $j\leq 0$ and for every $j\geq d+1$, the function $g_j$ is equal to a polynomial of degree at most $\alpha -1$ over the interval $\{1,...,d+\alpha\}$.



\vspace{0pt}

\item[(iv)]  For every $m\in \mathbb{N}$ the sum
\[
\sum_{j=-m}^{m} g_j(k)
\]
is equal over $\{-m,...,m\}$ to a polynomial of degree $\alpha$ and with leading coefficient $(-1)^{\alpha}/(\alpha !)$.

\end{itemize}

Indeed, (i) follows from the definition of the Lagrange interpolation basis. Next, (ii) follows by keeping in mind property (i) and applying Lemma \ref{lemma sign structure}. For (iii), we recall the definition of $f_{\alpha}$: If $j\leq 0$ then $f_{\alpha}(j-k)=0$ over $\{1,...,d+\alpha\}$, while if $j\geq d+1$ then $f_\alpha(j-k)=r_{\alpha-1}(j-k)$ over this interval. Finally, (iv) follows from Lemma \ref{sum f_a}.




We are now ready to construct the required left inverse. Put $\rho=\max \{|k_n|: n=1,..., \alpha\}$ and fix $m\in\mathbb{N}$ so that $m\geq \max\{d+\alpha, \rho\}$. Denote
\[
G(k)=\sum_{j=-m}^0g_j(k)+\sum_{j=d+1}^m g_j(k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},
\]
and note that properties (i) and (ii) hold for $G$ in place of $g_j$. Moreover, observe that, by Property (iii), it follows that $G$ is a polynomial of degree at most $\alpha-1$ over the interval $\{1,...,d+\alpha\}$.

We define the first row of the left inverse by declaring
\begin{align*}
B(1,k)&=g_1(k)+G(k) \\[2mm] &=f_\alpha(1-k)+s_1(k)+G(k) \qquad k\in\{1,...,d+\alpha\}.
\end{align*}
We next define all other rows of the matrix by putting, for $j=2,...,d$,
\begin{align*}
B(j,k) &=g_j(k) \\[2mm] &=f_\alpha(j-k)+s_j(k) \qquad k\in\{1,...,d+\alpha\}.
\end{align*}
Keeping in mind that the functions $s_j$ are polynomials of degree at most $\alpha-1$, and that over the interval $\{1,...,d+\alpha\}$ the same is true for $G$, we may apply Lemma \ref{lemma:StructureOfLeftInverses} to conclude that $B(j,k)$ is indeed a left inverse of $\Delta_d^{\alpha}$.

Moreover, due to property (ii), and since this property holds also for $G$, we find that for every $j=1,...,d$ the sign structure of $\{B(j,k)\}_{k=1}^{d+\alpha}$ follows the sign structure of  $\{p(k)\}_{k=1}^{d+\alpha}$.

It remains to show that the sum of the rows is as required. For this, note that due to the definition of $G$ we have
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{d}B(j,k)=\sum_{j=-m}^{m}g_j(k)\qquad k \in\{1,...,d+\alpha\}.
\]
The sum on the right hand side may be considered as a function over $\mathbb{Z}$. Property (iv) implies that over the interval $\{-m,...,m\}$ this function is equal to some polynomial $q$ of degree $\alpha$ and
with leading coefficient $(-1)^{\alpha}/(\alpha !)$. The zero set of the polynomial $q$ is equal to $\{k_n\}_{n=1}^\alpha$. Indeed, since $m\geq |k_n|$ for every $n\in\{1,...,\alpha\}$, and $q$ is a polynomial of degree $\alpha$, this is implied by property (i), above. 

Finally, noting that the polynomials $p$ and $q$ are of the same degree, and that they have the same zero set and the same leading coefficient, we may conclude that $q=p$. This completes the proof for the first step.

\vspace{5pt}

\underline{\textit{Step II:}} Assume that the zeroes of $p$ are  integers, but not necessarily distinct. This means that $p$ has a decomposition
\[
p(x)=\frac{(-1)^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\prod_{n=1}^{K}(x-k_n)^{\beta_{n}},
\]
with $k_1, \ldots, k_K\in\mathbb{Z}$ distinct. We prove by induction on the \textit{multiplicity degree} of $p$, which we define by
\[
\mathrm{md}(p):=\alpha-K=\sum_{n=1}^{K}\beta_{n}-K.
\]
The case $\mathrm{md}(p)=0$ was proved in Step I above. So, assume that $\mathrm{md}(p)\geq 1$. This means that there exists $n_0 \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$ so that $\beta_{n_0}\geq 2$.
For this fixed value of $n$, put
\[
v :=\min\{t\in\mathbb{Z}: k_{n_0}<t,\: p(t)\neq 0\}
\]
and
\[
\quad
u :=\max\{t\in\mathbb{Z}: t<k_{n_0},\:p(t)\neq 0\}.
\]
Denote
\[
p_u(x):=\frac{x- u}{x-k_{n_0}}p(x) \quad \textrm{ and } \quad \quad p_v(x):=\frac{x- v}{x-k_{n_0}}p(x).
\]
Observe that the multiplicity degrees of $p_u$ and $p_v$ satisfy $$\mathrm{md}(p_u)=\mathrm{md}(p_v)=\mathrm{md}(p)-1.$$ By the induction assumption, there exist left inverses of $\Delta_d^\alpha$ which realize $p_{u}$ and $p_{v}$. Denote them by $B_{\alpha}^{u}$ and $B_{\alpha}^{v}$ respectively.

It is straight-forward to check that both $p_{u}$ and $p_{v}$ follow the sign structure of $p$ over $\mathbb{Z}$. We may therefore apply the transitivity property to conclude that for every $j=1,..., d$, the sign structures of $B_{\alpha}^u(j,\cdot)$ and $B_{\alpha}^v(j,\cdot)$ follow the sign structure of $p$ over  $\{1,\ldots,d+\alpha\}$.

Next, as $u<k_{n_0}<v$  there exists $0<\tau<1$ so that $k_{n_0} = \tau u  +(1-\tau)v$. Put
\[
B_{\alpha}:=\tau B_{\alpha}^{u}+(1-\tau)B_{\alpha}^{v}.
\]
As $\tau$ is positive, we find that for every $1\leq j\leq d$, the sign structure of $B_{\alpha}(j,\cdot)$ follows the sign structure of $p$ over $\{1,\ldots,d+\alpha\}$. Moreover, since $B_{\alpha}^u$ and $B_{\alpha}^v$ realize $p_u$ and $p_v$, we have for every $k \in \{1, \ldots, d+\alpha\}$
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{j=1}^d B_{\alpha}(j,k)&=\tau \sum_{j=1}^dB_{\alpha}^{u}(j,k)+(1-\tau)\sum_{j=1}^dB_{\alpha}^{v}(j,k)\\[0mm]
&=\tau p_{u}(k)+(1-\tau)p_{v}(k)\\[2mm]
&=\frac{\tau(k-u)+(1-\tau)(k-v)}{k-{k_{n_0}}}p(k)=p(k).
\end{aligned}
\]
We may therefore conclude that $B_{\alpha}$ realizes $p$ and the result holds.

\vspace{5pt}

\underline{\textit{Step III:}} Assume that the zeroes of $p$ are real. This means that $p$ has a decomposition
\[
p(x)=\frac{(-1)^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\prod_{n=1}^{\alpha}(x-x_n),
\]
with $x_n\in\mathbb{R}$ not necessarily distinct.  We prove by induction on the \textit{non-integer degree} of $p$, which we define by
\[
\mathrm{ni}(p):=\#\{n:x_n\notin\mathbb{Z}\}.
\]
The case $\mathrm{ni}(p)=0$ was proved in \textit{Step II} above. So, assume that $\mathrm{ni}(p)\geq 1$. This means that there exists some $n_0\in\{1,...,\alpha\}$ so that $x_{n_0}\notin \mathbb{Z}$. Let
$u = \lfloor x_{n_0} \rfloor$ be the biggest integer smaller than $x_{n_0}$, and put $v =u+1$.
 
We now complete the proof exactly as in \textit{Step II}. That is, we define $p_u$ and $p_v$, as above, and note that their non-integer degree is $\mathrm{ni}(p)-1$. We then apply the induction assumption to find left inverses $B^u$ and $B^v$, respectively, that realise them. Finally, we take a convex combination of these to complete the proof.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{chevilemma}
For $N\in\mathbb{N}$, the following hold.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] For every $n\in \{0,...,N-1\}$, the polynomial $T^{(N)}_n$ is monic and of degree $n$.

\vspace{5pt}

\item[(ii)] The family $\{T^{(N)}_n\}_{n=0}^{N-1}$ is orthogonal in the space $L^2(\mu_N)$.

\vspace{5pt}

\item[(iii)] For every $n\in \{0,...,N-1\}$ we have
\[
\|T^{(N)}_n\|_{L^2(\mu_{N})}^2= (n!)^2\binom{2n}{n}^{-1}\binom{N+n}{2n+1}.
\]
\end{itemize}

Part (i) follows from applying $\Delta^{n}$ to a polynomial of degree $2n$ with leading coefficient $(n!)^{-2}$. Parts (ii) and (iii) are the formulas (2.8.2) and (2.8.3)
from \cite{Szego1975}, reformulated in the notations used here (note, in particular, that the operator $\Delta$ in \cite{Szego1975} is the forward difference operator $\Delta a_j = a_{j+1}-a_j$, whereas we use $\Delta$ to denote the backward difference operator).

<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
We first note that, due to the sign structure of $r_{\alpha}$, we have
\[
\| a \|_{\ell^1}=(-1)^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^dr_{\alpha}(j-1)r_{\alpha}\big(j-(d+\alpha+1)\big).
\]


Next, we observe that by \eqref{Delta r_alpha}, we have $r_\alpha = \Delta^\alpha r_{2\alpha}$. Applying this above, we obtain
\[
\| a \|_{\ell^1}=(-1)^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^d\Delta^{\alpha}r_{2\alpha}(j-1)r_{\alpha}\big(j-(d+\alpha+1)\big).
\]
Applying summation by parts several times,  keeping in mind that the boundary terms vanish, we get
\[
\| a \|_{\ell^1}=\sum_{j=1}^dr_{2\alpha}(j-1)\Delta^{\alpha} r_{\alpha}(j-(d+1)).
\]
Finally,   $\Delta^{\alpha} r_{\alpha}=r_{0}=1$, and so we are left with
\[
\begin{aligned}
\| a \|_{\ell^1}&=\sum_{j=1}^dr_{2\alpha}(j-1)
=\sum_{j=1}^d\binom{2\alpha+j-1}{2\alpha}\\
&=\sum_{j=2\alpha}^{2\alpha+d-1}\binom{j}{2\alpha}
=\binom{d+2\alpha}{2\alpha+1},
\end{aligned}
\]
where the last equality is due to the \textit{hockey-stick} binomial identity. This completes the proof.
