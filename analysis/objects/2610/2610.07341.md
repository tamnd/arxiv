<!-- assumption-1: body_md -->
\label{ass:stab}
For every $k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ the eigenvalues of the matrix $M(k)$ have negative real part (i.e., $M(k)$ is Hurwitz). Since $D_i \geq 0$ for all $i$, a sufficient condition is that the symmetric part of $A$ is negative definite.
<!-- end assumption-1 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:setup}
$\Theta$ denotes the set of triples $\theta=(A,D,\Sigma)$ satisfying Assumption~\ref{ass:stab} with $D\geq0$ diagonal and $\Sigma$ positive definite. We have $\Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^{n_U}$ with $n_U=K^2+K+\binom{K+1}{2}$. The observation map is denoted as $\Phi:\Theta\to\{S(\kappa):\kappa\ge0\}$, $\theta\mapsto S_\theta(\cdot)$, with $S_\theta(\kappa)$ the solution of equation \eqref{eq:lyap}. The inverse image of $\theta_0 \in \Theta$ is $\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\theta_0))\cap\Theta$, namely the set of parameters in $\Theta$ with the same structure factor as $\theta_0$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:ident}
Let $\theta_0\in\Theta$. The triple $(A,D,\Sigma)$ is globally identifiable at $\theta_0$ up to the time scale if the inverse image of $\theta_0$ is the half-line $\{s\theta_0: s>0\}$, and globally identifiable if the inverse image is $\{\theta_0\}$. It is locally identifiable at $\theta_0$ up to the time scale if $\ker d\Phi|_{\theta_0}=\mathrm{span}\{\theta_0\}$, and locally identifiable if $\ker d\Phi|_{\theta_0}=\{0\}$. For fixed $(D,\Sigma)$ the matrix $A$ is called identifiable if from $\Phi(A',D,\Sigma)=\Phi(A,D,\Sigma)$ and $(A',D,\Sigma)\in\Theta$ follows $A'=A$.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:generic}
A feature holds for a generic $\theta \in \Theta$ if there is a nonzero polynomial $q$ on $\mathbb{R}^{n_U}$ such that the feature holds for any $\theta \in \Theta$ with $q(\theta) \neq 0$. The zero set of $q$ has Lebesgue measure zero. The feature therefore holds on a dense subset of full measure of the interior $\Theta^o = \{\theta \in \Theta : D_i>0 \ \forall i\}$.
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:sym}
With Assumption~\ref{ass:stab} and for every $k$ the solution $S(k)$ of \eqref{eq:lyap} is a real symmetric positive-definite matrix that only depends on $|k|^2$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $S(k)=S_R(k)+iS_I(k)$ with $S_R(k),S_I(k)$ real valued and $i$ the imaginary unit. Substitute into \eqref{eq:lyap}, then separate real and imaginary parts to receive
\[
M S_R + S_R M^{T} + \Sigma = 0, \qquad M S_I + S_I M^{T} = 0.
\]
The second equation is a homogeneous Lyapunov equation. Since $M$ is Hurwitz, the only solution is $S_I(k)=0$. We receive $S(k)=S_R(k)$, i.e., $S(k)$ is real. Transposing \eqref{eq:lyap} with $\Sigma=\Sigma^{T}$ shows that $S^{T}(k)$ also fulfills the equation, and by uniqueness we receive $S^{T}(k)=S(k)$. From $\Sigma$ positive definite and $M$ Hurwitz we have $S(k) = \int_0^\infty e^{Mt} \Sigma e^{M^{T}t} dt$, and thus $S$ is positive definite. $M(k)$ depends on $|k|^2$, and thus also $S(k)$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:scale}
For any $c>0$, we have that $\Phi(A,D,\Sigma) = \Phi(cA,cD,c\Sigma)$, and $\lambda$ remains identical. Consequently, if $\Sigma$ is a free parameter, $(A,D,\Sigma)$ is identifiable only up to the one-parameter scaling, and the absolute time scale remains not identifiable.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The stationary state $\lambda$ around which \eqref{eq:rde} is linearized is independent of $(A,D,\Sigma)$. In the affine model $\partial_t \rho = A \rho + b + D \nabla^2 \rho + \xi$ we have $\lambda=-A^{-1}b=-(cA)^{-1}(cb)$, thus $\lambda$ is unchanged under rescaled $b$ with factor $c$. For the mapping $(A,D,\Sigma)\mapsto(cA,cD,c\Sigma)$ we receive $M(k)\mapsto cM(k)$, so the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:lyap} maps to $c\left(M(k)S(k)+S(k)M(k)^{T}+\Sigma\right)$. The transformed equation is also solved by $S(k)$, since $c>0$. Equivalently, the transformation is a time rescaling $t\mapsto t/c$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:scaleknown}
If $\Sigma$ is known, the rescaling is not available. In that case, the scale is identifiable (see Theorem~\ref{thm:global}). Non-identifiability is therefore a consequence of unknown $\Sigma$. The single-population calibration in \cite{schlicke2026hdtd} addressed this same invariance through a normalized time scale. The dispersion length $\sqrt{D/\gamma}$, with $\gamma$ the net proliferation rate, is invariant under the time rescaling and can be recovered without knowledge about the time scale in simulated biopsies \citep{hofmann2026}.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:inv}
Let $(D,\Sigma)$ be fixed. For $A+J-|k|^2D$ Hurwitz for all $k$, the perturbation of the interaction matrix $A\mapsto A+J$ leaves every structure factor $S(k)$ unchanged if and only if
\begin{equation}
J S(k) + S(k) J^{T} = 0 \qquad \text{for all } k.
\label{eq:inv}
\end{equation}
In particular, a purely symmetric $J$ satisfies \eqref{eq:inv} if and only if $J=0$, while a purely antisymmetric $J$ satisfies it if and only if $[J,S(k)] = 0$ for all $k$.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Replacing $A \mapsto A+J$ yields $M(k) \mapsto M(k)+J$. If $S(k)$ solves \eqref{eq:lyap} with the same $\Sigma$ before and after the perturbation, subtracting gives $JS(k)+S(k)J^{T}=0$. Conversely, if $S(k)$ solves \eqref{eq:inv}, then $S(k)$ solves the perturbed Lyapunov equation and, since it is the unique solution, it also is the new structure factor. We have $S(k)=S(k)^{T}$ (cf. proof of Lemma \ref{lem:sym}) and $(J S(k))^{T} = S(k) J^{T}$, so \eqref{eq:inv} yields that $JS(k)$ is antisymmetric. Let $J$ be symmetric, then $J S(k) + S(k) J^{T} = J S(k) + S(k) J$, and the linear map $X \mapsto X S(k) + S(k) X$ on symmetric matrices $X$ has the eigenvalues $\mu_i+\mu_j>0$, where $\mu_1 , ... , \mu_K > 0$ are the eigenvalues of $S(k)$. This means the map is injective, and $J=0$. Let $J$ be antisymmetric, then $JS(k) + S(k) J^{T} = JS(k) - S(k) J = [J,S(k)]$. For a general $J=J_s + J_a$ the two parts couple, since \eqref{eq:inv} reads $J_s S(k) + S(k) J_s+[J_a,S(k)]=0$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:id}
Let $(D,\Sigma)$ be fixed. The interaction matrix $A$ is identifiable from $\{S(k)\}$ if and only if $JS(k)+S(k)J^{T}=0$ for all $k$ is only fulfilled by $J=0$. In that case both the symmetric part $A_s$ and the antisymmetric part $A_a$ are determined.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Two matrices $A,A'$ produce the same $\{S(k)\}$ for a fixed $(D,\Sigma)$ if and only if $J:=A'-A$ satisfies equation \eqref{eq:inv}.

If $J$ solves \eqref{eq:inv} we have $(A+J,D,\Sigma) \in \Theta$ and the positive definite $S(k)$ solves the Lyapunov equation of $A+J-\kappa D$ with positive definite $\Sigma$. By Lyapunov's theorem, we receive that $A+J-\kappa D$ is Hurwitz for every $k$. Thus, $A$ is identifiable if and only if $J=0$ is the unique solution.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:finite}
Let $\theta_0\in\Theta$, let $N_\ast:=\binom{K+1}{2}$, and let $p(\kappa)$ be the determinant of the linear map $L_\kappa: X\mapsto M(\kappa)X+XM(\kappa)^{T}$ on $\mathrm{Sym}(K)$ of symmetric $K\times K$ matrices. For every perturbation $(\delta A,\delta D,\delta\Sigma)$ the product $p(\kappa)E(\kappa)$ is a polynomial in $\kappa$ of degree less or equal $N_\ast$, and the following three statements are equivalent: (i) $E(\kappa)=0$ for all $\kappa\ge0$; (ii) $E(\kappa_j)=0$ at $N_\ast+1$ pairwise distinct values $\kappa_0,\dots,\kappa_{N_\ast}\ge0$; (iii) $E_0=\dots=E_{N_\ast}=0$, where $E(\kappa)=\sum_{n\ge0}E_n\kappa^n$ is the power series of $E$ at $\kappa=0$. The coefficients are $E_0=\delta A S_0+S_0 \delta A^{T}+\delta\Sigma$ and $E_n=\delta A S_n+S_n \delta A^{T}-(\delta D S_{n-1}+S_{n-1} \delta D)$ for $n\ge1$, where $S(\kappa)=\sum_{n\ge0}S_n\kappa^n$ and the matrices $S_n$ are recursively available by $AS_0+S_0A^{T}=-\Sigma$ and $AS_n+S_nA^{T}=DS_{n-1}+S_{n-1}D$ for $n\ge1$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
In a basis of $\mathrm{Sym}(K)$ that has dimension $N_\ast$, the map $L_\kappa$ is an $N_\ast\times N_\ast$ matrix whose entries are affine functions of $\kappa$, since $M(\kappa)=A-\kappa D$. Hence $p(\kappa)=\det L_\kappa$ is a polynomial of degree less or equal $N_\ast$, and the entries of $\mathrm{adj}(L_\kappa)$ are polynomials of degree less or equal $N_\ast-1$. Both bounds use only the dimension of $\mathrm{Sym}(K)$ and they also hold if some $D_i=0$. By Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}, $L_\kappa$ is invertible for every $\kappa\ge0$, so $p(\kappa)\ne0$ there, and Cramer's rule gives $S(\kappa)=\mathcal{N}(\kappa)/p(\kappa)$, where $\mathcal{N}(\kappa)\in\mathrm{Sym}(K)$ is obtained by applying $\mathrm{adj}(L_\kappa)$ to $-\Sigma$ and has entries that are polynomials of degree less or equal $N_\ast-1$. Multiplying $E$ by $p$ gives
\[
p(\kappa)E(\kappa)=\delta A \mathcal{N}(\kappa)+\mathcal{N}(\kappa) \delta A^{T}-\kappa\bigl(\delta D \mathcal{N}(\kappa)+\mathcal{N}(\kappa) \delta D\bigr)+p(\kappa) \delta\Sigma,
\]
and every term on the right is a polynomial of degree less or equal $N_\ast$. This proves the first claim and in particular, $E$ is a rational function of $\kappa$ without poles on $[0,\infty)$.
(i) implies (ii) trivially. If (ii) holds, the polynomial $pE$, of degree less or equal $N_\ast$, vanishes at $N_\ast+1$ distinct points, so it is the zero polynomial, and $E=0$ wherever $p\ne0$, in particular on $[0,\infty)$. This yields (i). For (iii), note that $E$ is analytic in a neighborhood of $\kappa=0$ with a power series of positive radius of convergence, since $p(0)\ne0$. If (i) holds, then $E$ and all coefficients $E_n$ vanish. Conversely, if $E_0=\dots=E_{N_\ast}=0$, then $E(\kappa)=O(\kappa^{N_\ast+1})$ near $\kappa=0$, thus $p(\kappa)E(\kappa)=O(\kappa^{N_\ast+1})$. A polynomial of degree less or equal $N_\ast$ with a zero of order $N_\ast+1$ at the origin is zero, so $E\equiv0$. The formulas for $E_n$ and $S_n$ follow by inserting $S(\kappa)=\sum_n S_n\kappa^n$ into the definition of $E$ and into \eqref{eq:lyap} and comparing the coefficients of $\kappa^n$.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:generic}
There is a polynomial $q$ on $\mathbb{R}^{n_U}$ such that for $\theta\in\Theta$
\[
\dim\ker d\Phi|_{\theta}=1 \quad\text{if and only if}\quad q(\theta)\neq0 .
\]
Consequently, if $\dim\ker d\Phi|_{\theta}=1$ at one point of $\Theta$, then $q$ is not the zero polynomial and $\dim\ker d\Phi|_{\theta}=1$ holds for generic $\theta\in\Theta$ in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:generic}.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
By \eqref{eq:kernel} and Lemma~\ref{lem:finite}(iii), $\ker d\Phi|_{\theta}$ is the solution space of the finite linear system $E_0=\dots=E_{N_\ast}=0$ in the unknown $(\delta A,\delta D,\delta\Sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_U}$. Let $C(\theta)$ be its coefficient matrix, such that $\dim\ker d\Phi|_{\theta}=n_U-\mathrm{rank} C(\theta)$. The entries of $C(\theta)$ are linear in the entries of $S_0,\dots,S_{N_\ast}$, and these are received from the recursion of Lemma~\ref{lem:finite} through solving $N_\ast+1$ Lyapunov equations with $L_0: X\mapsto AX+XA^{T}$. By Cramer's rule these are rational functions of $\theta$ and their denominators are powers of $\det L_0$. Since $A=M(0)$ is Hurwitz on $\Theta$ we have $\det L_0\neq0$, and multiplying $C(\theta)$ by a sufficiently high power of $\det L_0$ yields a matrix $\tilde C(\theta)$ with polynomial entries and $\mathrm{rank} \tilde C(\theta)=\mathrm{rank} C(\theta)$ for all $\theta\in\Theta$. The scaling direction $\theta$ solves the system, and $\mathrm{rank} C(\theta)\le n_U-1$ on $\Theta$, with equality exactly when $\dim\ker d\Phi|_{\theta}=1$. Let $q(\theta)$ be the sum of the squares of all minors of size $n_U-1$ of $\tilde C(\theta)$. Then $q$ is a polynomial on $\mathbb{R}^{n_U}$, and $q(\theta)\neq0$ if and only if at least one of these minors is nonzero, i.e., if and only if $\mathrm{rank} \tilde C(\theta)\ge n_U-1$. For $\theta\in\Theta$ this is exactly the case when $\dim\ker d\Phi|_{\theta}=1$. If this happens at one point of $\Theta$, then $q$ cannot be the zero polynomial, and Definition~\ref{def:generic} applies.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
At a generic $(A,D,\Sigma)\in\Theta$ and for every number $K \ge 1$ of populations, the differential of the observation map $\Phi$ has a one-dimensional kernel that is spanned by the scaling direction of Proposition~\ref{prop:scale}. Thus, $(A,D,\Sigma)$ is locally identifiable from $\{S(\kappa)\}$ up to that rescaling. If $\Sigma$ is known in addition, the kernel is trivial and the scale becomes identifiable.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:generic} it suffices to find one point of $\Theta$ with $\dim\ker d\Phi=1$. We take a point at which $A$ is diagonal, so that the Lyapunov equation decouples into scalar equations. Let $A=\mathrm{diag}(a_1,\dots,a_K)$ and $D=\mathrm{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_K)$ with $a_i<0$ and $d_i>0$, chosen such that
\begin{equation}
\text{the } \tbinom{K+1}{2} \text{ numbers } \kappa_{ij}:=\frac{a_i+a_j}{d_i+d_j}, \ i\le j,
\ \text{are pairwise distinct,}
\label{eq:polesep}
\end{equation}
and let $\Sigma$ be symmetric positive definite with nonzero entries. Such points exist for every $K$: the choice $d_i=1$, $a_i=-2^{i}$ satisfies \eqref{eq:polesep}, since $\kappa_{ij}=-(2^{i}+2^{j})/2$ and the binary expansion of $2^{i}+2^{j}$ determines the pair $\{i,j\}$ (for $i=j$ the number is $2^{i+1}$). $\Sigma=(1-\varepsilon)I+\varepsilon \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}$ with $0<\varepsilon<1$ and the vector of ones $\mathbf{1}$ has all entries nonzero and the positive eigenvalues $1+\varepsilon(K-1)$ and $1-\varepsilon$ (for $K=1$ read $\Sigma=1$). Condition \eqref{eq:polesep} is stronger than requiring distinct ratios $a_i/d_i$: for example for $a=(-1,-2,-3)$ and $d=(1,1,1)$ the ratios are distinct, but $\kappa_{13}=\kappa_{22}=-2$. $A_a=0$ is not relevant for our purpose, since our claim is about the rank of $d\Phi$.

At that point $M(\kappa)$ is diagonal and \eqref{eq:lyap} decouples for each entry:
\[
S_{ij}(\kappa) = \frac{-\Sigma_{ij}}{\mathcal{D}_{ij}(\kappa)}, \qquad
\mathcal{D}_{ij}(\kappa) = (a_i+a_j) - \kappa(d_i+d_j).
\]
Each entry has a single simple pole at $\kappa_{ij}<0$, and these poles are pairwise distinct by \eqref{eq:polesep}. Let $(\delta A,\delta D,\delta\Sigma)\in\ker d\Phi$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:finite}, $E(\kappa)=0$ for $\kappa\ge0$ implies that $E$ vanishes identically as a rational function, and in particular every residue of every entry of $E$ vanishes. Consider now the diagonal entry $E_{ii}(\kappa)$. Among its terms, only $\delta A_{il}S_{li}(\kappa)+S_{il}(\kappa)\delta A_{il}=2\delta A_{il}S_{il}(\kappa)$ (using $S=S^{T}$) has a pole at $\kappa_{li}$ for $l\ne i$, and
\[
\operatorname*{Res}_{\kappa=\kappa_{li}} E_{ii}
= \frac{2\delta A_{il}\Sigma_{li}}{d_l+d_i}.
\]
Since $\Sigma_{li}\ne0$, this yields $\delta A_{il}=0$. Letting $l\ne i$ and $i$ range over all indices shows that $\delta A$ is diagonal. This step uses only that for fixed $i$ the $K$ poles $\kappa_{1i},\dots,\kappa_{Ki}$ are distinct.

With $\delta A$ diagonal, the $(i,j)$ entry of $E$ for $i\ne j$ reads $\big((\delta A_{ii}+\delta A_{jj})-\kappa(\delta D_i+\delta D_j)\big)S_{ij}(\kappa)+\delta\Sigma_{ij}=0$. Multiply by $\mathcal{D}_{ij}(\kappa)$ to receive an identity between polynomials of degree one in $\kappa$, and compare coefficients for
\[
\delta\Sigma_{ij} = \Sigma_{ij}\frac{\delta D_i+\delta D_j}{d_i+d_j}, \qquad
\delta A_{ii}+\delta A_{jj} = (a_i+a_j)\frac{\delta D_i+\delta D_j}{d_i+d_j}.
\]
The $(i,i)$ entry gives in the same way $\delta A_{ii}=a_i\delta D_i/d_i$ and $\delta\Sigma_{ii}=\Sigma_{ii}\delta D_i/d_i$. Writing $f_i := \delta D_i/d_i$, the off-diagonal relation becomes $a_i f_i + a_j f_j = (a_i+a_j)(d_i f_i + d_j f_j)/(d_i+d_j)$, which simplifies to $(f_i-f_j)(a_i d_j - a_j d_i)=0$. By \eqref{eq:polesep} the values $\kappa_{ii}=a_i/d_i$ are pairwise distinct, so $a_id_j\ne a_jd_i$ and $f_i=f_j=:c$ for all $i,j$. Then $\delta D=cD$, $\delta A=cA$ and $\delta\Sigma=c\Sigma$, i.e., $\ker d\Phi=\mathrm{span}\{(A,D,\Sigma)\}$ at this point, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:generic} at generic points of $\Theta$. If $\Sigma$ is known, $\delta\Sigma=0$ forces $c=0$, which yields scale identifiability.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:inverseimage}
For $\theta_0\in\Theta$,
\[
\Phi(\theta) = \Phi(\theta_0)
\quad \text{ if and only if } \quad
\theta-\theta_0 \in \ker d\Phi|_{\theta_0},
\qquad \theta\in\Theta .
\]
If $\ker d\Phi|_{\theta_0}=\mathrm{span}\{\theta_0\}$, then the inverse image of $\theta_0$ is exactly the half-line: $\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\theta_0)) = \{s\theta_0 : s>0\}$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $S:=\Phi(\theta_0)$. If $\Phi(\theta)=S$, then both $\theta$ and $\theta_0$ satisfy \eqref{eq:lyap} with the same $S(\kappa)$ for every $\kappa\ge0$. Subtracting the two results in relation \eqref{eq:kernel} of $\ker d\Phi|_{\theta_0}$ for $\theta-\theta_0$. Conversely, if $\theta-\theta_0\in\ker d\Phi|_{\theta_0}$, adding \eqref{eq:lyap} for $\theta_0$ shows that $S(\kappa)$ solves the Lyapunov equation for $\theta$. Since $M_\theta(\kappa)$ is Hurwitz, this solution is unique, and therefore $\Phi(\theta)=S$. Finally, $\theta=s\theta_0$ lies in $\Theta$ if and only if $s>0$. A negative $s<0$ reverses the spectrum of $A$ and destroys stability, and $s=0$ would violate that $\Sigma$ is positive definite.
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:global}
At a generic $\theta_0\in\Theta$ the inverse image $\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\theta_0))\cap\Theta$ is exactly $\{s\theta_0:s>0\}$. Therefore, $(A,D,\Sigma)$ is globally identifiable up to a positive time rescaling. In particular, the signs and the ratios of the entries of the directed part $A_a$ are identifiable. If $\Sigma$ is known additionally, it is even globally identifiable.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Combine Theorem~\ref{thm:main} with Lemma~\ref{lem:inverseimage}.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:shells}
Let $\theta,\theta_0\in\Theta$ and let $\kappa_0,\dots,\kappa_{N_\ast}\ge0$ be $N_\ast+1=\binom{K+1}{2}+1$ pairwise distinct wavenumbers. If $S_\theta(\kappa_j)=S_{\theta_0}(\kappa_j)$ for all $j$, then we receive $S_\theta=S_{\theta_0}$ on $[0,\infty)$. For a generic $\theta_0$ equal structure factors at $\binom{K+1}{2}+1$ distinct wavenumbers implies $\theta=s\theta_0$ for $s>0$.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Let $E$ be the function as in equation \eqref{eq:E} with $S=S_{\theta_0}$ and $(\delta A,\delta D,\delta\Sigma)=\theta-\theta_0$. For each $\kappa_j$, subtracting the Lyapunov equations of $\theta_0$ and $\theta$ (which share the matrix $S_{\theta_0}(\kappa_j)$) gives $E(\kappa_j)=0$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:finite}, $E=0$ on $[0,\infty)$, thus $\theta-\theta_0\in\ker d\Phi|_{\theta_0}$, and from Lemma~\ref{lem:inverseimage} we receive $\Phi(\theta)=\Phi(\theta_0)$. For the second claim, let $\theta_0$ be generic. Then $\theta$ lies in the inverse image of $\theta_0$, which is the half-line $\{s\theta_0:s>0\}$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:global}.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:degen}
Theorem~\ref{thm:global} holds at generic points, but there is a set of exceptions. The first is full isotropy. For $A_s=\alpha I$, $D=dI$ and $\Sigma=\sigma I$ the family is $S(\kappa)=s(\kappa)I$ with $s(\kappa)=\sigma/(2(\kappa d-\alpha))$, for any $A_a$, and $E=0$ reduces to $\delta\Sigma=(\sigma/d)\delta D$ and $\delta A_s=(\alpha/d)\delta D$ with $\delta D$ diagonal and free $\delta A_a$. The kernel has dimension $K(K+1)/2$ and $A_a$ is lost. Equal motilities $D_i$ are not alone responsible for this behavior. On the set $D\propto I$, the kernel stays one-dimensional at generic points, since $q$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:generic} does not vanish identically there. The reference point of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has $D=I$. The second we focus on is decoupling. If $A$, $D$ and $\Sigma$ split simultaneously into $m$ non-interacting blocks, then each block has its own time scale and $\dim\ker d\Phi\ge m$. For example, $K=2$ with $A=\mathrm{diag}(-3,-5)$, $D=\mathrm{diag}(5,2)$, $\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(7,3)$ already has kernel dimension $2$: the off-diagonal entry of $E$ forces $\delta A_{12}=\delta A_{21}=\delta\Sigma_{12}=0$, and each block is a $K=1$ problem with the kernel of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. A split into $r$ and $K-r$ populations results in $3r(K-r)$ conditions, so this set has positive codimension and Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is unaffected. On the decoupled set the half-line of $A_a$ can remain identifiable: for $A$ with diagonal blocks $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-2&1\\-1&-3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $-1$, $D=\mathrm{diag}(1,2,\tfrac32)$ and $\Sigma$ with diagonal blocks $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1/2\\1/2&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $1$, an exact computation gives a kernel spanned by the two block scalings, which change $A_a$ only by a factor. The third is the reversible case: for diagonal $\Sigma$ with $\Sigma ^{-1} A$ symmetric, including the case $A=A^{T}$ and $\Sigma=\sigma I$, the matrix $\Sigma^{-1} M(k)$ is symmetric, so $S(\kappa) = -1/2 \ M(\kappa)^{-1} \Sigma$ is symmetric and a solution of \eqref{eq:lyap} since $M(\kappa)S(\kappa)=S(\kappa)M(\kappa)^T=-1/2 \ \Sigma$.
For every diagonal $G$ with positive entries the triple $(GA,GD,G\Sigma)$ has the same family, since 

$(GM(\kappa))^{-1} G \Sigma = M(\kappa)^{-1} \Sigma$. Then the kernel contains the $K$-dimensional space of directions $(FA,FD,F\Sigma)$ with $F$ diagonal, and $GA$ is in general not symmetric. This means that a symmetric operator with isotropic noise cannot be distinguished from a directed operator with the same sign pattern. This set has codimension $K^2-K$, and with $A=A^{T}$ and generic diagonal $\Sigma$ the kernel is one-dimensional in exact computations for $K \leq 5$.

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:norm}
Let the field of population $i$ be only observed up to an unknown scalar $g_i>0$, such that the observation is $\{GS(\kappa)G\}$ with $G=\mathrm{diag}(g_1,\dots,g_K)$ unknown. Equivalently, let the family be normalized per population, $S_{ij}(\kappa)/(\sigma_i\sigma_j)$ with $\sigma_i$ a fixed amplitude for population $i$. At a generic $\theta_0=(A,D,\Sigma)\in\Theta$ the set of admissible triples with the same normalized observation is therefore
\[
\bigl\{(sGAG^{-1}, sD, sG\Sigma G): s>0, G=\mathrm{diag}(g_1,\dots,g_K) \text{ with $g_i>0$ for all $i$}\bigr\},
\]
a manifold of dimension $K+1$. The signs and zero pattern of the entries of $A$ are identifiable, the products of entries along closed paths and the diffusivities are identifiable up to the time scale, and the $K$ factors $g_i$ are not identifiable from the second order.
<!-- end corollary-3 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
For diagonal positive $G$ and $\theta=(A,D,\Sigma)\in\Theta$ define $\theta^{G}:=(GAG^{-1},D,G\Sigma G)$. Since $G$ commutes with the diagonal $D$, $M_{\theta^{G}}(k)=GM_{\theta}(k)G^{-1}$, and multiplying \eqref{eq:lyap} for $\theta$ from the left and from the right by $G$ shows that $GS_{\theta}(\kappa)G$ solves the Lyapunov equation of $\theta^{G}$. By uniqueness we receive $\Phi(\theta^{G})=G\Phi(\theta)G$. Moreover $\theta^{G}\in\Theta$, because $GAG^{-1}$ has the eigenvalues of $A$ and $G\Sigma G$ is positive definite, and both $\theta\mapsto\theta^{G}$ and $S\mapsto GSG$ are linear isomorphisms, such that $\dim \ker d\Phi|_{\theta^{G}} = \dim \ker d\Phi|_\theta$ and $\theta^G$ is generic whenever $\theta$ is. Now let $\theta,\theta'\in\Theta$ have the identical normalized observation, that is, $\Phi(\theta')=G\Phi(\theta)G$ for a $G$ diagonal matrix with positive entries. Then $\Phi(\theta')=\Phi(\theta^{G})$ with $\theta^{G}$ generic, and from Theorem~\ref{thm:global} we have $\theta'=s\theta^{G}$ for $s>0$.
Conversely, every triple $(sGAG^{-1},sD,sG\Sigma G)$ has the normalized observation of $\theta$ by $\Phi(\theta^{G})=G\Phi(\theta)G$ and Proposition~\ref{prop:scale}. The parameters $(s,g_1,\dots,g_K)$ are determined by the triple, so the set is a manifold of dimension $K+1$. The invariants follow from $(GAG^{-1})_{ij}=g_iA_{ij}/g_j$ with $g_i/g_j>0$. For products of entries along a closed path these factors cancel. The individual factors $g_i$ themselves do not appear in the normalized observation and are therefore not identifiable from it.
