<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:positive}
Let $1\leq p<\infty$ and $\varepsilon>0$. There is a non-negative Schauder
basis $(f_n)$ of $L^p(\mathbb R)$ with basis constant $K((f_n))<1+\varepsilon$.
Moreover, no non-negative Schauder basis $(f_n)$ of $L^p(\mathbb R)$ has $K((f_n))=1$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:functionals}\label{prop:endpoint}
Let $1\leq p<\infty$ and $\varepsilon>0$. There is a Schauder basis
$(f_n)$ of $L^p(\mathbb R)$ with $K((f_n))<1+\varepsilon$ whose coefficient
functions $\gamma_n$ are non-negative.
Moreover, no basis with positive
biorthogonal functionals has basis constant one.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:translates}
For every $2\le p<\infty$, there is a nonzero real-valued function
$f\in L^p(\mathbb R)$ and an ordered sequence of real numbers
$(\lambda_n)$ such that $(T_{\lambda_n}f)$ is a Schauder basis of
$L^p(\mathbb R)$ over either scalar field.

\label{thm:translate-properties}
 The Schauder basis of translates $(T_{\lambda_n} f)$ in Theorem \ref{thm:translates} can be chosen with the following additional regularity and structural properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $p=2$ then for every $\varepsilon>0$ one can choose $f$ which extends to an entire function and $(\lambda_n)$ so that the basis constant satisfies $K((T_{\lambda_n}f)) < 1+\varepsilon$.
\item If $p>2$ and $S\subset\mathbb R$ is unbounded, there are distinct parameters $(\lambda_n)\subset S$ and a non-negative function $f\in L^p(\mathbb R)$ extending to an entire function such that $(T_{\lambda_n}f)$ is a Schauder basis of $L^p(\mathbb R)$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:translation-restrictions}
There are several known restrictions on any basis of translates.
As mentioned above, such a basis must be
conditional. Another interesting fact from \cite{DH,OSSZ} is that the generator cannot
belong to $L^1(\mathbb R)$. In particular,
there is no basis of translates of $L^1(\mathbb R)$.

There are also several Fourier restrictions. For example, when $p=2$, completeness
requires $\widehat f\ne0$ almost everywhere~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{OSSZ}.
Moreover, for every finite $p$, the Fourier transform of $f$, interpreted as
a distribution when necessary, must have support equal to $\mathbb R$.
Interestingly, for $1<p<2$, Lev and Olevskii~\cite[Corollary~2]{LO} constructed two
functions in $L^1(\mathbb R)\cap L^p(\mathbb R)$ whose Fourier transforms have the same
zero set, but whose families of all real translates have different
completeness properties in $L^p(\mathbb R)$. Thus, in this range, the zero set
alone does not determine completeness of the family of all real translates.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:translation-sets}
For a basis $(T_{\lambda_n}f)$, it is evident that the translation parameters $\lambda_n$ must be \emph{uniformly discrete}, meaning that
$\inf_{n\ne m}|\lambda_n-\lambda_m|>0$.
For $p>2$, \Cref{thm:translates} applies with the parameters $\lambda_n$ chosen from any prescribed
unbounded subset of $\mathbb R$. However, even for $p>2$, the whole
lattice $a\mathbb Z+b$ cannot be ordered as a Schauder basis of
translates~\cite[Proposition~3.2]{OSSZ}.

When $p=2$, our construction allows for rational parameters whose pairwise
separations tend to infinity;
see \Cref{h2:ss:transfer}. In particular, the translation set can have zero
upper uniform density, that is,
$$
\limsup_{R\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}
\frac{\#\{n:\lambda_n\in[x,x+R]\}}{R}=0.
$$
However, one cannot prescribe an arbitrary unbounded set at the endpoint $p=2$. Indeed,
it is a classical result of Atzmon and Olevskii~\cite{AO} that
no subset of $a\mathbb Z+b$ gives a complete system of
translates in $L^2(\mathbb R)$. Nonetheless, small nonzero perturbations of the integers can support
complete systems; see \cite{LT-sparse,Ol97,OU} for additional information.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:aux}
Fix $1<p\le\infty$ and $\delta>0$. There is a constant $M\ge1$
and, for every $N\ge1$, a pair of biorthogonal bases
$(s_j)_{j=1}^{2N}$ and $(d_j)_{j=1}^{2N}$ of $\mathbb F^{2N}$ and a vector
$z_N\in\mathbb F^{2N}$ such that
\begin{align}
&s_j\ge0,\quad \left\lVerts_j\right\rVert_p\le M,\quad
\sup_{0\le r\le2N}
\left\|\sum_{j=1}^r s_j\otimes d_j\right\|_{\ell_p^{2N}\to\ell_p^{2N}}
\le1+\delta,\label{eq:aux-bounds}\\
&z_N\ge0,\quad
\left\langles_j,z_N\right\rangle\ge1\quad(1\le j\le N),\quad
\omega_N:=N^{-1/q}\left\lVertz_N\right\rVert_q\longrightarrow0
\quad(N\to\infty).\label{eq:aux-smallness}
\end{align}
The constant $M$ is independent of $N$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:inverse}
For $0<\alpha\le1$, the matrix in \eqref{eq:toeplitz} satisfies
$$
\left\lVertT_m^{-1}\right\rVert_{\ell_r^m\to\ell_r^m}\le2
\quad(1\le r\le\infty).
$$
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $V_m$ be the coordinate shift given by
$V_m e_j=e_{j+1}$ for $j<m$ and $V_m e_m=0$.
We set $\mu_0=1$ and $\mu_n=\alpha/(n+1)$ for $n\ge1$, so that
$$
T_m=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\mu_kV_m^k.
$$
To compute the inverse, we define $c_n$ recursively by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:renewal}
\mu_n=\sum_{k=1}^n c_k \mu_{n-k}\quad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
Since $V_m^m=0$, this identity gives
$$
T_m^{-1}=I-\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}c_kV_m^k.
$$
Indeed, \eqref{eq:renewal} makes the coefficient of every positive
power of $V_m$ vanish in the product. Since each power of $V_m$
is contractive on $\ell_r^m$, it remains to prove that $c_n\ge0$
and $\sum_{k=1}^n c_k\le1$.

We use the elementary argument behind Kaluza's sign criterion
\cite[Theorem~1.3]{BVV}. The ratios $\mu_n/\mu_{n-1}$ are
$\alpha/2,2/3,3/4,\ldots$ and are therefore nondecreasing.
Since $c_1=\mu_1>0$, we can prove $c_n\ge0$ by induction.
If $c_1,\ldots,c_{n-1}\ge0$, then
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_k \mu_{n-k}
\le\frac{\mu_n}{\mu_{n-1}}
 \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_k \mu_{n-1-k}
=\mu_n.
$$
It follows from \eqref{eq:renewal} that $c_n\ge0$. Moreover,
since $(\mu_n)$ is decreasing, we have
$$
\mu_n=\sum_{k=1}^n c_k \mu_{n-k}
\ge \mu_n\sum_{k=1}^n c_k,
$$
which proves the bound on the sum. Consequently,
$$
\left\lVertT_m^{-1}\right\rVert_{\ell_r^m\to\ell_r^m}
\le1+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}c_k\le2.
$$

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:hilbert}
Let $1<r<\infty$ and $r'=r/(r-1)$.
The matrix $\mathcal H=(1/(i+j))_{i,j\ge1}$ defines a bounded
operator on $\ell_r$, with norm at most $r+r'$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For $0<u<1$, comparison with the integral of a decreasing function gives
$$
\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{j^{-u}}{i+j}
\le\int_0^\infty\frac{t^{-u}}{i+t}\,dt
\le\left(\frac{1}{1-u}+\frac{1}{u}\right)i^{-u}.
$$
In the last step, we split the integral at $i$, and then bounded the
denominator below by $i$ on $(0,i)$ and by $t$ on $(i,\infty)$.
Taking either $u=1/r$ or $u=1/r'$ gives the same constant
$C=r+r'$.

For a finitely supported sequence $x$, H\"older's inequalty
and the estimate with $u=1/r$ yield
$$
\left|\sum_j\frac{x_j}{i+j}\right|^r
\le
\left(\sum_j\frac{j^{-1/r}}{i+j}\right)^{r-1}
\sum_j\frac{j^{1/r'}|x_j|^r}{i+j}
\le C^{r-1}i^{-1/r'}
\sum_j\frac{j^{1/r'}|x_j|^r}{i+j}.
$$
Summing in $i$, interchanging the sums, and applying the estimate with
$u=1/r'$ gives
$$
\sum_i\left|\sum_j\frac{x_j}{i+j}\right|^r
\le C^r\sum_j|x_j|^r.
$$
The result then follows by density.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We use the vectors in \eqref{eq:toeplitz-systems}. They are
biorthogonal because $T_m^{-1}T_m=I$, and the columns $s_j$ are
non-negative. Their norms satisfy
$$
\left\lVerts_j\right\rVert_p^p
=1+\alpha^p\sum_{r=2}^{m-j+1}r^{-p}
\le1+\int_1^\infty t^{-p}\,dt
=q.
$$
Thus, we may take $M=q^{1/p}$. Since each of the first $N$ columns
has coordinate sum at least $\Lambda_N$, we also have
$$
\left\langles_j,z_N\right\rangle\ge1,\quad
\omega_N=\frac{2^{1/q}}{\Lambda_N}\longrightarrow0.
$$

It remains to estimate the partial sum projections. If $D_r$
denotes the projection onto the first $r$ coordinates, then the
corresponding projection for $(s_j)$ is $T_mD_rT_m^{-1}$.
For $1\le r<m$, we write
$$
T_m=
\begin{pmatrix}
T_r&0\\
B_r&T_{m-r}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here, $B_r$ consists of the last $m-r$ rows and the first $r$
columns of $T_m$. Block multiplication gives
$$
T_mD_rT_m^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_r&0\\
B_rT_r^{-1}&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus, we only need to bound $B_rT_r^{-1}$. Reversing the columns
of $B_r$ gives the entries $\alpha/(u+v)$, where
$1\le u\le m-r$ and $1\le v\le r$. Coordinate reversal
preserves the norm, so \Cref{lem:hilbert} gives
$\left\lVertB_r\right\rVert_{\ell_p^r\to\ell_p^{m-r}}\le\alpha(p+q)$.
Combining this with \Cref{lem:inverse}, we obtain
$$
\left\lVertT_mD_rT_m^{-1}\right\rVert_{\ell_p^m\to\ell_p^m}
\le1+\left\lVertB_r\right\rVert\left\lVertT_r^{-1}\right\rVert
\le1+2\alpha(p+q)\le1+\delta.
$$
The cases $r=0,m$ give the zero operator and the identity.
Since $T_mD_rT_m^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^r s_j\otimes d_j$, this proves
\eqref{eq:aux-bounds}. The transposed projections on $\ell_q^m$
have the same norms.

Here, $q=1$. We set $m=2N$ and take

d_1=e_1,\quad d_j=e_j-e_{j-1}\quad(2\le j\le m),\quad z_N=e_m.

For $A,\mathcal P,L$ as above, the following assertions hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The families $(w_l)$ and $(\psi_l)$ are biorthogonal step

\label{lem:positive-extension}
Suppose that $(f_i)_{i=1}^d$ is a non-negative basis of
$E_{\mathcal P}L^p$ with basis constant $K$.
Let $(w_l)_{l=1}^{m+N}$ be the list in \Cref{prop:stage}, set
$\widehat w_l=(I+S_A^*)w_l$, and write $\tau=\left\lVertS_A\right\rVert$.
Then
$$
f_1,\ldots,f_d,\widehat w_1,\ldots,\widehat w_{m+N}
$$
is a non-negative basis of $E_{\mathcal P'}L^p$ with basis constant
at most $\max\{K,K_0\}(1+\tau)^2$.
For fixed $A,\mathcal P,\delta,\eta$, the number $\tau$ can be
made arbitrarily small by increasing $L$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We first add the vectors $w_l$ in the order \eqref{eq:stage-order},
before applying $I+S_A^*$. Biorthogonality
makes them linearly independent, and $E_{\mathcal P}w_l=0$ shows
that their span has zero intersection with the old span. Since
\Cref{prop:stage}(ii) gives
$$
E_{\mathcal P'}=E_{\mathcal P}
 +\sum_{l=1}^{m+N}w_l\otimes\psi_l,
$$
the combined sequence spans $E_{\mathcal P'}L^p$ and is therefore
a basis of that space. A partial sum ending among the old vectors
is an old partial sum composed with $E_{\mathcal P}$, so its norm
is at most $K$. A partial sum ending among the new vectors is
$E_{\mathcal P}+\sum_{l=1}^r w_l\otimes\psi_l$, whose norm is
at most $K_0$.

We now apply $C_A=I+S_A^*$. This fixes the old vectors and makes
the new ones non-negative. Since $S_A^*$ takes values in the old
span, both $C_A$ and $C_A^{-1}$ preserve $E_{\mathcal P'}L^p$.
Each partial sum projection $T$ becomes $C_ATC_A^{-1}$. By
\eqref{eq:local-inverse}, its norm is at most $(1+\tau)^2\left\lVertT\right\rVert$. This proves the bound on the
basis constant, and \eqref{eq:common-estimate} shows that $\tau$
can be made arbitrarily small.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:raw}
If $1<p<\infty$, the sequence $(v_n)$ is a Schauder basis of
$L^p(\mathbb R)$ with coefficient functions $(\xi_n)$.
For the full range $1<p\le\infty$, the sequence $(\xi_n)$ is a
Schauder basis of $L^q(\mathbb R)$ with coefficient functions $(v_n)$.
The partial sum projections
\begin{equation}\label{eq:raw-projections}
P_nf=\sum_{j=1}^n\left\langlef,\xi_j\right\rangle v_j,\quad
R_ng=\sum_{j=1}^n\left\langlev_j,g\right\rangle\xi_j
\end{equation}
satisfy $\left\lVertP_n\right\rVert_{L^p\to L^p}=\left\lVertR_n\right\rVert_{L^q\to L^q}
\le K_0=1+\delta+\eta$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Each stage gives a biorthogonal system by \Cref{prop:stage}(i).
For $n\le\nu_k$, the functions $v_n$ and $\xi_n$ are supported
in $A_k$ and constant on each interval of $\mathcal P_k$, whereas
every $w_{k,l}$ and $\psi_{k,l}$ has integral zero on these intervals.
Thus, the pairings between different stages vanish in both orders,
and the full system is biorthogonal.

The first projection is $P_1=E_{\mathcal P_1}$.
If $P_{\nu_k}=E_{\mathcal P_k}$, then the projections during
stage $k$ are
$$
P_{\nu_k+r}=E_{\mathcal P_k}
 +\sum_{l=1}^r w_{k,l}\otimes\psi_{k,l}
\quad(0\le r\le m_k+N_k).
$$
By \Cref{prop:stage}(ii), these projections have norm at most
$K_0$, and the last one equals $E_{\mathcal P_{k+1}}$.
Induction therefore gives
$$
P_{\nu_k}=E_{\mathcal P_k},\quad \sup_n\left\lVertP_n\right\rVert\le K_0.
$$
Since $q<\infty$, we have $(L^q)^*=L^p$ and $R_n^*=P_n$, so
$\left\lVertR_n\right\rVert=\left\lVertP_n\right\rVert$. Moreover, the averaging projections
satisfy $\left\langleE_{\mathcal P_k}f,g\right\rangle=\left\langlef,E_{\mathcal P_k}g\right\rangle$.
It follows that $R_{\nu_k}=E_{\mathcal P_k}$ on $L^q$ as well.

We have already proved that $E_{\mathcal P_k}h\to h$ in $L^r$
for every $h\in L^r$ and every $1\le r<\infty$. To pass from the
ends of stages to all partial sums, we let $T_n$ denote $P_n$ on
$L^p$ when $p<\infty$, or $R_n$ on $L^q$, and write $L^r$ for
the corresponding space. For $n\ge\nu_k$, biorthogonality gives
$T_nT_{\nu_k}=T_{\nu_k}$, and hence
$$
\left\lVerth-T_nh\right\rVert_r
=\left\lVert(I-T_n)(h-E_{\mathcal P_k}h)\right\rVert_r
\le(1+K_0)\left\lVerth-E_{\mathcal P_k}h\right\rVert_r.
$$
The right-hand side tends to zero as $k\to\infty$, uniformly
for $n\ge\nu_k$. Thus, the full sequence of partial sums converges,
and biorthogonality gives uniqueness of the expansions.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:corrected}
Every $f_n$ is a non-negative compactly supported step function.
If $1<p<\infty$, the sequence $(f_n)$ is a Schauder basis of
$L^p(\mathbb R)$ with coefficient functions $(\gamma_n)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:final-bound}
K((f_n))\le(1+\delta+\eta)\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}.
\end{equation}
At $p=\infty$, the sequence $(\gamma_n)$ is a Schauder basis
of $L^1(\mathbb R)$ with coefficient functions $(f_n)$ and the same
upper bound for its basis constant.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We first show that $S_l^*v_n=0$ when $v_n$ is a vector from
stage $k$ and $l\ne k$. The adjoint of \eqref{eq:global-S}
is given by
$$
S^*f=\sum_{l=1}^\infty
\kappa_l\theta_l\left\langlef,z_l\right\rangle\1_{A_l},
$$
with convergence in $L^p$, including when $p=\infty$.
Each $z_l$ is constant on every unit interval, while
$\varphi_{k,j}$ has integral zero on every such interval by
\eqref{eq:haar}. Hence, $\left\langle\varphi_{k,j},z_l\right\rangle=0$ for every $l$. The function
$s_{k,j}$ is supported in $F_k$, and the sets $F_l$ are pairwise
disjoint and disjoint from $I_1$. Therefore,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:global-identities}
S^*\varphi_{k,j}=0,\quad
S^*s_{k,j}=S_k^*s_{k,j},\quad S^*v_*=0.
\end{equation}
Every vector from stage $k$ is either $s_{k,j}$ or
$\kappa_k^{-1}s_{k,j}+\varphi_{k,j}$. Since
$S_k^*\varphi_{k,j}=0$ as well, \eqref{eq:global-identities}
shows that each such vector $v_n$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive-functions}
f_n=(I+S^*)v_n=(I+S_k^*)v_n\ge0
\end{equation}
by the construction at stage $k$. This also proves the first
identity in \eqref{eq:final-basis}. Formula \eqref{eq:positive-functions} also shows that $f_n$
is a step function supported in $A_k\cup F_k$, because $v_n$
is such a function and $S_k^*v_n$ is a multiple of $\1_{A_k}$. The initial vector remains $f_1=\1_{I_1}$.
In particular, every $f_n$ has compact support despite the
infinite sum defining $S$.

The formula $\gamma_n=U^{-1}\xi_n$ preserves biorthogonality,
since
$$
\left\langleU^*v_n,U^{-1}\xi_l\right\rangle=\left\langlev_n,\xi_l\right\rangle=\delta_{nl}.
$$
For $f\in L^p(\mathbb R)$, \eqref{eq:final-basis} gives
$\left\langlef,\gamma_j\right\rangle=\left\langlef,U^{-1}\xi_j\right\rangle
=\left\langle(U^*)^{-1}f,\xi_j\right\rangle$.
Thus, for $p<\infty$, the partial sum projections are obtained
from $P_n$ in \eqref{eq:raw-projections} by
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n f_j\otimes\gamma_j
=U^*P_n(U^*)^{-1}.
$$
By \Cref{lem:raw}, these partial sums converge in $L^p$ to the
function being expanded. Biorthogonality gives uniqueness, and
the equalities $\left\lVertU^*\right\rVert=\left\lVertU\right\rVert$ and
$\left\lVert(U^*)^{-1}\right\rVert=\left\lVertU^{-1}\right\rVert$, together with
\eqref{eq:U-bounds}, give \eqref{eq:final-bound}.

When $p=\infty$, we use $R_n$ from \eqref{eq:raw-projections},
which acts on $L^1$. The partial sum projections for $(\gamma_n)$
are
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n\gamma_j\otimes f_j=U^{-1}R_nU.
$$
For every $g\in L^1$, \Cref{lem:raw} gives $R_nUg\to Ug$
in $L^1$. Applying the bounded operator $U^{-1}$ proves convergence
of these partial sums to $g$. Their norms satisfy the same bound,
and biorthogonality again gives uniqueness. This proves the
endpoint assertion.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:l1-extension}
Let the notation be as above. Appending the two non-negative functions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-pair}
u=e_C+e_J,\quad v=e_B+e_J,
\end{equation}
in this order, extends the old basis to a basis of
$E_{\mathcal P'}L^1$. On this enlarged space, let $R$ retain
the old vectors but discard $u,v$, and let $S$ retain the old
vectors and $u$ but discard $v$. Then we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-extension-bounds}
\left\lVertR\right\rVert=1,\quad \left\lVertS\right\rVert=1+2\theta.
\end{equation}
Moreover, every partial sum projection ending among the old vectors has
the same norm on the enlarged space as on $E_{\mathcal P}L^1$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The old span contains $e_D$. Since
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-span}
e_D=\theta e_B+(1-\theta)e_C,\quad
e_J=(1-\theta)u+\theta v-e_D,
\end{equation}
the enlarged list spans $e_J$ and hence also
$e_B=v-e_J$ and $e_C=u-e_J$. It therefore spans the whole
space $E_{\mathcal P'}L^1$. This space has two more dimensions
than the old space, so the enlarged list is a basis.

The functions $e_V$, $V\in\mathcal P'$, have disjoint supports
and norm one. Thus, for arbitrary real or complex coefficients, we have
$$
\left\|\sum_{V\in\mathcal P'}a_Ve_V\right\|_1
=\sum_{V\in\mathcal P'}|a_V|.
$$
It follows that the norm of any operator on this space is the
largest of the norms of its values on these functions. The map
$R$ fixes every unaffected $e_V$ and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-old-projection}
Re_B=e_D,\quad Re_C=e_D,\quad Re_J=-e_D.
\end{equation}
Indeed, these formulas fix $e_D$ and annihilate $u,v$.
Every image has norm one, so $\left\lVertR\right\rVert=1$.

The map $S$ also fixes the unaffected $e_V$. On the three
remaining functions, it is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-intermediate}
\begin{aligned}
Se_B&=\theta e_B-(1-\theta)e_J,\\
Se_C&=\theta e_B+e_C+\theta e_J,\\
Se_J&=-\theta e_B+(1-\theta)e_J.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These formulas imply that $Se_D=e_D$, $Su=u$, and $Sv=0$, as required.
The three images have norms $1$, $1+2\theta$, and $1$,
respectively. Hence, $\left\lVertS\right\rVert=1+2\theta$.

Finally, if $P$ is an old partial sum projection, its extension
to the enlarged space is $PR$. Since $R$ is contractive,
$\left\lVertPR\right\rVert\le\left\lVertP\right\rVert$. Restricting $PR$ to the old space
gives the reverse inequality. Thus, its norm is unchanged.

We enumerate the unit intervals as $(I_n)_{n\ge1}$, with
$I_1=[0,1)$, and start with $f_1=\1_{I_1}$. At each splitting,
we use the first unit interval in this enumeration that has not
yet been included. We organize the splittings into finite rounds.
At the beginning of a round, we list the intervals of the current
partition in increasing order of their left endpoints. We split
each listed interval once and append the pair from
\Cref{lem:l1-extension}. The two pieces of a split interval and
the newly added unit interval are first split in the next round.

We number the splittings consecutively and denote the intervals
at splitting $n$ by $D_n,B_n,C_n,J_n$. The basis vectors added
at this step are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l1-basis}
f_{2n}=e_{C_n}+e_{J_n},\quad
f_{2n+1}=e_{B_n}+e_{J_n}.
\end{equation}
By \Cref{lem:l1-extension}, the first $2n+1$ vectors form a
non-negative basis of the full step-function space for the
partition after $n$ splittings. When a partial sum ending after
a complete pair is first formed, it is the identity on that
space and has norm one. A partial sum ending between the two
functions of a pair has norm $1+2\theta$. Every later extension
preserves these norms, by the last assertion of the lemma.
Consequently, \eqref{eq:l1-exact-projections} holds on every
finite span large enough to contain the corresponding pair.

It remains to prove that the finite spans have dense union.
Every unit interval is eventually included. At each subsequent
round, every interval of its partition is replaced by two
intervals, each of length at most $1-\theta$ times the old
length. The longest interval of the partition on each fixed
$I_n$ therefore has length tending to zero. As in
\Cref{ss:positive-completeness}, uniform continuity gives
approximation of continuous compactly supported functions by
these step-function spaces. Their union is consequently dense
in $L^1(\mathbb R)$.

The partial sum maps agree on the smaller spans and are uniformly
bounded by $1+2\theta$. They therefore extend to bounded operators
$P_m$ on $L^1$. To see that $P_mh\to h$, choose a finite linear
combination $h_0$ of the constructed vectors. Once $m$ includes
its last index, we have
$$
\left\lVerth-P_mh\right\rVert_1
\le(2+2\theta)\left\lVerth-h_0\right\rVert_1.
$$
Density proves convergence. The differences $P_m-P_{m-1}$
give bounded coefficient functionals and hence uniqueness of
the expansions. Thus, the sequence is a Schauder basis.

The operator norms on the dense union are preserved under
extension to $L^1$, so the equalities in
\eqref{eq:l1-exact-projections} continue to hold. In particular,
the lower bound for $P_{2n}$ is attained on $e_{C_n}$ by
\eqref{eq:l1-intermediate}. All the functions are real and
non-negative, and the norm calculations apply to real and
complex coefficients alike.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:lower}
Let $(f_n)$ be a Schauder basis of $L^p(\mathbb R)$ with coefficient
functions $(\gamma_n)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $1<p<\infty$ and every $f_n$ is non-negative, then
$f_1\otimes \gamma_1$ is not contractive.
\item If $1\le p<\infty$ and every $\gamma_n$ is non-negative, then
$f_1\otimes \gamma_1$ is not contractive.
\end{enumerate}
In either case, $K((f_n))>1$.

<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
To prove (i), suppose that the first coordinate projection is
contractive. Its norm must then equal one, so
$\left\lVertf_1\right\rVert_p\left\lVert\gamma_1\right\rVert_q=1$.
We set $h=\max\{\operatorname{Re}\gamma_1,0\}$.
Since $f_1\ge0$, H\"older's inequality gives
$$
1=\operatorname{Re}\int f_1\gamma_1
\le\int f_1h\le\left\lVertf_1\right\rVert_p\left\lVerth\right\rVert_q
\le\left\lVertf_1\right\rVert_p\left\lVert\gamma_1\right\rVert_q=1.
$$
Since $0\le h\le|\gamma_1|$ and $q<\infty$, equality of their norms
forces $h=|\gamma_1|$ almost everywhere. Consequently, $\gamma_1$ is real
and non-negative, even when the scalar field is complex.

The set $B=\{\gamma_1>0\}$ has positive measure. For $n\ge2$,
non-negativity and biorthogonality give $\int f_n\gamma_1=0$, so
$f_n=0$ almost everywhere on $B$. Restricting the basis expansion
of a function $f\in L^p$ supported in $B$ therefore gives
$$
f=\left\langlef,\gamma_1\right\rangle f_1\1_B.
$$
This would make $L^p(B)$ at most one-dimensional, whereas $B$
contains two disjoint subsets of positive finite measure whose
indicators are linearly independent. This contradiction proves (i).

For (ii) with $p>1$, duality gives a non-negative Schauder basis
$(\gamma_n)$ of $L^q(\mathbb R)$ with coefficient functions $(f_n)$.
Part (i) gives $\left\lVert\gamma_1\otimes f_1\right\rVert>1$. This operator is the
adjoint of $f_1\otimes \gamma_1$, so they have the same norm.

For $p=1$, we argue directly. If the first projection were
contractive, we would have
$\left\lVertf_1\right\rVert_1\left\lVert\gamma_1\right\rVert_\infty=1$.

we obtain
$$
1=\operatorname{Re}\int f_1\gamma_1
\le\int h\gamma_1\le\left\lVerth\right\rVert_1\left\lVert\gamma_1\right\rVert_\infty
\le\left\lVertf_1\right\rVert_1\left\lVert\gamma_1\right\rVert_\infty=1.
$$
As in (i), equality forces $f_1$ to be real and non-negative.
On the set $B=\{f_1>0\}$, which has positive measure, every
$\gamma_n$ with $n\ge2$ vanishes by non-negativity and biorthogonality.

its first basis coefficient equal to zero and would satisfy
$f=\left\langlef,\gamma_1\right\rangle f_1$. Choosing two disjoint subsets of $B$ of positive finite measure
gives two linearly independent indicator functions. They cannot
both be multiples of $f_1$, which proves (ii).

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{tr:lem:haar}
Let $p\ge2$. The normalized dyadic Haar basis of $L^p(0,1)$,
ordered with the constant first and every parent before its
children, has contractive initial projections and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{tr:eq:haar-lower}
\left(\sum_j|t_j|^p\right)^{1/p}
\le\left\lVert\sum_jt_jh_j\right\rVert_p.
\end{equation}
The same assertions hold for the Haar basis of the mean-zero
subspace, with the constant omitted. They are preserved by
isometries, by disjoint $L^p$ sums, and by interleavings that
preserve the order within each summand.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
A finite initial Haar segment consists of the constant and the
differences obtained by successively splitting atoms of a finite
dyadic partition. Its projection is conditional expectation
onto that partition, hence is contractive. On the mean-zero
subspace the constant coefficient is zero, so the same statement
holds after omitting the constant.

For complex numbers $a,b$, and therefore also for real numbers,
\begin{equation}\label{tr:eq:clarkson}
\frac{|a+b|^p+|a-b|^p}{2}
\ge (|a|^2+|b|^2)^{p/2}
\ge |a|^p+|b|^p.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the average of $|a+b|^2$ and $|a-b|^2$ is
$|a|^2+|b|^2$. The first inequality follows from the convexity of
$u\mapsto u^{p/2}$, and the second from $p/2\ge1$. Just before a Haar function is added, the preceding partial sum is constant on its support. The new Haar function takes opposite
values of equal modulus on the two halves of that support.
Integrating~\eqref{tr:eq:clarkson} there, and using its
normalization, shows that adding $t_jh_j$ increases the $p$th
powerr of the norm by at least $|t_j|^p$.
Induction proves~\eqref{tr:eq:haar-lower} for finite sums.
The infinite version follows from convergence of the partial sums.

An initial projection in an order-preserving interleaving is the
direct sum of initial projections on its summands, some possibly
zero. Its norm is at most one. Summing
\eqref{tr:eq:haar-lower} to the $p$-th power over the disjoint
summands gives the remaining assertion.

Use the construction in \Cref{sec:assembly} with fixed
$\delta,\eta,\rho$. We first estimate its uncorrected vectors
$(v_n)$. Let $E$ average on the integer unit intervals. For a
finite linear combination $F$ of these vectors, put $G=EF$ and
$H=F-G$. Write the contribution of each pair in \eqref{eq:pairs} to $F$
as $a_js_j+b_j(\kappa^{-1}s_j+\varphi_j)$.
Here we suppress the dependence of $\kappa$ on the stage. The coefficient on $s_j$ in
$G$ is $a_j+\kappa^{-1}b_j$, while
$H=\sum_jb_j\varphi_j$. The remaining columns and the initial
indicator contribute only to $G$.

For real or complex scalars $a,b$, there is $c_p>0$ such that
$$
|a+b|^p\ge |a|^p+p|a|^{p-2}\operatorname{Re}(\overline a b)
+c_p|b|^p.
$$
Indeed, the second derivative of $|a+tb|^p$ is at least
$p|a+tb|^{p-2}|b|^2$. If $b\ne0$, on a subset of $[0,1/2]$
of length at least $1/4$ we have $|a/b+t|\ge1/8$.
Taylor's formula therefore gives $c_p=p8^{1-p}$.
Apply this inequality in the actual order of the unequal
splittings, inserting zero coefficients for omitted differences.
Before each splitting, the preceding sum is constant on $D_j$,
and $\int_{D_j}\varphi_j=0$. The linear term integrates to zero,
so telescoping gives
$$
\sum_j|b_j|^p\left\lVert\varphi_j\right\rVert_p^p\le c_p^{-1}\left\lVertH\right\rVert_p^p.
$$

The auxiliary blocks occupy disjoint unit intervals.
By \Cref{lem:inverse} and $\left\lVerts_j\right\rVert_p\le M$, the weighted
$\ell_p$ norm of their combined coefficients in $G$, including
the initial coefficient, is at most $2M\left\lVertG\right\rVert_p$.
Moreover, the identities in  \eqref{eq:haar-identities} and the estimate following equation \eqref{eq:a} imply
$\kappa^{-1}\left\lVerts_j\right\rVert_p\le\eta\left\lVert\varphi_j\right\rVert_p$.
Since $s_j$ and $\varphi_j$ have disjoint supports, the triangle
inequality in the coefficient space gives, for $F=\sum_n t_nv_n$,
\begin{align*}
\left(\sum_n|t_n|^p\left\lVertv_n\right\rVert_p^p\right)^{1/p}
&\le 2M\left\lVertG\right\rVert_p
+(1+2\eta^p)^{1/p}c_p^{-1/p}\left\lVertH\right\rVert_p\\
&\le \bigl(2M+2(1+2\eta^p)^{1/p}c_p^{-1/p}\bigr)\left\lVertF\right\rVert_p.
\end{align*}
For the first inequality, the difference on a paired column has
weighted norm $|b_j|(2\kappa^{-p}\left\lVerts_j\right\rVert_p^p+
\left\lVert\varphi_j\right\rVert_p^p)^{1/p}$. The last inequality uses
$\left\lVertE\right\rVert\le1$ and $\left\lVertI-E\right\rVert\le2$.
Apply the bounded invertible operator $U^*$ from
\eqref{eq:final-basis} and normalize its images. The estimate
changes by at most the factor $\left\lVertU^*\right\rVert\left\lVert(U^*)^{-1}\right\rVert$.
These images are non-negative, and the initial indicator is fixed
by \eqref{eq:global-identities}. This proves the lemma.

We first record an estimate for real skew-symmetric matrices. If $J$
is skew-symmetric and $b$ is a real vector, write
$$
T(s)=\begin{pmatrix}J&sb\\-sb^T&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Then
\begin{equation}\label{h2:eq:arrowhead}
\left\lVertT(s)\right\rVert^2\le\left\lVertT(r)\right\rVert^2+(s^2-r^2)\left\lVertb\right\rVert^2
\quad(0\le r\le s).
\end{equation}
Indeed, put $J$ into real two-dimensional rotation blocks. Within
each block, rotate the coordinates so that the component of $b$ lies
on the first axis. A similar choice can be made in the kernel of $J$.
Grouping the first axes separately from the second axes and the new
coordinate gives
$$
T(s)=\begin{pmatrix}0&R_s\\-R_s^T&0\end{pmatrix},
\quad R_sR_s^T=D+s^2aa^T,
$$
where $D\ge0$ is fixed and $\left\lVerta\right\rVert=\left\lVertb\right\rVert$. Taking norms proves
\eqref{h2:eq:arrowhead}.

Let $E_P$ be the coordinate projection onto $P$, and set
$$
x=B^{1/2}e_j,\quad y=B^{-1/2}e_j,\quad
P_0=B^{1/2}E_PB^{-1/2},\quad P_1=P_0+xy^T.

R_t=\begin{pmatrix}
P_0+txy^T&sx\\ sy^T&1-t
\end{pmatrix},\quad s=\sqrt{t(1-t)},

$$
(B^{1/2}e_i,0)\quad(i\in P),\quad
(\sqrt t\,x,\sqrt{1-t}),
$$
and the biorthogonal vectors obtained by replacing $B^{1/2}$
with $B^{-1/2}$ and $x$ with $y$. Their Gram matrices are

and synthesis gives $\left\lVertR_t\right\rVert^2=F(t)$.

\begin{equation}\label{h2:eq:projection-skew}
\left\lVertR\right\rVert^2=1+\left\lVertR-R^*\right\rVert^2.

To see this, write $R=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I&Z\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$

the same argument starts with a zero last column. This proves

\label{h2:lem:copies}
Let $\mathcal H=V_0\oplus\bigoplus_iH_i$ be an orthogonal Hilbert
sum, with finitely many $H_i$. Suppose that each $H_i$ has an ordered
finite basis $(x_{ik})_k$ with basis constant at most $\sqrt Q$.

$$
\left\|\sum_iT_iT_i^*\right\|\le Z,\quad
\left\lVertT_iT_i^*\right\rVert\le\eta,\quad Q>1+Z.
$$
Concatenate the families $(x_{ik}+T_ix_{ik})_k$ after $V_0$.
Every coordinate projection retaining $V_0$ and an initial segment
of this list has squared norm at most
\begin{equation}\label{h2:eq:copies-bound}
Q+\frac{Q^2\eta}{Q-1-Z}.
\end{equation}
The same bound holds on the span of the concatenated families alone.

<!-- proof-25: body_md -->
At a partial sum ending inside family $i$, let $P$ be its centered
coordinate projection on $H_i$. The preceding centered spaces are
retained in full and split off as identity operators. On the remaining
spaces, the partial sum has the form
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}A&-U\\0&P\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here $A$ maps the old component and all later centered components
to $V_0$, and $U=T_i(I-P)$. Therefore
$$
\left\lVertA\right\rVert^2\le1+Z,\quad
\left\lVertU\right\rVert^2\le Q\eta,\quad \left\lVertP\right\rVert^2\le Q.
$$
For the bound on $U$, we used that a nontrivial projection on a
Hilbert space has the same norm as its complementary projection.
The endpoint cases $P=0,I$ satisfy the same bound directly.

For vectors $x,y$ in the two domain summands, these bounds give
\begin{align*}
\left\lVertM(x,y)\right\rVert^2
&\le\bigl(\sqrt{1+Z}\,\left\lVertx\right\rVert+\sqrt{Q\eta}\,\left\lVerty\right\rVert\bigr)^2
 +Q\left\lVerty\right\rVert^2\\
&\le Q\left\lVertx\right\rVert^2+
 \left(Q+\frac{Q^2\eta}{Q-1-Z}\right)\left\lVerty\right\rVert^2.
\end{align*}
The second inequality is Cauchy--Schwarz with weights $1+Z$ and
$Q-1-Z$, whose sum is $Q$. This proves \eqref{h2:eq:copies-bound}.
Finally, the span of the concatenated families is invariant under
these coordinate projections, so restriction to this span cannot
increase their norms.

simultaneous inversion and $e(-s)=\overline{e(s)}$, then the
weights may also be chosen invariant under simultaneous inversion.

<!-- proposition-9: body_md -->
\label{h2:prop:extension}
Let $w>0$ almost everywhere be even and integrable on $\mathbb R$, and let a finite
ordered list of distinct characters span $E$ with basis constant $K$
in $L^2(w)$. Let $f$ be a character outside $E$, and let $w_0$ be
a fixed probability density on $\mathbb R$. For every $\delta,\tau>0$,
there are an even positive trigonometric polynomial $0<W\le1$ on $\mathbb R$
and a finite appended list of distinct new characters such that,
with $w'=wW$ and $E'$ the enlarged span,
\begin{equation}\label{h2:eq:extension-conclusions}
\int_\mathbb R w_0(1-W)<\delta,\quad
\operatorname{dist}_{w'}(f,E')^2\le\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dist}_w(f,E)^2.
\end{equation}
The basis constant of the concatenated list in $L^2(w')$ is at most
 $\max\{K,K_*\}+\tau$. If the old frequencies and the frequency
of $f$ are rational, the new frequencies can also be rational.
Moreover, given $B>0$, they can have distance greater than $B$
from zero, from all old frequencies, and from each other.

<!-- proof-27: body_md -->
Take $w_\infty$ and $(\mu_n)$ from \Cref{h2:thm:weighted}, and define
$g\in L^2(\mathbb R)$ by $\widehat g=\sqrt{w_\infty}$. Multiplication
by $\sqrt{w_\infty}$ is unitary from $L^2(w_\infty)$ onto
$L^2(\mathbb R)$: its inverse is division by $\sqrt{w_\infty}$, which
is defined almost everywhere by \eqref{h2:eq:limit-weight}.
Consequently, $(e_{\mu_n}\sqrt{w_\infty})$ is a Schauder basis
of $L^2(\mathbb R)$ with the same basis constant. Inverse Fourier transform
sends its $n$-th vector to $g(\,\cdot+\mu_n)$. Taking
$\lambda_n=-\mu_n$ gives the required basis.

The weight $w_\infty$ is even and bounded by the even Gaussian
$w_0$. Hence $\sqrt{w_\infty}$ is real, even, and integrable, so
its inverse Fourier transform $g$ is real and even. It is nonzero,
since Plancherel's theorem and \eqref{h2:eq:limit-mass} give
$\left\lVertg\right\rVert_2^2=\int w_\infty>1/2$.

 For every $z\in\mathbb C$, the integral
$$
g(z)=\int_\mathbb R\sqrt{w_\infty(t)}e^{2\pi izt}\,dt
$$
converges absolutely and locally uniformly in $z$, as do the
integrals obtained by differentiating any number of times.
Thus $g$ extends to an entire function. For every integer $k\ge0$, the function $(2\pi it)^k\sqrt{w_\infty(t)}$ belongs to both $L^1$ and $L^2$. The Riemann--Lebesgue lemma and Plancherel's theorem therefore give $g^{(k)}\in C_0(\mathbb R)\cap L^2(\mathbb R)$.

All translates of $g$ are real. If a real function is expanded in this complex basis, conjugating its expansion and using uniqueness shows that every coefficient is real. Thus the same sequence is a basis over the real scalar field as well.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
The basis constant one cannot occur for a basis of translates in $L^2(\mathbb R)$.
Suppose a Schauder basis of a Hilbert space has basis constant one.
Every nonzero initial projection is contractive, and
\eqref{h2:eq:projection-skew} makes it orthogonal. Each later basis vector lies in the kernel of every earlier initial projection, so the basis is orthogonal. If the vectors are translates of one
function, they all have the same nonzero norm; after normalizing
the generator, they form an orthonormal basis.

Assume therefore that $(T_{\lambda_n}g)$ is an orthonormal basis of $L^2(\mathbb R)$ and $\left\lVertg\right\rVert_2=1$, where $T_\lambda g(x)=g(x-\lambda)$. Strong continuity of translations gives $\langle g,T_tg\rangle\to1$ as $t\to0$. Orthogonality therefore forces the parameters to be uniformly separated by some $d>0$. Choose a bounded interval $I$ of positive length less than $d$. The intervals $I-\lambda_n$ are pairwise disjoint up to null
sets, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{h2:eq:local-trace}
 \sum_n\left\lVert\mathbf1_I T_{\lambda_n}g\right\rVert_2^2
 =\sum_n\int_{I-\lambda_n}|g(x)|^2\,dx\le1.
\end{equation}
Choose two orthonormal functions $h_1,h_2$ supported in $I$.
Parseval’s identity for this basis of translates and Bessel's inequality
for this pair imply
$$
 2=\sum_n\sum_{j=1}^2
 |\langle h_j,T_{\lambda_n}g\rangle|^2
 \le\sum_n\left\lVert\mathbf1_I T_{\lambda_n}g\right\rVert_2^2\le1,
$$
a contradiction. The argument applies over either scalar field
and proves that the infimum one is not attained.
