<!-- head-1: body_md -->
Let $m\ge1$ be a rational number, $a,b,d\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $a,d>0$ and $\Delta:=ad-mb^2>0$, and $B,C\in\mathbb{Q}$. Put
\[
F(q)=\sum_{n,k\ge0}\frac{q^{\frac a2n^2+mbnk+\frac{md}2k^2+Bn+Ck}}{(q;q)_n(q^m;q^m)_k},
\]
with Nahm point $(x,y)\in(0,1)^2$ defined by $1-x=x^ay^b$, $1-y=x^{mb}y^d$, and $K=\mathbb{Q}(x,y)$.
In Mizuno's notation \cite{Miz} this is the Nahm sum of $(A,\mathbf b,D)$ with
$A=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\ mb&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, $\mathbf b=(B,C)$, symmetrizer $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,m)$,
so that $AD$ is our positive-definite matrix; his Nahm equation is ours. For non-integral $m$, $F$ is a series in
fractional powers of $q$.
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:norm}
Let $d_1,d_2\ge1$ be integers, $m=d_2/d_1$, $A=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\ mb&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ with
$A\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2)$ positive definite, $b_1,b_2,c\in\mathbb{Q}$, and let
\[
 f(q)=\sum_{n_1,n_2\ge0}\frac{q^{\frac12n^{\mathsf T}A\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2)n+b_1n_1+b_2n_2+c}}{(q^{d_1};q^{d_1})_{n_1}(q^{d_2};q^{d_2})_{n_2}}
\]
be the rank-two Nahm sum of \cite{Miz}. Then $f(q)=Q^{c/d_1}F(Q)$ with $Q=q^{d_1}$, where $F$ is the sum above with
this $m$ and $(B,C)=(b_1,b_2)/d_1$. The matrix $A$, and hence the Nahm point and the field $K$, are unchanged, and
$f$ is modular if and only if $Q^{c/d_1}F(Q)$ is. Exchanging the two summation indices replaces
$(a,b,d,m)$ by $(d,mb,a,\frac1m)$. Hence every rank-two symmetrizable Nahm sum with rational data is, up to the
substitution $\tau\mapsto d_1\tau$ and the order of the indices, a sum $q^cF$ with $m\ge1$ rational.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
With $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2)$, $\frac12n^{\mathsf T}ADn=d_1\bigl(\frac a2n_1^2+mbn_1n_2+\frac{md}2n_2^2\bigr)$ and
$(q^{d_2};q^{d_2})_{n_2}=(Q^m;Q^m)_{n_2}$. The substitution $\tau\mapsto d_1\tau$ preserves invariance under a subgroup
of finite index, because $\delta^{-1}\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)\delta$ and $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ are commensurable for
$\delta=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,1)$, and it preserves meromorphy at the cusps. The Nahm equations involve $A$ only.
For the exchange, the matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}d&mb\\ b&a\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ has lower-left entry
$\frac1m\cdot mb$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Determine the Newton polygons, edge polynomials and corners of $\Psi_2,\dots,\Psi_4$ (Section~\ref{sec:structure})
and of the first-coefficient polynomial $f_r$ (Section~\ref{sec:first}). Every edge of $f_r$ is a rank-one

$1$. A common factor of $f_r$ and $\Psi_2$ of full width would force both opposite effective exponents to equal
$1$, contradicting their strict inequality for $b\ne0$; so such a factor has bidegree at most $(2,2)$. The
decoupled case $b=0$ is treated separately.
\item Show that a common irreducible factor of $f_r,\Psi_2,\Psi_3,\Psi_4$ has no diagonal edges, using the

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:rk1}
Let $A>0$ and $\beta$ be rational. $N_2,N_3,N_4(A,\beta;\cdot)$ have a common root other than
$0,-1$ if and only if $(A,\beta)$ is one of Zagier's seven modular rank-one pairs; the extra common roots

$X^2-X-1$ for $(2,0),(2,1)$; $X^2+X-1$ for $(\frac12,0),(\frac12,\frac12)$; $X-1$ for $(1,0),(1,\pm\frac12)$.
Moreover $(1+X)^2$ divides all $N_j$ only when $A=1$; there $(1+X)^4$ divides all $N_j$, and for Euler

With $M_j=N_j/(X(1+X))$,

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:worder}
Let $b\ne0$ and let $\kappa_p\ne0$ be a root of $\widehat Q$ of multiplicity $r$. Near
$p=(X_{\kappa_p},Y_{\kappa_p})$ use the coordinates $s=\kappa-\kappa_p$ and $t$, where
\[
X=-a-\kappa b,\qquad Y=-d-\frac{mb}{\kappa}-\frac{t}{mb\kappa}
\]
(a local isomorphism near $p$, with Jacobian $1/(m\kappa)$). Then $\det\widetilde H=t$, and for every $j\ge2$
the polynomial $\Psi_j$ has weighted order at least $2rj$ for the weights $(\operatorname{wt}s,\operatorname{wt}t)=(1,2r)$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Direct computation gives $\det\widetilde H=t$ and
\[
\operatorname{adj}\widetilde H=-\frac b\kappa\,uu^{\mathsf T}-\frac{t}{b\kappa}\,e_1e_1^{\mathsf T},\qquad u=(m,\kappa)^{\mathsf T}.
\]
The cubic Taylor term of the potential is $-[X(1+X)\xi^3+m^2Y(1+Y)\eta^3]$, and on $u$ it equals
$V_3(u):=-[m^3X(1+X)+m^2\kappa^3Y(1+Y)]$, which is $-m^2\widehat Q(\kappa)$ at $t=0$.

By Wick's theorem $\ell_j$ is a sum over connected graphs $\Gamma$ whose vertices carry the terms of the formal
integral \cite[(15)--(18)]{Miz}. A vertex $v$ of valence $r_v$ carries $\varepsilon^{(r_v-2)/2+\delta_v}$ with
$\delta_v\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$, and $\delta_v=0$ exactly for the Taylor terms of the potential, which have $r_v\ge3$.
Edges carry $\widetilde H^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}\widetilde H/t$. Comparing powers of $\varepsilon$ gives
$E=j+V-\sum_v\delta_v$ for the numbers $E$ of edges and $V$ of vertices.

Expand every edge into its $uu^{\mathsf T}$-part and its $e_1e_1^{\mathsf T}$-part (an \emph{$e$-edge}). A resulting
term of $\Psi_j=t^{3j}\ell_j$ with $e$ $e$-edges has the factor $t^{k_0}$, $k_0=3j-E+e$. Every potential cubic
vertex none of whose three edges is an $e$-edge contributes the factor $V_3(u)$. If $V_3$ denotes the number
of potential cubic vertices, there are at least $V_3-2e$ such vertices. Now
$2E-4j=\sum_v(4-r_v-4\delta_v)$, where each summand is at most $0$ except for potential cubic vertices, where
it is $1$. Hence $V_3\ge2E-4j$, and there are at least $2E-4j-2e=2j-2k_0$ factors $V_3(u)$.

In the coordinates $(s,t)$, $V_3(u)=-m^2\widehat Q(\kappa)+tR$ has weighted order $\ge r$, and all other
factors are analytic at $p$ because $\kappa_p\ne0$ and $b\ne0$. So each term has weighted order at least
$2rk_0+r\max(0,2j-2k_0)\ge2rj$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:edgediv}
Let $A\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$, $\beta\in\mathbb{Q}$, $\rho\in\mathbb{Q}$. The polynomial $N_1(A,\beta;Z)-\rho(A+Z)^3$ is nonzero, and it divides
$N_2(A,\beta;Z)$ only if $A=1$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Write $H=N_1-\rho(A+Z)^3$ and $N_2=Z(1+Z)M_2$ with $M_2$ cubic. $H$ has degree $3$ unless $\rho=\frac1{24}$, and
$H(0)=0$ iff $\rho=\beta^2/(2A)$; if both hold then $A=12\beta^2$. For $A\ne1$ we treat four cases, each an
elimination in $(A,\beta)$ (\texttt{fr\_divides2.py}, \texttt{fr\_divides3.py}, \texttt{fr\_divides4.py}).
(i) $\deg H=3$, $H(0)\ne0$: then $H\mid(1+Z)M_2$, so either $H=cM_2$, or $H(-1)=0$ and $H/(1+Z)$ divides $M_2$.
In the first case the $4\times3$ matrix of the coefficients of $(A+Z)^3$, $M_2$, $N_1$ has rank $\le2$; its
$3\times3$ minors have the common factor $A^2(A-1)^2$, and the resultant in $\beta$ of two of them, divided by it,
has rational roots only at $A\in\{0,1\}$. In the second case
$\rho=N_1(A,\beta;-1)/(A-1)^3$, and the remainder of $M_2$ modulo $H/(1+Z)$ gives two equations whose resultant
in $\beta$ has positive rational roots only at $A=1$ and $A=\frac23$; at $A=\frac23$ the unique solution
$\beta=\frac23$ has $\rho=\frac1{24}$, outside this case. (ii) $\rho=\frac1{24}$: $H$ is a quadratic, whose leading
coefficient vanishes only for $A=1-2\beta\pm\frac{\sqrt3}3$, and the two coefficients of the remainder of
$Z(1+Z)M_2$ modulo $H$ have a resultant with rational roots only at $A\in\{0,1\}$. (iii) $\rho=\beta^2/(2A)$,
$\rho\ne\frac1{24}$: $H=Zq$ with $q$ quadratic, and the same elimination for $q\mid(1+Z)M_2$ gives positive
rational roots $A\in\{1,3,\frac43,\frac13,\frac34,12\}$, of which all but $A=1$ lie on $A=12\beta^2$, where the
leading coefficient of $q$ vanishes and the resulting linear polynomial does not divide $N_2$. (iv) $A=12\beta^2$
is contained in case (ii). Finally $H\not\equiv0$: for $A\ne1$, $H(-A)=N_1(A,\beta;-A)=\frac5{24}A^2(A-1)^2\ne0$, and for $A=1$,
$H=(1+Z)^2\bigl[(\frac1{24}-\rho)Z+\frac{\beta^2}2-\rho\bigr]$, which vanishes identically only if $12\beta^2=1$.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:rig}
Let $u,w\in\mathbb{Q}$ and $\gamma,s\in\mathbb{C}^*$ satisfy $B_n(u)=\gamma s^{n-1}B_n(w)$ for $n=2,3,4,5$ (a relation in which
both sides vanish is allowed). Then $s^2\in\{1,\frac74,\frac47\}$. In particular these relations are impossible
for $s\in\{\pm m,\pm\frac1m\}$ with $m\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$, $m\ne1$, since $\frac74$ and $\frac47$ are not squares in $\mathbb{Q}$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
$B_2$ and $B_4$ have no rational roots, and the rational roots of $B_3=u(u-\frac12)(u-1)$ and
$B_5=B_3\cdot(u^2-u-\frac13)$ are $0,\frac12,1$ \cite{Ink}. If $B_3(u)=0$ then $B_3(w)=0$, so $u,w\in\{0,\frac12,1\}$, and
the relations for $n=2,4$ give $s^2=\frac{B_4(u)/B_2(u)}{B_4(w)/B_2(w)}$ with $B_4/B_2=-\frac15$ at $0,1$ and $-\frac7{20}$
at $\frac12$; so $s^2\in\{1,\frac74,\frac47\}$.

Otherwise put $x=2u-1$, $y=2w-1$, $U=x^2$, $V=y^2$. Then
\[
B_2(u)=\tfrac{3U-1}{12},\quad B_3(u)=\tfrac{x(U-1)}8,\quad B_4(u)=\tfrac{15U^2-30U+7}{240},\quad B_5(u)=\tfrac{x(U-1)(3U-7)}{96},
\]
and similarly for $w$. Since $B_3(u)\ne0$ and $x$ is rational, $U,V\notin\{0,1,\frac13,\frac73\}$, so all eight values
are nonzero. Taking ratios of the relations,
\[
s^2=\frac{3U-7}{3V-7},\qquad \frac{B_4(u)}{B_2(u)}=s^2\frac{B_4(w)}{B_2(w)},\qquad
\Bigl(\frac{B_3(u)B_2(w)}{B_2(u)B_3(w)}\Bigr)^2=s^2 .
\]
Substituting the first into the other two and clearing denominators gives $(U-V)g=0$ and $(U-V)h=0$ with
\[
g=15UV-7U-7V+7,\qquad h=27U^2V^2-18U^2V+7U^2-18UV^2+16UV-14U+7V^2-14V+7.
\]
If $U\ne V$ then $g=0$; as $15U-7=0$ is incompatible with $g=0$, $V=7(U-1)/(15U-7)$, and then
$h=112U(U-1)(3U-7)(3U-1)/(15U-7)^2\ne0$. Hence $U=V$, and $s^2=1$.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:nodiag}
Let $m\ne1$ and let $\Gamma$ be an irreducible common factor of $f_r,\Psi_2,\Psi_3,\Psi_4$, with $f_r\not\equiv0$. Then
$\operatorname{Newt}\Gamma$ has no edge in a diagonal direction.

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->

with a diagonal inner normal $\nu$ only if $\operatorname{Newt} f_r$ has one, that is, only if the corresponding corner of $f_r$
vanishes. The face of $f_r$ in direction $\nu$ is then the segment joining the two adjacent coefficients, both
nonzero by \eqref{eq:B2}. Write $\Psi_1:=f_r$. For $j=1,\dots,4$, $\operatorname{in}_\nu\Gamma=\alpha X^\cdot Y^\cdot+\beta X^\cdot Y^\cdot$

left), $(x_1,x_8,-m)$ (lower right), $(x_3,x_6,-\frac1m)$ (upper left); the signs come from $(-1)^{j+1}$ at far
corners and the powers of $m$ from the edge prefactors. If exactly one corner vanishes, the face is a vertex
and $\Gamma$ cannot have the edge. Both corners can vanish only for $j=2,4$, where the relation holds trivially.
Lemma~\ref{lem:rig} with $s=\pm m^{\pm1}$ gives a contradiction.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:poles}
If $\ell_1$ is regular at $\tau=0$ and $\ell_2=\ell_3=0$, then $\operatorname{ord}\ell_4\ge-6e$.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
A linear change over $\mathbb{C}[[\tau]]$ makes one quadratic direction a unit; the formal Morse lemma in that direction (only
division by a unit is needed) and Gaussian integration in it leave a one-variable expansion with regular jets and
quadratic coefficient $h$ of order $e$. After scaling by $\sqrt\varepsilon$ the phase vertices are
$a_k(\tau)\varepsilon^{(k-2)/2}z^k$ ($k\ge3$, $a_k$ regular), with $\mathbb{E}_hz^{2n}=(2n-1)!!\,h^{-n}$, and the amplitude
terms are $b_{q,k}\varepsilon^{q+k/2}z^k$ ($q,k\ge0$, not both $0$), each contributing a pole of at most $e$ per unit
of $\varepsilon$. A phase vertex of degree $k$ contributes at most $M_k=(ke-2\operatorname{ord} a_k)/(k-2)$ per unit of $\varepsilon$,
and $M_k\le\frac32e$ for $k\ge6$. With $M=\max\{\frac32e,M_3,M_4,M_5\}$, pairing Gaussian half-edges shows that every
contribution to $\ell_j$ has valuation at least $-jM$, and taking the logarithm preserves this.
Suppose $M>\frac32e$. After a finite ramified extension, at valuation exactly $-jM$ only the vertices of degree $3,4,5$
attaining $M$ contribute, with leading coefficients $u,v,w$ not all zero, and the leading model is exactly
$\mathbb{E}\exp(usZ^3+vs^2Z^4+ws^3Z^5)$. Since $M,2M,3M>0$, regularity of $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ forces the first three
coefficients of its logarithm, hence $c_1,c_2,c_3$, to vanish, contradicting Lemma~\ref{lem:moments}. So
$M\le\frac32e$ and $\operatorname{ord}\ell_4\ge-4M\ge-6e$.

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:euler}

<!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{lem:ideal}

<!-- lemma-14: body_md -->
\label{lem:tr1}
Let $t\in(0,1)$ be algebraic with $1-t=t^\eta$ for some $\eta\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$, and suppose that every conjugate of $t$ is
real. Then $(\eta,t)$ is $(1,\frac12)$, $(2,\frac{\sqrt5-1}2)$ or $(\frac12,\frac{3-\sqrt5}2)$; in particular
$[\mathbb{Q}(t):\mathbb{Q}]\le2$.
<!-- end lemma-14 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
Write $\eta=p/q$ in lowest terms. Every conjugate of $t$ is a root of $P(T)=T^p-(1-T)^q$, which has exactly one root
in $(0,1)$. A root $T=-v<0$ requires $p$ even and $q$ odd, and then $v^p=(1+v)^q$: there is no solution if $p\le q$,
and exactly one if $p>q$, since $v^p/(1+v)^q$ increases strictly from $0$ to $\infty$. By the symmetry
$T\mapsto1-T$, $(p,q)\mapsto(q,p)$, a root $T>1$ requires $q$ even and $q>p$, and then there is exactly one. The two
outside cases exclude each other, so $P$ has at most two distinct real roots and $[\mathbb{Q}(t):\mathbb{Q}]\le2$. If $p=q$ then
$p=q=1$ and $t=\frac12$. Otherwise $P$ has leading coefficient $\pm1$ and $|P(0)|=|P(1)|=1$, so $t$ and $1-t$ are
units, which excludes $t\in\mathbb{Q}$. For $[\mathbb{Q}(t):\mathbb{Q}]=2$ the minimal polynomial $T^2-uT+n$ has $n=\pm1$ and
$1-u+n=\pm1$; the only cases with a root in $(0,1)$ and real roots are $T^2+T-1$ and $T^2-3T+1$, with
$\eta=2$ and $\eta=\frac12$ respectively, since $\eta$ is determined by $t$.
