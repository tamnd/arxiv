\label{prop:density_agnostic} The union $\bigcup_{q\ge 1}\mathcal{F}_{q}$
		is dense in the Hilbert tensor product $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}}\otimes
		\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}\subset L^{2}
		(\mathcal{T}\times\Omega\times\mathbf{Z})$.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Define the algebraic tensor product of these three spaces, denoted as 
\[
\mathcal{V} = \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}} \otimes_{\text{alg}} \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}} \otimes_{\text{alg}} \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}.
\]
The elements of the algebraic tensor product $\mathcal{V}$ are strictly defined as formal finite linear combinations. That is, any element $f \in \mathcal{V}$ can be expressed as
\[
    f(t, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \sum_{r=1}^{q} \tau^{(r)}(t) \, \phi^{(r)}(\mathbf{x}) \, \psi^{(r)}(\mathbf{z}),
\]
where $q \in \mathbb{N}$ is a finite positive integer, and $\tau^{(r)} \in \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}}$, $\phi^{(r)} \in \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}}$, $\psi^{(r)} \in \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}$. 

By definition, the function class $\mathcal{F}_q$ of separable functions of rank $q$ precisely describes the set of functions formed by the addition of at most $q$ pure tensors. Therefore, taking the union over all possible finite ranks $q \ge 1$ exactly yields the entire algebraic tensor product space $\bigcup_{q \ge 1} \mathcal{F}_q = \mathcal{V}.$

For two tensors $u_1 \otimes v_1 \otimes w_1$ and $u_2 \otimes v_2 \otimes w_2$, their inner product is defined as the product of the inner products within their respective spaces
\[
    \langle u_1 \otimes v_1 \otimes w_1, u_2 \otimes v_2 \otimes w_2 \rangle = \langle u_1, u_2 \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}}} \langle v_1, v_2 \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}}} \langle w_1, w_2 \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}}.
\]
Since all three spaces are subspaces of $L^2$ spaces, the aforementioned inner product is equivalent to the standard $L^2$ inner product under the product measure $\mu_t \otimes \mu_x \otimes \mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Z}}$. 

Extending this inner product bilinearly to the entire algebraic tensor product $\mathcal{V}$ induces a norm $\|\cdot\|_{L^2(\mathcal{T} \times \Omega \times {\mathbf{Z}})}$. We denote the Hilbert tensor product as $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}$. By definition, it is the completion (or closure) of the algebraic tensor product $\mathcal{V}$ with respect to this induced $L^2$ norm
\[
    \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}} := \overline{\mathcal{V}}^{\|\cdot\|_{L^2}}.
\]

According to the fundamental principles of topology and metric spaces, the closure of a set is the smallest closed set containing it. Furthermore, a subset $\mathcal{V}$ is dense in a space $\mathcal{H}$ if and only if the closure of $\mathcal{V}$ is exactly $\mathcal{H}$. 

Given that the Hilbert tensor product is intrinsically the closure of the algebraic tensor product, we have
\[
    \overline{\bigcup_{q \ge 1} \mathcal{F}_q}^{\|\cdot\|_{L^2}} = \overline{\mathcal{V}}^{\|\cdot\|_{L^2}} = \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}.
\]
This establishes that for any arbitrary function $f$ in the Hilbert tensor product space and any given error tolerance $\epsilon > 0$, there necessarily exists a sufficiently large finite integer $q$ and an approximant $\tilde{f} = \sum_{r=1}^{q}\phi^{(r)}\tau^{(r)}\psi^{(r)} \in \mathcal{F}_q$ such that:
\[
    \|f - \tilde{f}\|_{L^2(\mathcal{T} \times \Omega \times {\mathbf{Z}})} < \epsilon.
\]
Consequently, the union of finite-rank separable functions, $\bigcup_{q \ge 1} \mathcal{F}_q$, is dense in the Hilbert tensor product space $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}$.

Finally, under the mild density conditions stated in \Cref{sec:theory}, the closures $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}}$, $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}}$, $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}$ coincide with the full $L^{2}$ spaces, so the Hilbert tensor product satisfies
\[
    \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{time}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{space}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{stoc}}
    = L^{2}(\mathcal{T}) \otimes L^{2}(\Omega) \otimes L^{2}(\mathbf{Z})
    \cong L^{2}(\mathcal{T} \times \Omega \times \mathbf{Z}),
\]
where the final isomorphism is the standard one for $\sigma$-finite product measures (a consequence of Fubini's theorem). Combining this identification with the density just established yields
\[
    \overline{\bigcup_{q \ge 1} \mathcal{F}_q}^{\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}} = L^{2}(\mathcal{T} \times \Omega \times \mathbf{Z}).
\]

\[
    h = h_{\parallel} + h_{\perp},
\]
where $h_{\parallel} = \mathsf{P}_{V_p}h \in V_p$ is the orthogonal projection of $h$ onto the subspace $V_p$, and $h_{\perp} \in V_p^{\perp}$ is the residual component such that $\langle h_{\perp}, v \rangle_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})} = 0$ for all $v \in V_p$. Given that $V_p = \mathrm{span}\{\Psi_{g}\}_{g=1}^{G}$, the projection $h_{\parallel}$ can be expressed as a linear combination of the basis functions
\[
    h_{\parallel} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \alpha_{g} \Psi_{g},
\]
where $\alpha_{g} \in \mathbb{R}$ denote the expansion coefficients.

{Part I: Proof of the First Equality.}
In the $L^2(\mathbf{Z})$ space, the inner product is equivalent to the expectation under the probability measure $\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{Z}}$, i.e., $\langle u, v \rangle_{L^2} = \mathbb{E}[uv]$. Taking the inner product of $h$ with the $g'$-th basis function $\Psi_{g'}$, we have
\[
    \mathbb{E}[\,h\,\Psi_{g'}\,] = \langle h, \Psi_{g'} \rangle_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})}.
\]
Substituting the orthogonal decomposition $h = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \alpha_{g} \Psi_{g} + h_{\perp}$ into the expression above yields
\[
    \mathbb{E}[\,h\,\Psi_{g'}\,] = \left\langle \sum_{g=1}^{G} \alpha_{g} \Psi_{g} + h_{\perp}, \Psi_{g'} \right\rangle_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})}=\sum_{g=1}^{G} \alpha_{g} \langle \Psi_{g}, \Psi_{g'} \rangle_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})} + \langle h_{\perp}, \Psi_{g'} \rangle_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})}.
\]
                                                        
    
                                                                                                                                                                              
    
We now invoke the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Since $h_{\perp} \perp V_p$ and $\Psi_{g'} \in V_p$, it follows that $\langle h_{\perp}, \Psi_{g'} \rangle_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})} = 0$.
    \item The basis $\{\Psi_{g}\}$ is orthonormal in $L^2(\mathbf{Z})$, meaning $\langle \Psi_{g}, \Psi_{g'} \rangle_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})} = \delta_{g,g'}$, where $\delta_{g,g'}$ is the Kronecker delta.
\end{enumerate}
Substituting these properties, all terms in the summation vanish except for $g = g'$, leading to $\mathbb{E}[\,h\,\Psi_{g'}\,] = \alpha_{g'}$.
    
                                                 
    
This confirms that the projection coefficients are precisely the expectations (inner products) of $h$ with the basis functions. 

Next, we compute the squared $L^2$-norm of the projection $h_{\parallel} = \mathsf{P}_{V_p}h$,
\[
    \big\|\mathsf{P}_{V_p}h\big\|_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})}^{2} = \left\| \sum_{g=1}^{G} \alpha_{g} \Psi_{g} \right\|_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})}^{2}.
\]
Applying the orthonormality of $\{\Psi_{g}\}$ (a generalized form of the Pythagorean theorem or Parseval’s identity), we obtain
\[
    \big\|\mathsf{P}_{V_p}h\big\|_{L^2(\mathbf{Z})}^{2} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \alpha_{g}^{2}.

Substituting $\alpha_{g} = \mathbb{E}[\,h(\mathbf{Z})\,\Psi_{g}(\mathbf{Z})\,]$ into the equation above directly yields the first desired identity
\[

\]

\]
Substituting this back into the expression yields
\[
    \mathbb{E}[\widehat{m}_{g}] = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} m_{g} = \frac{1}{B} (B \cdot m_{g}) = m_{g} = \mathbb{E}[\,h\Psi_{g}\,].
\]
This confirms that $\widehat{m}_{g}$ is an unbiased estimator of $m_{g}$.


\textbf{Part II: Variance of the Estimator $\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{g}) = \mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g})/B$.}
We now consider the variance of $\widehat{m}_{g}$. Recall that for independent random variables $\{X_i\}$, $\mathrm{Var}(\sum X_i) = \sum \mathrm{Var}(X_i)$, and for a constant $C$, $\mathrm{Var}(CX) = C^2 \mathrm{Var}(X)$. Applying these properties, we have
\[
    \mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{g}) = \mathrm{Var}\left( \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}h(\mathbf{Z}_{b})\Psi_{g}(\mathbf{Z}_{b}) \right) = \frac{1}{B^2} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathrm{Var}\big(h(\mathbf{Z}_{b})\Psi_{g}(\mathbf{Z}_{b})\big).
\]
Because the samples $\mathbf{Z}_{b}$ are identically distributed, the variance is constant for all $b$,
\[
    \mathrm{Var}\big(h(\mathbf{Z}_{b})\Psi_{g}(\mathbf{Z}_{b})\big) = \mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g}).
\]
Substituting this back, we obtain
\[
    \mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{g}) = \frac{1}{B^2} \big(B \cdot \mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g})\big) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g})}{B}.
\]
This demonstrates that the variance of the estimator decays at the standard Monte Carlo rate of $O(1/B)$ relative to the batch size $B$.

\textbf{Part III: Expected Squared $L_2$ Norm $\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathbf{m}}\|_{2}^{2} = \|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2} + \frac{1}{B}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g})$.}
Using the definition of the squared $L_2$ norm and applying the linearity of expectation, we obtain
\[
    \mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathbf{m}}\|_{2}^{2} = \mathbb{E} \bigg[ \sum_{g=1}^{G} \widehat{m}_{g}^{2} \bigg] = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \mathbb{E}[\widehat{m}_{g}^{2}].
\]
Utilizing the fundamental relation $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 + \mathrm{Var}(X)$, we can decompose the second moment of each coefficient and get
\[
    \mathbb{E}[\widehat{m}_{g}^{2}] = (\mathbb{E}[\widehat{m}_{g}])^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{g}).
\]
Substituting the unbiasedness result ($\mathbb{E}[\widehat{m}_{g}] = m_{g}$) and the variance result ($\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{g}) = \frac{1}{B}\mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g})$) derived previously, we have
\[
    \mathbb{E}[\widehat{m}_{g}^{2}] = m_{g}^{2} + \frac{1}{B}\mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g}).
\]
Summing over all $g \in \{1, \dots, G\}$ gives
\[
    \mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathbf{m}}\|_{2}^{2} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} m_{g}^{2} + \frac{1}{B} \sum_{g=1}^{G} \mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g}).
\]
Recognizing that $\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} m_{g}^{2}$ is the squared norm of the true coefficient vector, we obtain
\[
    \mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathbf{m}}\|_{2}^{2} = \|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2} + \frac{1}{B}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\mathrm{Var}(h\Psi_{g}).
\]

		)$,

			\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\le g\le G}\big|\widehat{m}_{g}-\mathbb{E}[h\Psi

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Since the samples $\mathbf{Z}_b$ are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), the sequence of random variables $\{X_{g}(\mathbf{Z}_b)\}_{b=1}^B$ are mutually independent, each being a sub-Gaussian random variable with proxy variance $\sigma^2$. 

By the properties of sub-Gaussian random variables, the empirical mean $\widehat{m}_{g}$ of independent sub-Gaussian variables is also sub-Gaussian. Specifically, the proxy variance of the mean $\widehat{m}_g$ scales by a factor of $1/B$ relative to the single-sample proxy variance, given by $\sigma_{\text{mean}}^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{B}.$
    
                                                  
    
Applying the Hoeffding-type tail bound for centered sub-Gaussian random variables, the probability that the empirical mean deviates from the true mean $m_g$ by more than $\epsilon$ satisfies
\[
    \mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\widehat{m}_{g} - m_g\big| > \epsilon\Big) \le 2 \exp\left( - \frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma_{\text{mean}}^2} \right)=2 \exp\left( - \frac{B \epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2} \right).
\]
This inequality establishes an upper bound on the probability that the estimation error for a specific feature basis $g$ exceeds the margin $\epsilon$.

Then, we aim to bound the maximum deviation across all $g \in \{1, \dots, G\}$ by $\epsilon$. The event where the maximum deviation exceeds $\epsilon$ is mathematically equivalent to the event that at least one component $g$ has a deviation exceeding $\epsilon$.

Applying Boole's inequality (the union bound), i.e., $\mathbb{P}(\cup A_i) \le \sum \mathbb{P}(A_i)$, we can upper bound the total failure probability
\[
    \mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\le g\le G}\big|\widehat{m}_{g} - m_g\big| > \epsilon\Big) = \mathbb{P}\left( \bigcup_{g=1}^{G} \Big\{ \big|\widehat{m}_{g} - m_g\big| > \epsilon \Big\} \right)\le \sum_{g=1}^{G} \mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\widehat{m}_{g} - m_g\big| > \epsilon\Big).
\]
Because the failure probability bound is identical for each $g$, substituting and summing over the $G$ components gives
\[
    \mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\le g\le G}\big|\widehat{m}_{g} - m_g\big| > \epsilon\Big) \le \sum_{g=1}^{G} 2 \exp\left( - \frac{B \epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) = 2G \exp\left( - \frac{B \epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2} \right).
\]

To guarantee that the probability of the maximum error exceeding $\epsilon$ (the failure probability) is controlled below our predefined tolerance level $\delta$, we enforce the condition
\[
    2G \exp\left( - \frac{B \epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \le \delta.
\]
Dividing by $2G$ and solving for $B$, we obtain
\[
    B \ge \frac{2\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2} \log\left(\frac{2G}{\delta}\right).
\]
We can decompose the logarithmic term as $\log(2G/\delta) = \log(G/\delta) + \log(2)$. In an asymptotic sense (denoted by the Big-O or $\gtrsim$ notation), the absolute constant factor $2$ can be absorbed. Therefore, provided that the batch size $B$ satisfies:
\[
    B \gtrsim \frac{\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2} \log\left(\frac{G}{\delta}\right)
\]
(where $\gtrsim$ hides absolute constants, such as $2$ in this derivation), the probability that the maximum error exceeds $\epsilon$ is at most $\delta$. 

Taking the complement of this event (the success event) yields the final uniform bound
\[
    \mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\le g\le G}\big|\widehat{m}_{g} - \mathbb{E}[h\Psi_{g}]\big| \le \epsilon\Big) \ge 1 - \delta.
\]

\label{prop:cond_agnostic} If $\|\widehat{G}_{\Phi}-I\|_{2}\le \delta<1$,
		then the singular values of $F_{\mathrm{s}}/\sqrt{B}$ lie in
		$[\sqrt{1-\delta},\sqrt{1+\delta}]$ and
		$\kappa(F_{\mathrm{s}})\le \sqrt{\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}}$.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Define the matrix $A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}$. By the properties of the singular value decomposition (SVD), the squared singular values of $A$, denoted by $\sigma_j^2(A)$, coincide with the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix $A^\top A$, denoted by $\lambda_j(A^\top A)$. Observing the expansion of $A^\top A$,
\[
    A^\top A = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}\right)^\top \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}\right) = \frac{1}{B}F_{\mathrm{s}}^\top F_{\mathrm{s}}.
\]
According to the given definition, this is precisely the empirical Gram matrix $\widehat{G}_{\Phi}$. Consequently, we establish
\[
    \lambda_j(\widehat{G}_{\Phi}) = \sigma_j^2\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}\right).
\]

We are given the condition $\|\widehat{G}_{\Phi}-I\|_{2} \le \delta < 1$. For any real symmetric matrix $M$, its spectral norm (or 2-norm) is equal to the maximum absolute value of its eigenvalues, which implies
\[
    \|M\|_{2} = \max_j |\lambda_j(M)|.
\]
Substituting $M = \widehat{G}_{\Phi}-I$ yields that for all $j$,
$    |\lambda_j(\widehat{G}_{\Phi}-I)| \le \delta.
$
Since the identity matrix $I$ has all eigenvalues equal to 1 and shares the same eigenspace with $\widehat{G}_{\Phi}$, the eigenvalues of $\widehat{G}_{\Phi}-I$ can be expressed simply as $\lambda_j(\widehat{G}_{\Phi}) - 1$. Substituting this into the inequality gives
\[
    |\lambda_j(\widehat{G}_{\Phi}) - 1| \le \delta.
\]
Expanding the absolute value, we obtain the upper and lower bounds for all eigenvalues of the empirical Gram matrix
\[
    1 - \delta \le \lambda_j(\widehat{G}_{\Phi}) \le 1 + \delta,
\]
which means
\[
    1 - \delta \le \sigma_j^2\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}\right) \le 1 + \delta.
\]
Since singular values are inherently non-negative ($\sigma_j \ge 0$) and the condition $\delta < 1$ ensures that $1-\delta > 0$, we get
\[
    \sqrt{1 - \delta} \le \sigma_j\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}\right) \le \sqrt{1 + \delta}.
\]
This concludes the proof of the first part: all singular values of $F_{\mathrm{s}}/\sqrt{B}$ are bounded within the interval $[\sqrt{1-\delta}, \sqrt{1+\delta}]$.

The spectral condition number (2-norm condition number) of a matrix $X$ is defined as the ratio of its maximum singular value to its minimum singular value
\[
    \kappa(X) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(X)}{\sigma_{\min}(X)}.
\]
Importantly, scaling a matrix by a non-zero scalar does not alter its condition number, as both the maximum and minimum singular values are scaled by the exact same proportion. Thus,
\[
    \kappa(F_{\mathrm{s}}) = \kappa\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}\right).
\]
Based on the singular value bounds, we have:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Upper bound for the maximum singular value: $\sigma_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}\right) \le \sqrt{1 + \delta}$
    \item Lower bound for the minimum singular value: $\sigma_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}F_{\mathrm{s}}\right) \ge \sqrt{1 - \delta}$
\end{itemize}
Substituting these bounds into the definition of the condition number yields the desired upper bound
\[
    \kappa(F_{\mathrm{s}}) \le \frac{\sqrt{1 + \delta}}{\sqrt{1 - \delta}} = \sqrt{\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}}.
\]

\label{prop:gram_conc} Assume $\|\Phi(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\le L$ almost surely.
		Then for any $\delta\in(0,1)$, with probability $\ge 1-\delta$.
		\[
				\big\|\widehat{G}_{\Phi}-G_{\Phi}\big\|_{2} \lesssim L^{2}\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(M/\delta)}{B}}+\tfrac{\log(M/\delta)}{B}\Big) 
		\]
		In particular, if $G_{\Phi}=I$ (population-orthonormalized features), then
		$\widehat{G}_{\Phi}$ is near-identity with the above rate.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
{Let $\Phi_{b}:=\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ be the spatial feature vector at the $b$-th collocation point, and define the centered, scaled rank-one summands
\[
    X_{b} \;:=\; \tfrac{1}{B}\big(\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top} - G_{\Phi}\big), \qquad b=1,\dots,B.
\]
By construction $\widehat{G}_{\Phi}-G_{\Phi}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}X_{b}$ and $\mathbb{E}\,[X_{b}]=0$, so the deviation $\|\widehat{G}_{\Phi}-G_{\Phi}\|_{2}$ is a sum of $B$ independent, mean-zero, symmetric matrices. The result therefore follows from the matrix Bernstein inequality once we control (i) a uniform almost-sure norm bound $R:=\max_{b}\|X_{b}\|_{2}$ and (ii) a matrix-variance parameter $\sigma^{2}:=\big\|\sum_{b}\mathbb{E}[X_{b}^{2}]\big\|_{2}$. We compute these two ingredients next.}

By the triangle inequality for matrix norms, we obtain
\[
    \|X_{b}\|_{2} \le \frac{1}{B} \big( \|\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}\|_{2} + \|G_{\Phi}\|_{2} \big).
\]
We are given the condition $\|\Phi(\mathbf{x})\|_{2} \le L$ almost surely (a.s.). Since $\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}$ is a rank-one matrix, its unique non-zero eigenvalue (which coincides with its spectral norm) is given by its trace
\[
    \|\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}\|_{2} = \mathrm{Tr}(\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}) = \|\Phi_{b}\|_{2}^{2} \le L^{2}.
\]
By Jensen's inequality and the convexity of matrix norms, the spectral norm of the population covariance matrix is also bounded by
\[
    \|G_{\Phi}\|_{2} = \|\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}]\|_{2} \le \mathbb{E}\big[\|\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}\|_{2}\big] \le L^{2}.
\]
Substituting these two terms back yields a uniform upper bound on the norm
\[
    \|X_{b}\|_{2} \le \frac{1}{B} (L^{2} + L^{2}) = \frac{2L^{2}}{B} := R.
\]

Consider the squared term $X_{b}^{2} = \frac{1}{B^{2}}\big(\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top} - G_{\Phi}\big)^{2}$. In terms of the positive semi-definite (PSD) partial ordering ($\preceq$), the second moment of a centered random matrix is always bounded above by the second moment of the uncentered matrix (i.e., $\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)^2] \preceq \mathbb{E}[Y^2]$). Then, we have
\[
    \mathbb{E}[X_{b}^{2}] \preceq \frac{1}{B^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[(\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top})^{2}\big].
\]
Expanding the squared term, we have
\[
    (\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top})^{2} = (\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top})(\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}) = \Phi_{b}(\Phi_{b}^{\top}\Phi_{b})\Phi_{b}^{\top} = \|\Phi_{b}\|_{2}^{2} (\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}).
\]
Since $\|\Phi_{b}\|_{2}^{2} \le L^{2}$, it follows that
\[
    (\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top})^{2} \preceq L^{2}(\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}).
\]
Taking the expectation and summing over all $b=1,\dots,B$, we bound the total variance matrix as
\[
    \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{E}[X_{b}^{2}] \preceq \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{L^{2}}{B^{2}} \mathbb{E}[\Phi_{b}\Phi_{b}^{\top}] = \frac{L^{2}}{B} G_{\Phi}.
\]
Evaluating the spectral norm yields the variance parameter $\sigma^2$,
\[
    \sigma^{2} = \Big\|\sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{E}[X_{b}^{2}]\Big\|_{2} \le \frac{L^{2}}{B} \|G_{\Phi}\|_{2} \le \frac{L^{4}}{B}.
\]

The Matrix Bernstein inequality states that for any $t \ge 0$, we have
\[
    \mathbb{P}\left( \left\| \sum_{b=1}^{B} X_{b} \right\|_{2} \ge t \right) \le 2M \exp\left( - \frac{t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2} + Rt/3} \right).
\]
Setting the failure probability bound equal to the given confidence level $\delta$, we obtain
\[
    2M \exp\left( - \frac{t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2} + Rt/3} \right) = \delta \implies \frac{t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2} + Rt/3} = \log\left(\frac{2M}{\delta}\right).
\]
Solving this quadratic equation for $t$ yields the high-probability upper bound for $t$ given by
\[
    t \le \sqrt{2\sigma^{2}\log\left(\frac{2M}{\delta}\right)} + \frac{2}{3}R\log\left(\frac{2M}{\delta}\right).
\]

Substituting the bounds, specifically $\sigma^{2} \le \frac{L^{4}}{B}$ and $R = \frac{2L^{2}}{B}$, we obtain
\[
    t \le \sqrt{\frac{2L^{4}}{B}\log\left(\frac{2M}{\delta}\right)} + \frac{4L^{2}}{3B}\log\left(\frac{2M}{\delta}\right).
\]
Factoring out $L^{2}$ and subsuming the absolute constants (e.g., $\sqrt{2}$, $4/3$, and the constant offset in the logarithmic term, treating $\log(2M/\delta) \approx \log(M/\delta)$) into the asymptotic $\lesssim$ notation, we arrive at
\[
    t \lesssim L^{2} \left( \sqrt{\frac{\log(M/\delta)}{B}} + \frac{\log(M/\delta)}{B} \right).
\]
This implies that with probability at least $1 - \delta$, the deviation is bounded by
\[
    \|\widehat{G}_{\Phi} - G_{\Phi}\|_{2} \lesssim L^{2} \left( \sqrt{\frac{\log(M/\delta)}{B}} + \frac{\log(M/\delta)}{B} \right).
\]
Notably, when $G_{\Phi} = I$, this result immediately demonstrates that the empirical Gram matrix $\widehat{G}_{\Phi}$ approaches the identity matrix at the derived rate (satisfying the near-identity property), which asymptotically guarantees the approximate orthogonality of the features.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
\textit{Parameter count.} The dense parameterization stores every entry $C_{ikg}$ of the 3-way coupling tensor independently, so it uses $MM_{t}G$ scalar parameters, i.e.\ $O(MM_{t}G)$. The CP kernel instead stores three factor matrices of shapes $M{\times}q$, $M_{t}{\times}q$, $G{\times}q$ and a weight vector $w\in\mathbb{R}^{q}$, totalling $q(M+M_{t}+G)+q = O(q(M+M_{t}+G))$ scalar parameters.

Hence the CP kernel reduces both parameter count and per-batch arithmetic from multiplicative $O(MM_{t}G)$ to additive $O(q(M+M_{t}+G))$, an asymptotic improvement whenever $q\ll MM_{t}G/(M+M_{t}+G)$.

<!-- theorem-9: body_md -->
\label{prop:cp_gauge} For any invertible diagonal $\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
		,\Lambda_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}$, the mapping
		\[
			(A,B,D,w)\ \mapsto\ \big(A\Lambda_{1},\ B\Lambda_{2},\ D\Lambda_{3}\,
			\Lambda_{1}^{-1}\Lambda_{2}^{-1}\Lambda_{3}^{-1}w\big)
		\]
		leaves $u$ unchanged. Column-wise normalization of $(A,B,D)$ fixes a gauge
		(up to column permutations/signs) and improves Lipschitz conditioning of
		the forward map with respect to each factor.
<!-- end theorem-9 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $\Lambda_{1}, \Lambda_{2}, \Lambda_{3} \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times q}$ be invertible diagonal matrices. We define the transformed parameters as follows
\begin{align}
    \tilde{A} &= A\Lambda_{1}, \\
    \tilde{B} &= B\Lambda_{2}, \\
    \tilde{D} &= D\Lambda_{3}, \\
    \tilde{w} &= \Lambda_{1}^{-1}\Lambda_{2}^{-1}\Lambda_{3}^{-1}w.
\end{align}
The computation for the forward propagation is formulated as
\[
    \tilde{u} = \big((F_{\mathrm{s}}\tilde{A}) \odot (F_{\mathrm{t}}\tilde{B}) \odot (F_{\mathrm{z}}\tilde{D})\big) \tilde{w}.
\]

First, we consider the transformation of the feature projection matrices. Since $F_{\mathrm{s}}$ is post-multiplied by $\tilde{A}$ and $\Lambda_{1}$ is a diagonal matrix, this operation is equivalent to scaling the columns of $F_{\mathrm{s}}A$, which means
\[
    F_{\mathrm{s}}\tilde{A} = F_{\mathrm{s}}(A\Lambda_{1}) = (F_{\mathrm{s}}A)\Lambda_{1}.
\]
Similarly, we have
\begin{align}
    F_{\mathrm{t}}\tilde{B} &= (F_{\mathrm{t}}B)\Lambda_{2}, \\
    F_{\mathrm{z}}\tilde{D} &= (F_{\mathrm{z}}D)\Lambda_{3}.
\end{align}

Next, we examine the Hadamard product (denoted by $\odot$). For arbitrary matrices $X, Y$ and diagonal matrices $\Lambda_X, \Lambda_Y$, their Hadamard product satisfies
\[
    (X\Lambda_X) \odot (Y\Lambda_Y) = (X \odot Y)(\Lambda_X\Lambda_Y).
\]
This holds because post-multiplying by a diagonal matrix essentially scales the columns of the target matrix, and the scaling factors for the $r$-th column are directly multiplied together. Expanding the Hadamard product of the three transformed matrices, we obtain
\[
    \big((F_{\mathrm{s}}A)\Lambda_{1}\big) \odot \big((F_{\mathrm{t}}B)\Lambda_{2}\big) \odot \big((F_{\mathrm{z}}D)\Lambda_{3}\big) = \big((F_{\mathrm{s}}A) \odot (F_{\mathrm{t}}B) \odot (F_{\mathrm{z}}D)\big) (\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{3}).
\]

Finally, we multiply this result by the transformed weight vector $\tilde{w}$ and we get
\[
    \tilde{u} = \big((F_{\mathrm{s}}A) \odot (F_{\mathrm{t}}B) \odot (F_{\mathrm{z}}D)\big) (\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{3}) \big(\Lambda_{1}^{-1}\Lambda_{2}^{-1}\Lambda_{3}^{-1}w\big).
\]
Since multiplication between diagonal matrices is commutative, and $\Lambda_i \Lambda_i^{-1} = I$ (the identity matrix), all the diagonal matrices cancel out, yielding
\[
    (\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{3}) (\Lambda_{1}^{-1}\Lambda_{2}^{-1}\Lambda_{3}^{-1}) = I.
\]
Therefore, 
\[
    \tilde{u} = \big((F_{\mathrm{s}}A) \odot (F_{\mathrm{t}}B) \odot (F_{\mathrm{z}}D)\big) w = u.
\]
This concludes the proof that the forward propagation mapping is strictly gauge invariant under the aforementioned transformations.

As demonstrated in the preceding proof, for any non-zero constant $\lambda$, we can scale the $r$-th column of the factor matrix $A$ by $\lambda$ while simultaneously dividing the $r$-th element of the weight $w$ by $\lambda$ without altering the network output. This symmetry induces infinitely many equivalent global optima in the parameter space (i.e., continuous scaling valleys). 

To eliminate this continuous ambiguity, we apply column normalization to $A, B$, and $D$ (typically by constraining the $L_2$ norm of each column to 1). Enforcing the constraint $\|A_{:, r}\|_2 = 1$ strictly limits the permissible diagonal scaling factors to $|\lambda| = 1$. This implies that the diagonal entries of $\Lambda_{1}, \Lambda_{2}$, and $\Lambda_{3}$ are restricted to $\pm 1$. Consequently, the continuous scaling ambiguity collapses into discrete sign permutations. Combined with the inherent column permutation symmetry of the rank-1 components in CP decomposition, we successfully fix the gauge up to permutations and signs.

In neural network optimization, the smoothness of the loss function with respect to a specific parameter (e.g., matrix $A$) is governed by the Lipschitz constant of its partial derivative. In the multilinear CP format, the partial derivative of $u$ with respect to $A$ heavily depends on the magnitudes of $B, D$, and $w$. Roughly speaking, the gradient norm is proportional to
\[
    \left\| \frac{\partial u}{\partial A} \right\| \propto \|B\| \cdot \|D\| \cdot \|w\|.
\]

Without normalization constraints, the optimizer might drift into ill-conditioned regions where certain columns of $B$ and $D$ become pathologically large (e.g., $10^6$) while $w$ becomes vanishingly small (e.g., $10^{-12}$) to compensate. Although this leaves $u$ invariant, it causes the gradient with respect to $A$ to explode, driving the Lipschitz constant toward infinity and severely destabilizing the optimization process (Gradient Explosion). 

By enforcing column normalization on $A, B$, and $D$, their norms are rigidly bounded near 1. The network is thus compelled to absorb all global magnitude information into the unique linear weight vector $w$. This provides well-bounded control over the Jacobians of the factor matrices, significantly improving the Lipschitz conditioning of the forward mapping with respect to each factor. As a result, the optimization process (such as gradient descent) becomes substantially more stable and efficient.

        \item The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings. 
        \item It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals are not attained by the paper. 
    \end{itemize}
