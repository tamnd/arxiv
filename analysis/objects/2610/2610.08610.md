\label{thm:BCPZZ}
  Let $\rho, \varepsilon > 0$, and let $k, n$ be large enough positive integers with $\nicefrac{k}{n} \geq \rho$.
  Let $\mathbb{F}_q$ be any finite field of size $q=p^r$ for prime $p$ satisfying $q \geq n$ and $p > k$. Then, there is an algorithm $\mathcal{A}(\varepsilon)$ that on input a list $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n)$ of $n$ distinct elements of $\mathbb{F}_q$ and a received word $\mathbf{y} = (y_1,\ldots, y_n) \in \mathbb{F}_q^n$, outputs \emph{all} polynomials $f \in \mathbb{F}_q[X]$ of degree less than $k$ satisfying $\left| \operatorname{agr}(f,\mathbf{y}) \right| \geq k(1+\varepsilon)$ where 
  \[
  \operatorname{agr}(f, \mathbf{y}) := \left\{  i\in [n] \ \colon \ f(\alpha_i) = y_i \right\}
  \]
  in time $q^{C(\varepsilon, \rho)}$ where $C$ is some function of $\varepsilon$ and the rate $\nicefrac{k}{n}$\footnote{The original writeup of \cite{BrakensiekCPZZ2026} proved this result only for low rates and this was then extended to all rates in a subsequent revision using a reduction due to Alrabiah, Goyal and Guruswami. The proof presented in this exposition directly works for all rates and all sets of evaluation points and  avoids the use of the Alrabiah-Goyal-Guruswami reduction (we note the revised version of \cite{BrakensiekCPZZ2026} already mentions, without proof, that such a simplification is feasible).}.

\label{thm:Jeronimo}

  \end{quote}

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
A subset $V$ of $\subseteq \mathbb{F}^n$ is said to be an algebraic variety if there is a finite set of polynomials $f_1,\ldots, f_m \in \mathbb{F}[x_1,\ldots, x_n]$ such that 
\[
V = \mathbb{V}(f_1,\ldots, f_m) := \left\{  \mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{F}^n \ \colon \ f_i(\mathbf{a}) = 0 \text{ for all $i \in [m]$} \right\}. 
\]
A variety is said to be \emph{irreducible} if it cannot be written as non-trivial finite union of two varieties. In other words, if $V = V_1 \cup V_2$ for two algebraic varieties $V_1,V_2$, then either $V_1 = V$ or $V_2 = V$. 

The \emph{dimension of the variety $V$} (denoted by $\dim V$) is defined as the smallest $r$ such that a \emph{generic} affine subspace of co-dimension $r$ (or dimension, as standard vector space dimension, $n - r$) intersects $V$ at a finitely many points. The \emph{degree of the variety} (denoted by $\deg V$) is the finite number that results from such a generic intersection of a co-dimension $r$ affine subspace.
<!-- end definition-1 -->

  \end{align*}

  \begin{itemize}\itemsep 0pt
  \item $V$ is irreducible,
  \item $\dim V \leq d$,
  \item $\deg V \leq \Delta^d$. 
  \end{itemize}

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The set $\mathbb{A}^d \setminus U$ is irreducible, and hence its image under $\Psi$ (a continuous map) is also irreducible. Hence, the closure of the image is an irreducible algebraic variety. Since $\Psi$ maps $d$ coordinates into the space $\mathbb{A}^n$, we have $\dim V$ is at most $d$. 

  For the final point, let us assume for simplicity that $\dim V = d$ (otherwise, we have to work with a \emph{generic} $(\dim V)$-dimemsional subspace of $\mathbb{A}^d$ and repeat the argument). Let us take a generic affine subspace $L = \mathbb{V}(\ell_1, \cdots, \ell_d)$ of co-dimension $d$, where $\ell_i = a_{i0} + \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} y_j$, and intersect $V$ with it. For $i = 1,\ldots, d$, define
  \[
  P_i(x_1,\ldots, x_d) = c_{i0} G(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_{j=1}^n c_{ij} N_j(\mathbf{x})
  \]
  and $Z = \mathbb{V}(P_1,\ldots, P_d)$, and note $\deg P_i \leq \Delta$ for all $i$. Since $\dim V = d$ and the $\ell_i$'s were generic, turns out it can be argued that each constraint $P_i = 0$ reduces the dimension of the space by one, and hence $Z$ will be finite. \cref{thm:bezout} shows that $|Z| \leq \Delta^d$. Each point of $V \cap L$ is certainly in $\Psi(Z)$. Therefore, 
  \[
  \deg V = \left| V \cap L \right| \leq \left| Z \right| \leq \Delta^n\qedhere
  \]

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Consider the continuous relaxation $\lambda(W) = \left\{  (b_1,\ldots, b_d) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^d \ \colon \ \sum i b_i \leq W \right\}$. Under the transformation $a_i = i \cdot b_i$, then $(a_1,\ldots, a_d)$ is an element of the standard simplex $S(W) = \left\{  (a_1,\ldots, a_d) \ \colon \ \sum a_i \leq W \right\}$. Let us add a slack variable $a_0$ so that $a_0 + \cdots + a_d = W$ and consider the set $\tilde{S}(W) = \left\{  (a_0,\ldots, a_d) \ \colon \ \sum a_i = W \right\}$. 

Clearly, $\tilde{S}(W)$ is symmetric over all the coordinates and hence $\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{a} \in \tilde{S}(W)}[a_i] = \frac{W}{d+1}$ for any $i$. Since the transformation from $a_i$'s to $b_i$'s is a coordinate-wise scaling, we get that 
\[
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{b} \in \lambda(W)} [b_i] = \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{a} \in \tilde{S}(W)}\left[ \frac{a_i}{i} \right] = \frac{W}{i \cdot (d+1)}.
\]
Once again, we wish to relate the expectation over $\lambda(W)$ to the expectation over discrete set $\Lambda(W)$. As in \cref{lem:relating-discrete-continuous-simplex}, let us add a half-open cube $[0,1)^d$ to each point of $\Lambda(W)$. For the cube $(a_1,\cdots, a_d) + [0,1)^d$, the expectation of the $i$-th coordinate is just $a_i + \tfrac{1}{2}$. Hence, if $\Gamma = \mathop{\bigcup}_{\mathbf{b} \in \Lambda(W)} (\mathbf{b} + [0,1)^d)$, then 
$\lambda(W) \subseteq \Gamma \subseteq \lambda(W + d^2)$. Hence, 
\begin{align*}
\int_{\lambda(W)} x_i \, d x & \leq \int_{\Gamma} x_i \, d x \leq \int_{\lambda(W+d^2)} x_i \, d x\\
\text{i.e.,} \quad \operatorname{vol}(\lambda(W)) \cdot \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{b} \in \lambda(W)}[b_i] &\leq \operatorname{vol}(\Gamma) \cdot \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{b} \in \Gamma}[b_i] \leq \operatorname{vol}(\lambda(W + d^2)) \cdot \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{b} \in \lambda(W + d^2)}[b_i]\\
\implies \operatorname{vol}(\lambda(W)) \cdot \frac{W}{(d+1)i} & \leq \left( \sum_{\mathbf{b} \in \Lambda(W)} b_i \right) + \tfrac{1}{2}\left| \Lambda(W) \right| \leq \operatorname{vol}(\lambda(W + d^2)) \cdot \frac{(W+d^2)}{(d+1)i}.
\end{align*}
Dividing throughout by $\left| \Lambda(W) \right|$, we get
\begin{align*}
\frac{W}{(d+1)\cdot i} \cdot (1 - O(\tfrac{d^3}{W})) & \leq \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{b} \in \Lambda(W)}[b_i] + \frac{1}{2} \leq \frac{W + d^2}{(d+1)\cdot i} \cdot (1 + O(\tfrac{d^3}{W}))\\
\implies \operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{b} \in \Lambda(W)}[b_1 + \cdots + b_d] & = \frac{W}{(d+1)} \cdot \sum_{i=1}^d \frac{1}{i} \pm O_d(1) = \frac{W \cdot H_d}{(d+1)} \pm O_d(1)
\end{align*}
where $H_d  = \sum_{i=1}^d \frac{1}{i} = \ln d + \gamma + o(1)$.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
Let $P \in \mathbb{F}[Z, X, Y_0,\ldots, Y_d]$ be a nonzero polynomial with $\operatorname{TopVar}(P) = j \leq d$. Assume that $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F}) > \max(k, \deg_{Y_i}(P))$ for all $i$. There are rational functions $\Phi_{P,i}(Z, X, Y_0,\ldots, Y_d)$ defined  for $0 \leq i < k$ as 
  \[
  \Phi_{P,i}(Z, X, Y_0,\ldots, Y_d) = \begin{cases} Y_i & \text{if $0 \leq i \leq d$,}\\
    \frac{N_{P,i}(Z, X, Y_0,\ldots, Y_d)}{(\partial_{Y_j}P)^{2e}} & \text{if $d < i < k$},
  \end{cases}
  \]
with $\deg N_{P,i} \leq 2(i-j) \cdot \deg P$ and $e \leq 2(i - j)$ with the following property:
\begin{quote}
Suppose $\zeta \in \mathbb{F}$ is such that $\operatorname{TopVar}(P) = \operatorname{TopVar}(P_{Z=\zeta})$, $f(X)$ is a polynomial of degree less than $k$, and $\gamma \in \mathbb{F}$ with $a_i = f^{(i)}(\gamma)$ for $0 < i < k$ such that 
\begin{itemize}\itemsep 0pt
  \item $P(\zeta, X, f, f^{(1)}, \ldots, f^{(d)}) = 0$,
  \item $(\partial_{Y_j} P)(\zeta, \gamma, a_0,\ldots, a_d) \neq 0$.
\end{itemize}
Then, for all $0 \leq i < k$,  
\[
  a_i = \Phi_{P,i}(\zeta, \gamma, a_0,\ldots, a_d). 
\]
Consequently, $f(X) = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \Phi_{P,i}(a_0,\ldots, a_d) \cdot (X - \gamma)^i$. 
\end{quote}

<!-- observation-1: body_md -->
For any $h(Z, X, Y_0,Y_1,\dots, Y_d)$, we have 
\[
\frac{d}{dX}(h(Z, X, \Delta(f))) = (\mathcal{D}h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)). 
\]
More generally, 
\[
\frac{d^t}{dX^t}(h(Z, X, \Delta(f))) = (\mathcal{D}^t h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)). 
\]
<!-- end observation-1 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
By the chain rule, 
\begin{align*}
\frac{d}{dX}(h(Z, X, \Delta(f))) & = (\partial_X h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) + \sum_{i=0}^{d} (\partial_{Y_i} h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) \cdot \frac{d}{dX} f^{(i)}\\
& = (\partial_X h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) + \sum_{i=0}^{d} (\partial_{Y_i} h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) \cdot (i+1) f^{(i+1)}\\
& = (\partial_X h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) + \sum_{i=0}^{d-1} (\partial_{Y_i} h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) \cdot (i+1) f^{(i+1)}\\
& \quad \quad  + (\partial_{Y_d} h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) \cdot (d+1)f^{(d+1)}\\
& = (\partial_X h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) + \sum_{i=0}^{d-1} (\partial_{Y_i} h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) \cdot (i+1) f^{(i+1)}\\
& \quad \quad  + (\partial_{Y_d} h)(Z, X, \Delta(f)) \cdot (d+1) \cdot \Gamma_{P}(Z, X, \Delta(f))\\
& = (\mathcal{D}h) (Z, X, \Delta(f)). 
\end{align*}
The statement for higher derivatives follow by iterating the above $t$ times.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:degree-bounds-of-derivation}
For any $g,h \in \mathbb{F}(Z, X, Y_0,\ldots, Y_d)$, the following properties hold:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item $\mathcal{D}(g + h) = \mathcal{D}g + \mathcal{D}h$,
  \item $\mathcal{D}(gh) = h \mathcal{D}g + g\mathcal{D}h$,
  \item If $h \in \mathbb{F}[Z, X, Y_0, \ldots, Y_d]$ of degree at most $\Delta$, then $\mathcal{D}h$ is a rational function whose numerator has degree $\Delta + \deg P$ with denominator being a scalar multiple of $P'= \partial_{Y_d}(P)$. 
  \item More generally, for any $h \in \mathbb{F}[Z, X, Y_0,\ldots, Y_d]$, and any integer $e \geq 0$
  \[
  \mathcal{D}\left( \frac{h}{(P')^e} \right) = \frac{g}{(P')^{e+2}}
  \]
  for some polynomial $g \in \mathbb{F}[Z, X, Y_0,\ldots, Y_d]$ with $\deg g \leq \deg h + 2 \deg P'$. 
  \end{itemize}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
The first two points follow readily from the definition of $\mathcal{D}$. The degree bounds also hold since the only introduction of denominators is from the $\Gamma_P \cdot \partial_{Y_d}$ term in $\mathcal{D}$ and the denominator is just $(d+1) P'$. For the last point, 
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{D}\left( \frac{h}{(P')^e} \right) & = \frac{\mathcal{D}(h)}{(P')^e} - \frac{e \cdot h \cdot \mathcal{D}(P')}{(P')^{e+1}}\\
& = \frac{P' \cdot \tilde{h} + e \cdot h \cdot \tilde{P} }{(P')^{e+2}}\\
\text{where } \mathcal{D}(P') & = \frac{\tilde{P}}{P'} \text{ and }\mathcal{D}(h) = \frac{\tilde{h}}{P'}.
\end{align*}
Then $g = P' \cdot \tilde{h} + e \cdot h \cdot \tilde{P}$, which is a polynomial of degree at most $\deg(h) + 2 \deg(P')$.
