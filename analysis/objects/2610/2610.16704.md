<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:classical}
A functional regular of order at least 3 is classical with
respect to $X$ if it satisfies the structural equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:structural}
 \mathbf D(\phi\mathbf u)=\mathbf S(\psi\mathbf u),
\end{equation}
where $\phi,\psi$ have degrees at most 2 and 1, respectively,
and are not both zero.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:one-progression}
Suppose that the nodes $X(a),\ldots,X(a+r)$ are distinct,
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_-(a)=0,\quad \lambda_+(a+r)=0,
\end{equation}
and $\lambda_+(a+k)\lambda_-(a+k+1)\ne0$ for $0\le k<r$.
Then, for any $\omega_0\ne0$, the weights
\begin{equation}
 \omega_k=\omega_0\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}
       \frac{\lambda_+(a+i)}{\lambda_-(a+i+1)},
 \quad 0\le k\le r,
\end{equation}
give a solution of \eqref{eq:structural} of the form
\eqref{eq:finite-ansatz}. 

If $Z(a),Y(a),\ldots,Y(a+r)$
are also distinct, the structural equation forces the endpoint
conditions and the neighbouring cancellations for every solution
with non-zero weights. Under the stated non-zero conditions,
the weights are therefore unique up to their common factor.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The product gives
$\omega_{k+1}\lambda_-(a+k+1)=\omega_k\lambda_+(a+k)$,
so every coefficient in the collected sum is zero. Conversely,
evaluations at distinct points are independent: a polynomial can
be chosen to be one at any one of them and zero at the others.
Hence each coefficient must vanish.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:PK-masses}
For the affine map and pair in Table~\ref{tab:structural-location},
\[
 \lambda_-(s)=-\frac{2s(s-\gamma/2)}m,\quad
 \lambda_+(s)=-\frac{2(s-r)(s-\gamma/2-\ell)}m.
\]
The two progressions start at $0,\gamma/2$ and end at
$r,\gamma/2+\ell$. For $0<\gamma<2$ the nodes are distinct
and all interior weight ratios are positive:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PK-ratios}
 \begin{aligned}
 &\frac{(k-r)(k-\gamma/2-\ell)}{(k+1)(k+1-\gamma/2)},
 \quad 0\le k<r,\\[7pt]
 &\frac{(k-\ell)(k+\gamma/2-r)}{(k+1)(k+1+\gamma/2)},
 \quad 0\le k<\ell.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
With empty products equal to 1, write $(b)_k=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(b+i)$
and $(b_1,\ldots,b_p)_k=\prod_i(b_i)_k$. The two products are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PK-products}
 \begin{aligned}
 W_k^{(0)}&=\frac{(-r,-\gamma/2-\ell)_k}{k!(1-\gamma/2)_k},
 \quad 0\le k\le r,\\[7pt]
 W_k^{(1)}&=\frac{(-\ell,\gamma/2-r)_k}{k!(1+\gamma/2)_k},
 \quad 0\le k\le\ell.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The finite Vandermonde identity
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^{R}\frac{(-R,b)_k}{k!(c)_k}
 =\frac{(c-b)_R}{(c)_R}
\]
gives their sums as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PK-normalisations}
 \begin{aligned}
 Z_0&=\sum_{k=0}^{r}W_k^{(0)}
     =\frac{(\ell+1)_r}{(1-\gamma/2)_r},\\[7pt]
 Z_1&=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}W_k^{(1)}
     =\frac{(r+1)_\ell}{(1+\gamma/2)_\ell}.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Consequently $\mathbf u^{(0)}=Z_0^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^r
W_k^{(0)}\delta_{2k}$ and
$\mathbf u^{(1)}=Z_1^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}
W_k^{(1)}\delta_{2k+\gamma}$ are independent mass-one solutions.
<!-- end example-1 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:free-moment}
If $d_n\ne0$ for every $n\ge0$, the space of solutions of
\eqref{eq:structural} in $\mathcal P'$ has dimension one.
Fixing $\mu_0=1$ gives exactly one solution.

Suppose instead that, for some $m\ge0$, $d_m=0$ and
$d_n\ne0$ for $n\ne m$, and that a solution of mass one exists.
Then the solution space has dimension two. After fixing $\mu_0=1$,
the moments $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m$ are fixed, $\mu_{m+1}$ is free,
and all later moments are determined by it. Any two linearly
independent solutions of mass one therefore give every solution
of mass one through \eqref{eq:family-mixture}.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
If every $d_n$ is non-zero, choose $\mu_0$ and solve
\eqref{eq:triangular-moments} in order. Each equation determines
one new moment. The sequence defines exactly one functional
on $\mathcal P$, since every polynomial is a finite linear
combination of monomials.

If only $d_m$ vanishes, the first $m$ equations determine
$\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m$ as multiples of $\mu_0$. The equation
at $n=m$ then leaves a scalar multiple of $\mu_0$ equal to zero.
A solution with $\mu_0=1$ exists, so that scalar is zero.
The equation places no restriction on $\mu_0$ or $\mu_{m+1}$.
Every later equation again determines one new moment. These
two choices therefore determine exactly one functional, and
the solution space has dimension two.

Two independent solutions form a basis. If both have mass one,
their combination has mass one exactly when the coefficients
sum to one. This gives \eqref{eq:family-mixture}.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- example-2: body_md -->
\label{ex:PK-moments}
Return to the pair in Example~\ref{ex:PK-masses}. For an affine
map, $D(x^n)$ has leading coefficient $n$ and $S(x^n)$ has
leading coefficient one. Writing $\phi_2=[x^2]\phi$ and
$\psi_1=[x]\psi$, we have $d_n=\phi_2n+\psi_1$, as in
\cite[Theorem~7.8]{C26}. This leading-coefficient identity does
not require every $d_n$ to be non-zero. Here $\phi_2=-1/m$ and $\psi_1=1$, so
\begin{equation}
 d_n=-\frac nm+1,\quad n\ge0,\quad
 d_{N-1}=0,\quad d_n\ne0,\quad n\ne N-1.
\end{equation}
The two finite sums already constructed show that a mass-one
solution exists, so the moment equation at $n=N-1$ is compatible.
Proposition~\ref{prop:free-moment} now proves that these sums give
all solutions. Every normalised solution is
\[
 \mathbf u_\alpha
 =\frac{1-\alpha}{Z_0}\sum_{k=0}^rW_k^{(0)}\delta_{2k}
  +\frac{\alpha}{Z_1}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}W_k^{(1)}\delta_{2k+\gamma}.
\]
In particular, the two components have the same moments through
degree $N-1$. Their moments of degree $N$ differ; otherwise the
remaining triangular equations would make the functionals equal.
The free moment is therefore $\mu_N$, and changing $\alpha$
changes it. No new map or structural pair is needed.

For $0<\alpha<1$, all $N+1$ weights are positive, so the
functional is regular of order $N+1$. At $\alpha=0,1$ only
one component remains. We shall now determine the recurrence
for the full combination.
<!-- end example-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The conditions $d_m=0$ and $d_n\ne0$ for $n\ne m$ are hypotheses of
Proposition~\ref{prop:free-moment}; the degree bounds on
$\phi,\psi$ alone do not impose them. For the four specific pairs
in Table~\ref{tab:structural-location},
\eqref{eq:four-dn-lambda} verifies that the only vanishing
coefficient is $d_{N-1}$, assuming that $q$ is not a root of
unity in the $q$-cases. Once a mass-one solution exists, the
proposition gives a two-dimensional solution space and a second
independent mass-one solution. Finite support does not follow
from this dimension count; in the applications it is proved by
the endpoint construction. The alternating calculus can leave several moments free
and need not satisfy these hypotheses.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:combined-polynomials}
Under the assumptions above, $\mathbf u_\alpha$ is regular
of order $N+1$ for every $\alpha\ne0,1$. Its monic orthogonal
polynomials satisfy
\[
 P_n=Q_n,\quad 0\le n\le j.
\]
Put
\[
 M_N=\langle\mathbf u^{(1)}-\mathbf u^{(0)},x^N\rangle.
\]
Then $M_N\ne0$, $\deg R_{N-k}=N-k$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:construct-remaining}
 P_{N-k}=\frac{M_N}{h_k}R_{N-k},
 \quad
 H_{N-k}=\alpha(1-\alpha)\frac{M_N^2}{h_k},
 \quad 0\le k\le\ell.
\end{equation}
The terminal polynomial is $P_{N+1}=\Pi_0\Pi_1$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The displayed orthogonality and the prescribed values give
\[
 \langle\mathbf u_\alpha,R_{N-k}R_{N-t}\rangle
 =\alpha(1-\alpha)h_k\delta_{k,t},
 \quad 0\le k,t\le\ell.
\]
Starting at $R_N$, its non-zero norm and orthogonality to all
lower degrees force degree $N$: a lower degree would make it
orthogonal to itself. Suppose the degrees of
$R_{d+1},\ldots,R_N$ have been established. Subtracting their
leading terms writes $R_d=\sum_{n>d}c_nR_n+T$, with
$\deg T\le d$. Each $R_n$ is orthogonal to $T$ and to $R_d$.
Its non-zero norm therefore forces $c_n=0$. The norm of $R_d$
then forces
$\deg R_d=d$. This proves all the claimed degrees.

Since $Q_kx^{N-k}$ is monic of degree $N$,
\[
 \langle\mathbf u_\alpha,R_{N-k}x^{N-k}\rangle
 =\alpha(1-\alpha)M_N.
\]
If $a_{N-k}$ is the leading coefficient, comparison with its
squared norm gives $h_k=a_{N-k}M_N$. The terms below degree
$N-k$ disappear by orthogonality. Since $h_k\ne0$, we have
$M_N\ne0$, and
\eqref{eq:construct-remaining} follows. The remaining lower
norms are $H_k=h_k$ for $k\le\ell$; when $N=2j$,
\[
 H_j=(1-\alpha)\langle\mathbf u^{(0)},Q_j^2\rangle\ne0.
\]
The monic change of basis has determinant one, so
$\Delta_n=\prod_{k=0}^{n}H_k\ne0$ for $0\le n\le N$.
Thus the functional is regular of order $N+1$, and
$\Pi_0\Pi_1$, which vanishes on the entire
support, is its terminal polynomial.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:central-block}
Let the two components in \eqref{eq:two-components} have mass
one, be regular of orders $r+1,\ell+1$, and have the same moments
through degree $N-1$, where $N=r+\ell+1$. Assume their $N+1$
nodes are distinct and $r,\ell$ are as in \eqref{eq:r-ell}.
For $\alpha\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}$, the functional
$\mathbf u_\alpha$ is regular of order $N+1$. Its recurrence
coefficients are given as follows.

If $N=2j+1$, retain the common coefficients
$B_0,\ldots,B_{j-1}$ and $C_1,\ldots,C_j$. Put
$b^{(i)}=B_j^{(i)}$ for $i=0,1$. The central coefficients are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 B_j&=(1-\alpha)b^{(0)}+\alpha b^{(1)},\\[7pt]
 B_{j+1}&=\alpha b^{(0)}+(1-\alpha)b^{(1)},\\[7pt]
 C_{j+1}&=\alpha(1-\alpha)(b^{(1)}-b^{(0)})^2.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The remaining entries satisfy
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 B_{N-n}&=B_n,\quad 0\le n<j,\\[7pt]
 C_{N+1-n}&=C_n,\quad 1\le n\le j.
 \end{aligned}
\]

If $N=2j$, retain $B_0,\ldots,B_{j-1}$ and
$C_1,\ldots,C_{j-1}$. Put $b^{(0)}=B_j^{(0)}$ and
$c^{(0)}=C_j^{(0)}$. The central coefficients are
\[
 B_j=b^{(0)},\quad
 C_j=(1-\alpha)c^{(0)},\quad
 C_{j+1}=\alpha c^{(0)}.
\]
The remaining entries satisfy
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 B_{N-n}&=B_n,\quad 0\le n<j,\\[7pt]
 C_{N+1-n}&=C_n,\quad 1\le n<j.
 \end{aligned}
\]
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Lemma~\ref{lem:combined-polynomials} proves regularity and
identifies the polynomials and their norms. The first recurrence
coefficients are already fixed by
\eqref{eq:common-recurrence-ranges}. To compute the remaining
ones, orthogonality and \eqref{eq:TTRR} give
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 B_n&=\frac{\langle\mathbf u_\alpha,xP_n^2\rangle}{H_n},
 \quad 0\le n\le N,\\[7pt]
 C_n&=\frac{H_n}{H_{n-1}},\quad 1\le n\le N.
 \end{aligned}
\]

First suppose $N=2j+1$. We have $H_j=h_j$, and the construction
leading to \eqref{eq:construct-first-odd} already gives
\[
 B_j=(1-\alpha)b^{(0)}+\alpha b^{(1)}.
\]
Put $K=b^{(1)}-b^{(0)}$. Since $xQ_j^2$ is monic of degree
$N$ and the lower moments of the two components agree,
\[
 M_N
 =\langle\mathbf u^{(1)}-\mathbf u^{(0)},xQ_j^2\rangle
 =(b^{(1)}-b^{(0)})h_j
 =Kh_j.
\]
Formula \eqref{eq:construct-remaining}, with $k=j$, therefore
gives
\[
 H_{j+1}=\alpha(1-\alpha)K^2h_j,
 \quad
 C_{j+1}=\frac{H_{j+1}}{H_j}
        =\alpha(1-\alpha)K^2.
\]
The values of $P_{j+1}$ on the two supports give
\begin{align*}
 \langle\mathbf u_\alpha,xP_{j+1}^2\rangle
 ={}&(1-\alpha)\alpha^2K^2
       \langle\mathbf u^{(0)},xQ_j^2\rangle\\[7pt]
 &+\alpha(1-\alpha)^2K^2
       \langle\mathbf u^{(1)},xQ_j^2\rangle\\[7pt]
 ={}&\alpha(1-\alpha)K^2h_j
       \bigl(\alpha b^{(0)}+(1-\alpha)b^{(1)}\bigr).
\end{align*}
Dividing by $H_{j+1}$ gives the stated value of $B_{j+1}$.

Now suppose $N=2j$. Here $P_j=Q_j=\Pi_1$, so $Q_j$
vanishes on the smaller support. The last row of the larger
component is
\[
 \Pi_0=(x-b^{(0)})Q_j-c^{(0)}Q_{j-1}.
\]
Its last non-zero norm is
\[
 \langle\mathbf u^{(0)},Q_j^2\rangle
 =c^{(0)}h_{j-1}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 H_j=(1-\alpha)c^{(0)}h_{j-1},
 \quad
 \langle\mathbf u_\alpha,xQ_j^2\rangle=b^{(0)}H_j.
\]
Since $H_{j-1}=h_{j-1}$, these equalities give
\[
 B_j=b^{(0)},\quad C_j=(1-\alpha)c^{(0)}.
\]
Also, $Q_j^2$ is monic of degree $N$, and hence
\[
 M_N
 =\langle\mathbf u^{(1)}-\mathbf u^{(0)},Q_j^2\rangle
 =-c^{(0)}h_{j-1}.
\]
Using \eqref{eq:construct-remaining} with $k=j-1$, we obtain
\[
 H_{j+1}
 =\alpha(1-\alpha)(c^{(0)})^2h_{j-1},
 \quad
 C_{j+1}=\frac{H_{j+1}}{H_j}=\alpha c^{(0)}.
\]

It remains to recover the reflected entries. For either parity
and $0\le k<j$, the common component coefficient gives
\[
 \langle\mathbf u^{(i)},xQ_k^2\rangle=B_kh_k,
 \quad i=0,1.
\]
By \eqref{eq:interpolated-polynomials} and
\eqref{eq:construct-remaining},
\begin{align*}
 \langle\mathbf u_\alpha,xP_{N-k}^2\rangle
 ={}&\frac{M_N^2}{h_k^2}
 \bigl((1-\alpha)\alpha^2
       \langle\mathbf u^{(0)},xQ_k^2\rangle
       +\alpha(1-\alpha)^2
       \langle\mathbf u^{(1)},xQ_k^2\rangle\bigr)\\[7pt]
 ={}&\alpha(1-\alpha)\frac{M_N^2}{h_k}B_k
 =H_{N-k}B_k.
\end{align*}
Thus $B_{N-k}=B_k$.

For $0\le k<\ell$, both consecutive norms are given by
\eqref{eq:construct-remaining}, so
\[
 C_{N-k}
 =\frac{H_{N-k}}{H_{N-k-1}}
 =\frac{h_{k+1}}{h_k}
 =C_{k+1}.
\]
These are exactly the claimed reflections. The terminal
polynomial was already identified in
Lemma~\ref{lem:combined-polynomials}.

\label{prop:BI-classical}
The complementary functional is classical in the alternating
calculus with $\left(\phi,\psi\right)=\left(0,x-A\right)$.
In addition, its Bannai partner is classical in the same calculus with
$\left(\phi,\psi\right)
 =\left(\left(x-\rho\right)\left(x-A\right),0\right)$.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The terminal row is included in the published alternating diagonal.
Let $J$ be its finite monic recurrence matrix, with diagonal
$B_n^{\rm C}$, lower diagonal one and upper diagonal $C_{n+1}^{\rm C}$,
and put $H=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1,\ldots,1)$. Then $J=AH+K$, where $K$ has
zero diagonal and $HK=-KH$. Consequently
\[
 J^2=A^2I+K^2,\quad HJ^2=J^2H.
\]
The finite recurrence and its terminal condition represent the
normalised functional as
$\langle\mathbf u^{\rm C},p\rangle=e_0^{\mathsf T}p(J)e_0$,
where $e_0$ is the first coordinate vector and $\mathsf T$ denotes transpose.
Indeed, both functionals agree on the orthogonal basis through
degree $2j$ and are annihilated by its terminal polynomial;
polynomial division identifies them on all of $\mathcal P$.
Since $He_0=e_0$ and $K$ exchanges the two parity subspaces,
$e_0^{\mathsf T}K(J^2)^ne_0=0$ for every $n\ge0$. It follows that
\[
 \langle\mathbf u^{\rm C},(x-A)x^{2n}\rangle=0,
 \quad n\ge0.
\]
This proves the complementary annihilation identity on all polynomials,
including degrees above the finite orthogonal system. For the Bannai
functional, \eqref{eq:BI-Geronimus} gives:
\[
 \langle\mathbf u^{\rm B},
 (x-\rho)(x-A)p(x^2)\rangle=0.
\]
Criterion \eqref{eq:alternating-annihilation} proves both claims.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:PST-functional}
For $N\ge2$ and a real Jacobi matrix with positive off-diagonal
entries, the following conditions are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $J$ is a $T$-PST matrix.
\item $J$ is persymmetric and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PST-gaps}
 x_{s+1}-x_s=g m_s,\quad
 m_s\in\{1,3,5,\ldots\},\quad 0\le s<N.
\end{equation}
\item Its normalised functional is
\[
 \mathbf u=\tfrac12\mathbf u^{(0)}+\tfrac12\mathbf u^{(1)},
\]
where
\[
 \mathbf u^{(0)}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\omega_k^{(0)}\delta_{x_{2k}},
 \quad
 \mathbf u^{(1)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\omega_k^{(1)}\delta_{x_{2k+1}},
\]
have strictly positive weights, mass one, and the same moments
through degree $N-1$, and the nodes satisfy \eqref{eq:PST-gaps}.
The integers $r,\ell$ are those in \eqref{eq:r-ell}.
\end{enumerate}
For any fixed ordered nodes, the functionals in \textup{(iii)}
are unique. The weights of their equal combination are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PST-weights}
 \omega_s=\frac1{Z_Q|Q'(x_s)|}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, the ordered spectra satisfying \eqref{eq:PST-gaps}
are in one-to-one correspondence with $T$-PST matrices of size $N+1$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We recall the standard spectral argument
\cite[Sections~III--IV]{VZPST12}. Let $v_s$ be a normalised real
eigenvector at $x_s$. Perfect transfer gives
$e^{-iTx_s}v_s(0)=e^{i\theta}v_s(N)$.
Thus the spectral measures at the two ends coincide. Uniqueness
of the Jacobi matrix associated with the measure implies that
$J$ coincides with its reversal: it is persymmetric.
For such a matrix, the oscillation theorem gives
$v_s(N)=(-1)^{N+s}v_s(0)$, so transfer holds precisely when
$e^{-iTx_s}=e^{i\theta}(-1)^{N+s}$ for every $s$.
Consecutive phases give \eqref{eq:PST-gaps}, and that condition
conversely gives the required phases.

The same signs give
$P_N(x_s)=(-1)^{N+s}\sqrt{h_N}$, where
$h_N=C_1\cdots C_N$. The cardinal polynomial
$\ell_s(x)=Q(x)/((x-x_s)Q'(x_s))$ has leading coefficient
$1/Q'(x_s)$. Pairing it with $P_N$ gives
$\omega_sP_N(x_s)=h_N/Q'(x_s)$, which yields
\eqref{eq:PST-weights} after normalisation
\cite[Section~3]{GTVZ17}. We now prove its functional formulation.

Lagrange interpolation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PST-interpolation}
 \sum_{s=0}^{N}\frac{p(x_s)}{Q'(x_s)}=0,
 \quad \deg p\le N-1.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the left side is the coefficient of $x^N$ in the
interpolation polynomial for $p$. Since
$\operatorname{sgn}Q'(x_s)=(-1)^{N-s}$, the weights
\eqref{eq:PST-weights} give
\[
 \sum_{s\text{ even}}\omega_s p(x_s)
 =\sum_{s\text{ odd}}\omega_s p(x_s),\quad \deg p\le N-1.
\]
Taking $p=1$ shows that each sum has mass $1/2$.
Multiplying each restriction by two therefore gives the two
functionals in \textup{(iii)}. Their uniqueness follows because the
matrix $(x_s^k)_{0\le k<N,\,0\le s\le N}$ has rank $N$:
its kernel is generated by $(1/Q'(x_s))_s$, and the two
mass-one conditions fix the scale.

Conversely, suppose these two functionals have the stated
moments. Proposition~\ref{prop:central-block} at $\alpha=1/2$
gives persymmetry directly. Adding \eqref{eq:PST-gaps} gives
\textup{(ii)} and proves the equivalence.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:classical-PST}
Fix $N\ge2$ and $T>0$. Suppose one admissible map $X$ and one
pair $(\phi,\psi)$ satisfy Proposition~\ref{lem:one-progression}
on the two progressions in \eqref{eq:two-components}, with
$r,\ell$ as in \eqref{eq:r-ell}. Suppose the nodes are real
and distinct, both normalised component functionals have
strictly positive weights, and
\[
 d_{N-1}=0,\quad d_n\ne0,\quad n\ne N-1.
\]
If the ordered union of the nodes satisfies \eqref{eq:PST-gaps},
then the Jacobi matrix of
$\tfrac12\mathbf u^{(0)}+\tfrac12\mathbf u^{(1)}$ is $T$-PST.
Its functional is classical with respect to the same map
and structural pair.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Proposition~\ref{prop:free-moment} gives equality of the
component moments through degree $N-1$. In the ordered union,
write $\varepsilon_s=-1$ on the first support and $\varepsilon_s=1$
on the second, and let $\nu_s>0$ be the corresponding component
weight. The moment equality gives
\[
 \sum_{s=0}^{N}\varepsilon_s\nu_sx_s^k=0,
 \quad 0\le k<N.
\]
The Vandermonde matrix has a one-dimensional kernel, generated by
$(1/Q'(x_s))_{s=0}^{N}$. Its entries alternate in sign; positivity
of $\nu_s$ therefore forces the supports to alternate in the
ordered union. They are the two alternate-node supports in
Theorem~\ref{thm:PST-functional}, after labelling.
The structural equation is linear,
so the combination solves \eqref{eq:structural}. All its
weights are positive, hence it is regular of order $N+1$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:PST-functional} gives the transfer property.

<!-- example-3: body_md -->
Fix $j\ge1$ and $T>0$, and put $N=2j+1$ and $g=\pi/T$.
Consider
\[
 X(s)=\kappa s^2+\eta s,\quad \kappa>0,\quad \eta\in\mathbb R.
\]
Let the progressions start at $a$ and $c=a+\delta$, with
$0<\delta<1$. Write
\[
 h=\frac{\eta}{2\kappa},\quad
 u=a+h>0,\quad v=u+\delta,\quad
 x_*=-\frac{\eta^2}{4\kappa}.
\]
Then $X(s)=\kappa\left(s+h\right)^2+x_*$, on the admissible domain
$U=\mathbb C\setminus\{-h+n/2:n\in\mathbb Z\}$.

We first choose the endpoint factors. With $\xi=s+h$, take
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \lambda_-(s)
 &=-\frac{\kappa}{4j\xi}
   \left(\xi-u\right)\left(\xi-v\right)
   \left(\xi+u+j\right)\left(\xi+v+j\right),\\[7pt]
 \lambda_+(s)
 &=-\frac{\kappa}{4j\xi}
   \left(\xi+u\right)\left(\xi+v\right)
   \left(\xi-u-j\right)\left(\xi-v-j\right).
 \end{aligned}
\]
These close the progressions at $a+j$ and $c+j$.
Reconstructing the pair from \eqref{eq:lambdas}, and putting
\[
 H=u\left(u+j\right)+v\left(v+j\right),\quad
 K=uv\left(u+j\right)\left(v+j\right),
\]
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic-PST-pair}
 \begin{aligned}
 \phi(x)
 &=-\frac{\left(x-x_*\right)^2
          +\kappa\left(j^2-H\right)\left(x-x_*\right)
          +\kappa^2K}{2j},\\[7pt]
 \psi(x)&=x-x_*-\frac{\kappa H}{2}.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The interior weight ratios are positive, so
Proposition~\ref{lem:one-progression} gives two normalised
positive finite functionals. Moreover, $d_n=1-n/(2j)$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:free-moment} therefore gives agreement
of their moments through degree $N-1$. Choosing $\alpha=1/2$
now gives a persymmetric Jacobi matrix by
Proposition~\ref{prop:central-block}.

We still have to choose the parameters for transfer at time $T$.
The nodes $x_{2k}=X(a+k)$ and $x_{2k+1}=X(c+k)$, $0\le k\le j$,
interlace, and their gaps are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic-PST-gaps}
 \begin{aligned}
 x_{2k+1}-x_{2k}
 &=\kappa\delta\left(2u+2k+\delta\right),
       &&0\le k\le j,\\[7pt]
 x_{2k+2}-x_{2k+1}
 &=\kappa\left(1-\delta\right)
             \left(2u+2k+1+\delta\right),
       &&0\le k<j.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Whenever these are odd multiples of $g$, the matrix is
$T$-PST by Corollary~\ref{cor:classical-PST}.

The recurrence can be written explicitly without computing
moments. Put $\Sigma=u+v$ and $\theta_n=j+1-n$.
The standard quadratic formulas, together with
Proposition~\ref{prop:central-block}, give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic-PST-recurrence}
 \begin{aligned}
 B_n
 &=x_*+\frac{\kappa}{2}
       \left(H+n\left(N-n\right)\right),\\[7pt]
 C_n
 &=\frac{\kappa^2n\left(N+1-n\right)
          \left(\Sigma+n-1\right)\left(\Sigma+N-n\right)
          \left(\theta_n^2-\delta^2\right)}
         {4\left(4\theta_n^2-1\right)}.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The ranges are $0\le n\le N$ and $1\le n\le N$, respectively.
Both reflection identities are visible in these expressions. To obtain
transfer matrices with both map coefficients $\kappa,\eta$ non-zero, take
\[
 \kappa=8Mg,\quad
 \delta=\frac14,\quad
 u=\frac{4L+1}{8},\quad
 a=u-\frac{\eta}{2\kappa},\quad c=a+\frac14,
\]
where $M$ is any positive odd integer, $L$ is any non-negative
integer, and $\eta\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ is arbitrary. The nodes are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 x_{2k}&=x_*+\frac{Mg}{8}\left(8k+4L+1\right)^2,\\[7pt]
 x_{2k+1}&=x_*+\frac{Mg}{8}\left(8k+4L+3\right)^2.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Their gaps divided by $g$ are
\[
 M\left(4k+2L+1\right),\quad
 M\left(12k+6L+9\right),
\]
respectively. These are positive odd integers, so every such
choice gives a classical $T$-PST matrix. The parameter $\eta$
remains free and changes only the common spectral shift $x_*$.

For a concrete matrix, take $j=1$, $L=0$, $M=1$ and $\eta=4g$.
Then
\[
 X(s)=8gs^2+4gs,\quad a=-\frac18,\quad c=\frac18,
\]
and the ordered nodes are $-3g/8$, $5g/8$, $77g/8$ and $117g/8$.
Formula \eqref{eq:quadratic-PST-recurrence} gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 B_0=B_3&=\frac{17g}{8},&
 B_1=B_2&=\frac{81g}{8},\\[7pt]
 C_1=C_3&=\frac{75g^2}{4},&
 C_2&=9g^2.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus
\[
 J=\frac g8
 \begin{pmatrix}
 17&20\sqrt3&0&0\\[7pt]
 20\sqrt3&81&24&0\\[7pt]
 0&24&81&20\sqrt3\\[7pt]
 0&0&20\sqrt3&17
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its spectral gaps are $g,9g,5g$, giving perfect transfer
at $T=\pi/g$.
