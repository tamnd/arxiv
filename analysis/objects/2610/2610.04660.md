<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $G$ be a bridgeless graph with diameter $d\ge1$, radius $\rho$,
and $\eta=\eta(G)$. Let $R=\lfloor d/2\rfloor$.
Then $G$ has a strong orientation $\vec G$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 \begin{aligned}
 \operatorname{diam}(\vec G)
 &\le 6\rho+2\sum_{i=1}^{R}\min\{2i,\eta-1\}\\
 &\le 2R(R+1)+6\rho
 \le \tfrac12d^2+7d.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In particular, $f(d)\le\tfrac12d^2+7d$ for every $d\ge1$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:core}
Every bridgeless graph $G$ of diameter $d\ge1$ and radius $\rho$ contains
an $\lfloor d/2\rfloor$-step dominating subgraph $K$ with a strong
orientation $\vec K$ satisfying
$\operatorname{diam}(\vec K)\le6\rho$. We refer to any such strongly oriented subgraph $\vec K$
as an \emph{oriented core} of $G$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:ear-extension}
Let $G$ be a finite connected bridgeless graph with at least two vertices,
and let $H$ be a nonempty
$t$-step dominating subgraph of $G$, where $t$ is a nonnegative integer. Put $\eta=\eta(G)$.
Every strong orientation $\vec H$ of $H$ extends to a strong orientation $\vec G$
of $G$ satisfying
\[
 \operatorname{diam}(\vec G)
 \le \operatorname{diam}(\vec H)
       +2\sum_{i=1}^{t}\min\{2i,\eta-1\}
 \le \operatorname{diam}(\vec H)+2t(t+1).
\]
A single vertex is allowed as a strongly oriented subgraph of diameter zero.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For a nonempty set $A\subsetneq V(G)$, an $A$-ear is a path with its
endpoints in $A$ and all internal vertices outside $A$; we also allow a
cycle meeting $A$ in exactly one vertex. For an edge $e=av$ with $a\in A$
and $v\notin A$, an $(A,e)$-ear has first edge $av$ when traversed from
$a$. Shortest always refers to this prescribed first undirected edge.
The eventual consistent orientation of an ear may follow either traversal.

We first record the distance profile of a shortest $(A,e)$-ear
$P=x_0x_1\cdots x_\ell$, where $x_0=a$ and $x_1=v$.
Such an ear exists: follow a cycle containing $e$ from $a$ along $e$
until its first return to $A$. Moreover,
$P[v,x_\ell]$ is a shortest $v$--$A$ path in $G-e$, since prepending
$av$ to any shortest such path gives an $(A,e)$-ear.
For $1\le j<\ell$, every $x_j$--$A$ path avoiding $e$ has length at
least $\ell-j$, since adjoining it to $P[v,x_j]$ and deleting
closed subwalks would otherwise give a shorter $v$--$A$ path in
$G-e$. A shortest
$x_j$--$A$ path using $e$ must traverse $v a$ as its last edge.
Its preceding part has length at least $j-1$, because $P[v,x_j]$ is
geodesic in $G-e$. Following either side of $P$ gives the reverse
inequality, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ear-profile}
 \operatorname{dist}_G(x_j,A)=\min\{j,\ell-j\}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $A$ is $s$-step dominating, a middle vertex gives
$\ell\le2s+1$. The cycle construction above also gives
$\ell\le\ell_G(e)\le\eta$.

Now fix an $s$-step dominating set $A$, with $s\ge1$, carrying the
current strong orientation. We enlarge its vertex set to contain every
neighbour of $A$, while keeping every existing arc. Throughout this
round, $A$ remains fixed. Record every ear used in the round, including
its consistent orientation. Call a vertex covered if it belongs to $A$
or to an already recorded ear.

If an uncovered neighbour $v$ of $A$ remains, choose $e=av$ and a
shortest $(A,e)$-ear $P$. If all internal vertices of $P$ are uncovered,
orient it consistently and record it. Otherwise let $w$ be the first
covered internal vertex of $P$, starting from $a$. Choose an earlier
recorded ear $Q$ containing $w$. A shorter side of $Q$ from $w$ to $A$
has length $\operatorname{dist}_G(w,A)$ by~\eqref{eq:ear-profile}.
Replace the part of $P$ after $w$ by that side of $Q$, obtaining $P'$.

All internal vertices of $P[a,w]$ are uncovered, whereas every vertex
of $Q$ is covered. Thus the two pieces meet only at $w$ and possibly
at their endpoints in $A$. They form a valid $(A,e)$-ear, open or closed.
Its length is no greater than that of $P$, so the minimality of $P$
makes $P'$ a shortest $(A,e)$-ear as well. The chosen side of $Q$ is
already directed in one direction. Orient the new prefix $P[a,w]$
in the matching direction to make all of $P'$ a directed ear, and
record $P'$. No old edge is reoriented, since every prefix edge has
an uncovered endpoint. The newly recorded ear contains $v$, so this
procedure terminates after finitely many additions.

Every newly covered vertex lies on a recorded directed ear of length
at most $\min\{2s+1,\eta\}$. It can therefore reach $A$ and be reached
from $A$ within
$b(s)=\min\{2s,\eta-1\}$ steps in the enlarged orientation.
All these paths coexist, and strong connectivity is preserved.
Since all neighbours of $A$ have been covered, the enlarged vertex set
is $(s-1)$-step dominating: a shortest path to $A$ meets a neighbour
of $A$ one step before reaching $A$.

Starting with $A=V(H)$, perform these rounds with
$s=t,t-1,\ldots,1$, stopping earlier if every vertex has been covered.
Every vertex can then reach $V(H)$ and be reached from $V(H)$ within
$\sum_{i=1}^{t}b(i)$ steps. Routing an ordered pair through the
unchanged orientation $\vec H$ proves the asserted bound. Orient all
remaining edges arbitrarily. The case $t=0$ only requires this final step.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:central}
Let $X$ be a connected graph of diameter $d$.
Every tree decomposition of $X$ has a bag $B$ such that
\[
 \operatorname{dist}_X(x,B)\le\lfloor d/2\rfloor
 \qquad\text{for every }x\in V(X).
\]
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Put $R=\lfloor d/2\rfloor$. For each vertex $x$, let
$A_x=\{y:\operatorname{dist}_X(x,y)\le R\}$.
This set is connected, since the initial segments of a shortest path
from $x$ stay in $A_x$. The nodes of the decomposition tree whose bags
meet $A_x$ therefore form a nonempty subtree $T_x$.
Indeed, the bags containing a fixed vertex form a subtree, and the
supports of adjacent vertices meet in an edge-containing bag.

For any $x,y$, a shortest $x$--$y$ path has integer length at most
$d\le2R+1$. Its portions within distance $R$ of its ends either meet
or are joined by an edge. Thus $A_x,A_y$ intersect or have
an edge between them. In both cases $T_x\cap T_y\ne\varnothing$.
The Helly property of subtrees of a tree gives a node belonging to
every $T_x$. Its bag meets every $A_x$, as required.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:cycle-bags}
Let $G$ be a connected bridgeless graph with at least two vertices,
and let $T$ be a spanning tree of $G$.
For each $x\in V(T)$, take a path $Q_x$ on $\deg_T(x)$ nodes, with
these paths pairwise disjoint. Assign the tree edges incident with
$x$ bijectively to the nodes of $Q_x$. For each $xy\in E(T)$, join
the nodes of $Q_x$ and $Q_y$ assigned to $xy$, and denote the resulting
graph by $\mathcal T$. Choose a representative $r_x\in V(Q_x)$ for each $x$.
For each edge $e=ab\in E(G)\setminus E(T)$, write
$P_e=T[a,b]$, $C_e=P_e+e$ and $\widehat P_e=\mathcal T[r_a,r_b]$.
For $t\in V(Q_v)$, let $H_t$ consist of the vertex $v$ and all cycles
$C_e$ with $t\in V(\widehat P_e)$, and let $B_t=V(H_t)$.
Then $\mathcal T$ is a tree of maximum degree at most three, and
$(\mathcal T,\{B_t:t\in V(\mathcal T)\})$ is a tree decomposition of $G$.
Moreover, $H_t$ is nontrivial, connected and bridgeless,
$T_t=H_t\cap T$ is a spanning tree of $H_t$, and every fundamental
cycle of $H_t$ relative to $T_t$ contains $v$.
If the cycles $C_e$ have length at most $L$, the same holds for these
fundamental cycles of $H_t$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Contracting the paths $Q_x$ gives $T$, so $\mathcal T$ is a tree.
Each node has at most two neighbours on its path $Q_x$ and exactly
one neighbour outside that path. Thus its degree is at most three.
The contraction maps $\widehat P_e$ onto $P_e$. In particular,
$\widehat P_e$ meets $Q_x$ if and only if $x\in V(P_e)$.

Every vertex $x$ lies in every bag indexed by a node of $Q_x$.
The ends of a non-tree edge $e$ lie together in each bag indexed
by $\widehat P_e$.
Every tree edge $xy$ belongs to some $C_e$, since otherwise its
fundamental cut would make it a bridge of $G$.
The corresponding $\widehat P_e$ uses the edge joining $Q_x$ to $Q_y$,
so a bag indexed by either end of that edge contains both $x$ and $y$.
For a fixed vertex $x$ of $G$, its support in $\mathcal T$ is exactly
\[
 \{t:x\in B_t\}
   =V(Q_x)\cup
     \bigcup_{\substack{e\in E(G)\setminus E(T)\\x\in V(P_e)}}
        V(\widehat P_e).
\]
Each path in the union meets $Q_x$. Hence the support is connected,
which proves the tree-decomposition axioms.

For each $t\in V(Q_v)$, let $vy$ be the tree edge assigned to $t$.
This edge belongs to some $C_e$, and the corresponding $\widehat P_e$
uses the edge joining $Q_v$ to $Q_y$ at $t$. Hence $H_t$ contains
$C_e$ and is nontrivial.
If $t\in V(Q_v)$ and $t\in V(\widehat P_e)$, then $v\in V(P_e)$.
Thus all cycles defining $H_t$ contain $v$. Their union with $v$ is
connected and bridgeless, and the union of their tree paths with $v$
is a subtree spanning $H_t$. Each non-tree edge of $H_t$ is one of
the selected edges and retains its original tree path, so its
fundamental cycle is exactly its original $C_e$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:asymmetric}
Let $H$ be a connected bridgeless graph with at least two vertices,
and let $U$ be a spanning tree rooted at $z$.
Suppose that every non-tree fundamental cycle contains $z$ and has
length at most $L$. Suppose also that there is a map
$\lambda:V(H)\setminus\{z\}\to\{1,2,3\}$ which is constant on each
component of $U-z$ and satisfies $\lambda(a)\ne\lambda(b)$ for every
$ab\in E(H)\setminus E(U)$ with $a,b\ne z$.
Then $H$ has a strongly oriented spanning subgraph $\vec K$ satisfying
\[
 \operatorname{dist}_{\vec K}(x,z)\le L-1,\qquad
 \operatorname{dist}_{\vec K}(z,x)\le2(L-1)
 \quad\text{for every }x\in V(H).
\]
In particular, $\operatorname{diam}(\vec K)\le3(L-1)$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Call the components of $U-z$ the \emph{branches}, and let $A,B,C$
be the unions of the branches with labels $1,2,3$, respectively.
Each tree edge joining a branch to $z$ is treated as part of that branch.
A non-tree edge not incident with $z$ joins two different label classes.

Let $J_1$ be the union of all fundamental cycles except those whose
non-tree edge joins $B$ to $C$, and let $J_2$ be the union of these
remaining cycles. Include $z$ in each group, even when its family
of cycles is empty.
In $J_1$, orient the tree edges in $A$ away from $z$ and those in
$B,C$ towards $z$. Direct non-tree edges between label classes from
$A$ to $B$ or $C$. Direct a non-tree edge incident with $z$ towards
$z$ if its other endpoint is in $A$, and away from $z$ otherwise.
In $J_2$, orient the tree edges in $B$ away from $z$, those in $C$
towards $z$, and the non-tree edges from $B$ to $C$.
Every selected fundamental cycle is directed consistently within its
group. Every tree edge of $H$ belongs to a fundamental cycle, since
$H$ is bridgeless, so $J_1\cup J_2=H$.
Each nonroot vertex of either group lies on a selected directed cycle
of length at most $L$, and hence has distances at most $L-1$ in both
directions between itself and $z$ within that group.

Put $S=V(J_1)$. The tree part $J_1\cap U$ is a subtree containing
$z$ and spanning $S$. Thus the entire tree path to $z$ of a vertex
in $S$ lies in $J_1$; in particular, no descendant of a vertex
outside $S$ belongs to $S$.
Keep all arcs of $\vec J_1$, and add every arc of $\vec J_2$ having
at least one endpoint outside $S$. No added edge was already oriented,
so this defines an oriented spanning subgraph $\vec K$ of $H$.

Let $x\notin S$, and choose a selected directed cycle of $J_2$
through $x$. If $x\in B$, its directed segment to $z$ first goes
away from $z$ in $B$, crosses to $C$, and then follows $C$ towards $z$.
All vertices of the first part are descendants of $x$ and hence
outside $S$, so its edges and the crossing edge are retained.
On the $C$ part, an edge with an endpoint outside $S$ is added,
whereas an edge with both endpoints in $S$ already belongs to $J_1$
and has the same direction towards $z$.
Thus the whole return segment survives. If $x\in C$, its return
segment is its tree path to $z$, and the same argument applies.
In either case, $\operatorname{dist}_{\vec K}(x,z)\le L-1$.

On the directed $z$--$x$ segment of the chosen cycle, let $w$ be
the last vertex in $S$. Every edge of the suffix from $w$ to $x$
has an endpoint outside $S$, so this suffix survives and has length
at most $L-1$. Reach $w$ from $z$ inside $\vec J_1$ within $L-1$
steps, using a length-zero path if $w=z$, and then follow the suffix.
Hence $\operatorname{dist}_{\vec K}(z,x)\le2(L-1)$.
Vertices in $S$ retain their original paths in $\vec J_1$.
These bounds prove strong connectivity and the stated diameter bound.
<!-- end proof-4 -->

Write $d=\operatorname{diam}(G)$, $\rho=\operatorname{rad}(G)$ and $R=\lfloor d/2\rfloor$.
Choose a center $c$ of $G$ and a breadth-first-search spanning tree
$T$ rooted at $c$. Its depth is at most $\rho$, so every fundamental
cycle $C_e$ has length at most $L=2\rho+1$.
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle-bags} to $G,T$, obtaining an indexing tree
$\mathcal T$, paths $Q_x$, representatives $r_x$ and bags $B_t=V(H_t)$.
Only the indexing tree is refined; the graph $G$ and its distances
are unchanged.
Lemma~\ref{lem:central} supplies a bag $B_t$ which is $R$-step dominating
in $G$. Let $t\in V(Q_v)$.

Assign distinct labels from $\{1,2,3\}$ to the at most three
components of $\mathcal T-t$.
For each $x\ne v$, give $x$ the label of the component containing $r_x$.
This defines a map $\lambda:V(G)\setminus\{v\}\to\{1,2,3\}$.
For every component $A$ of $T-v$, the paths $Q_x$ with $x\in V(A)$
and the edges corresponding to $E(A)$ form a connected subtree of
$\mathcal T$ avoiding $t$.
Hence $\lambda$ is constant on $V(A)$, and therefore on each component
of $T_t-v$, where $T_t=H_t\cap T$.

For each non-tree edge $e=ab$ of $H_t$ with $a,b\ne v$, the path
$\widehat P_e=\mathcal T[r_a,r_b]$ contains $t$.
Neither endpoint is $t$, so $r_a,r_b$ lie in different components
of $\mathcal T-t$, and therefore $\lambda(a)\ne\lambda(b)$.
All fundamental cycles of $H_t$ relative to $T_t$ contain $v$ and
have length at most $L$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle-bags}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:asymmetric} therefore gives a strongly oriented
spanning subgraph $\vec K$ of $H_t$, within the original graph $G$,
such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:group-root}
 \operatorname{dist}_{\vec K}(x,v)\le L-1=2\rho,\qquad
 \operatorname{dist}_{\vec K}(v,x)\le2(L-1)=4\rho
 \quad(x\in V(K)).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\[
 V(K)=B_t,\qquad \operatorname{diam}(\vec K)\le3(L-1)=6\rho.
\]
Since $B_t$ is $R$-step dominating in $G$, so is $K$.
This completes the proof.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:algorithm}
The proof gives a polynomial-time construction.
Distances and a center can be found by breadth-first search in $G$.
If $G$ has $n$ vertices, the indexing tree $\mathcal T$ has
$\sum_x\deg_T(x)=2n-2$ nodes and $2n-3$ edges.
The spanning tree, its fundamental cycles, the paths $\widehat P_e$
and all bags can be enumerated in polynomial time.
Testing the bags finds a central bag without requiring an algorithmic
form of the Helly theorem. The component labels and the asymmetric
two-group construction are explicit. Finally, a shortest prescribed-leg ear is obtained by
breadth-first search after deleting its prescribed edge; each ear
addition covers a new vertex. All steps therefore take polynomial time.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:cactus-root}
The root of $B_r$ has eccentricity $r$.
In every strong orientation of $B_r$, its maximum directed distance
to a vertex and its maximum directed distance from a vertex are
both equal to $a_r$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The assertion is immediate for $r=0$.
Every vertex on the new cycle is within distance $r$ of its root,
and every vertex in an attached copy is within distance
$1+(r-1)=r$. Equality follows from the induction hypothesis in an
attached copy, so the undirected eccentricity is $r$.

Every component outside a cycle block of a cactus attaches to that
block at a single vertex. An excursion outside the block therefore
returns to its departure vertex and can be deleted from a directed
walk. The same argument applies to an attached copy, which meets the
rest of the graph only at its root.
Thus a strong orientation restricts to a strong orientation of each
cycle block and attached copy, and their internal directed distances
are unchanged. In particular, every cycle block is oriented cyclically.

Let $p,q$ be the two neighbours of the root on the new cycle, labelled
so that $p\to z\to q$.
The directed distance from $z$ to $p$, and from $q$ to $z$, is $2r$.
By induction, the copy rooted at $p$ contains a vertex at directed
distance $a_{r-1}$ from $p$, while the copy rooted at $q$ contains
a vertex at directed distance $a_{r-1}$ to $q$.
They give root distances at least
$2r+a_{r-1}=a_r$ in the two directions.
Conversely, a route from or to the root uses at most $2r$ edges of
the new cycle and at most $a_{r-1}$ edges in an attached copy.
Both maximum directed distances are therefore exactly $a_r$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:lower}
For every integer $d\ge1$, there is a bridgeless cactus $G_d$ such that
\[
 \operatorname{diam}(G_d)=d,\qquad
 \overrightarrow{d}(G_d)=\left\lfloor\frac{(d+1)^2}{2}\right\rfloor.
\]
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
First let $d=2r$, where $r\ge1$.
Take two copies of $B_r$ and identify their roots.
Lemma~\ref{lem:cactus-root} gives diameter $2r$: vertices at distance
$r$ from the common root in different copies attain this value.
In any strong orientation, choose a vertex in the first copy whose
directed distance to the root is $a_r$, and a vertex in the second
whose directed distance from the root is $a_r$.
Every directed path between them passes through the root, so its
length is at least $2a_r$. Conversely, routing through the root
gives diameter at most $2a_r$.
Thus $\overrightarrow{d}(G_{2r})=2r(r+1)$.

Now let $d=2r+1$, where $r\ge0$.
Take a cycle $C$ of length $2r+3$ and attach copies of $B_r$
at two adjacent vertices $p,q$ of $C$.
Two vertices in different copies are at distance at most $2r+1$.
Two vertices in one copy are at distance at most $2r$;
a vertex of a copy and a vertex of $C$ are at distance at most
$r+(r+1)$; and two vertices of $C$ are at distance at most $r+1$.
Vertices at distance $r$ from $p$ and $q$ in the two copies attain
distance $2r+1$. This also covers $r=0$.

Every strong orientation directs $C$ cyclically.
In one of the two directions between $p$ and $q$, the directed
distance along $C$ is $2r+2$.
Choose vertices in the two copies whose directed distances to the
initial attachment vertex and from the terminal attachment vertex
are both $a_r$. Any directed path between them must use this
long directed segment of $C$, giving a lower bound
\[
 2a_r+2r+2=2(r+1)^2.
\]
For the reverse inequality, pairs within an attached copy can be
routed through its root in at most $2a_r$ steps.
All other pairs can be joined using at most $a_r$ steps in each
attached copy and at most $2r+2$ steps on $C$.
Thus the oriented diameter is exactly $2(r+1)^2$.
The two parity cases give the stated formula.

\label{cor:asymptotic}
For every integer $d\ge1$,
\[
 \left\lfloor\frac{(d+1)^2}{2}\right\rfloor
 \le f(d)\le\tfrac12d^2+7d.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 f(d)=\tfrac12d^2+O(d)
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{f(d)}{d^2}=\frac12.
\]
