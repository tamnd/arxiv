<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $n\ge3$ and $2\le k<n$.  If
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_k(A_u)=f(x,u)\quad\text{in }\Omega,
\end{equation*}
then there are $\beta=\beta(n,k,M)>1$ and $C<\infty$ such that
\begin{equation*}
\sup_\Omega \rho^\beta\lvert A_u\rvert\le C.
\end{equation*}
The constant depends only on $n,k,M,U,\lVert \rho\rVert_{L^\infty(\Omega)},f_0^{-1},f_1,\lVert f\rVert_{C^{1,1}}$ and the fixed coordinate range.  It is independent of $\lVert D^2w\rVert_{L^\infty}$, of any positive lower bound for $\sigma_k(A_w)$, and of the convexity of $\Omega$.  In particular, the result applies to $f=f(x)$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:gradient}
Let $n\ge3$ and $n/2\le k<n$.  If
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_k(A_u)=f(x,u,Du)\quad\text{in }\Omega,
\end{equation*}
then
\[
\sup_\Omega \rho^\beta\lvert A_u\rvert\le C
\]
for some $\beta>1$ and $C<\infty$ depending only on the data listed in \cref{thm:main}.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:intro}
The strict gap between the two graphs is assumed, not derived.  Constructing the comparison graph from boundary data or from a subsolution is a separate problem.  The graph $w$ need not solve a curvature equation, but its full admissibility $A_w\in\Gamma_k$ is essential for the mixed G\aa rding inequality.  The simple-root assumption is used only to keep the largest eigenvalue smooth in the pointwise calculation.  The restriction $k<n$ comes from the spectral inequality quoted in Section~2.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:cone}
If $\kappa\in\Gamma_k$ and $2\le k<n$, then
\begin{equation}
F^{ii}>0,\qquad \sum_iF^{ii}\kappa_i=kf,\qquad
\kappa_i> -\frac{n-k}{k}a.
\label{eq:cone-basic}
\end{equation}
Moreover, with $H=\sigma_1(\kappa)$,
\begin{equation}
Q\ge\frac{k}{n}Hf\ge\frac{k}{n}f_0a,
\qquad \lvert A_u\rvert<H\le na,
\label{eq:Q-lower}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
a\ge \left(\frac{f_0}{\binom nk}\right)^{1/k}>0.
\label{eq:a-lower}
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Since $\kappa\in\Gamma_k$, G\aa rding cone theory implies that the linearization of $\sigma_k$ is elliptic \cite{Garding1959}; hence
\[
F^{ii}=\sigma_{k-1}(\kappa|i)>0.
\]
Euler's identity for the homogeneous polynomial $\sigma_k$ gives
\[
\sum_iF^{ii}\kappa_i=k\sigma_k(\kappa)=kf.
\]
For the individual principal curvatures it is enough to consider the smallest component $\kappa_n$, since $\kappa_i\ge\kappa_n$ for every $i$.  If $\kappa_n\ge0$, there is nothing to prove.  Suppose therefore that $\kappa_n<0$ and set $\mu=\kappa|n\in\mathbb R^{n-1}$.  We claim that $\mu\in\Gamma_k$.  Indeed, starting from $\sigma_0(\mu)=1$ and using
\[
\sigma_j(\kappa)=\sigma_j(\mu)+\kappa_n\sigma_{j-1}(\mu),
\]
an induction on $j$ gives $\sigma_j(\mu)>0$ for $1\le j\le k$, because $\sigma_j(\kappa)>0$ and $\kappa_n<0$.  Consequently
\[
0<\sigma_k(\kappa)=\sigma_k(\mu)+\kappa_n\sigma_{k-1}(\mu),
\]
so
\[
-\kappa_n<\frac{\sigma_k(\mu)}{\sigma_{k-1}(\mu)}.
\]
Since $\mu_i\le a$ and the first derivatives of $\sigma_k$ are positive on $\Gamma_k$ \cite{Garding1959},
\[
k\sigma_k(\mu)
 =\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mu_i\sigma_{k-1}(\mu|i)
 \le a\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sigma_{k-1}(\mu|i)
 =a(n-k)\sigma_{k-1}(\mu).
\]
Therefore
\[
\kappa_n> -\frac{n-k}{k}a,
\]
and the same estimate holds for every $\kappa_i$.  This proves \eqref{eq:cone-basic}.

Because $k\ge2$, one has $\kappa\in\Gamma_2$.  Thus $H=\sigma_1(\kappa)>0$ and
\[
H^2-\lvert A_u\rvert^2=2\sigma_2(\kappa)>0,
\]
which yields $\lvert A_u\rvert<H$.  Since $a=\max_i\kappa_i$, we also have $H=\sum_i\kappa_i\le na$.

For $Q$ we use the identity
\[
\sigma_1\sigma_k=(k+1)\sigma_{k+1}
 +\sum_i\kappa_i^2\sigma_{k-1}(\kappa|i).
\]
The last sum is precisely $Q$, and hence
\[
Q=H\sigma_k-(k+1)\sigma_{k+1}.
\]
If $\sigma_{k+1}\le0$, then $Q\ge Hf$, which is stronger than the desired estimate.  If $\sigma_{k+1}>0$, then $\kappa\in\Gamma_{k+1}$.  For the normalized elementary symmetric functions
\[
S_j=\frac{\sigma_j}{\binom nj},
\]
Newton--Maclaurin implies that the ratios $S_{j+1}/S_j$ are nonincreasing.  In particular,
\[
\frac{S_{k+1}}{S_k}\le S_1=\frac{H}{n}.
\]
Since $\binom n{k+1}/\binom nk=(n-k)/(k+1)$, this gives
\[
(k+1)\sigma_{k+1}\le\frac{n-k}{n}H\sigma_k.
\]
Substitution into the preceding identity yields
\[
Q\ge\frac{k}{n}Hf.
\]
Finally, the normalized Maclaurin inequality gives
\[
\left(\frac{f}{\binom nk}\right)^{1/k}
 =S_k^{1/k}\le S_1=\frac{H}{n}\le a.
\]
Using $f\ge f_0$ proves \eqref{eq:Q-lower} and \eqref{eq:a-lower}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:semiconvex}
Let $\Phi\in C^{1,1}$ on a coordinate neighborhood of a Riemannian manifold, and let $Y(t)$ be a $C^2$ curve. Assume that $g=\Phi\circ Y$ is $C^2$. Then
\begin{equation*}
g''(0)\ge D\Phi(Y(0))[\nabla_tY'(0)]-C\lvert Y'(0)\rvert^2,
\end{equation*}
where $C$ depends only on the Lipschitz constant of $D\Phi$ and the chosen compact coordinate neighborhood.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Choose normal coordinates centered at $Y(0)$ and identify the coordinate neighborhood with a ball in Euclidean space.  Let $L$ be a Lipschitz constant for $D\Phi$ on a slightly smaller compact neighborhood containing the image of $Y(t)$ for $|t|$ small.  The fundamental theorem of calculus gives, for every small vector $z$,
\[
\Phi(z)-\Phi(0)-D\Phi(0)[z]
 =\int_0^1\big(D\Phi(sz)-D\Phi(0)\big)[z] \,ds,
\]
and therefore
\[
\Phi(z)\ge \Phi(0)+D\Phi(0)[z]-\frac L2\lvert z\rvert^2.
\]
Write the coordinate representation of the curve as $y(t)$.  Since the Christoffel symbols vanish at the center of normal coordinates,
\[
y(t)=tV+\frac{t^2}{2}A+o(t^2),
\qquad
V=Y'(0),\quad A=\nabla_tY'(0).
\]
Consequently
\[
\lvert y(t)\rvert^2=t^2\lvert V\rvert^2+o(t^2).
\]
Applying the preceding semiconvex Taylor inequality with $z=y(t)$ gives
\[
g(t)\ge g(0)+tD\Phi(V)
 +\frac{t^2}{2}\big(D\Phi(A)-L\lvert V\rvert^2\big)+o(t^2).
\]
On the other hand, $g$ is $C^2$, so
\[
g(t)=g(0)+tg'(0)+\frac{t^2}{2}g''(0)+o(t^2),
\qquad g'(0)=D\Phi(V).
\]
Comparing the quadratic terms yields
\[
g''(0)\ge D\Phi(A)-L\lvert V\rvert^2.
\]
This gives the stated estimate, after enlarging the constant to account for the fixed coordinate chart.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:source-second}
At a principal frame,
\begin{equation*}
\sum_iF^{ii}h_{ii;11}+\sigma_k^{pq,rs}h_{pq;1}h_{rs;1}
\ge -aD_X\widehat f(\nu)+\sum_\ell h_{11;\ell}D_\nu\widehat f(e_\ell)-C(1+a^2).
\end{equation*}
If $f=f(x,u)$, then
\begin{equation*}
\sum_iF^{ii}h_{ii;11}+\sigma_k^{pq,rs}h_{pq;1}h_{rs;1}
\ge -aD_X\widehat f(\nu)-C.
\end{equation*}
In both cases,
\begin{equation}
\sum_iF^{ii}h_{ii;j}=D_X\widehat f(e_j)+\kappa_jD_\nu\widehat f(e_j).
\label{eq:source-first}
\end{equation}
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $X(t)$ be the unit-speed graph geodesic chosen above, and parallel transport an orthonormal frame along $X(t)$ so that it agrees with the principal frame at $t=0$.  Set
\[
g(t)=\widehat f(X(t),\nu(t))=\sigma_k(A_u(X(t))).
\]
Since $u\in C^4$, the second fundamental form is $C^2$ and hence $g$ is $C^2$.  Differentiating the right-hand side at $t=0$ gives
\[
g''(0)=\sum_iF^{ii}h_{ii;11}
 +\sigma_k^{pq,rs}h_{pq;1}h_{rs;1}.
\]
For the geometric curve $Y(t)=(X(t),\nu(t))$, formula \eqref{eq:Y-derivs} gives
\[
Y'(0)=(e_1,ae_1),
\qquad
\nabla_tY'(0)=\left(-a\nu,\sum_\ell h_{11;\ell}e_\ell\right).
\]
Applying \cref{lem:semiconvex} to $\Phi=\widehat f$ therefore yields
\begin{align*}
g''(0)
&\ge -aD_X\widehat f(\nu)
 +\sum_\ell h_{11;\ell}D_\nu\widehat f(e_\ell)
 -C\big(\lvert e_1\rvert^2+a^2\lvert e_1\rvert^2\big)\\
&= -aD_X\widehat f(\nu)
 +\sum_\ell h_{11;\ell}D_\nu\widehat f(e_\ell)-C(1+a^2),
\end{align*}
which is the first assertion.

If $f=f(x,u)$, then $\widehat f$ depends only on the ambient position $X$.  We apply the same one-sided Taylor estimate to the curve $X(t)$ alone.  Since $\lvert X'(0)\rvert=1$ and $\nabla_tX'(0)=-a\nu$, the semiconvex error is now bounded by a fixed constant rather than by $C(1+a^2)$.  Hence
\[
g''(0)\ge -aD_X\widehat f(\nu)-C,
\]
which proves the second assertion.

For the first derivative, differentiate the equation in an arbitrary principal direction $e_j$.  At the diagonal point,
\[
\frac{d}{ds}\sigma_k(A_u)=\sum_iF^{ii}h_{ii;j}.
\]
On the other hand,
\[
\frac{d}{ds}\widehat f(X,\nu)
 =D_X\widehat f(e_j)+D_\nu\widehat f(\nabla_{e_j}\nu)
 =D_X\widehat f(e_j)+\kappa_jD_\nu\widehat f(e_j),
\]
because $\nabla_{e_j}\nu=\kappa_j e_j$ in the principal frame.  This is \eqref{eq:source-first}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:jacobi}
Assume \eqref{eq:small-level}, and define
\begin{equation*}
\theta_1=\gamma-1,\qquad
\theta_i=\frac{a+\kappa_i}{a-\kappa_i}\quad (i\ge2).
\end{equation*}
For a general right-hand side $f(x,u,Du)$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L\ell\ge kfa-Q+\sum_iF^{ii}\theta_i\ell_i^2+D_\nu\widehat f(\nabla\ell)-C(1+a).
\label{eq:jacobi-general}
\end{equation}
If $f=f(x,u)$, then
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L\ell\ge kfa-Q+\sum_iF^{ii}\theta_i\ell_i^2-C.
\label{eq:jacobi-position}
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
All computations are made at the fixed point where the frame is orthonormal, geodesic, and diagonalizes $h$.  Put
\[
\xi_i=h_{ii;1}.
\]
The simple-eigenvalue formula in \eqref{eq:eigenvalue-second} gives
\begin{align*}
\mathcal L a
&=\sum_iF^{ii}h_{11;ii}
 +2\sum_i\sum_{p=2}^n\frac{F^{ii}}{a-\kappa_p}h_{1p;i}^2\\
&=\sum_iF^{ii}h_{ii;11}
 +a\sum_iF^{ii}\kappa_i(a-\kappa_i)
 +2\sum_i\sum_{p=2}^n\frac{F^{ii}}{a-\kappa_p}h_{1p;i}^2.
\end{align*}
Using $\sum_iF^{ii}\kappa_i=kf$ and the definition of $Q$,
\[
a\sum_iF^{ii}\kappa_i(a-\kappa_i)=a^2kf-aQ.
\]
Therefore
\begin{align*}
\mathcal L\ell
={}&kfa-Q+\frac1a\sum_iF^{ii}h_{ii;11}
 +\frac2a\sum_i\sum_{p=2}^n\frac{F^{ii}}{a-\kappa_p}h_{1p;i}^2
 -\frac1{a^2}\sum_iF^{ii}h_{11;i}^2.
\end{align*}
Apply \cref{cor:source-second} to the third term.  At a diagonal matrix the second derivative of $\sigma_k$ satisfies
\[
\sigma_k^{ij,rs}B_{ij}B_{rs}
 =\sum_{p\ne q}F^{pp,qq}B_{pp}B_{qq}
 -2\sum_{p<q}F^{pp,qq}B_{pq}^2.
\]
For $\kappa\in\Gamma_k$, the quantities $F^{pp,qq}=\sigma_{k-2}(\kappa|pq)$ are positive by the G\aa rding cone structure \cite{Garding1959}.  Hence the off-diagonal part contributes nonnegative terms after the preceding expression is inserted with a minus sign.

Separate the terms according to their indices.  Codazzi gives
\[
h_{1p;1}=h_{11;p}=a\ell_p,
\qquad
h_{1p;p}=h_{pp;1}=\xi_p,
\qquad
h_{11;1}=\xi_1=a\ell_1.
\]
The positive off-diagonal part coming from $-a^{-1}\sigma_k^{ij,rs}h_{ij;1}h_{rs;1}$ contains
\[
\frac2a\sum_{p=2}^nF^{11,pp}h_{1p;1}^2
 =2a\sum_{p=2}^nF^{11,pp}\ell_p^2,
\]
while the eigenvalue-variation term in the preceding identity contributes
\[
2a\sum_{p=2}^n\frac{F^{11}}{a-\kappa_p}\ell_p^2
 +\frac2a\sum_{p=2}^n\frac{F^{pp}}{a-\kappa_p}\xi_p^2.
\]
All terms with $2\le i\le n$, $i\ne p$, together with the off-diagonal terms indexed by $2\le p<q\le n$, are nonnegative and may be discarded.  Finally,
\[
-\frac1{a^2}\sum_iF^{ii}h_{11;i}^2
 =-\frac{F^{11}}{a^2}\xi_1^2-
 \sum_{p=2}^nF^{pp}\ell_p^2.
\]
For $p\ge2$, the total coefficient of $\ell_p^2$ is therefore
\begin{align*}
2aF^{11,pp}+\frac{2aF^{11}}{a-\kappa_p}-F^{pp}
&=\frac{2aF^{pp}}{a-\kappa_p}-F^{pp}\\
&=F^{pp}\frac{a+\kappa_p}{a-\kappa_p}.
\end{align*}
In the first equality we used
\[
F^{pp}-F^{11}=(a-\kappa_p)F^{11,pp}.
\]

At this point the remaining third-order expression is exactly the one appearing in $Q_\gamma$.  Add and subtract
\[
\frac{2}{af}\left(\sum_iF^{ii}\xi_i\right)^2
\]
and split
\[
-\frac{F^{11}}{a^2}\xi_1^2
 =-\gamma\frac{F^{11}}{a^2}\xi_1^2
 +(\gamma-1)F^{11}\ell_1^2.
\]
Using the definition \eqref{eq:Qgamma}, we obtain
\begin{align*}
\mathcal L\ell\ge{}&kfa-Q+Q_\gamma(\kappa;\xi)
 +(\gamma-1)F^{11}\ell_1^2
 +\sum_{p=2}^n\frac{a+\kappa_p}{a-\kappa_p}F^{pp}\ell_p^2\\
&\quad -\frac{2}{af}\left(\sum_iF^{ii}\xi_i\right)^2
 +D_\nu\widehat f(\nabla\ell)-C(1+a)
\end{align*}
in the gradient-dependent case.  In the position-dependent case the normal drift is absent and the last error improves to $-C$ after division by $a$ and use of \eqref{eq:a-lower}.

It remains to control the explicit negative square.  By \eqref{eq:source-first},
\[
\sum_iF^{ii}\xi_i=D_X\widehat f(e_1)+aD_\nu\widehat f(e_1).
\]
The $C^1$ norm of $\widehat f$ is bounded on the fixed compact range, while $f\ge f_0$.  Thus, in the general case,
\[
\frac{2}{af}\left(\sum_iF^{ii}\xi_i\right)^2
 \le C\frac{(1+a)^2}{a}
 \le C(1+a),
\]
where the last inequality uses the fixed positive lower bound \eqref{eq:a-lower}.  If $f=f(x,u)$, then $D_\nu\widehat f=0$, and the same term is bounded by $C/a\le C$.

Finally, \eqref{eq:small-level} and \cref{thm:yan} imply $Q_\gamma(\kappa;\xi)\ge0$.  Recalling the definition of $\theta_i$ gives \eqref{eq:jacobi-general} and \eqref{eq:jacobi-position}.

\label{lem:good-bad}
In either of the two settings just described, there are constants $c_G,C_B>0$ such that
\begin{equation}
\sum_iF^{ii}(\theta_i\ell_i^2+q\omega_i^2)
\ge c_G\sum_{i\in\mathcal G}F^{ii}G_i^2

\label{eq:goodbad-gradient}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}

\label{eq:Q-bad}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sum_iF^{ii}(\theta_i\ell_i^2+q\omega_i^2)

\label{eq:all-good}
\end{equation}

Two elementary square completions will be used.  If $\theta>0$, then
\[
\theta X^2+qY^2
=\frac{\theta}{1+q\theta}(X+qY)^2
 +\frac{q}{1+q\theta}(\theta X-Y)^2.
\]
Hence, whenever $\theta\ge1/2$,
\[
\theta X^2+qY^2\ge c(q)(X+qY)^2,
\qquad
c(q)=\frac{1/2}{1+q/2}>0.
\]
If $\theta=-d<0$ and $qd<1$, then
\[
-dX^2+qY^2
=-\frac{d}{1-qd}(X+qY)^2
 +\frac{q}{1-qd}(dX+Y)^2,
\]
so
\[
-dX^2+qY^2\ge -\frac{d}{1-qd}(X+qY)^2.
\]
Finally, if $0\le\theta<1/2$, the left-hand side is nonnegative, and therefore it is certainly bounded below by $-C(X+qY)^2$.

Apply these identities with $X=\ell_i$ and $Y=\omega_i$, so that $X+qY=G_i$.  It remains only to check that the denominator $1-qd$ stays uniformly positive whenever $\theta_i<0$. \qquad

\]

\theta_i> -\frac{n-2k}{n},
\]
so $d<(n-2k)/n$ and \eqref{eq:position-extra-margin} yields a uniform positive lower bound for $1-qd$.  Hence the negative coefficient in the preceding square completion is uniformly bounded.  Summing with weights $F^{ii}>0$ proves \eqref{eq:goodbad-gradient}.

\kappa_i<-\frac a3.

Thus $\kappa_i^2>a^2/9$ for every bad index, and

\]
so $D_r^+$ is nonempty and the weighted quantity used below is positive somewhere.

For \eqref{eq:eta-bounds},  Fix $(x,y)\in D_r$.  By \eqref{eq:dist-boundary}, the Euclidean ball $B_{r/(2M)}(x)$ lies in $\Omega$, so the line segment from $x$ to $y$ remains inside $\Omega$.  The gradient bound for $w$ gives
\[
w(y)-w(x)\le M\lvert y-x\rvert.
\]
Since $h(x)=w(x)-u(x)-\varepsilon$, putting $t=\lvert y-x\rvert$ gives
\begin{align*}
\eta(x,y)
&\le h(x)+Mt-\frac r2-\frac r{32}-\frac{8M^2}{r}t^2\\
&\le h(x)-\frac r2,
\end{align*}
because the quadratic polynomial $Mt-8M^2t^2/r$ has maximum $r/32$, attained at $t=r/(16M)$.  Since $h<2r$ on $E_r$, this also gives $\eta<3r/2$.

It remains to locate the maximum in the interior.  The closure of $E_r$ is compactly contained in $\Omega$.  Indeed, on $\overline E_r$ one has $\rho=\varepsilon+h\ge r/2$, while $\lvert D\rho\rvert\le M$ and $\rho=0$ on $\partial\Omega$ imply
\[
\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\ge\frac{\rho(x)}{M}\ge\frac{r}{2M}.
\]
Thus the closed set
\[
\overline D_r
=\left\{(x,y):x\in\overline E_r,
\ \lvert y-x\rvert\le\frac{r}{2M}\right\}
\]
is compact in $\Omega\times\Omega$.  The function
\[
\Psi(x,y)=Z(x)\max\{\eta(x,y),0\}^{\beta/2}
\]
is continuous on $\overline D_r$ and satisfies $\Psi(x_0,x_0)>0$, so it attains a positive maximum.

No boundary point can be a maximizer.  If $h=r/2$, then the first estimate above gives $\eta\le0$.  If $\lvert y-x\rvert=r/(2M)$, then
\[
Mt=\frac r2,
\qquad
\frac{8M^2}{r}t^2=2r,
\]
and hence, using $h\le2r$ on $\overline E_r$,
\[
\eta\le h-\frac r2-\frac r{32}+\frac r2-2r\le-\frac r{32}<0.
\]
The remaining part of $\partial E_r$ is $h=2r$.  By the maximality of $x_0$ in \eqref{eq:x0-max},
\[
Z(x)\le2^{-\beta}Z(x_0).
\]
Moreover $\eta(x,y)\le3r/2$, while $\eta(x_0,x_0)=15r/32$.  Therefore
\[
\frac{\Psi(x,y)}{\Psi(x_0,x_0)}
\le 2^{-\beta}\left(\frac{16}{5}\right)^{\beta/2}
=\left(\frac45\right)^{\beta/2}<1.
\]
Hence the positive maximum lies at an interior point $(x_*,y_*)$ with $\eta(x_*,y_*)>0$.  Maximizing $\Psi$ is equivalent there to maximizing $P=\log Z+(\beta/2)\log\eta$.

Finally differentiate $P$ in the $y$ variables.  Since $G$ depends only on $x$, the $y$-gradient of $\eta$ must vanish at the maximum:
\[
0=D_y\eta
 =Dw(y_*)-\frac{16M^2}{r}(y_*-x_*).
\]
Hence the first identity in \eqref{eq:ycritical} holds.  Since $\lvert Dw(y_*)\rvert\le M$,
\[
\lvert y_*-x_*\rvert
=\frac{r}{16M^2}\lvert Dw(y_*)\rvert
\le\frac{r}{16M},
\]
which is the second identity.

\label{lem:paired-second}
At the interior maximum of $P$, let

S=\sum_iF^{ii}\eta_i^2.

Then

\lvert \eta_i\rvert\le C(M),

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Write the paired unit tangent vectors in graph coordinates as
\[
e_i=(X_i,Du(x_*)\cdot X_i),
\qquad
e_i'=(Y_i,Dw(y_*)\cdot Y_i),
\]
and set $B_i=Y_i-X_i$.  Since $D_y\eta(x_*,y_*)=0$, \eqref{eq:ycritical} gives
\[
Dw(y_*)=\frac{16M^2}{r}(y_*-x_*).
\]
Differentiating $\eta$ once along the paired geodesics therefore yields
\begin{align*}
\eta_i
&=Dw(y_*)\cdot Y_i-Du(x_*)\cdot X_i
 -\frac{16M^2}{r}(y_*-x_*)\cdot(Y_i-X_i)\\
&=(Dw(y_*)-Du(x_*))\cdot X_i.
\end{align*}
Because $\nu_w=(Dw,-1)/W_w$,
\[
W_w\langle \nu_w,e_i\rangle
=Dw\cdot X_i-Du\cdot X_i,
\]
and hence
\[
\eta_i=W_w\langle \nu_w,e_i\rangle.
\]
Thus $\lvert \eta_i\rvert\le W_w\le\sqrt{1+M^2}$, which proves \eqref{eq:eta-first-bound}.

Next use the explicit formula for the natural rotation.  Since $e_i'=Re_i$ and $e_i\perp\nu_u$,
\[
e_i'-e_i
=-\frac{\langle \nu_w,e_i\rangle}{1+c}(\nu_u+\nu_w)
=-\frac{\eta_i}{W_w(1+c)}(\nu_u+\nu_w).
\]
The vector $B_i$ is the horizontal projection of $e_i'-e_i$, so
\begin{align}
\lvert B_i\rvert^2
&\le\lvert e_i'-e_i\rvert^2
 =\frac{\eta_i^2}{W_w^2(1+c)^2}\lvert \nu_u+\nu_w\rvert^2
 =\frac{2\eta_i^2}{W_w^2(1+c)}
\le C(M)\eta_i^2.
\label{eq:Bi-bound}
\end{align}
Here we used \eqref{eq:angle-lower}.

For the second derivative of $\eta$, put
\[
\alpha=\frac{8M^2}{r},
\qquad p=y_*-x_*.
\]
Let
\[
b_i=A_w(e_i',e_i').
\]
Because the curves on the two graphs are unit-speed geodesics, their ambient accelerations at $s=0$ are
\[
-\kappa_i\nu_u,
\qquad
-b_i\nu_w.
\]
Taking horizontal components gives
\[
x_i''(0)=-\frac{\kappa_i}{W_u}Du(x_*),
\qquad
y_i''(0)=-\frac{b_i}{W_w}Dw(y_*),
\]
while the second derivative of the vertical difference is
\[
\frac{d^2}{ds^2}\big(w(y_i(s))-u(x_i(s))\big)\Big|_{s=0}
=\frac{b_i}{W_w}-\frac{\kappa_i}{W_u}.
\]
The second derivative of the quadratic cost is
\[
-2\alpha\left(\lvert B_i\rvert^2+p\cdot(y_i''(0)-x_i''(0))\right).
\]
Since the $y$-critical relation is $2\alpha p=Dw(y_*)$, the acceleration part of the cost becomes
\begin{align*}
-2\alpha p\cdot(y_i''-x_i'')
&=-Dw\cdot\left(-\frac{b_i}{W_w}Dw
 +\frac{\kappa_i}{W_u}Du\right)\\
&=\frac{b_i}{W_w}\lvert Dw\rvert^2
 -\frac{\kappa_i}{W_u}Dw\cdot Du.
\end{align*}
Combining these formulas and using
\[
W_w^2=1+\lvert Dw\rvert^2,
\qquad
c=\langle \nu_u,\nu_w\rangle
 =\frac{1+Du\cdot Dw}{W_uW_w},
\]
we obtain
\begin{align*}
\eta_i''
&=\frac{b_i}{W_w}-\frac{\kappa_i}{W_u}
 +\frac{b_i}{W_w}\lvert Dw\rvert^2
 -\frac{\kappa_i}{W_u}Dw\cdot Du
 -2\alpha\lvert B_i\rvert^2\\
&=W_w\big(b_i-c\kappa_i\big)
 -\frac{16M^2}{r}\lvert B_i\rvert^2.
\end{align*}

Taking the $F$-trace,  Since $\{e_i\}$ diagonalizes $A_u$,
\[
\sum_iF^{ii}b_i
=D\sigma_k(A_u):R^*A_wR,
\qquad
\sum_iF^{ii}\kappa_i=kf.
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}
\sum_iF^{ii}\eta_i''
=W_w\left\{D\sigma_k(A_u):R^*A_wR-ckf\right\}
 -\frac{16M^2}{r}\sum_iF^{ii}\lvert B_i\rvert^2.
\label{eq:eta-second-trace}
\end{equation}
Because $R$ is an isometry and $A_w\in\Gamma_k$, the conjugate $R^*A_wR$ also lies in the matrix G\aa rding cone.  Concavity and one-homogeneity of $\sigma_k^{1/k}$ give the mixed G\aa rding inequality
\[
D\sigma_k(A_u):R^*A_wR
\ge k\sigma_k(A_u)^{(k-1)/k}\sigma_k(A_w)^{1/k}\ge0.
\]
Only the nonnegativity of this term is used; no positive lower bound for $\sigma_k(A_w)$ is required.  The remaining zero-order term satisfies
\[
-W_wckf\ge-C(n,k,M,f_1),
\]
while \eqref{eq:Bi-bound} gives
\[
-\frac{16M^2}{r}\sum_iF^{ii}\lvert B_i\rvert^2
\ge-\frac{C(M)}{r}\sum_iF^{ii}\eta_i^2
=-C\frac{S}{r}.
\]
Combining the last three estimates proves \eqref{eq:eta-second-bound}.

\label{rem:natural-rotation}
The natural rotation is essential. A fixed-coordinate contraction involving $D^2w$ at the same base point can have the wrong sign and arbitrarily large magnitude even for admissible $\Gamma_2$ jets with bounded slopes; see \cite[Remark 3.3]{QiuYan2026}. \label{prop:contact}
Under the assumptions of either \cref{thm:main} or \cref{thm:gradient}, $\beta$ can be fixed in terms of the corresponding theorem data so that

If \eqref{eq:small-level} fails at $(x_*,y_*)$, then \eqref{eq:smalllevel-fail} gives a fixed upper bound for $a(x_*)$.  Since \eqref{eq:eta-bounds} implies $\eta\le3r/2\le(3/2)\lVert \rho\rVert_{L^\infty}$, \eqref{eq:contact} follows immediately.  We may therefore assume throughout the remainder of the proof that \eqref{eq:small-level} holds.

At the product-space maximum, differentiating \eqref{eq:P} in the paired directions gives \eqref{eq:first-P}; hence
\[
G_i^2=\frac{\beta^2}{4\eta^2}\eta_i^2.
\]
Combine this relation with \eqref{eq:second-P} in the three parameter regimes.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Case 1: $f=f(x,u)$ and $2\le k<n$.}
By \eqref{eq:LG-position} and \cref{lem:good-bad},
\[
\mathcal L G\ge kf_0a+(q-1)Q
+c_G\sum_{i\in\mathcal G}F^{ii}G_i^2
-C_B\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}F^{ii}G_i^2-C.
\]
Set
\[
S_G=\sum_{i\in\mathcal G}F^{ii}\eta_i^2,
\qquad
S_B=\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}F^{ii}\eta_i^2.
\]
Substitution into \eqref{eq:second-P} yields
\[
0\ge kf_0a+(q-1)Q
+\left(\frac{c_G\beta^2}{4}-C\beta\right)\frac{S_G}{\eta^2}
-C(\beta^2+\beta)\frac{S_B}{\eta^2}
-C\frac\beta\eta-C.
\]
Choose $\beta=\beta(n,k,M)>2$ so large that the coefficient of $S_G/\eta^2$ is nonnegative.  By \eqref{eq:eta-first-bound} and \eqref{eq:Q-bad},
\[
S_B\le C(M)\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}F^{ii}
\le\frac{C(M)}{a^2}Q.
\]

\[
C(\beta^2+\beta)\frac{S_B}{\eta^2}
\le C\frac{\beta^2}{a^2\eta^2}Q,
\]
and the coefficient on the right is as small as desired once $K$ is chosen large.  If $a\eta<K\beta$, \eqref{eq:contact} already holds.  Otherwise we may absorb the bad term and obtain
\[
0\ge kf_0a+\frac{q-1}{2}Q-C\frac\beta\eta-C.
\]
Dropping the nonnegative $Q$ term and multiplying by $\eta$ gives
\[
kf_0a\eta\le C\beta+C\eta.
\]
Since $\eta\le(3/2)\lVert \rho\rVert_{L^\infty}$ and $\beta$ is fixed, this proves \eqref{eq:contact}.

\noindent\emph{Case 2: $f=f(x,u,Du)$ and $k>n/2$.}
By \cref{lem:weights-general,lem:good-bad}, after absorbing the linear loss as in \eqref{eq:linear-absorb},
\[
\mathcal L G\ge c_0a+\mu Q+c_G\sum_iF^{ii}G_i^2
+D_\nu\widehat f(\nabla G)-C.

\[
\nabla G=-\frac\beta{2\eta}\sum_i\eta_i e_i.

Thus, using the $C^1$ bound for $\widehat f$ and \eqref{eq:eta-first-bound},
\[
D_\nu\widehat f(\nabla G)
=-\frac\beta{2\eta}\sum_iD_\nu\widehat f(e_i)\eta_i
\ge-C\frac\beta\eta.

\sum_iF^{ii}G_i^2=\frac{\beta^2}{4\eta^2}S.

+\left(\frac{c_G\beta^2}{4}-C\beta\right)\frac{S}{\eta^2}
-C\frac\beta\eta-C.

Choose $\beta>2$ so that the coefficient of $S/\eta^2$ is nonnegative.  Dropping the nonnegative terms then yields

Fix the value of $q$ prescribed by the corresponding case above, and choose $\beta>2$ as in \cref{prop:contact}.  Since $1\le W_u\le\sqrt{1+M^2}$,
\[
a\le Z=aW_u^q\le C(M,q)a.
\]
At the product-space maximum, \cref{prop:contact} gives $a\eta\le C$, while \eqref{eq:eta-bounds} gives $\eta\le3r/2$.  Hence
\begin{align*}
Z(x_*)\eta(x_*,y_*)^{\beta/2}
&\le C a(x_*)\eta(x_*,y_*)^{\beta/2}\\
&=C\big(a\eta\big)(x_*,y_*)
  \eta(x_*,y_*)^{\beta/2-1}\\
&\le C r^{\beta/2-1}.
\end{align*}
The exponent is positive because $\beta>2$.

The maximality of $P$ and the diagonal value
\[
\eta(x_0,x_0)=\frac{15r}{32}
\]
therefore imply
\[
Z(x_0)\left(\frac{15r}{32}\right)^{\beta/2}
\le Z(x_*)\eta(x_*,y_*)^{\beta/2}
\le Cr^{\beta/2-1}.
\]
After cancelling $r^{\beta/2}$ we obtain
\[
rZ(x_0)\le C.
\]
By the definition of $x_0$ in \eqref{eq:x0-max},
\begin{align*}
\sup_{\Omega_\varepsilon}h^\beta a
&\le\sup_{\Omega_\varepsilon}h^\beta Z
 =r^\beta Z(x_0)\\
&\le Cr^{\beta-1}
 \le C\lVert \rho\rVert_{L^\infty(\Omega)}^{\beta-1}.
\end{align*}
All constants are independent of $\varepsilon$.

Fix $x\in\Omega$.  Since $\rho(x)>0$, for every sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$ one has $x\in\Omega_\varepsilon$, and $h(x)=\rho(x)-\varepsilon\to\rho(x)$.  Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ in the preceding estimate gives
\[
\rho(x)^\beta\kappa_1[u](x)\le C.
\]
Taking the supremum over $x$ yields
\[
\sup_\Omega\rho^\beta\kappa_1[u]\le C.
\]
Finally, because $k\ge2$ and $A_u\in\Gamma_k\subset\Gamma_2$, \cref{lem:cone} gives
\[
\lvert A_u\rvert<H\le n\kappa_1[u].
\]
Therefore
\[
\sup_\Omega\rho^\beta\lvert A_u\rvert\le C.
\]
The preceding proof also shows the stated dependence of the constants.  In particular, the comparison graph enters at second order only in \cref{lem:paired-second}, where the mixed G\aa rding inequality uses merely $A_w\in\Gamma_k$ and never a quantitative lower bound for $\sigma_k(A_w)$ or an upper bound for $D^2w$.
