<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:intro-fixed-ratio}
For every monic polynomial \(P\) of degree \(d\ge2\) and every \(\alpha\in(0,d)\), there is a probability measure \(\mu_{\alpha,P}\) supported in \(\mathsf H_P\) such that for any sequence \(\{m_n\}\) satisfying \(0\le m_n<dn\) with \(m_n/n\to\alpha\)

 \smallskip
\noindent
{\rm(i)}
\[
 \mu_{P;m_n,n}^{\mathrm{root}}\Longrightarrow\mu_{\alpha,P},\qquad
 Z(R_{P;m_n,n})\longrightarrow\operatorname{supp}\mu_{\alpha,P}
 \quad\text{in Hausdorff distance}.
\]

\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm(ii)} Moreover, if \(\{P_n\}\) is a sequence of monic polynomials of degree \(d\) converging coefficientwise to \(P\), i.e. \(P_n\to P\), then
\[
 \mu_{P_n;m_n,n}^{\mathrm{root}}\Longrightarrow\mu_{\alpha,P},\qquad
 Z(R_{P_n;m_n,n})\longrightarrow\operatorname{supp}\mu_{\alpha,P}
 \quad\text{in Hausdorff distance}.
\]
\medskip

\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm(iii)} The measure \(\mu_{\alpha,P}\) depends only on \((P,\alpha)\). It is jointly weakly continuous in these parameters, and its support is jointly Hausdorff-continuous.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm(iv)} At fixed degree, convergence of test-function integrals is uniform on compact coefficient families and compact subintervals of \((0,d)\); the corresponding support convergence is uniform in Hausdorff distance.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The above integral is understood in the weak sense: its integral against a continuous test function is the corresponding scalar integral of the test-function integrals. The weight reflects the degree \(dn-m\) of each descendant. For constants and polynomials with one distinct root, the core is, respectively, empty or the root singleton.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:intro-sets}
For any sequence \(\{P_n\}\) of monic degree \(d\) polynomials such that \(P_n\to P\) coefficientwise,

\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm (i)}
\[
 \overline\mu_{P_n,n}^{\mathrm{root}}\Longrightarrow\rho_P,
 \qquad \operatorname{supp}\rho_P=\mathcal K_P,
\]
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-set-limits-intro}
 \mathcal Z_{P_n,n}\longrightarrow\mathcal K_P,
 \qquad \mathcal F_{P_n,n}\longrightarrow\mathcal S_P
 \quad\text{in Hausdorff distance}.
\end{equation}

\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm (ii)}
The maps \(P\mapsto\rho_P\), \(P\mapsto\mathcal K_P\), and \(P\mapsto\mathcal S_P\) are continuous in their respective topologies. For each continuous test function, the measure convergence is uniform on compact coefficient families of fixed degree, and both set convergences are uniform in Hausdorff distance. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail-core-intro}
 \mathcal K_P=\bigcap_{M\ge1}
       \overline{\bigcup_{n\ge M}\mathcal Z_{P,n}}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:p1-perfect-summary} For any polynomial \(P\) of degree at least \(2\),

\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm (i)}
The limiting core \(\mathcal K_P\) is a compact connected subset of \(\mathsf H_P\) containing all roots and all critical points of \(P\), as well as the centroid of its root multiset.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm (ii)}
Every point of \(T_P\) is an actual finite-level zero, is isolated in \(\mathcal S_P\), and occurs at only finitely many power levels. The set \(T_P\) is locally finite in \(\mathbb C\setminus\mathcal K_P\): every compact subset of \(\mathbb C\setminus\mathcal K_P\) meets it in finitely many points. Consequently the limiting core and the full shadow of \(P\) have the same interior and planar area.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm (iii)}
If \(P\) has at least two distinct roots, then
\[
 \operatorname{acc}(\mathcal S_P)=\mathcal K_P=\operatorname{acc}(\mathcal K_P),
\]
and \(\mathcal K_P\) is the perfect kernel of \(\mathcal S_P\). For every nonconstant \(P\),
\[
 \mathcal K_P=\bigcap_{k\ge1}\mathcal S_{P^k},
 \qquad \mathcal S_{P^k}\longrightarrow\mathcal K_P
 \quad\text{in Hausdorff distance as }k\to\infty.
\]
\smallskip
\noindent
{\rm (iv)}
The family \(\{\mathcal S_{P^k}\}\) is nested along divisibility,
\[
 k\mid j\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \mathcal S_{P^j}\subseteq\mathcal S_{P^k},
\]
but need not be nested in the natural order \(k=1,2,\ldots\); indeed, \(\mathcal S_{P^2}\) and \(\mathcal S_{P^3}\) can be incomparable.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
By Theorem~\ref{thm:p1-perfect-summary}(i) the set \(\mathcal K_P\) is a canonical connected refinement of \(\operatorname{conv} Z(P)\) from the point of view of the Gauss--Lucas theorem, i.e., it is a canonically defined connected subset of \(\operatorname{conv} Z(P)\) containing all the critical points of \(P\), while \(\mathcal S_P\) retains the exact finite-level exceptions. Moreover, in many numerical experiments the critical points of \(P\) lie on the boundary of \(\mathcal K_P\). Therefore \(\mathcal K_P\) might play an important role in the geometry of polynomials. For classical background on the geometry of polynomial zeros and critical points, see \cite[Chapters~I--II]{Marden}.

\label{thm:intro-infinite}
For \(0<a<4\), let
\[
 \Pi_a(z)=z^4+az^3-(a-a^2/8)z^2+az+1.
\]
Its core is disjoint from \((0,\infty)\). For every sufficiently large odd \(n\), the polynomial \((\Pi_a^n)^{(2n)}\) has a simple positive zero
\[
 x_n=\frac{\sigma_a}{n}+O(n^{-2}),
\]
where \(\sigma_a\) is the unique solution in \((0,\pi/\sqrt{16-a^2})\) of
\[
 e^{(a+4)\sigma_a/2}
   =\frac4{\sqrt{4-a}}
       \cos\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{16-a^2}}2\sigma_a\right).
\]
Hence \(T_{\Pi_a}\) is infinite. Every sufficiently large even power level also contains a positive transient, after a bounded shift of the derivative order from \(2n\).

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:barrier}
For any real \(\ell\), the set \(E_\ell\) has at most two connected components. For sufficiently large positive \(\ell\) it has two, one containing \(z\) and one containing infinity respectively. For sufficiently large negative \(\ell\) the set \(E_\ell\) is connected. If \(B=\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)\), then \(E_\ell\) is connected for \(\ell\le B\) and has two components for \(\ell>B\). Moreover,
\[
 B=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathscr J_z}\max_{w\in\gamma}L(w).
\]
The value \(B\) is a critical height attained at a finite saddle.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Every superlevel component contains a positive end. Otherwise the component is compactly contained in the harmonicity domain of \(L\) and has nonempty interior: a regular boundary point has a superlevel side, whereas an isolated stationary point cannot be a local maximum of a nonconstant harmonic function by the maximum principle. There are only two positive ends, so there are at most two components.

For large positive \(\ell\), the superlevel set consists of disjoint neighborhoods of \(z\) and infinity. For sufficiently negative \(\ell\), it contains the complement of small disjoint disks about the roots; an additional component would again have to contain a positive end. It is therefore connected. As the level decreases, the positive-end components can merge but cannot separate. The connected levels form a half-line whose endpoint belongs to it, by the nested-intersection property of compact connected sets. The high- and low-level descriptions show that this endpoint is finite.

For \(\ell<B\), there is a path in \(E_\ell\) from \(z\) to infinity, after an arbitrarily small decrease in the level if needed. Every curve in \(\mathscr J_z\) meets this path, and hence has maximum at least \(\ell\). Conversely, if \(\ell>B\) is regular, a Jordan curve in \(\{L<\ell\}\) separates the two compact superlevel components. One may take the outer boundary of a small regular neighborhood of the component containing \(z\) and perturb it to avoid the finitely many roots. Letting \(\ell\downarrow B\) proves the min--max formula.

Finally, on a compact level band without saddles, the vector field \(\nabla L/|\nabla L|^2\) identifies neighboring levels. Finite level bands avoid all ends. Their topology can therefore change only at a saddle, so \(B\) is a critical height.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:barrier-cont}
For monic polynomials \(P\) of a fixed degree \(d\), \(\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)\) is jointly continuous in the coefficients of \(P\), in \(z\notin Z(P)\), and in \(0<\alpha<d\).
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Write \(B=\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)\). For the upper estimate, choose a curve in \(\mathscr J_z\) whose maximum is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(B\). The curve avoids the roots, the landscape varies uniformly on it, and its winding number about the moving observation point is unchanged by a small perturbation.

For the lower estimate, join the positive ends by a path in \(\{L>B-\varepsilon\}\) and truncate it in small neighborhoods of \(z\) and infinity. The remaining compact path avoids all roots and persists under perturbation. The end neighborhoods can be chosen so that the perturbed landscapes are uniformly above \(B-2\varepsilon\): the polynomial stays nonzero near the moving observation point, and \(\alpha\) and \(d-\alpha\) stay positive. Reattach the path to the two ends and apply \eqref{eq:barrier-def}. Since \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary, the two estimates prove continuity. Root collisions away from \(z\) do not affect the argument.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:not-max}
A critical height can exceed \(B\): the barrier is not the maximum over all saddles. Nor does equality with \(B\) force a saddle to contribute to a contour. What matters is contact between the two positive-end components. The distinction already occurs for quadratic polynomials.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:full-boundary-cycle}
Let \(K\subset\mathbb C\) be nonempty, compact, connected, and \emph{regular closed}, that is, \(K=\overline{\operatorname{int}K}\). Assume that \(U:=\mathbb C\setminus K\) is connected, and suppose that \(\partial K\) is contained in a finite embedded graph \(G\). At every vertex \(v\in\partial K\), assume that the local sectors belonging to \(U\) form one nonempty proper cyclic block. Then \(\partial K\) is a single Jordan cycle in \(G\). At each vertex it uses exactly the two half-edges that bound the \(U\)-block.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
In a small neighborhood of a regular point of \(G\), each of the two sides of the edge lies entirely in \(U\) or entirely in \(\operatorname{int}K\), because \(\partial K\subset G\). If both sides lie in \(U\), regular closedness implies that the intervening edge also lies in \(U\): a point of \(K\) there could not be approached by interior points of \(K\). If both sides lie in \(\operatorname{int}K\), closedness of \(K\) puts the whole neighborhood in \(K\). Thus a regular point lies in \(\partial K\) exactly when the two sides have opposite labels, and then the boundary is locally the entire edge. In particular, each open edge of \(G\) is either wholly in \(\partial K\) or disjoint from it.

The same argument applies to the half-edges in a vertex neighborhood: a half-edge belongs to \(\partial K\) exactly when its adjacent sectors have different labels. Half-edges between two \(U\)-sectors belong to \(U\), so no boundary slit is overlooked. The single nonempty proper \(U\)-block therefore has exactly two boundary half-edges. Every boundary vertex has degree two in the boundary subgraph. This is a nonempty finite embedded graph with degree two at every vertex.

The plane is unicoherent: two closed connected sets covering it have connected intersection. Applying this to \(K\) and \(\overline U\) shows that \(\partial K=K\cap\overline U\) is connected. A connected finite graph of degree two is a single cycle, and its embedded realization is a Jordan curve. This includes a loop edge or a pair of parallel edges.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:outer-regularization}
Let \(G=\{L_{P,z,\alpha}=B\}\), let \(\Omega_{\mathrm{ext}}\) be the unbounded component of \(\mathbb C\setminus\overline{\Omega_z}\), and set
\[
 \widehat{\Omega}_z:=\mathbb C\setminus\Omega_{\mathrm{ext}}.
\]
Thus \(\widehat{\Omega}_z\) is the filled set of \(\overline{\Omega_z}\). Then \(\partial\widehat{\Omega}_z\) is one Jordan cycle in the finite level graph \(G\). Every connecting saddle lies on this cycle and is visited once. At such a saddle the higher sectors belonging to \(\Omega_z\) and \(\Omega_\infty\) form two cyclic blocks. The two lower sectors between these blocks are the exterior passages on the two sides of the cycle.

Choose disjoint small disks about the vertices of this cycle and disjoint thin collars about its remaining edges. Its edges can be moved into \(\{L_{P,z,\alpha}<B\}\) within the collars. In each nonconnecting vertex disk they can be joined in the lower sector on the exterior side. In a connecting vertex disk they can instead be joined through the vertex along any prescribed pair of central rays in the two exterior lower sectors. These replacements give a single Jordan curve, with no repeated vertex or additional component.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The level set \(G\) stays in a compact region away from all ends. The reduced saddle equation gives only finitely many critical points. At a regular point the real-analytic implicit-function theorem gives one smooth level arc, while at a saddle the phase normal form gives finitely many analytic branches. If \(G\) had infinitely many distinct edges or closed regular components, points on them would accumulate in \(G\); the corresponding local normal form would then contain more than its one regular branch or its finitely many saddle branches. Thus \(G\) is a finite embedded graph.

Put \(K=\widehat{\Omega}_z\) and \(U=\Omega_{\mathrm{ext}}\). Then \(K\) is compact and connected, \(U=\mathbb C\setminus K\) is connected, and \(\partial K\subseteq\partial\Omega_z\subseteq G\). Moreover \(\Omega_\infty\subset U\), so filling cannot capture the infinity component. The set \(\overline{\Omega_z}\) is regular closed. Each bounded component of its complement is open and is included in \(K\); together with \(\Omega_z\) these components have closure \(K\). Thus \(K\) is also regular closed, as required in Lemma~\ref{lem:full-boundary-cycle}.

We use the following noncrossing observation. Two disjoint connected open sets cannot occupy four alternating sectors at one vertex. Indeed, join two sector points in each open set, truncate the two disjoint arcs outside a small disk, and extend their endpoints radially to the circle. In the complementary disk on the sphere, two disjoint arcs would then join alternating boundary points, contrary to the Jordan curve theorem.

At a connecting saddle, apply this observation to \(\Omega_z\) and \(\Omega_\infty\), reading only the cyclic order of the higher sectors. Since every higher sector has one of these two labels and both labels occur, the sectors carrying each label form one cyclic block.

Now consider all local sectors at a vertex of \(\partial K\). Every sector outside \(\overline{\Omega_z}\) belongs to one component of \(\mathbb C\setminus\overline{\Omega_z}\). Two adjacent such sectors must have the same component label: otherwise a thin neighborhood crossing the intervening level ray, but avoiding the vertex, would join them in \(\mathbb C\setminus\overline{\Omega_z}\). Hence sectors with different complement labels are separated in the cyclic order by an \(\Omega_z\)-sector. Consequently, if the unbounded component \(U\) occurred in two cyclic blocks, each of the two intervals between those blocks would contain an \(\Omega_z\)-sector. This would give four alternating sectors belonging to the two connected open sets \(U\) and \(\Omega_z\), contrary to the noncrossing observation. Thus the sectors belonging to \(U\) form one block at every vertex of \(\partial K\).

Lemma~\ref{lem:full-boundary-cycle} now shows that \(\partial K\) is one Jordan cycle and that each of its vertices is visited once. At a connecting saddle, the two interfaces between the \(\Omega_z\)- and \(\Omega_\infty\)-blocks are lower sectors in \(U\); these are precisely the two exterior passages. Every connecting saddle lies on the cycle, because it is approached both from \(\Omega_z\) and from \(\Omega_\infty\subset U\).

For the regularization, choose vertex disks with disjoint closures, each meeting the cycle only in its two incident arcs, and choose disjoint rectangular collars around the remaining edges. Move each regular edge slightly into its lower \(U\)-side. In a nonconnecting vertex disk, join the displaced ends inside the unique exterior lower sector. In a connecting disk, join each displaced end to the prescribed central ray in its exterior lower sector and join the two rays at the vertex. Each replacement is a simple arc with the same two boundary endpoints as the original cycle segment. The disks and collars are disjoint, so the replacements preserve one Jordan curve and introduce neither a repeated vertex nor an additional component. The curve keeps \(z\) on its bounded side and infinity on its unbounded side.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:arbitrary-contour}
There is a barrier contour \(\gamma\) for \((P,z,\alpha)\). Near each \(w_j\) there is a holomorphic coordinate \(v_j\), with \(v_j(w_j)=0\) and \(v_j'(w_j)\ne0\), such that
\[
 \Phi(w)=\Xi_j-v_j(w)^{q_j}.
\]
The contour enters and leaves \(w_j\) along distinct decreasing rays \(v_j=\zeta_j^-t\) and \(v_j=\zeta_j^+t\), \(t\ge0\), where \((\zeta_j^\pm)^{q_j}=1\). Outside small coordinate neighborhoods its compact remainder has a strict height gap below \(B=\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)\).
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Use the local coordinates constructed above and the two lower sectors between the higher-sector blocks identified in Lemma~\ref{lem:outer-regularization}. Apply that lemma with their central rays prescribed in each connecting disk. The resulting curve winds once about \(z\), avoids all roots, and has \(L<B\) except at the connecting saddles. On the compact remainder outside smaller disks, the continuous function \(B-L\) is strictly positive, hence has a positive minimum. This is the claimed gap. When \(k=1\) and \(q_1=2\), the two rays form a smooth descending axis and the contour is a one-saddle separating contour.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:multiple-gates}
Suppose the connecting saddles at \((P,z_0,\alpha_0)\) are simple, and enumerate them as \(w_1,\ldots,w_k\). Other saddles may be multiple or have coincident heights. The connecting saddles continue simply on a parameter neighborhood, with real critical heights \(\psi_j(z,\alpha)\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multiple-gate-max}
 \mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)=\max_{1\le j\le k}\psi_j(z,\alpha).
\end{equation}
This description persists under coefficient perturbation. If \(k=1\), the saddle has a locally persistent one-saddle separating contour.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Choose disjoint small Morse disks about the connecting saddles at the base parameter. At each saddle, the two higher-level sectors lead to different positive ends. Join them to the ends within the corresponding strict components. Outside the saddle disk, the compact part of each path has a positive height margin. These paths above the barrier and their end attachments persist under perturbation. The path through \(w_j\) therefore proves \(\mathcal B_P\ge\psi_j\) nearby.

For the reverse inequality use the contour of Lemma~\ref{lem:arbitrary-contour} at the base parameter. It passes through all connecting saddles once and has a strict height gap away from their disjoint Morse disks. Its single outer-boundary cycle is supplied by Lemma~\ref{lem:outer-regularization}.

After smaller saddle neighborhoods are removed, the compact remainder has a strict gap. Under perturbation, it and its connections to the local decreasing arcs remain below every nearby \(\psi_j\). The arcs pass through the continued simple saddles, so the maximum on the perturbed contour is \(\max_j\psi_j\). The min--max formula proves the upper bound in \eqref{eq:multiple-gate-max}. For \(k=1\) this is a one-saddle separating contour. All constructions persist in the coefficients as well as in \((z,\alpha)\).
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:regular}
Fix \(P\) and \(0<\alpha<d\). Almost every observation point, with respect to planar Lebesgue measure, is regular. At a regular point there is exactly one saddle at barrier height, and it has a locally persistent one-saddle separating contour. Persistence holds also under coefficient perturbations.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The numerator and denominator of \(F_{P,\alpha}\) are coprime, since \(\alpha\ne0\) and \(Q_P,N_P\) are coprime. This rational map therefore has degree \(s\). Its branch values, meaning images of its critical points, form a finite set. Away from them and the roots of \(P\), write the inverse branches locally as \(\omega_1(z),\ldots,\omega_s(z)\). Choose local logarithms and put
\[
 \Xi_i(z,\alpha):=\Phi_{P,z,\alpha}(\omega_i(z,\alpha)).
\]
The saddle equation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase-z}
 \partial_z\Xi_i=\frac{\alpha}{\omega_i-z}.
\end{equation}
For \(i\ne j\) the derivative of \(\Xi_i-\Xi_j\) never vanishes. Equality of their real critical heights thus defines a smooth real-analytic arc locally and has planar measure zero. A countable cover proves the almost-everywhere assertion.

At a regular point, Lemma~\ref{lem:barrier} puts the barrier at a critical height. Distinctness of the heights leaves one connecting saddle, and regularity makes it simple. Lemma~\ref{lem:arbitrary-contour} provides the one-saddle separating contour; Lemma~\ref{lem:multiple-gates} provides its persistence. In the quadratic normal coordinate the landscape has the form \(B+\operatorname{Re}(\zeta^2)\), and the contour follows the imaginary axis, where it equals \(B-|\zeta|^2\). This is the separating-contour geometry used in \cite[Section~4.2]{BHS}; the argument here only requires the signs at the ends, not simple roots of \(P\) or \(P'\).

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:harmonic-gate}
Fix \(P\) and \(0<\alpha<d\). If \(\mathcal B_P(\cdot,\alpha)\) is harmonic on an open disk \(D\subset\mathbb C\setminus Z(P)\), then every \(z\in D\) has a one-saddle separating contour whose unique maximum is one simple saddle. The contour and its strict gap away from the saddle persist under small changes of \(P\), \(z\), and \(\alpha\). No simplicity or distinct-height condition is imposed on the other saddles.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Fix \(z_0\in D\), put \(L_0=L_{P,z_0,\alpha}\) and \(B_0=\mathcal B_P(z_0,\alpha)\), and choose a connecting saddle \(w_0\). Its local level set has finitely many alternating higher and lower sectors. Join a higher sector from \(\Omega_{z_0}\) to one from \(\Omega_\infty\) through \(w_0\), and continue within the strict components to the ends. Truncation gives a compact path \(\gamma_0\) on which \(L_0\) has its unique minimum \(B_0\) at \(w_0\). The end attachments stay strictly above \(B_0\) under perturbation, so for \(z\) near \(z_0\),
\[
 \mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)\ge \underline B(z):=\min_{w\in\gamma_0}L_{P,z,\alpha}(w),
 \qquad \underline B(z_0)=B_0.
\]
The truncated path avoids \(z_0\) and all roots. Its unique minimizing point and uniform first-order parameter expansion give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gate-gradient}
 \underline B(z_0+\delta)=B_0+
 \operatorname{Re}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{w_0-z_0}\delta\right)+o(|\delta|).
\end{equation}
More explicitly, minimizers for \(z_0+\delta\) approach \(w_0\) by uniqueness and compactness. Evaluating first at \(w_0\) and then at a perturbed minimizer gives the two bounds in \eqref{eq:gate-gradient}.

The barrier is differentiable at \(z_0\). Compare its differential with that of the lower touching function in the directions \(\delta\) and \(-\delta\). We obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gate-selected}
 2\partial_z\mathcal B_P(z_0,\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{w_0-z_0}.
\end{equation}
Distinct connecting saddles would give distinct right-hand sides. There is therefore exactly one connecting saddle at each point of \(D\), and the derivative in \eqref{eq:gate-selected} never vanishes. Harmonicity makes
\[
 w(z):=z+\frac{\alpha}{2\partial_z\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)}
\]
holomorphic on \(D\). This is the connecting saddle, and \(F_{P,\alpha}(w(z))=z\). Differentiation gives \(F_{P,\alpha}'(w(z))w'(z)=1\), so the saddle is simple. Lemma~\ref{lem:multiple-gates}, with \(k=1\), now supplies the one-saddle separating contour and its persistence in every parameter.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:uniform-saddle}
Suppose \((z_0,\alpha_0)\) admits a one-saddle separating contour as in Lemma~\ref{lem:regular} or Lemma~\ref{lem:harmonic-gate}. On a neighborhood, let \(w_*(z,\alpha)\) be the separating saddle and put \(\Xi_*(z,\alpha)=\Phi_{P,z,\alpha}(w_*(z,\alpha))\). These functions can be chosen holomorphic in \(z\) and real-analytic in \(\alpha\). There is a nonvanishing amplitude \(\mathfrak a(z,\alpha)\) of the same regularity such that, at the exact ratio \(\alpha=m/n\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:saddle-asymptotic}
 \frac{R_{P;m,n}(z)}{m!}
 =\frac{e^{n\Xi_*(z,\alpha)}}{\sqrt n}
 \bigl(\mathfrak a(z,\alpha)+O(n^{-1})\bigr),
 \qquad \mathfrak a(z,\alpha)\ne0.
\end{equation}
The expansion is uniform on smaller compact neighborhoods, also when the monic degree-\(d\) polynomial varies in its coefficients. In particular, the Rodrigues descendants are zero-free on such compact parameter sets for all sufficiently large \(n\).
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Cauchy's formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cauchy}
 \frac{R_{P;m,n}(z)}{m!}=
 \frac1{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma
 \exp\bigl(n[\log P(w)-\alpha\log(w-z)]\bigr)
 \frac{\,\mathrm d w}{w-z}.
\end{equation}
At the exact ratio \(\alpha=m/n\) the integrand is single-valued, so the value of the integral is independent of the local logarithm branches. Shrink the parameter neighborhood and then take a compact subset of it. Persistence of the simple saddle and of the separating contour provides a family \(\gamma_\lambda\), where \(\lambda=(P,z,\alpha)\), with uniformly bounded length and a number \(c>0\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-saddle-gap}
 L_\lambda(w)\le \operatorname{Re}\Xi_\lambda-c
\end{equation}
away from one fixed-size saddle disk. The contour tubes and the saddle disks stay a positive distance from \(z\) and from every root lying on them. This remains true under coefficient perturbation, even if roots split elsewhere.

The holomorphic Morse lemma with parameters gives a coordinate \(t\mapsto W_\lambda(t)\), defined for \(|t|<2\delta\) with one common \(\delta>0\), such that
\[
 W_\lambda(0)=w_*(\lambda),\qquad
 \Phi_\lambda(W_\lambda(t))=\Xi_\lambda-t^2.
\]
Choose the sign of \(t\) so that a short oriented passage of \(\gamma_\lambda\) enters the negative descending sector and leaves the positive one. In the Morse coordinate, replace this passage by \([-\delta,\delta]\) and two straight endpoint connectors within those sectors; a local primitive preserves the integral. The parameter interval and sector margins can be chosen uniformly, and both the connectors and the complementary contour arc have a uniform strict height gap. Include the connectors in the remainder, reducing \(c\) if necessary. All derivatives of \(W_\lambda\) needed below are uniformly bounded, and \(|W_\lambda'(0)|\) and \(|W_\lambda(0)-z|\) are uniformly bounded away from zero. The local contribution to \eqref{eq:cauchy} is therefore
\[
 \frac{e^{n\Xi_\lambda}}{2\pi i}
 \int_{-\delta}^{\delta}e^{-nt^2}A_\lambda(t)\,\mathrm d t,
 \qquad
 A_\lambda(t):=\frac{W_\lambda'(t)}{W_\lambda(t)-z}.
\]
Write \(A_\lambda(t)=A_\lambda(0)+A_{1,\lambda}t+t^2E_\lambda(t)\), with \(E_\lambda\) uniformly bounded. The odd term integrates to zero, and comparison with the full Gaussian integral gives, uniformly in \(\lambda\),
\[
 \int_{-\delta}^{\delta}e^{-nt^2}A_\lambda(t)\,\mathrm d t
 =\sqrt{\frac\pi n}\left(A_\lambda(0)+O(n^{-1})\right).
\]
By \eqref{eq:uniform-saddle-gap}, the remainder of the contour is \(O(e^{n(\operatorname{Re}\Xi_\lambda-c)})\) in absolute value, uniformly in all parameters; after the factor \(e^{n\Xi_\lambda}n^{-1/2}\) is removed, this is \(O(n^{-1})\).

Consequently \eqref{eq:saddle-asymptotic} holds with
\[
 \mathfrak a(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2\pi i}
 \frac{W_\lambda'(0)}{w_*(\lambda)-z}.
\]
The orientation is incorporated in the chosen sign of \(t\), so the two local halves add rather than cancel. The displayed amplitude is nonzero and has a uniform positive lower bound on compact parameter sets. The parameter-dependent Morse lemma gives its stated regularity, and all constants above remain uniform for nearby monic degree-\(d\) coefficients. This proves both the expansion and the uniform zero-free conclusion.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:pot-compact}
If probability measures \(\nu_n\) are supported in one compact set and \(\nu_n\Longrightarrow\nu\), then \(U_{\nu_n}\to U_\nu\) in \(L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb C)\). Their potentials are uniformly integrable on each bounded planar set. The same assertion holds for any family with that common support whose closure is weakly compact.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Truncate the kernel to \(\max\{\log|z-\xi|,\log\varepsilon\}\). On compact products this is bounded and continuous, so the corresponding potentials converge in \(L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}\). The discarded part has \(L^1\) norm at most a constant times \(\varepsilon^2(1+|\log\varepsilon|)\), uniformly in \(\xi\). For uniform integrability, integrate \(|\log|z-\xi||\) on a set of small area and split at \(|z-\xi|=\varepsilon\). The near part has the preceding uniform bound, and the far part is bounded by a constant times the area. Fubini's theorem proves the assertions.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:fixed}
Let \(P\) be monic of degree \(d\ge2\), and let \(0\le m_n<dn\) with \(m_n/n\to\alpha\in(0,d)\). Then
\[
 \mu_{P;m_n,n}^{\mathrm{root}}=\frac1{dn-m_n}\operatorname{div}_0 R_{P;m_n,n}
\]
converges weakly to a probability measure \(\mu_{\alpha,P}\) supported in \(\mathsf H_P\). The logarithmic potentials converge in \(L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}\) to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-potential-intro}
 U_{\mu_{\alpha,P}}(z)=
 \frac{\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)-\mathfrak h_d(\alpha)}{d-\alpha}.
\end{equation}
At a root, the right-hand side means its subharmonic extension. The limit is independent of the integer sequence. More generally, if monic degree-\(d\) polynomials \(P_n\) converge in coefficients to \(P\), then the normalized root-counting measures of \((P_n^n)^{(m_n)}\) have the same limit \(\mu_{\alpha,P}\).
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Gauss--Lucas gives a common compact support. For almost every \(z\), Lemma~\ref{lem:regular} applies at \((z,\alpha)\), and Proposition~\ref{prop:uniform-saddle} applies at \((z,m_n/n)\) for large \(n\). Therefore
\[
 \frac1n\log\left|\frac{R_{P;m_n,n}(z)}{m_n!}\right|
 \longrightarrow\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha).
\]
The leading coefficient of \(R_{P;m_n,n}/m_n!\) is \(\binom{dn}{m_n}\), and Stirling's formula gives
\[
 \frac1n\log\binom{dn}{m_n}\longrightarrow \mathfrak h_d(\alpha).
\]
After division by \((dn-m_n)/n\), the normalized logarithmic potentials converge almost everywhere to \eqref{eq:fixed-potential-intro}. Their root measures have one common compact support, so the uniform-integrability conclusion of Lemma~\ref{lem:pot-compact} applies without first assuming weak convergence. Vitali's theorem on each bounded disk therefore gives \(L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}\) convergence. Taking distributional Laplacians identifies every weakly convergent subsequence, and compactness gives convergence of the full sequence to a positive measure of mass one.

The subharmonic representative of the limiting potential supplies its extension at the roots. Off the roots, Lemma~\ref{lem:barrier-cont} gives a continuous expression agreeing almost everywhere with that potential. The submean characterization of the subharmonic representative gives equality everywhere there.

For varying polynomials \(P_n\), their root convex hulls still lie in one compact set. At almost every limiting observation point, the selected simple saddle and its separating contour for \((P,z,\alpha)\) persist at \((P_n,z,m_n/n)\) by Proposition~\ref{prop:uniform-saddle}. The same almost-everywhere and compactness argument applies. Root splitting may change the degree of the reduced saddle equation, but it does not affect this selected saddle or the contour tube; any additional saddle branches created near the split roots are irrelevant to the local asymptotic.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:atoms}
For \(x\in\mathbb R\), write \(x_+=\max\{x,0\}\). The atomic part of the limiting measure \(\mu_{\alpha,P}\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-atoms}
 \mu_{\alpha,P}^{\mathrm{at}}=
 \sum_{j=1}^s\frac{(r_j-\alpha)_+}{d-\alpha}\delta_{a_j}.
\end{equation}
When \(\alpha<r_j\), the root \(a_j\) is isolated in \(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{\alpha,P}\). The potential is continuous off \(Z(P)\). Wherever one saddle branch realizes the barrier, the potential is that branch's critical height minus \(\mathfrak h_d(\alpha)\), divided by \(d-\alpha\).
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Continuity off the roots follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:barrier-cont} and rules out atoms there. Fix a root \(a\) of multiplicity \(r\). For \(m<rn\), its multiplicity in \(R_{P;m,n}\) is exactly \(rn-m\). Thus if \(\alpha<r\), weak convergence forces an atom of mass at least \((r-\alpha)/(d-\alpha)\).

Since \(h_P(w)=(w-a)/r+O((w-a)^2)\), the inverse branch of \(F_{P,\alpha}\) through \(a\), for \(\alpha\ne r\), satisfies
\[
 w-a=\frac{r}{r-\alpha}(z-a)+O((z-a)^2),\qquad
 w-z=\frac{\alpha}{r-\alpha}(z-a)+O((z-a)^2).
\]
Its critical height is \((r-\alpha)\log|z-a|\) plus a harmonic function; all other heights remain bounded near \(a\). For \(\alpha<r\), the forced atom makes the limiting potential tend uniformly to \(-\infty\) as \(z\to a\), because the potential of the remaining positive measure is locally bounded above. Hence the root branch is selected on a punctured neighborhood. This gives the exact mass and isolation.

For \(\alpha>r\), the root branch tends to \(+\infty\), whereas a fixed contour around \(a\) bounds the barrier from above. The root branch is therefore unselected, and the selected heights remain bounded. At \(\alpha=r<d\), the one-root case \(P(w)=(w-a)^d\) cannot occur. The order of vanishing of \(F_{P,r}(w)-a\) at \(w=a\) (the local mapping degree) is therefore some \(\mathfrak e_a\ge2\). On the corresponding inverse branches, \(w-a\) has order \((z-a)^{1/\mathfrak e_a}\) and \((w-z)/(w-a)\to1\). The logarithmic terms cancel, so the critical heights are bounded here as well. There is no atom at the threshold \(\alpha=r\), completing \eqref{eq:fixed-atoms}.

\label{prop:selected-C}

 \widehat C_{\alpha,P}(z)^s

 \qquad F_{P,\alpha}'(w_\alpha(z))\ne0.
\end{equation}
The last derivative is with respect to \(w\). At a point of \(Z(P)\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu_{\alpha,P}\), selection means the unique holomorphic continuation of the barrier-selected branch from a punctured neighborhood.

<!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{lem:merged-end}
Let \(a\) have multiplicity \(r\), let \(r<\alpha<d\), and assume \(a\notin\operatorname{supp}\mu_{\alpha,P}\). At the coalesced observation point \(a\), the landscape
\[
 w\longmapsto\log|P(w)|-\alpha\log|w-a|
\]
has a one-saddle separating contour winding once about \(a\). Moreover, there are neighborhoods \(\mathscr U\) of \(P\) in the monic coefficient space, \(V\) of \(a\), and \(I\Subset(r,d)\) of \(\alpha\) such that, for every \((\widetilde P,z,\beta)\in\mathscr U\times V\times I\), the corresponding landscape has a one-saddle separating contour with a uniform strict gap. This remains true when \(z\) is a root of \(\widetilde P\). At exact ratios \(\beta=m/n\), the Cauchy integral has the uniform nonzero asymptotic of Proposition~\ref{prop:uniform-saddle}.

<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
Write \(P(w)=(w-a)^rG(w)\), where \(G(a)\ne0\). At observation point \(a\), the coalesced landscape is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coalesced-landscape}
 L^{\mathrm c}(w)=(r-\alpha)\log|w-a|+\log|G(w)|.
\end{equation}
This is the logarithmic landscape for the polynomial \(G\), the observation point \(a\), and the ratio \(\alpha-r\in(0,d-r)\). The barrier arguments of Section~\ref{sec:barrier} use only this strict ratio inequality and apply verbatim also when \(\deg G=1\). Thus they apply to \eqref{eq:coalesced-landscape}; denote its barrier by \(B^{\mathrm c}\).

We first identify \(B^{\mathrm c}\) with the limiting barrier as the observation point approaches \(a\). A fixed contour that is near-minimizing for \(B^{\mathrm c}\) also winds around every sufficiently nearby \(z\), and its landscape converges uniformly, giving
\[
 \limsup_{z\to a}\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)\le B^{\mathrm c}.
\]
For the reverse inequality, take a path for the coalesced landscape joining \(a\) to infinity and lying above \(B^{\mathrm c}-\varepsilon\), and truncate it on a circle \(|w-a|=\delta\). On the compact truncated part, the moving landscapes converge uniformly to the coalesced one. On the circle and on a radial attachment from \(z\) to the circle,
\[
 \log|P(w)|-\alpha\log|w-z|
 \ge C+(r-\alpha)\log\delta,
\]
uniformly for \(|z-a|<\delta/4\); here \(C\) is independent of small \(\delta\). Since \(r-\alpha<0\), this lower bound tends to \(+\infty\) as \(\delta\downarrow0\). The attachment therefore gives \(\liminf_{z\to a}\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)\ge B^{\mathrm c}\). Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coalesced-barrier-limit}
 \lim_{z\to a}\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)=B^{\mathrm c}.
\end{equation}

Because \(a\notin\operatorname{supp}\mu_{\alpha,P}\), the fixed-ratio potential is harmonic on a disk about \(a\). The potential formula and \eqref{eq:coalesced-barrier-limit} therefore extend \(\mathcal B_P(\cdot,\alpha)\) to a harmonic function \(H\) on that disk, with \(H(a)=B^{\mathrm c}\). Let \(w_0\) be any connecting saddle of the coalesced landscape. Choose an ascending path through \(w_0\) joining the two positive ends and having its unique minimum \(B^{\mathrm c}\) at \(w_0\). The preceding attachment construction turns its truncated part into a path for nearby observation points. Its minimum is a lower touching function for \(H\), and expansion in \(z=a+\zeta\) gives
\[
 H(a+\zeta)\ge B^{\mathrm c}
   +\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{w_0-a}\zeta\right)+o(|\zeta|).
\]
Applying this in the directions \(\zeta\) and \(-\zeta\) yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coalesced-gradient}
 2\partial_zH(a)=\frac{\alpha}{w_0-a}.
\end{equation}
Distinct connecting saddles would give distinct right-hand sides, so the coalesced landscape has exactly one connecting saddle.

On the punctured disk, Lemma~\ref{lem:harmonic-gate} gives
\[
 w(z)=z+\frac{\alpha}{2\partial_zH(z)},
 \qquad F_{P,\alpha}(w(z))=z.
\]
The first formula extends holomorphically to \(z=a\) by \eqref{eq:coalesced-gradient}; the second then extends by the identity theorem. Differentiating at \(a\) gives \(F_{P,\alpha}'(w(a))w'(a)=1\), so the unique coalesced connecting saddle is simple. Lemma~\ref{lem:arbitrary-contour}, applied to \eqref{eq:coalesced-landscape}, now gives a one-saddle separating contour with a strict gap.

It remains to prove uniform persistence when the multiplicity-\(r\) root splits. Choose an open interval \(I\) with \(\alpha\in I\Subset(r,d)\) and put \(\vartheta_r=\pi/(2r+2)\) and \(c_r=\sin\vartheta_r\). Choose \(\delta>0\) so small that \(a\) is the only root of \(P\) in \(|w-a|\le4\delta\), the base barrier contour is disjoint from \(|w-a|\le2\delta\), and \(|G(w)|\ge2g\) there for some \(g>0\). Since \(\inf_{\beta\in I}(\beta-r)>0\), decrease \(\delta\) further so that, for every \(\beta\in I\), both
\begin{align}
 \log g+r\log c_r+(r-\beta)\log(2\delta),
 \label{eq:split-root-ray-bound}\\
 \log g+r\log(7\delta/8)-\beta\log(9\delta/8)
 \label{eq:split-root-circle-bound}
\end{align}
exceed \(B^{\mathrm c}+3\).

After shrinking the coefficient neighborhood, every \(\widetilde P\in\mathscr U\) has exactly \(r\) roots \(b_1,\ldots,b_r\), counted with multiplicity, in \(|w-a|<\delta/8\) and can be factored on \(|w-a|\le2\delta\) as
\[
 \widetilde P(w)=\widetilde G(w)\prod_{j=1}^r(w-b_j),
 \qquad |\widetilde G(w)|\ge g.
\]
For \(z\in V:=\{|z-a|<\delta/8\}\), remove from the unit circle the \(r\) arcs of angular radius \(\vartheta_r\) centered at the directions of the nonzero vectors \(b_j-z\). Their total length is less than \(2\pi\), so a remaining unit vector \(u\) exists. Elementary minimization in \(t>0\) then gives
\[
 |z+tu-b_j|\ge c_r t\qquad(1\le j\le r),
\]
with the same inequality when \(b_j=z\). Along this ray from \(z\) until it meets \(|w-a|=\delta\), one has \(t\le2\delta\) and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split-root-attachment}
 \log|\widetilde P(z+tu)|-\beta\log t
 \ge \log g+r\log c_r+(r-\beta)\log(2\delta).
\end{equation}
On the whole circle \(|w-a|=\delta\) one has \(|w-b_j|\ge7\delta/8\) and \(|w-z|\le9\delta/8\), which gives the lower bound in \eqref{eq:split-root-circle-bound}.

The base contour and an ascending path through its saddle have compact parts outside this circle that avoid all roots. Under coefficient, observation-point, and ratio perturbations, the simple saddle continues by the implicit-function theorem. Replace the local contour segment by its continued descending axis and the local path segment by its continued ascending axis. Shrink the parameter neighborhoods so that the continued saddle height differs from \(B^{\mathrm c}\) by less than one. Uniform convergence on the remaining compact parts, together with \eqref{eq:split-root-attachment} and \eqref{eq:split-root-circle-bound}, gives an upper contour and a lower joining path with the same unique saddle height and with uniform strict gaps away from the saddle. The min--max formula therefore identifies that height with the perturbed barrier. The strict superlevel component containing \(z\) lies inside the contour and the component containing infinity lies outside it; their closures can meet only on the contour, where the continued saddle is the sole point at barrier height. It is therefore the unique connecting saddle. Proposition~\ref{prop:uniform-saddle} now gives the asserted uniform nonzero asymptotic, irrespective of root splitting or of a collision between \(z\) and a perturbed root.

\label{thm:fixed-Hausdorff}
Let \(P_j\to P\) be a sequence of monic polynomials of degree \(d\) converging to \(P\), let \(n_j\to\infty\), and let \(m_j/n_j\to\alpha\in(0,d)\). Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-Hausdorff}
 d_{\mathrm H}\!\left(Z((P_j^{n_j})^{(m_j)}),
                  \operatorname{supp}\mu_{\alpha,P}\right)\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
The map \((P,\alpha)\mapsto\operatorname{supp}\mu_{\alpha,P}\) is Hausdorff-continuous. For every compact coefficient set \(\mathscr P\) of monic degree-\(d\) polynomials and compact interval \(I\subset(0,d)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-uniform-Hausdorff}
 \sup_{\substack{P\in\mathscr P,\ 0\le m<dn\\m/n\in I}}
 d_{\mathrm H}\!\left(Z((P^n)^{(m)}),\operatorname{supp}\mu_{m/n,P}\right)
 \longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
These statements include integer ratios, multiple roots, and multiple critical points of \(P\).

<!-- lemma-13: body_md -->
\label{lem:small-coeff}
Let \(\mathscr A(z,t)\) be jointly holomorphic on a neighborhood of \(X\times\{t\in\mathbb C:|t|\le r\}\), where \(r>0\) and \(X\subset\mathbb C\) is compact, and write \(\mathscr A_z(t)=\mathscr A(z,t)\). Suppose that \(|\mathscr A(z,0)|\) and \(|\mathfrak b(z)|\), where \(\mathfrak b(z):=\frac{\partial_t\mathscr A(z,0)}{\mathscr A(z,0)}\), are bounded below by positive constants on \(X\). If \(k_n\ge1\) and \(k_n/n\to0\), then
\[
 [t^{k_n}]\mathscr A_z(t)^n\ne0\qquad(z\in X)
\]
for all sufficiently large \(n\). This remains true for jointly holomorphic functions \(\mathscr A_n(z,t)\), with \(\mathscr A_{n,z}(t):=\mathscr A_n(z,t)\), that are uniformly bounded on a common neighborhood of \(X\times\{t\in\mathbb C:|t|\le r\}\), provided both lower bounds are uniform in \(n\). On a smaller parameter neighborhood the coefficients also satisfy the uniform logarithmic-derivative estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small-coeff-logder}
 \partial_z\log\bigl([t^{k_n}]\mathscr A_{n,z}(t)^n\bigr)
 =n\,\partial_z\log\mathscr A_{n,z}(0)+O(k_n).
\end{equation}
Here \(\partial_z\log f\) means \((\partial_z f)/f\), so no global logarithm is required. The estimate is valid for \(k_n=0\) as an exact identity.
<!-- end lemma-13 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
After enlarging \(X\) slightly inside the holomorphy neighborhood, Cauchy estimates and the uniform lower bound for \(|\mathscr A(z,0)|\) give a number \(\rho>0\) such that all functions under consideration are zero-free for \(|t|\le\rho\). On finitely many parameter charts choose
\[
 \varpi_z(t):=\log\frac{\mathscr A_z(t)}{\mathscr A_z(0)}.
\]
For a varying family, the normalized logarithms and all derivatives needed below are uniformly bounded on a common smaller polydisk. The quantity \(\mathfrak b(z)=\varpi_z'(0)\) is uniformly bounded away from zero. Thus the family index may be included among the parameters. Write \(k=k_n\).

If \(k\) remains bounded, direct coefficient expansion, uniformly on the parameter charts, gives
\[
 [t^k]\mathscr A_z(t)^n
 =\mathscr A_z(0)^n\frac{n^k\mathfrak b(z)^k}{k!}
  \bigl(1+O(n^{-1})\bigr).
\]
Now suppose \(k\to\infty\) and put \(\varepsilon=k/n\to0\). The implicit-function theorem, uniformly in the parameters, gives a unique solution of
\[
 t\varpi_z'(t)=\varepsilon,
 \qquad
 t_*=\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak b(z)}+O(\varepsilon^2),
\]
in \(|t|<\rho/2\). On the coefficient circle \(t=t_*e^{i\theta}\), \(-\pi\le\theta\le\pi\), the exponent relative to its value at \(\theta=0\) is
\[
 k(e^{i\theta}-1-i\theta)
 +n\sum_{j\ge2}c_j(z)t_*^j
 \bigl(e^{ij\theta}-1-j(e^{i\theta}-1)\bigr),
\]
where \(\varpi_z(t)=\sum_{j\ge1}c_j(z)t^j\). With \(x=e^{i\theta}\),
\[
 x^j-1-j(x-1)=(x-1)^2
       \sum_{q=0}^{j-2}(j-1-q)x^q.
\]
Since \(|x-1|^2=2(1-\cos\theta)\), this proves the uniform estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-circle-bound}
 \bigl|e^{ij\theta}-1-j(e^{i\theta}-1)\bigr|
 \le j^2(1-\cos\theta),
 \qquad j\ge2,
\end{equation}
for \(-\pi\le\theta\le\pi\). Cauchy bounds for \(c_j\) and \(|t_*|=O(\varepsilon)\) therefore bound the real part of the second sum by \(Ck\varepsilon(1-\cos\theta)\). For small \(\varepsilon\) the full real part is at most \(-\tfrac12k(1-\cos\theta)\), uniformly on the entire circle. Its quadratic coefficient at zero is
\[
 -\frac n2\bigl(t_*\varpi_z'(t_*)+t_*^2\varpi_z''(t_*)\bigr)
 =-\frac k2\bigl(1+O(\varepsilon)\bigr).
\]
The third through sixth derivatives of the exponent are \(O(k)\) uniformly on a fixed neighborhood of zero. After the scaling \(\theta=s/\sqrt{k}\), its cubic correction is odd and integrates to zero on the symmetric contour; the quartic term and the square of the cubic term are \(O(k^{-1})\) after Gaussian integration. On \(k^{-2/5}\le|\theta|\le\pi\), the preceding global bound is \(O(e^{-c k^{1/5}})\). Thus the uniform saddle estimate is
\[
 [t^k]\mathscr A_z(t)^n=
 \frac{\mathscr A_z(t_*)^n t_*^{-k}}
 {\sqrt{2\pi n\bigl(t_*\varpi_z'(t_*)+t_*^2\varpi_z''(t_*)\bigr)}}
 \bigl(1+O(k^{-1})\bigr).
\]
The radicand is \(2\pi k(1+O(\varepsilon))\), so the square root is chosen coherently near the positive real square root and is uniformly nonzero. All other displayed factors are nonzero. Every sequence has either a bounded-\(k\) subsequence or a subsequence with \(k\to\infty\), which proves the asserted eventual nonvanishing.

For \eqref{eq:small-coeff-logder}, carry out the same estimates on a slightly larger complex parameter neighborhood. The relative errors are holomorphic there and uniformly \(O(n^{-1})\) in the bounded-\(k\) case and \(O(k^{-1})\) in the saddle case, so Cauchy estimates control their parameter derivatives. In the saddle case, differentiation of
\[
 n\log\mathscr A_z(t_*)-k\log t_*
\]
produces no \(\partial_z t_*\) term because \(nt_*\varpi_z'(t_*)=k\). The remaining phase derivative beyond \(n\partial_z\log\mathscr A_z(0)\) is \(n\partial_z\varpi_z(t_*)=O(n|t_*|)=O(k)\), and the logarithmic derivative of the radicand is bounded. This proves \eqref{eq:small-coeff-logder}, uniformly also for varying families. The case \(k=0\) is the stated exact identity.

 =\frac{R_{P;dn-k,n}(z)}{(dn-k)!}.

\label{thm:sublinear-counts}
Let \(P_n\to P\) be a sequence of monic polynomials of degree \(d\), and let \(M_n\ge0\) be integers with \(M_n/n\to0\). Write \(a_j,r_j\) as in \eqref{eq:root-data}. Let \(c_1,\ldots,c_t\) be the distinct points of \(Z(P')\setminus Z(P)\), and put \(\varkappa_i=\operatorname{ord}_{c_i}P'\). Choose pairwise disjoint open disks \(D_j\) about \(a_j\) and \(E_i\) about \(c_i\), small enough that their closures contain no other point of \(Z(P)\cup Z(P')\). For all sufficiently large \(n\), simultaneously for every \(0\le m\le M_n\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sublinear-counts}
 (\operatorname{div}_0 (P_n^n)^{(m)})(D_j)=nr_j-m,
 \qquad
 (\operatorname{div}_0 (P_n^n)^{(m)})(E_i)=m\varkappa_i.
\end{equation}
All zeros lie in these disks. No assumption is made on the separation or multiplicity of the roots of \(P_n\) inside a disk.

Consequently, for \(1\le m_n<dn\) with \(m_n/n\to0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complete-low-endpoint}
 Z((P_n^n)^{(m_n)})\longrightarrow Z(P)\cup Z(P')
 \quad\text{in Hausdorff distance}.
\end{equation}
For \(0\le m_n<dn\) with \(m_n/n\to d\), the corresponding limit is \(\{\mathfrak c_P\}\).
<!-- end theorem-8 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
On a neighborhood of the union of the disk boundaries, both \(P\) and \(P'/P\) are nonzero. Apply the logarithmic-derivative estimate \eqref{eq:small-coeff-logder} with \(\mathscr A_{n,z}(t)=P_n(z+t)\). Writing \(R_{P_n;m,n}=(P_n^n)^{(m)}\), we obtain, uniformly there and for \(0\le m\le M_n\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sublinear-logder}
 \frac1n\frac{(R_{P_n;m,n})'}{R_{P_n;m,n}}
 =\frac{P_n'}{P_n}+O(m/n)
 \longrightarrow\frac{P'}P.
\end{equation}
Nonvanishing on these neighborhoods is part of the same lemma; at \(m=0\) it follows directly from \(P_n\to P\). To pass from the sequential lemma to the simultaneous range, argue by contradiction: any failure would produce indices \(n_j\) and orders \(m_j\le M_{n_j}\) with \(m_j/n_j\to0\), contradicting the lemma for that sequence. The same argument gives the uniform estimate in \eqref{eq:sublinear-logder}.

For \(1\le m\le M_n\), the meromorphic function \(R_{P_n;m,n}/R_{P_n;m-1,n}\), divided by \(n\), is uniformly close on each boundary to \(P'/P\). Its winding number about zero is therefore that of \(P'/P\). At \(a_j\) the rational function \(P'/P\) has a simple pole, even when \(r_j>1\), so this number is \(-1\). At \(c_i\) it is \(\varkappa_i\). Thus each differentiation changes the local zero count by \(-1\) in \(D_j\) and by \(\varkappa_i\) in \(E_i\). At order zero the counts are \(nr_j\) and zero, respectively, by coefficient convergence. Telescoping proves \eqref{eq:sublinear-counts}. Since \(\sum_{i=1}^t\varkappa_i=s-1\), the total count is
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^s(nr_j-m)+\sum_{i=1}^t m\varkappa_i
 =dn-sm+m(s-1)=dn-m,
\]
the full degree. There are no remaining zeros.

The disks can be arbitrarily small. For \(m_n\ge1\) all critical-point counts are positive, and \(nr_j-m_n>0\) for large \(n\). This proves \eqref{eq:complete-low-endpoint}. At the upper endpoint the reverse coefficient argument in Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-zeros} is uniform for \(P_n\to P\): its constant term is \(1\) and its linear coefficient is \(d(z-\mathfrak c_{P_n})\). It excludes every compact set away from \(\mathfrak c_P\). Gauss--Lucas gives a common compact enclosure and the zero sets are nonempty, proving the asserted Hausdorff limit.

<!-- theorem-10: body_md -->
\label{thm:averaging}
The pooled measures converge weakly to \(\rho_P\), with \(\operatorname{supp}\rho_P=\mathcal K_P\). More generally, \(P_n\to P\) in fixed degree implies \(\overline\mu_{P_n,n}^{\mathrm{root}}\Longrightarrow\rho_P\). For each continuous test function, convergence to the corresponding \(\rho_P\) is uniform on compact coefficient families. Their limiting logarithmic potential, the \emph{shadow potential}, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shadow-potential}
 \mathcal V_P(z):=\frac2{d^2}\int_0^d\mathcal B_P(z,\alpha)\,\mathrm d\alpha-1,
\end{equation}
and \(\rho_P=(2\pi)^{-1}\Delta\mathcal V_P\). The integral is interpreted in \(L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}\) and may be \(-\infty\) at an atom. Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atoms-intro}
 \rho_P^{\mathrm{at}}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}p_j^2\delta_{a_j}.
\end{equation}
The nonatomic mass is \(1-\sum_jp_j^2\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-Hausdorff}
 \sup_{z\in\mathcal K_P}\operatorname{dist}(z,\mathcal Z_{P,n})\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-10 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
Fix a continuous test function \(\varphi\) on \(\mathsf H_P\). The bounded step functions \(\alpha\mapsto\int\varphi\,\mathrm d\mu_{P;\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor,n}^{\mathrm{root}}\) converge pointwise on \((0,d)\) by Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed}. Multiply by \(2(d-\alpha)/d^2\) and apply dominated convergence. The resulting integral differs from the pooled sum by \(O(n^{-1})\|\varphi\|_\infty\): on each interval of length \(1/n\) the weight varies by \(O(n^{-1})\), and \(\mathcal N_{d,n}=d^2n^2/2+O(n)\). This proves the measure limit without a uniform saddle expansion in \(\alpha\). For \(P_n\to P\), the same argument works on a common compact root enclosure: the varying- polynomial statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed} gives pointwise convergence of the step functions at every interior ratio. Joint weak continuity from Lemma~\ref{lem:alpha-cont}, followed by dominated convergence in \eqref{eq:rho-intro}, gives continuity of \(P\mapsto\rho_P\). The sequential assertion and compactness then give the stated uniformity.

By definition, the support of the average is contained in \(\mathcal K_P\). Conversely, a nonnegative continuous function with positive integral against one \(\mu_{\alpha_0,P}\) has positive integral on an interval of ratios, by Lemma~\ref{lem:alpha-cont}. The averaging weight is positive there. Hence \(\operatorname{supp}\rho_P=\mathcal K_P\).

Integrate the fixed-ratio potential formula in local \(L^1\), using Lemma~\ref{lem:pot-compact} to justify Fubini. Since
\[
 \frac2{d^2}\int_0^d \mathfrak h_d(\alpha)\,\mathrm d\alpha
 =2\int_0^1[-t\log t-(1-t)\log(1-t)]\,\mathrm d t=1,
\]
we obtain \eqref{eq:shadow-potential}. Integrating \eqref{eq:fixed-atoms} gives mass \(r_j^2/d^2\) at \(a_j\); Tonelli's theorem applied to each singleton excludes all other atoms.

Every ball centered on \(\operatorname{supp}\rho_P\) has positive limiting mass. Weak convergence therefore forces a pooled zero into a smaller such ball for all large \(n\). A finite covering of the compact support gives \eqref{eq:lower-Hausdorff}.

<!-- lemma-14: body_md -->
\label{lem:connected-union}
Let \(I\) be a compact interval and let \(t\mapsto E_t\) be a Hausdorff-continuous family of nonempty compact subsets of a metric space with metric \(\delta\). If \(E_{t_*}\) is a singleton for some \(t_*\in I\), then \(\bigcup_{t\in I}E_t\) is compact and connected.
<!-- end lemma-14 -->
<!-- proof-27: body_md -->
The union \(E\) is compact: from any sequence \(x_j\in E_{t_j}\) choose \(t_j\to t\in I\); Hausdorff continuity supplies \(y_j\in E_t\) with \(\delta(x_j,y_j)\to0\), and compactness of \(E_t\) supplies a convergent subsequence. If \(E\) were disconnected, write it as the disjoint union of two nonempty compact sets \(A\) and \(B\). They have positive distance. The sets
\[
 I_A=\{t:E_t\cap A\ne\varnothing\},\qquad
 I_B=\{t:E_t\cap B\ne\varnothing\}
\]
are closed by compactness and open by Hausdorff continuity and the positive distance between \(A\) and \(B\). Both are nonempty, so connectedness of \(I\) forces \(I_A=I_B=I\). This contradicts the singleton fiber at \(t_*\). Thus \(E\) is connected.

<!-- theorem-12: body_md -->
\label{thm:connected}
For every nonzero polynomial \(P\), the limiting core \(\mathcal K_P\) is connected. For a nonconstant polynomial it is compact and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-continuum-chain}
 Z(P)\cup Z(P')\cup\{\mathfrak c_P\}
 \subseteq\mathcal K_P\subseteq\mathcal S_P\subseteq\mathsf H_P.
\end{equation}
If \(P\) has at least two distinct roots, the core is perfect.
<!-- end theorem-12 -->
<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
For degree \(d\ge2\), define the endpoint-completed support family by
\[
 E_P(t)=
 \begin{cases}
 Z(P)\cup Z(P'),&t=0,\\
 \operatorname{supp}\mu_{dt,P},&0<t<1,\\
 \{\mathfrak c_P\},&t=1.
 \end{cases}
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-support} and the joint continuity in Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed-Hausdorff} make this a Hausdorff-continuous family of nonempty compact sets. Its union is compact, and it is exactly \(\mathcal K_P\) by the definition of the core and the endpoint limits. Lemma~\ref{lem:connected-union} proves connectedness. Equation~\eqref{eq:distinguished} gives the first inclusion in \eqref{eq:canonical-continuum-chain}; the middle inclusion follows from \eqref{eq:complete-limit}, and the last is Gauss--Lucas. A connected closed set containing two distinct points has no isolated point, so it is perfect. A nonzero constant has empty full shadow and empty core, while a polynomial with one distinct root has singleton full shadow and singleton core; these cases are immediate.

<!-- theorem-13: body_md -->
\label{thm:literal}
Let \(T_P=\mathcal S_P\setminus\mathcal K_P\). Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:literal-decomp}
 \mathcal S_P=\mathcal K_P\cup T_P,
 \qquad
 T_P\subset\bigcup_{n\ge1}\mathcal Z_{P,n}.
\end{equation}
Every point of \(T_P\) is isolated in \(\mathcal S_P\) and occurs at only finitely many power levels. The set \(T_P\) is countable and locally finite in \(\mathbb C\setminus\mathcal K_P\): each compact subset of that open set meets \(T_P\) in finitely many points. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:literal-interior}
 \operatorname{int}\mathcal S_P=\operatorname{int}\mathcal K_P,
 \qquad \operatorname{Area}(\mathcal S_P)=\operatorname{Area}(\mathcal K_P),
 \qquad \partial\mathcal S_P=\partial\mathcal K_P\cup T_P.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-13 -->
<!-- proof-29: body_md -->
A point in the closure of the finite-level union is either a limit along unbounded power levels, and hence lies in \(\mathcal K_P\) by \eqref{eq:complete-limit}, or is a zero at a bounded power level. If a compact set avoids \(\mathcal K_P\), the upper Hausdorff estimate excludes all sufficiently large power levels from a small neighborhood of it. Only finitely many finite-level zeros remain there. This proves local finiteness, isolation, and finite occurrence, as well as countability. Adding isolated points outside a closed set changes neither its interior nor its area, and gives the boundary identity in \eqref{eq:literal-interior}.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:full-holes}
For every component \(\Omega\) of \(\mathbb C\setminus\mathcal K_P\), the set \(\Omega\setminus T_P\) is a component of \(\mathbb C\setminus\mathcal S_P\). This gives a bijection of complementary components preserving boundedness, area, and closure. In particular, the full shadow and the core have exactly the same number of holes.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-30: body_md -->
The set \(T_P\) is locally finite in \(\mathbb C\setminus\mathcal K_P\) by Theorem~\ref{thm:literal}. The open connected set \(\Omega\) is polygonally connected. A polygonal path joining two points of \(\Omega\setminus T_P\) has a compact neighborhood contained in \(\Omega\), which meets \(T_P\) in finitely many points. Small detours inside that neighborhood avoid those points. Thus \(\Omega\setminus T_P\) is path-connected. It is nonempty, open, and dense in \(\Omega\), and components coming from distinct \(\Omega\) cannot join in the smaller complement. These are therefore exactly the complementary components of \(\mathcal S_P\). Density preserves their closures and boundedness. Countability of \(T_P\) preserves their areas.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:power-transients}
For every \(k\ge1\),
\[
 \mathcal K_{P^k}=\mathcal K_P\subseteq\mathcal S_{P^k}\subseteq\mathcal S_P,
 \qquad
 \bigcap_{k\ge1}\mathcal S_{P^k}=\mathcal K_P,

Proposition~\ref{prop:covariance} gives power invariance of the limiting measure and core. A transient occurs at only finitely many original power levels and is absent once \(k\) exceeds them all. <!-- theorem-14: body_md -->
\label{thm:cumulative-shadow}
For \(M\ge1\) put
\[
 \varepsilon_M(P):=\sup_{n\ge M}d_{\mathrm H}(\mathcal Z_{P,n},\mathcal K_P).
\]
Then \(\varepsilon_M(P)\to0\) and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cumulative-bound}
 d_{\mathrm H}(\mathcal F_{P,M},\mathcal S_P)\le2\varepsilon_M(P).
\end{equation}
Both convergences are uniform on compact sets of monic polynomials of a fixed degree. Thus \(P_n\to P\) in that coefficient space implies
\[
 \mathcal F_{P_n,n}\longrightarrow\mathcal S_P,
 \qquad \mathcal Z_{P_n,n}\longrightarrow\mathcal K_P.
\]
These are in general different limits.
<!-- end theorem-14 -->
<!-- proof-32: body_md -->
Theorem~\ref{thm:no-outliers} and Corollary~\ref{cor:set-stability} give \(\varepsilon_M\to0\), uniformly on each compact coefficient family. A zero from a power level at most \(M\) already lies in \(\mathcal F_{P,M}\). A zero from a later power level is within \(\varepsilon_M\) of \(\mathcal K_P\), and every point of \(\mathcal K_P\) is within \(\varepsilon_M\) of \(\mathcal Z_{P,M}\subset\mathcal F_{P,M}\). Thus every zero, and then every point of their closure, is within \(2\varepsilon_M\) of the prefix. The reverse distance is zero since \(\mathcal F_{P,M}\subset\mathcal S_P\). This proves \eqref{eq:cumulative-bound} and its uniform version. Combine it with the continuity of the two compact-set maps to obtain the varying-polynomial statements.
<!-- end proof-32 -->
<!-- proof-33: body_md -->
The pooled measure convergence, the identity \(\operatorname{supp}\rho_P=\mathcal K_P\), continuity of \(P\mapsto\rho_P\), and the uniform measure statement are Theorem~\ref{thm:averaging}. Convergence of the complete zero sets and the tail formula \eqref{eq:tail-core-intro} are Theorem~\ref{thm:no-outliers}. Hausdorff continuity of \(P\mapsto\mathcal K_P\) and \(P\mapsto\mathcal S_P\), together with uniform complete-zero convergence, are Corollary~\ref{cor:set-stability}. Finally, convergence of the finite prefixes to the full shadow, including the varying-polynomial and uniform forms, is Theorem~\ref{thm:cumulative-shadow}.

<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:realization}

For a power of a linear polynomial, the full shadow is a singleton and both accumulation sets are empty.

Given a point of \(\mathcal K_P\) and any neighborhood of it, choose another support point in that neighborhood and a smaller disk around the latter avoiding the original point. <!-- lemma-15: body_md -->
\label{lem:infinite-modulus}
For every \(r>0\),
\[
 \Pi_a(r)>0,\qquad \Pi_a'(r)>0,\qquad
 \frac{\,\mathrm d}{\,\mathrm d r}\frac{r\Pi_a'(r)}{\Pi_a(r)}>0,
 \qquad |\Pi_a(re^{i\theta})|\le\Pi_a(r).
\]
Apart from \(\theta\in2\pi\mathbb Z\), equality in the last inequality holds exactly when \(r=1\) and \(\theta\equiv\pm\theta_a\pmod{2\pi}\).
<!-- end lemma-15 -->
<!-- proof-37: body_md -->
Set \(y=\cos\theta\) and \(\upsilon=r+r^{-1}-2\ge0\). Direct expansion gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-modulus-factor}
 \Pi_a(r)^2-|\Pi_a(re^{i\theta})|^2
   =2(1-y)r^4\,\mathcal E_a(\upsilon,y),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
 \mathcal E_a(\upsilon,y)={}&a\upsilon^3
 +\frac a4\bigl((a-8)y+a+16\bigr)\upsilon^2\\
 &+\frac a8\bigl(32y^2+(8a-32)y+a^2+16\bigr)\upsilon
 +\frac{(a+4y)^2(a+2y+2)}4.
\end{align*}
For \(-1\le y\le1\), all three coefficients of positive powers of \(\upsilon\) are strictly positive. For the linear coefficient, the quadratic in \(y\) has positive leading coefficient and discriminant \(-64(a+4)^2<0\). The constant term is nonnegative, and vanishes exactly at \(y=-a/4\). This proves the modulus inequality and its equality cases.

Writing \(u=r+r^{-1}\ge2\) gives
\[
 \Pi_a(r)=r^2\bigl(u^2+au-2-\beta_a\bigr)\ge M_a r^2>0.
\]
Also \(\Pi_a'(r)=4r^3+3ar^2-2\beta_a r+a>0\): the quadratic \(3ar^2-2\beta_a r+a\) has negative discriminant, since \(0<\beta_a<a\). Finally, with \(\mathfrak D_a(r)=r\Pi_a'(r)/\Pi_a(r)\), the coefficient of \(\theta^2\) in the left side of \eqref{eq:infinite-modulus-factor} is \(\Pi_a(r)^2r\mathfrak D_a'(r)\). On the right it is \(r^4\mathcal E_a(\upsilon,1)>0\). Thus \(\mathfrak D_a'(r)>0\).

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:infinite-ray}
For every \(0<a<4\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-ray}
 \mathcal K_{\Pi_a}\cap(0,\infty)=\varnothing,
 \qquad 0\in\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2,\Pi_a}\subseteq\mathcal K_{\Pi_a}.
\end{equation}
At \((z,\alpha)=(0,2)\) the barrier is \(\log M_a\). Its connecting saddles are exactly \(1,e^{i\theta_a},e^{-i\theta_a}\), and all three are simple.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-38: body_md -->
Fix \(x>0\). For \(r>0\) set \(u=x+r\) and \(\mathfrak D_{a,x}(r)=r\Pi_a'(x+r)/\Pi_a(x+r)\). If \(\lambda_a(u)=\log\Pi_a(u)\), then
\[
 \mathfrak D_{a,x}'(r)=\frac xu\lambda_a'(u)
       +\frac ru\bigl(\lambda_a'(u)+u\lambda_a''(u)\bigr)>0
\]
by Lemma~\ref{lem:infinite-modulus}. Its endpoint values are \(0\) and \(4\). For every \(0<\alpha<4\) there is therefore a unique \(r\) with \(\mathfrak D_{a,x}(r)=\alpha\). On the circle \(w=x+re^{i\theta}\),
\[
 |\Pi_a(w)|\le\Pi_a(|w|)\le\Pi_a(x+r),
\]
with equality only at \(\theta=0\); use strict increase of \(\Pi_a\) and \(|x+re^{i\theta}|<x+r\) otherwise. The angular maximum of the landscape is nondegenerate, with second derivative \(-r\mathfrak D_{a,x}'(r)<0\). The positive ray from \(x\) to infinity has its unique landscape minimum at the same point. The ray gives the lower min--max bound and the circle gives the matching upper bound. The circle separates \(x\) from infinity and is strictly below the common barrier value except at the stationary point. Hence the two strict superlevel components lie on opposite sides of the circle and their closures can meet the circle only at that point. It is therefore the unique connecting saddle, and the circle is a one-saddle separating contour. A circle passing through a root may be indented there, where the landscape is strictly below its maximum. The resulting contour persists for nearby observation points. Proposition~\ref{prop:uniform-saddle} therefore excludes \(x\) from every fixed-ratio support. The endpoint supports also stay away from \(x\): \(\Pi_a(x)\Pi_a'(x)>0\) and the centroid is \(-a/4<0\). By Hausdorff continuity, the support family completed at the two endpoints has compact union. This union is the core and misses \(x\), proving the first assertion of \eqref{eq:infinite-ray}.

At \(z=0\) the saddle equation for \(\alpha=2\) factors as
\[
 w\Pi_a'(w)-2\Pi_a(w)=(w^2-1)(2w^2+aw+2).
\]
On the unit circle,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-symbol}
 e^{-2i\theta}\Pi_a(e^{i\theta})
 =f_a(\theta):=2\cos(2\theta)+2a\cos\theta-\beta_a.
\end{equation}
Its largest absolute value is \(M_a\), attained only at \(0,\theta_a,-\theta_a\), with values \(M_a,-M_a,-M_a\), respectively. The fourth candidate, \(w=-1\), has strictly smaller height whenever it is a finite saddle. Roots on the circle can be avoided by small indentations strictly below \(\log M_a\), leaving the three equality neighborhoods unchanged. The positive radial path attains its minimum \(\log M_a\) at \(w=1\). The circle upper bound and radial lower bound therefore give the barrier \(\log M_a\).

The whole unit circle has landscape at most \(\log M_a\). At each of its three equality points the angular maximum is nondegenerate. The two local higher-level sectors lie on opposite sides of the circle. No strict superlevel component can cross that circle, and every such component contains a positive end. The inner and outer sectors therefore belong to the components of \(0\) and infinity, respectively. All three saddles are connecting. Define their normalized angular curvatures \(\varsigma_0,\varsigma_+,\varsigma_-\) by
\[
 \log|f_a(\theta_*+t)|
 =\log M_a-\frac12\varsigma_*t^2+O(t^3),
 \qquad \theta_*\in\{0,\theta_a,-\theta_a\}.
\]
A direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-variances}
 \varsigma_0=\frac{16}{a+4},\qquad
 \varsigma_+=\varsigma_-=\frac{4(4-a)}{a+4}.
\end{equation}
All three curvatures are positive. Lemma~\ref{lem:multiple-gates} now expresses the nearby barrier as the maximum of the three continued critical heights. The values \(2\partial_z\psi_j=2/w_j\) are distinct, so that maximum is not harmonic at the origin. The potential formula gives \(0\in\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2,\Pi_a}\).

<!-- theorem-15: body_md -->
\label{thm:infinite-transients}
Fix \(0<a<4\) and an integer \(h\). Uniformly for \(\sigma\) in compact subsets of \(\mathbb C\), as \(n\to\infty\),
\begin{align}
 &\frac{4\sqrt{2\pi n}}{\sqrt{a+4}\,M_a^n}
       \frac{(\Pi_a^n)^{(2n+h)}(\sigma/n)}{(2n+h)!}
 \label{eq:infinite-interference}\\
 &\hspace{1cm}=
 e^{2\sigma}+(-1)^n\chi_a e^{-a\sigma/2}
                   \cos(\omega_a \sigma+h\theta_a)+O(n^{-1}).\notag
\end{align}
The derivative order is in \([0,4n-1]\) for all sufficiently large \(n\).

Let \(\sigma_a\) be the unique solution in \((0,\pi/\sqrt{16-a^2})\) of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-scale}
 e^{(a+4)\sigma_a/2}=\chi_a\cos(\omega_a \sigma_a).
\end{equation}
For every sufficiently large odd \(n\), \((\Pi_a^n)^{(2n)}\) has a simple positive zero
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-zero-scale}
 x_n=\frac{\sigma_a}{n}+O(n^{-2}).
\end{equation}
Every such zero is a transient. In particular \(T_{\Pi_a}\) is infinite. In fact every sufficiently large power level has a positive transient: for even \(n\) one can use order \(2n+h_a\), where
\[
 h_a=\begin{cases}2,&0<a\le2,\\1,&2<a<4.\end{cases}
\]
Its simple positive zero has the form \(\widetilde \sigma_a/n+O(n^{-2})\), where \(\widetilde \sigma_a>0\) is the first positive zero of \(e^{2\sigma}+\chi_a e^{-a\sigma/2}\cos(\omega_a \sigma+h_a\theta_a)\).
<!-- end theorem-15 -->
<!-- proof-40: body_md -->
Cauchy's formula on the unit circle gives the exact integral
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinite-cauchy}
 \frac{(\Pi_a^n)^{(2n+h)}(\sigma/n)}{(2n+h)!}
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
 f_a(\theta)^n e^{-ih\theta}
 \left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}e^{-i\theta}\right)^{-2n-h-1}\,\mathrm d\theta.
\end{equation}
For bounded complex \(\sigma\), the product of the last two factors, together with all \(\theta\)-derivatives needed below, converges uniformly to \(e^{-ih\theta}\exp(2\sigma e^{-i\theta})\), with error \(O(n^{-1})\). The only maxima of \(|f_a|\) are the three points in Proposition~\ref{prop:infinite-ray}. Near zero,
\[
 f_a(\theta)=M_a-(a+4)\theta^2+O(\theta^4),
\]
and near either \(\pm\theta_a\) it is \(-M_a\) plus a positive quadratic term with second derivative \((16-a^2)/2\). Laplace's method on three fixed disjoint neighborhoods therefore gives Gaussian weights \(\varsigma_0^{-1/2}\) and \(\varsigma_+^{-1/2}\), while the rest of the integral is exponentially smaller. Taylor expansion in each Gaussian coordinate makes the remainder after normalization \(O(n^{-1})\), uniformly for bounded \(\sigma\); the odd first-order terms integrate to zero. At the negative extrema \(f_a^n\) contributes \((-1)^n\). The two conjugate contributions combine to a cosine. Since \(2\varsigma_+^{-1/2}/\varsigma_0^{-1/2}=\chi_a\), this proves \eqref{eq:infinite-interference}. The estimate is locally uniform as an identity of holomorphic functions of \(\sigma\).

For odd \(n\) and \(h=0\), the limiting function is negative at zero. After multiplication by \(e^{a\sigma/2}\), its first positive zero is the intersection of the strictly increasing function \(e^{(a+4)\sigma/2}\) and the strictly decreasing positive function \(\chi_a\cos(\omega_a \sigma)\) on
\[
 (0,\pi/(2\omega_a))=(0,\pi/\sqrt{16-a^2}).
\]
It is unique and simple. Rouch\'e's theorem on a small circle around \(\sigma_a\) gives exactly one nearby zero, with error \(O(n^{-1})\) in the \(\sigma\) coordinate. Real coefficients and uniqueness make this zero real; it is positive and simple. This proves \eqref{eq:infinite-zero-scale}.

For even \(n\), the stated choice of \(h_a\) gives \(\chi_a\cos(h_a\theta_a)<-1\). If \(a\le2\), then \(2\theta_a\in(\pi,4\pi/3]\); before the cosine next reaches zero, \(-\cos(2\theta_a+\omega_a \sigma)\) decreases strictly while the exponential increases. If \(a>2\), then \(\theta_a\in(2\pi/3,\pi)\) and the ratio
\[
 \frac{e^{(a+4)\sigma/2}}{-\cos(\theta_a+\omega_a \sigma)}
\]
increases strictly until the denominator next vanishes. On the part before angle \(\pi\), its logarithmic derivative is \((a+4)/2+\omega_a\tan(\theta_a+\omega_a \sigma)\), whose initial value is \((a+4)(a-2)/a>0\); thereafter both terms are positive. The first positive zero is consequently simple in either case. The same Rouch\'e argument proves the assertion for even power levels.

Proposition~\ref{prop:infinite-ray} excludes every positive real point from the limiting core. Thus all these zeros belong to \(T_{\Pi_a}\). The zeros from odd power levels tend to zero through positive values, so there are infinitely many distinct ones. Their isolation follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:literal}.

<!-- theorem-16: body_md -->
\label{thm:barrier-first-variation}
Fix a monic degree-\(d\) polynomial \(P\) with \(s\) distinct roots, \(z_0\notin Z(P)\), and \(0<\alpha<d\). Let its connecting saddles be \(w_1,\ldots,w_k\), with phase orders \(q_1,\ldots,q_k\), and put \(\kappa_j=\alpha/(w_j-z_0)\). For a polynomial \(H\) of degree at most \(d-1\), a complex increment \(\zeta\), and a real increment \(\tau\), set \(\eta=\|H\|+|\zeta|+|\tau|\), using any fixed coefficient norm. Put \(q_*:=\max_j q_j\) and \(\theta_*:=1/(q_*-1)\ge1/s\). As \(\eta\to0\),
\begin{align}
 &\mathcal B_{P+H}(z_0+\zeta,\alpha+\tau)-\mathcal B_P(z_0,\alpha)\notag\\
 &\quad=\max_{1\le j\le k}
 \left\{\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{H(w_j)}{P(w_j)}+\kappa_j\zeta\right)
             -\tau\log|w_j-z_0|\right\}
                   +O(\eta^{1+\theta_*}).
 \label{eq:barrier-first-variation}
\end{align}
The constant depends on the base data, but not on the direction of the increments. In particular the error is always \(O(\eta^{1+1/s})\); if every connecting saddle is simple it is \(O(\eta^2)\). The barrier is locally Lipschitz in the monic coefficients, the nonroot observation point, and the interior derivative ratio. At fixed \(P,\alpha\), it is differentiable in \(z_0\) if and only if there is exactly one connecting saddle.
<!-- end theorem-16 -->
<!-- proof-43: body_md -->
For each connecting saddle choose a locally ascending arc on either side, one in each of the two positive-end components. Join these arcs to fixed neighborhoods of the corresponding ends. After truncation, the result is a compact path whose unique minimum is the chosen saddle; the parts used for attachment have a strict height margin. Together with the barrier contour of Lemma~\ref{lem:arbitrary-contour}, these paths, which lie strictly above the barrier away from their saddles, provide the lower and upper variational bounds. On each of these fixed compact paths and on the contour, uniformly in \(w\),
\[
 L_{P+H,z_0+\zeta,\alpha+\tau}(w)
 =L_{P,z_0,\alpha}(w)+
 \operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{H(w)}{P(w)}+\frac{\alpha\zeta}{w-z_0}\right)
 -\tau\log|w-z_0|+O(\eta^2).
\]
Here and below the error bounds are on fixed compact paths, so they are uniform over unit increment directions. In the local coordinate at \(w_j\), the unperturbed contour has height \(B-|v_j|^{q_j}\). Choose the ascending paths along centers of higher sectors, where the height is \(B+|v_j|^{q_j}\). The displayed linear perturbation changes from its value at \(w_j\) by at most \(C\eta|v_j|\). The elementary bound
\[
 \sup_{r\ge0}\{-r^q+C\eta r\}
       =O(\eta^{q/(q-1)}),\qquad q\ge2,
\]
and its negative give the upper contour bound and lower path bounds with errors \(O(\eta^{q_j/(q_j-1)}+\eta^2)\). Away from the saddle neighborhoods the fixed strict height margins make these estimates automatic for sufficiently small \(\eta\). The min--max formula bounds the perturbed barrier above by the contour maximum and below by the maximum of the path minima. Taking \(q_* =\max_j q_j\) proves \eqref{eq:barrier-first-variation} with the stated error. The phase-order budget \eqref{eq:phase-order-budget} gives \(q_*-1\le s\).

To justify the Lipschitz assertion also across saddle collisions, shrink a parameter neighborhood of the base data. By continuity of the barrier, every connecting saddle for data in its closure lies in one compact set away from roots, the observation point, and infinity: the phase height tends uniformly to \(-\infty\) near roots and to \(+\infty\) at the two positive ends. Thus every linear form on the right of \eqref{eq:barrier-first-variation}, now based at any point in that neighborhood, has norm bounded by one constant. On each straight parameter segment, the limsup and liminf of its one-sided difference quotients (the upper and lower Dini derivatives) are consequently bounded in absolute value by that constant times the segment speed. The elementary one-variable Dini-derivative mean-value inequality gives the Lipschitz estimate.

With only \(z_0\) varying, the first variation is \(\max_j\operatorname{Re}(\kappa_j\zeta)\). Distinct connecting saddles give distinct \(\kappa_j\). A maximum of distinct real-linear forms is not linear. This proves both directions of the differentiability claim.

<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:equal-height-not-connecting}
Take \(P(w)=w^2-1\), \(\alpha=1/2\), and \(z=3/(2\sqrt2)>1\). The two finite saddles are
\[
 w_\pm=\frac{3\pm\sqrt3}{3\sqrt2},\qquad
 \exp\bigl(2L_{P,z,1/2}(w_\pm)\bigr)=\frac{4\sqrt6}{9}.
\]
This common height is the barrier. Indeed, on the positive real ray from \(z\) to infinity the landscape has its unique minimum at \(w_+\). On the circle \(|w-z|=w_+-z\), both factors \(|w-1|\) and \(|w+1|\) are maximized uniquely at \(w=w_+\), so the circle has its unique maximum at that saddle. The two variational bounds coincide. The circle is a one-saddle separating contour through \(w_+\) and does not pass through \(w_-\). If \(w_-\) were connecting, an ascending path through it from one positive end to the other would have to cross that circle at height strictly greater than the barrier, which is impossible. Thus \(w_-\) is at barrier height but is not connecting. Also \(z\) is outside the root hull \([-1,1]\), and hence outside every limiting zero support.

<!-- theorem-17: body_md -->
\label{thm:appendix-differentiator-spectrum}
For every nonconstant polynomial \(P\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appendix-spectral-identities}
 \sigma(A_P)=\mathcal S_P,\qquad
 \sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_P)=\mathcal K_P,\qquad
 \sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(A_P)=T_P.
\end{equation}
For every compact \(E\subset\mathbb C\setminus\mathcal K_P\) there are \(n_E\) and \(C_E<\infty\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appendix-tail-resolvent}
 \sup_{n\ge n_E}\sup_{z\in E}\|(zI-A_{P,n})^{-1}\|\le C_E.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-17 -->
<!-- proof-44: body_md -->
If \(P\) has only one distinct root, all blocks are scalar multiples of the identity and the result is immediate. Otherwise Theorem~\ref{thm:no-outliers} separates all descendant zero sets at sufficiently large levels from \(E\) by a common distance \(\delta_E>0\). We claim that the quantities \(\mathscr C_H\) in \eqref{eq:appendix-compressed-resolvent}, for \(H=(P^n)^{(m)}\), \(0\le m\le dn-2\), are uniformly bounded away from zero on \(E\) at large levels. If not, choose \(n_j\to\infty\), \(z_j\to z\in E\), and \(m_j/n_j\to\alpha\in[0,d]\), set \(H_j=(P^{n_j})^{(m_j)}\), and suppose that \(\mathscr C_{H_j}(z_j)\to0\). The normalized root measures of \(H_j\) tend to
\[
 \mu^{\mathrm{root}}_P\quad(\alpha=0),\qquad
 \mu_{\alpha,P}\quad(0<\alpha<d),\qquad
 \delta_{\mathfrak c_P}\quad(\alpha=d).
\]
The interior limit is Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed}. At zero, the retained root factors carry asymptotically all the mass; at \(d\), use Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-zeros}, which concentrates the entire root carrier at the centroid. Since \(\mathscr C_{H_j}=C_{\mu_{H_j}^{\mathrm{root}}}\), separation from \(E\) permits passage to Cauchy transforms at the moving points \(z_j\). Indeed, truncate \((z-a)^{-1}\) continuously inside \(|z-a|<\delta_E\): the truncation changes none of these integrals, and its boundedness and uniform continuity justify the simultaneous weak and moving-point limit. The limiting values are respectively \(P'(z)/(dP(z))\), \(C_{\mu_{\alpha,P}}(z)\), and \((z-\mathfrak c_P)^{-1}\). They are nonzero: roots, critical points, and the centroid lie in \(\mathcal K_P\), and Proposition~\ref{prop:selected-C} treats the interior ratios. This contradiction proves the claim. Equation \eqref{eq:appendix-compressed-resolvent} now bounds all compression resolvents, while the diagonal blocks have bound \(\delta_E^{-1}\). This proves \eqref{eq:appendix-tail-resolvent}.

At \(z\notin\mathcal K_P\), the uniformly bounded block inverses in \eqref{eq:appendix-tail-resolvent} give an invertible tail for \(zI-A_P\). The remaining initial part is finite-dimensional, so \(zI-A_P\) is Fredholm of index zero. Off \(\mathcal S_P\), the initial part is also invertible; conversely, closedness of the spectrum includes the closure of all finite-block eigenvalues. This proves the full-spectrum identity. For \(z\in\mathcal K_P\), Hausdorff convergence supplies eigenvalues \(z_n\in\sigma(A_{P,n})\) tending to \(z\). Their unit eigenvectors, placed in distinct blocks, have pairwise distance at least one and satisfy \(\|(A_P-zI)x_n\|=|z_n-z|\to0\). A Fredholm operator has a continuous quasi-inverse modulo a finite-rank map, so every bounded sequence with images tending to zero has a convergent subsequence. This contradicts the separation of these eigenvectors and proves the essential-spectrum identity. Finally Theorem~\ref{thm:literal} makes every remaining spectral point isolated and present in only finitely many blocks. Choose a closed isolating disk disjoint from the core. The tail estimate puts this disk in every late block's resolvent set, so coordinate evaluation of the Riesz contour integral vanishes in those blocks. Its range is thus finite-dimensional. Conversely, an isolated spectral point with a finite-rank Riesz projection is outside the Fredholm essential spectrum: the Cauchy-weighted resolvent integral is a quasi-inverse modulo that projection.
