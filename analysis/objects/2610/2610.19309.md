<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $F$ be a field of characteristic zero. Every polynomial
$f\in F[x,y,z]$ satisfying \eqref{eq:intro-cyclic} and
\eqref{eq:intro-norm} can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-decomposition}
 f(x,y,z)=P(x,y)+Q(y,z)+R(z,x),\qquad P,Q,R\in F[X,Y].
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $f$ satisfies $\partial_x\partial_y\partial_z f=0$.
Every homogeneous solution of odd degree is zero.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:symmetric-classification}
Let $n\ge0$ be even. A symmetric polynomial $f\in F[x,y,z]_n$ satisfies
\eqref{eq:intro-norm} if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-classification}
 f=\Phi(q),
\end{equation}
where $q\in F[X,Y]_n$ is symmetric, odd in each variable, and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:binary-period-intro}
 q(X,Y)+q(-Y,X-Y)+q(Y-X,-X)=0.
\end{equation}
The binary polynomial is uniquely $q(X,Y)=f(X,Y,0)$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:complete-periods}
For every $n\ge0$, restriction is an $F$-linear isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:period-restriction}
 W_n(F)\longrightarrow\mathscr P_n^0(F),\qquad f\longmapsto f(X,Y,0).
\end{equation}
Its inverse is $q\mapsto\Phi(q)$. It identifies
$W_n^{\mathrm{sym}}(F)$ with $\mathscr P_n^{0,\mathrm{odd}}(F)$ and
$W_n^{\mathrm{alt}}(F)$ with $\mathscr P_n^{0,\mathrm{ev}}(F)$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:dimensions}
For every even $n>0$, the period maps induce complex linear isomorphisms
\[
 W_n^{\mathrm{sym}}(\mathbb C)\cong S_{n+2},\qquad
 W_n^{\mathrm{alt}}(\mathbb C)\cong S_{n+2}.
\]
For every characteristic zero field $F$ and every $n\ge0$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dimensions}
 \dim_F W_n^{\mathrm{sym}}(F)=\dim_F W_n^{\mathrm{alt}}(F)
   =\dim_{\mathbb C}S_{n+2}.
\end{equation}
All these spaces vanish when $n=0$ or $n$ is odd. Consequently, the generating function of the dimensions is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:W-Hilbert}
 \sum_{n\ge0}\dim_F W_n(F)t^n=\frac{2t^{10}}{(1-t^4)(1-t^6)}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:common-divisor}
Over every characteristic zero field, $D$ divides every solution of
\eqref{eq:intro-cyclic}--\eqref{eq:intro-norm}. Up to a nonzero scalar,
it is the greatest common divisor of all nonzero solutions.
<!-- end corollary-3 -->
<!-- example-1: body_md -->
The first nonzero solutions occur in degree ten. The two binary
polynomials in the original problem \cite[p.~481]{ABBI26} can be
written as
\begin{align*}
  q_{\mathrm s}(X,Y)
    &=XY(X^2-Y^2)^2(X^2-4Y^2)(4X^2-Y^2),\\
  q_{\mathrm a}(X,Y)
    &=X^2Y^2(X^2-Y^2)^3.
\end{align*}
Both satisfy \eqref{eq:normalized-period-space}.
The first is symmetric and odd in each variable, and the second is
alternating and even in each variable. Their cyclic sums
$f_{\mathrm s}=\Phi(q_{\mathrm s})$ and
$f_{\mathrm a}=\Phi(q_{\mathrm a})$ form a basis of $W_{10}(F)$.
Over $\mathbb C$, the binary polynomials $q_{\mathrm s}$ and
$q_{\mathrm a}$ are nonzero scalar multiples of the odd and
normalized even period polynomials, respectively, of Ramanujan's
weight twelve discriminant cusp form $\Delta(\tau)$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:sector-decomp}
Homogeneous components of a solution are solutions. Its symmetric and
alternating parts $(f+Sf)/2$ and $(f-Sf)/2$ are also solutions. There are no
nonzero solutions of degree at most three.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Each operator in \eqref{eq:polynomial-operators} preserves degree. Direct
substitution gives $SAS=A^2$ and $SCS=C^{-1}$. Hence $S$ preserves both
conditions $Nf=0$ and $Cf=f$. A three-cycle and a transposition generate the
full permutation group, which proves the two symmetry assertions. In degrees zero and one, the cyclic spaces are spanned by $1$ and
$x+y+z$. Their norms are $3$ and $3z$, respectively. In degree two, the
basis $x^2+y^2+z^2,xy+yz+zx$ has norms
$4(x^2-xy+y^2)+3z^2,\quad x^2-xy+y^2$.
These are linearly independent. The kernels in degrees zero, one, and two are therefore zero.

In degree three, the symmetric basis
$p_3=x^3+y^3+z^3$, $m_{21}=x^2y+x^2z+xy^2+xz^2+y^2z+yz^2$, and $xyz$ has norms
\[
 3z^3,\qquad -(x+y)(2x-y)(x-2y)+4z(x^2-xy+y^2),
 \qquad z(x^2-xy+y^2),
\]
which are linearly independent. The alternating basis
$\Delta=(x-y)(x-z)(y-z)$ has norm $3xy(x-y)\ne0$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:sym-boundary-factors}
A symmetric homogeneous solution $f$ is divisible by $c_+$. In positive even
degree it is divisible by $wc_+$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Write $f\in V_n$, put $\nu=(-1)^n$, and let $g(s,t)=f(s,-s,t)$.
Expansion and symmetry give
\begin{align*}
 &(N_{23}f)(-s,-t,s)-\nu(N_{23}f)(-s-t,-t,s)\\
 &\quad \quad -(N_{23}f)(-s-t,-s,-s)+(N_{12}f)(-s-t,0,s)
   =\nu\{g(s,t)-g(s+t,t)\}.
\end{align*}
Therefore, $g$ is translation-invariant in $s$ over $\mathbb Q(t)$, hence constant in $s$.
The norm at $(0,0,t)$ makes it zero. Symmetry gives the three factors of $c_+$.
For even degree, put $h(s,t)=f(s,t,-s-t)$. The identity
\[
 (N_{12}f)(-s-t,t,s)-(N_{23}f)(-s-2t,-t,s)
     =h(s,t)-h(s+t,t)
\]
makes $h$ independent of $s$. Since $h(s,t)=h(t,s)$, it is constant.
Positive homogeneity gives $h=0$. The distinct linear factors are
relatively prime, which proves the product divisibility.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:period-extraction}
Let $m>0$ be odd and let $v\in V_m$ be symmetric. Then we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:common-kernel}
 J_v=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 v\in\bigoplus_{j\ge0}w^{2j}L(\mathcal B_{m+1-2j}).
\end{equation}
Every lift on the right is polynomial, and the expression is unique.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We first read the first order data on $w=0$, then reconstruct a binary
period, and finally divide the remainder by $w^2$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{The first order data.}
First, direct substitution gives $J_v(X,0,Z)=Z v(Z,-X,X)$. Thus $J_v=0$ implies vanishing
on each opposite pair plane, and hence $c_+\mid v$.
Write $\partial_i$ for differentiation in the $i$th argument of $v$, and put
\[
 h(X,Y)=v(-Y,X,Y-X),\quad
 H(X,Y)=(\partial_1+\partial_2)v(-Y,X,Y-X),\quad
 p(X,Y)=-\int_0^X H(s,Y)\,ds.
\]
Define
\[
 C_v(X,Y)=p(X-Y,Y)-p(X,Y)-h(X,Y),\qquad
 \mathcal D=-Y\partial_X,\qquad \tau=e^{\mathcal D}.
\]
The operator $\tau$ translates $X$ to $X-Y$. We claim that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal-jet}
 J_v(X,Y,3Y)=0,\qquad
 \left.\partial_ZJ_v\right|_{Z=3Y}
     =\tau^2 B(3\mathcal D)B(\mathcal D)C_v.
\end{equation}
Here all power series are finite operators on the fixed polynomial degree.
To verify the claim, let $v_i=(\partial_i v)(-Y,X,Y-X)$ and set
$\psi_i^0=\psi_i|_{Z=3Y}$,
$\psi_i^1=(\partial_Z\psi_i)|_{Z=3Y}$. Odd homogeneity and symmetry give
\[
 \psi_1^0=-h,\quad \psi_2^0=-(1+\tau+\tau^2)h,\qquad
 \psi_1^1=v_1,\quad \psi_2^1=(1+\tau)v_1-\tau^2v_3.
\]
The chain rule also gives $h_X=v_2-v_3$, $h_Y=-v_1+v_3$, and $p_X=-(v_1+v_2)$.
Differentiate the defining expression for $J_v$, including its Bernoulli
operator. The required cancellations are exactly
\begin{align*}
 B(3t)(1+e^t+e^{2t})&=3B(t),\\
 -B(t)+tB'(3t)(1+e^t+e^{2t})+te^{2t}B(3t)
     &=-e^{2t}B(3t)B(t),\\
 B(\mathcal D)(\tau-1)&=\mathcal D.
\end{align*}
The first two identities follow by substituting $B(t)=t/(e^t-1)$ and
clearing denominators. The third is its defining finite-difference identity.
This proves \eqref{eq:normal-jet}. The operator on its right is invertible,
since each factor has constant term one. Consequently $J_v=0$ gives $C_v=0$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Reconstruction of the binary period.}
Symmetry and homogeneity give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-symmetry}
 h_X=H(X,Y)-H(Y-X,Y),\qquad H(-Y,-X)=H(X,Y).
\end{equation}
Differentiate $C_v=0$ in $X$ and use the first identity to obtain
$H(-X,Y)=H(X,Y)$. Since $H$ has even total degree, it is separately even
and symmetric. Thus $p$ is odd in $X$ and even in $Y$, and
$p_X(X,Y)=p_X(Y,-X)$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-extract}
 q(X,Y)=-\frac{Xp(Y,-X)+Yp(X,Y)}{m+1}.
\end{equation}
Euler's identity for the degree $m$ polynomial $p$ gives $q_Y=-p$.
The displayed formula and the parities of $p$ show that $q$ is symmetric,
odd in each variable, and zero on both axes. Since $c_+\mid v$, we have
$h(0,Y)=0$. Evaluating $C_v=0$ at $X=0$ therefore gives $p(Y,Y)=0$,
and hence $q(Y,Y)=0$.

Set
\[
 D_q=q(X,Y)-q(X-Y,Y)+q(X,X-Y).
\]
Using $q_Y=-p$, symmetry of $q$, and oddness of $p$ in its first
variable, we obtain
\[
 (\partial_X+\partial_Y)D_q
 =-p(Y,X)-p(X,Y)+p(X-Y,Y)-p(X-Y,X)
 =h(X,Y)+h(Y,X)=0.
\]
Here $C_v=0$ gives the second equality. A homogeneous polynomial of degree $m+1$
annihilated by this derivative is a multiple of $(X-Y)^{m+1}$.
At $Y=0$ it equals $q(X,X)=0$. Thus $D_q$ vanishes, which proves
$q\in\mathcal B_{m+1}$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{The lift and descent in degree.}
We check directly that every lift has zero obstruction. For
$q\in\mathcal B_d$, put $t=Z-2Y$ and
$r(X,Y,Z)=q(X,Y)-q(X,t)$. If $\tau_u$ translates $X$ to $X-u$, the two
frame polynomials of $\Phi(q)$ are
\[
 ((1-\tau_Y)r,\ (1-\tau_Z)r).
\]
This follows by expanding the three cyclic sums and using
\eqref{eq:binary-addition}. Since
$B(-u\partial_X)(1-\tau_u)=u\partial_X$, the two terms of
$J_{\Phi(q)}$ cancel. The identity $\Phi(q)=wL(q)$ and
\eqref{eq:J-w-mult} then give $J_{L(q)}=0$.
Cancellation on each opposite pair plane also gives $c_+\mid L(q)$.

The polynomial extracted from $L(q)$ is the original $q$. Indeed,
differentiate $\Phi(q)=wL(q)$ once in each of the first two variables
and restrict to $w=0$. The resulting first-plane data give
$p_{L(q)}=-q_Y$, which recovers \eqref{eq:q-extract}.
For a symmetric positive degree homogeneous polynomial, $p_v=0$ is
equivalent to $w^2\mid v$: its three pair sums of first derivatives
then vanish on $w=0$, so twice each individual derivative vanishes there.
Euler's identity gives $w\mid v$, and differentiation gives a second
factor of $w$. The converse is immediate.
It follows that $v-L(q)=w^2u$. The quotient $u$ is symmetric and satisfies
$J_u=0$ by \eqref{eq:J-w-mult}. Repeat in degree $m-2$. The final remainder
has degree below three and is divisible by $c_+$, so it is zero.
The extracted first-plane data at each step also prove uniqueness.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- corollary-4: body_md -->
\label{cor:period-towers}
If a symmetric solution $f\in V_n$ satisfies $J_f=0$, then uniquely
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sym-tower}
 f=\sum_{j\ge0}w^{e_j}\Phi(q_j),\qquad
 \begin{cases}
 e_j=2j,\ q_j\in\mathcal B_{n-2j},& n\text{ even},\\
 e_j=2j-1,\ q_j\in\mathcal B_{n+1-2j},& n\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The sum is finite. In odd degree, its first term is interpreted as the
polynomial lift $L(q_0)$.
<!-- end corollary-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For $n=0$, the assertion follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:sector-decomp}.
Assume $n>0$. In even degree, Lemma~\ref{lem:sym-boundary-factors} gives $f=wv$ with
$v$ symmetric of odd degree. Equation~\eqref{eq:J-w-mult} gives $J_v=0$.
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:period-extraction} and multiply its expansion by $w$.
In odd degree apply that lemma directly to $f$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:outer-values}
If $h$ is a normalized level one cusp eigenform of weight $k>6$, then
$L(h,k-2)$ and $L(h,k-1)$ are nonzero. The same holds after omitting any
finite set of Euler factors.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The Fourier coefficient integral at height $y=1/m$ and the bound
$y^{k/2}|h(\tau)|\ll1$ give $a_h(m)=O(m^{k/2})$.
Thus the Dirichlet series converges absolutely for $\Re s>k/2+1$.
To check each local denominator as well, the Hecke recurrence gives
\[
 \sum_{r\ge0}a_h(p^r)t^r=(1-a_h(p)t+p^{k-1}t^2)^{-1}.
\]
The coefficient bound makes this holomorphic for $|t|<p^{-k/2}$.
Consequently, if the denominator is $(1-\alpha_pt)(1-\beta_pt)$, then
$|\alpha_p|,|\beta_p|\le p^{k/2}$. Each local factor is therefore finite
and nonzero for $\Re s>k/2$. In the smaller half-plane
$\Re s>k/2+1$, the sum over primes of the absolute deviations of the
local denominators from one converges. Their product is nonzero there.
Since $k-2>k/2+1$ when $k>6$, both asserted values lie in that half-plane.
Removing finitely many nonzero factors preserves nonvanishing.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:good-apartment}
The graph $\mathcal T^\uparrow$ is connected, and we have

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Passage to the boundary.}
Choose a neat normal subgroup $\Gamma'\subset K\cap\operatorname{SL}_3(\mathbb Z)$ and put
$X_{\Gamma'}=\Gamma'\backslash\mathscr X$.
Proposition~\ref{ar:prop:coercivity}, proved in
Appendix~\ref{app:arithmetic-direct}, gives
\[
 H_c^2(X_{\Gamma'},E_{n,t}\otimes\mathbb C)
       \longrightarrow H^2(X_{\Gamma'},E_{n,t}\otimes\mathbb C)
       \quad\text{equal to zero}.
\]
Extension to $\mathbb C$ is faithful, so the rational map is zero as well.

The five-dimensional symmetric space has trivial orientation character:
on symmetric matrices $Q\mapsto g^{\mathsf t}Qg$ has determinant
$(\det g)^4>0$, and quotienting by positive scaling removes an oriented
radial direction. Poincar\'e--Lefschetz duality identifies the
absolute-to-relative map in degree three homology with the zero map
above. Hence boundary homology surjects onto ordinary top homology.
Put $\Delta=K/\Gamma'$. 
Rational Hochschild--Serre and averaging give
\[
 H_3(K,E_{n,t})\cong H_3(\Gamma',E_{n,t})_{\Delta}.
\]
The same finite-cover descent applies to boundary homology and commutes with
its map to ordinary homology, so exact averaging preserves the
surjection at level $K$. It remains to evaluate an arbitrary boundary
class.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Face cocycles and radical invariance.}
For evaluation, use $M=\operatorname{Ind}_K^G E_{n,t}$ and
$\partial=\partial\overline{\mathscr X}_G$ as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:apartment-boundary-pairing}. Shapiro's finite covector factor
retains every level three cusp. The two maximal parabolic types for $G$
are represented by the upper integral parabolics $P$ and $Q$ of block types
$(2,1)$ and $(1,2)$, with intersection the Borel group $B_0$.

Take collar neighborhoods of the two unions of closed maximal faces on
the neat cover. Their intersections are Borel strata, with no triple
intersection because a flag has only a line and a plane. Induction and
Shapiro give the Mayer--Vietoris sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MV-boundary}
  H^0(P,M)\oplus H^0(Q,M)\to H^0(B_0,M)\to H^1(\partial,M)
  \to H^1(P,M)\oplus H^1(Q,M)\to H^1(B_0,M).
\end{equation}
To keep the corner signs explicit, use the mapping cone
\[
\begin{gathered}
 \mathcal C^r=C^r(P,M)\oplus C^r(Q,M)\oplus C^{r-1}(B_0,M),\\
 d(a,b,c)=(da,db,\operatorname{Res} a-\operatorname{Res} b-dc).
\end{gathered}
\]
The dual chain differential is
$\partial(x,y,z)=(\partial x+i_Pz,\partial y-i_Qz,-\partial z)$, where $i_P,i_Q$ are the two inclusions. The evaluation pairing is the sum
of the three component pairings. These differentials are adjoint. In
particular a degree-one cocycle is $(A_P,A_Q,u)$ with
$du=\operatorname{Res} A_P-\operatorname{Res} A_Q$.
For $R=P$ or $Q$, let $N_R\cong\mathbb Z^2$ be its unipotent radical and
$L_R$ its Levi quotient. The
five-term sequence shows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inflated}
 H^1(R,M)=H^1(L_R,M^{N_R}).
\end{equation}
This means that face cocycles may be chosen to factor through the Levi,
with values fixed by the radical. We verify the necessary vanishing.
Decompose the covectors into $N_R$-orbits and apply Shapiro to their
stabilizer lattices. Their unipotent coefficient systems satisfy the
nilmanifold comparison \cite[Theorem~1.1]{Kasuya}. Lie and group cohomology
therefore agree, naturally for the Levi action, as explained in
Appendix~\ref{ar:sec:constantterms}. We may compute after complexification,
which detects the required rational vanishing.

We now compute the Lie cohomology explicitly. For the root operators $z\partial_x,z\partial_y$, the cocycle equation
for a pair $(a,b)$ is $a_y=b_x$. Such a pair is the gradient of a homogeneous
potential of degree $n+1$. Exact pairs are the gradients of potentials
divisible by $z$, so the quotient is the binary symmetric power of degree
$n+1$. For $x\partial_z,y\partial_z$, subtract an exact pair to remove
its common $z$-dependent part. The remaining binary pairs are taken modulo
$(xg,yg)$, and $(a,b)\mapsto xb-ya$ identifies that quotient with binary
forms of degree $n+1$. These descriptions cover the standard and dual
coefficient conventions.

It remains to take Levi invariants. In either case $H^1(N_R,E_{n,t})$ restricts to $\operatorname{Sym}^{n+1}(\mathbb Q^2)$ or its dual on the
derived subgroup $\mathrm{SL}_2$ of the Levi. The corresponding
level three subgroup fixes every covector. Its two opposite root
unipotents have no common invariant in this positive binary degree.
Consequently $H^1(N_R,M)^{L_R}$ vanishes, which proves \eqref{eq:inflated}.

If $v\in M^{N_R}$, the lattice fixing $\ell$ contains $3N_R$ and is
Zariski dense in the algebraic radical. Therefore $v(\ell)$ is invariant
under its root derivatives and belongs to $U_n$. The same conclusion holds
for invariants of the common root subgroup of two adjacent maximal radicals.
