\label{thm:compactification-intro}
Let $X$ be a set of countably many variables,
and let $Z$ be a set of graded variables with countably many variables of each grade.
Let $\mathcal{H}^{\textnormal{gr}}_d(Z)$ be the span of graded-degree-$d$ Hermite polynomials in $Z$.

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]

    \item If $p_n \in \mathcal{H}^{\textnormal{gr}}_d(Z)$ satisfies $p_n \xrightarrow{d} p$ for some $p$, then $p_n \xrightarrow{d} p'$ for some $p' \in \mathcal{H}^{\textnormal{gr}}_d(Z)$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For each graded variable $Z^{(r)}_i$ introduce new standard Gaussians $X_{ir,j}$ for $j \in [N]$.
Define approximants for $Z^{(r)}_i$ and $\operatorname{He}_m(Z^{(r)}_i)$ by,
\[
    S_{ir} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{j=1}^N \psi_r(X_{ir,j}), \qquad U_{m,ir} = \frac{1}{N^{m/2}} \sum_{\substack{j_1, \dots, j_m \in [N]\\ j_1, \dots, j_m \text{ distinct}}} \psi_r(X_{ir,j_1})\psi_r(X_{ir,j_2})\cdots \psi_r(X_{ir,j_m})\,.
\]
We approximate $p_k \in \mathcal{H}^{\textnormal{gr}}_{d_k}(Z)$ by:
\[
    p_{N,k} = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha \prod_{i,r} U_{\alpha_{ir},ir} \qquad \text{where}\qquad p_k = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha \operatorname{He}_\alpha(Z) = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha \prod_{i,r} \operatorname{He}_{\alpha_{ir}}(Z_i^{(r)})\,.
\]
All terms in $p_{N,k}$ have Hermite degree $d_k$ so $p_{N,k} \in {\cal H}_{d_k}(X)$. It remains to show $(p_{N,k})_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \xrightarrow{d} (p_k)_{k \in \mathbb{N}}$ as $N \to \infty$.
This is equivalent to $(p_{N,1}, \dots, p_{N,k}) \xrightarrow{d} (p_1,\dots, p_k)$ for every finite $k$.

First assume each $p_k=\operatorname{He}_{\alpha^k}(Z)$ is a single Hermite polynomial.
Consider $\tilde p_{N,k} = \operatorname{He}_{\alpha^k}(S)$. By the multivariate central limit theorem, $(\tilde p_{N,1}, \dots, \tilde p_{N,k}) \xrightarrow{d} (p_1, \dots, p_k)$.
On the other hand,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multilinear-apx}
    \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} (\tilde p_{N,k} - p_{N,k})^2 = O(N^{-1})\,.
\end{equation}
This is a combinatorial moment calculation which we include in \cref{lem:omitted-moment}.

Combine \cref{eq:multilinear-apx} and the convergence $(\tilde p_{N,1}, \dots, \tilde p_{N,k}) \xrightarrow{d} (p_1, \dots, p_k)$ with Slutsky's lemma to deduce $( p_{N,1},\dots, p_{N,k}) \xrightarrow{d} (p_1, \dots, p_k)$.
This completes the case of single Hermite polynomials.

The continuous mapping theorem gives $(p_{N,1}, \dots, p_{N,k}) \xrightarrow{d} (p_1, \dots, p_k)$ if every
$p_k$ is a finite sum.
For $p_k\in\mathcal{H}^{\textnormal{gr}}_{d_k}(Z)$ with an infinite number of terms,
take a sequence of finite $L_2$ approximants, approximate each
one, then take a diagonal sequence to obtain $(p_{N,1},\dots,p_{N,k}) \xrightarrow{d} (p_1,\dots, p_k)$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:degree2}
    Let $\varepsilon > 0$.
    For all $p \in {\cal H}_2(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ such that $\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^2 \leq 1$, there exist $Z_0 \in {\cal H}_2(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ and $Z_1, \dots, Z_m \in {\cal H}_1(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ with $m \leq 2\varepsilon^{-2}$ and coefficients $\lambda_a$ such that:
    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item $p = Z_0 + \sum_{a=1}^m \lambda_a\operatorname{He}_2(Z_a)$.
        \item $Z_0, Z_1,\dots, Z_m$ are independent and $W_2(Z_0, {\cal N}(0, s)) \leq C\varepsilon$ for an absolute constant $C$ where $s = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} Z_0^2$.
    \end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Write $p$ in the Hermite basis as $p(X) = \sum_{i \neq j} P_{ij} X_iX_j + \sum_{i=1}^n P_{ii} \operatorname{He}_2(X_i)$ for a symmetric coefficient matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}_\textnormal{sym}$.
Let $\sum_{a}\lambda_a v_av_a^\intercal$ be the spectral decomposition of $P$.
Then
\begin{equation}
     p(X) = X^\intercal P X - \operatorname{tr}(P) = \sum_{a} \lambda_a \ip{v_a, X}^2 - \operatorname{tr}(P) = \sum_{a} \lambda_a (\ip{v_a, X}^2 - 1) = \sum_a \lambda_a \operatorname{He}_2(\ip{v_a, X})\,.
\end{equation}
By the orthogonality of the eigendecomposition, $\ip{v_a, X}$ are independent standard Gaussians.

We have $\frac 12 \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^2 = \sum_{i,j=1}^n P_{ij}^2 =: \|{P}\|_F^2$.
On the other hand, $\|{P}\|_F^2 = \sum_a \lambda_a^2$.
Since $\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^2 \leq 1$, we deduce that $\sum_a \lambda_a^2 \leq \frac 12$.

Let $A = \{a : |\lambda_a| \geq \varepsilon/2\}$. By the above, $|A| \leq 2\varepsilon^{-2}$.
Write
\begin{align*}
    p = \sum_{a \in A} \lambda_a \operatorname{He}_2(\underbrace{\ip{v_a, X}}_{=: Z_a})
   + \underbrace{\sum_{a\notin A}\lambda_a \operatorname{He}_2(\ip{v_a, X})}_{=: Z_0}\,. 
\end{align*}

Since the $\operatorname{He}_2(\ip{v_a, X})$ are independent and mean-zero,
a central limit theorem implies $Z_0$ is approximately Gaussian if there are many summands.
If there are few summands, $Z_0$ is small since $|\lambda_a| \leq \varepsilon/2$.
Both cases are handled simultaneously by the CLT from \cite[Theorem 4.1]{rio2009upper} which yields for an absolute constant $C$, with $s = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} Z_0^2 = 2\sum_{a\not\in A} \lambda_a^2$,
\[
    W_2(Z_0, {\cal N}(0,s)) \leq \frac{C}{\sqrt{s}} \left(\sum_{a \notin A} \lambda_a^4 \right)^{1/2} \leq C \max_{a \notin A}|\lambda_a| \leq C \varepsilon\,. \qedhere
\]

    \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
        \item (Existence and uniqueness of limits)
        Let $S$ be a compact Hausdorff space.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:ultra-hilbert}
    $\mathscr H_{\le d}$ is a Hilbert space with inner product $\ip{[p_n], [q_n]}=\lim_{n \to \mathcal U}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p_nq_n$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
It is easy to check that $\ip{[p_n], [q_n]}$ defines a valid inner product on $\mathscr H_{\le d} = B_d/N_d$.
What remains is to check that the space is complete.

Consider the space $B_d$ with the supremum norm $\|{(p_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}}\|_\infty = \sup_n \|{p_n}\|_2$.
Since each coordinate space ${\cal H}_{\le d}(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ is complete, $B_d$ is complete in this norm ($B_d$ is a Banach space).
Moreover, $(p_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \mapsto\lim_{n\to{\cal U}}\|{p_n}\|_2$ is
$1$-Lipschitz in this norm, so the kernel $N_d$ is closed in this norm.
Thus $B_d/N_d$ is complete in the quotient norm $\|{[p_n]}\|_{B_d/N_d} := \inf_{(r_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \in N_d}\|{(p_n+r_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}}\|_\infty$.

It remains to show that the quotient norm agrees with the
inner-product norm $\sqrt{\ip{[p_n], [p_n]}} = \lim_{n \to {\cal U}} \|p_n\|_2$
which will prove that $\mathscr H_{\le d}$ is complete in the inner-product norm.
Set $L=\lim_{n\to{\cal U}}\|{p_n}\|_2$.
Every representative $(q_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \in B_d$ of $[p_n]$ satisfies
$\lim_{n\to{\cal U}}\|{q_n}\|_2=L$. Hence
$\sup_n\|{q_n}\|_2\ge L$ by \cref{lem:ultralimits}(iii) and therefore the quotient norm is at least $L$.

Conversely, for every $\varepsilon>0$, the set $A=\{n:\|{p_n}\|_2<L+\varepsilon\}$
belongs to ${\cal U}$ by \cref{lem:ultralimits}(i). Define $q_n=p_n$ for $n\in A$ and
$q_n=0$ otherwise. Then $(p_n-q_n)\in N_d$ and
$\sup_n\|{q_n}\|_2\le L+\varepsilon$.
Therefore the quotient norm is at most $L+\varepsilon$. Letting $\varepsilon\to0$ shows that it
equals $L$, completing the proof.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
$\diamond$ is a well-defined and continuous bilinear map $\mathscr H_r \times \mathscr H_s \to \mathscr H_{r+s}$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By contractivity of projection, Cauchy--Schwarz,
and hypercontractivity (\cref{lem:hypercontractivity}),
\[
    \|{p\diamond q}\|_2
    \le \|{pq}\|_2
    \le \|{p}\|_4\|{q}\|_4
    \le 3^{(r+s)/2}\|{p}\|_2\|{q}\|_2.
\]
Consequently, if $p_n \in {\cal H}_r(X)$ and $q_n \in {\cal H}_s(X)$ satisfy $\sup_n \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p_n^2 < \infty$ and $\sup_n \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} q_n^2 < \infty$,
then $\sup_n \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} (p_n\diamond q_n)^2 < \infty$ and hence $(p_n\diamond q_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \in B_{r+s}$. Lift $\diamond$ to $\mathscr H_{r} \times \mathscr H_s$ by $[p_n]\diamond[q_n]:=[p_n\diamond q_n]$.
To check that $\diamond$ is independent of the representatives, suppose
$[p_n]=[p'_n]$ and $[q_n]=[q'_n]$.
By bilinearity and the preceding bound,
\[
\begin{split}
    \|{p_n\diamond q_n-p'_n\diamond q'_n}\|_2
    \le 3^{(r+s)/2}\bigl(
        \|{p_n-p'_n}\|_2\|{q_n}\|_2
        +\|{p'_n}\|_2\|{q_n-q'_n}\|_2
    \bigr).
\end{split}
\]
The right-hand side tends to zero along ${\cal U}$, since $\|q_n\|_2, \|p'_n\|_2$
are bounded and the difference terms tend to zero along ${\cal U}$. Thus the product is well-defined.

Finally, taking ultralimits in the first inequality gives
\[
    \|{u\diamond v}\|_{\mathscr H_{r+s}}
    \le 3^{(r+s)/2}
       \|{u}\|_{\mathscr H_r}\|{v}\|_{\mathscr H_s}.
\]
Hence $\diamond$ is a bounded bilinear map and therefore
jointly continuous.

\]
Unflatten $\tilde U_n, \tilde V_n$ into tensors $U_n, V_n$.

\label{thm:regularity-lemma-compactness}
    Let $Z$ be a set of graded variables with countably many variables of each grade.
    Let $d_k \in \mathbb{N}$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}$.

First we show that, without loss of generality, we may assume $p_{n,k}$
are ordinary Gaussian polynomials (all variables have grade one).
By \cref{lem:approximability} applied to $(p_{n,1},\dots, p_{n,n})$,
also using that bounded 1-Lipschitz test functions determine convergence in distribution by the Portmanteau theorem,
there exist ordinary polynomials $q_{n,k}$
such that, for all $k \leq n$,
\begin{align*}
    &\left|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} \varphi(p_{n,1}, \dots, p_{n,k})  - \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(q_{n,1}, \dots, q_{n,k})\right| \leq \frac{1}{n}
\end{align*}
for all bounded 1-Lipschitz functions $\varphi : \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}$.
So, if a subsequence of $q_{n,k}$ converges in distribution, then the same subsequence of $p_{n,k}$ converges in distribution to the same limit.
By \cref{lem:moments}, the approximants $q_{n,i}$ also satisfy $\sup_n \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} q_{n,i}^2 < \infty$.

graded Gaussian polynomials $p_k \in \mathcal{H}^{\textnormal{gr}}_{d_k}(Z)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:up-1}
    (p_{n,k})_{k \in \mathbb{N}}
    \overset{d}{\underset{n \to {\cal U}}{\longrightarrow}}(p_k)_{k \in \mathbb{N}}
\end{equation}
Each $p_k$ is a countable or finite sum of Hermite polynomials in the $Z$ variables,
so the entire family $(p_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ can be represented using at most countably
many graded Gaussian variables.

The weak topology on probability measures on
$\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ is metrizable (by the sup over bounded 1-Lipschitz functions weighted by $2^{-i}$ in coordinate $i$),
so we may use \cref{lem:ultralimits}(iii) to extract a subsequence
such that \cref{eq:up-1} holds ordinarily.

    \le \liminf_{n\to\infty}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p_{n,i}^2
    \le1.
\]
Hence $p_i$ is also admissible, and the set of admissible laws is compact.

\label{lem:moments}
Let $d_1,\dots,d_k\in\mathbb{N}$ and $p_{n,i} \in {\cal H}_{\le d_i}(X)$.
If $(p_{n,1}, \dots, p_{n,k}) \xrightarrow{d} (p_1,\dots, p_k)$ then all mixed moments converge, and the convergence also holds in $W_p$ for $p \in [1,\infty)$.

Write $P_n=(p_{n,1},\dots,p_{n,k})$ and
$P=(p_1,\dots,p_k)$.
Since $P_n\xrightarrow{d}P$, each coordinate sequence
$(p_{n,i})_{n\in \mathbb{N}}$ has tight laws.
Recall a collection of probability measures on $\mathbb{R}^I$ is \textit{tight} if for every $\varepsilon > 0$
there is compact $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^I$ such that $\mu(K) \geq 1-\varepsilon$ for every measure $\mu$ in the collection.


By tightness, the Paley--Zygmund inequality, and \cref{lem:hypercontractivity},
we have for $p_{n,k} \neq 0$,
\[
    \Pr\left(
        |p_{n,k}|\ge\frac{\|{p_{n,k}}\|_2}{\sqrt2}
    \right)
    \ge
    \frac{\|{p_{n,k}}\|_2^4}
         {4\|{p_{n,k}}\|_4^4}
    \ge \frac{1}{4\cdot9^{d_k}}.
\]
Thus tightness implies $\sup_n\|{p_{n,k}}\|_2<\infty$.
Applying hypercontractivity again, for every $q\ge2$,
\[
    \sup_n\|{p_{n,k}}\|_q
    \le (q-1)^{d_k/2}\sup_n\|{p_{n,k}}\|_2
    <\infty.
\]
Consequently, we deduce $\sup_n \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}\|{P_n}\|^q<\infty$ and, by the Portmanteau theorem, $\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}\|{P}\|^q< \infty$ for all $q \geq 1$.
Then for every continuous function $\varphi$ of
polynomial growth, the sequence $(\varphi(P_n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is uniformly
integrable. The continuous mapping theorem and
\cite[Theorem~3.5]{billingsley1999convergence} imply
\[
    \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(P_n)\longrightarrow\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(P).
\]
Thus we have convergence of all mixed moments.
Taking $\varphi(x)=\|x\|^q$
gives convergence of the $q$th absolute moments.
Together with weak convergence, this implies
$W_q(\operatorname{Law}(P_n),\operatorname{Law}(P))\to0$ by
\cite[Theorem~6.9]{villani2009optimal}.
Forgetting grades shows $\mathcal{H}^{\textnormal{gr}}_{d}(Z) \subseteq {\cal H}_{\le d}(Z)$ so the same argument applies to graded polynomials.

\label{lem:tensor-frob}
        Let $(T, \pi)$ be a tensor network such that $\pi$ has no self-loops.
        Then:
        \[
            |\operatorname{val}_\pi(T)| \leq \prod_{a \in V(\pi)} \|{T_a}\|_F.
        \]

<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
$\operatorname{val}_\pi(T)$ factors across connected components of $\pi$, so we may assume $\pi$ is connected.
        Use induction on $|V(\pi)|$. We also upgrade the statement to allow non-symmetric tensors (in this case, each vertex of $\pi$ comes with an ordering of the incident edges).
        
        In the base case, $\pi$ is a single multiedge between two tensors $R,S$,
        \[
            |{\operatorname{val}_\pi(T)}| = \left|\sum_{i \in [n]^d} R_i S_i\right| \leq \left(\sum_{i \in [n]^d} R_i^2\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum_{i \in [n]^d} S_i^2\right)^{1/2} = \|{R}\|_F \|{S}\|_F\,.
        \]
        
        In the induction step, let $a,b$ be the endpoints of a multiedge in $\pi$ with associated tensors $R,S$.
        Let $\pi'$ be $\pi$ with the multiedge contracted.
        Let $Q$ be the contraction of $R,S$ along the multiedges between $a,b$
        and associate $Q$ to the contracted vertex in $\pi'$.
        Call this new tensor network $(T', \pi')$. Then $\operatorname{val}_\pi(T) = \operatorname{val}_{\pi'}(T')$ by definition. By induction,
        \[
            |{\operatorname{val}_{\pi'}(T')}| \leq \|{Q}\|_F \prod_{c \in V(\pi) \setminus \{a,b\}} \|{T_{c}}\|_F\,.
        \]
        We can express $Q$ as a flattened matrix multiplication $R^{(L_a,R_a)} S^{(L_b,R_b)}$
        followed by unflattening, where $R_a,L_b$ are the edges between $a,b$.
        Using $\|{AB}\|_F \leq \|{A}\|_F \|{B}\|_F$ on the matrix multiplication,
        \[
            \|{Q}\|_F \prod_{c \in V(\pi) \setminus \{a,b\}} \|{T_c}\|_F \leq \|{R}\|_F \|{S}\|_F \prod_{c \in V(\pi) \setminus \{a,b\}} \|{T_{c}}\|_F
            = \prod_{a \in V(\pi)} \|{T_a}\|_F\,. \qedhere
        \]

<!-- lemma-14: body_md -->
\label{lem:moment}
    Let $d_1, \dots, d_k \geq 1$. Let $p_a \in {\cal H}_{d_a}(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ such that $\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p_a^2 \leq 1$ and let $P_a$ be their Hermite coefficient tensors.
    Assume that $\|{P_a^{(r)}}\| \leq \delta$ for all $a \in [k]$ and $0 < r < d_a$.
    Let $Z=(Z_1,\ldots,Z_k)$ be a centered Gaussian vector with the same
    covariance matrix as $(p_1,\ldots,p_k)$, and let $D=\sum_{a=1}^k d_a$.
    If $D$ is even, then
      \begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-moment-comparison}
        \left|\mathbb E\prod_{a=1}^k p_a
              -\mathbb E\prod_{a=1}^k Z_a\right|
        \le
        \frac{(D-1)!!}{\sqrt{d_1!\cdots d_k!}}\,\delta.
      \end{equation}
  If $D$ is odd, both expectations vanish.
<!-- end lemma-14 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Take a set of $k$ vertices, where $a$ is incident to $d_a$ labeled half-edge endpoints. Let $\mathcal D$ be the
    set of perfect matchings of all $D$ half-edges such that no two half-edges at the
    same vertex are matched. $\pi\in\mathcal D$ forms a tensor network with $P_1, \dots, P_k$.
    We have,
    \begin{align*}
      \mathbb E\prod_{a=1}^k p_a
      &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_1!\cdots d_k!}} \sum_{i_a \in [n]^{d_a}} \prod_{a=1}^k (P_a)_{i_a} \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}\left[\prod_{a=1}^k\operatorname{He}_{i_a}(X)\right] & (\text{\cref{eq:coefficient-tensor}})\\
      &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_1!\cdots d_k!}}
        \sum_{\pi\in\mathcal D}
           \operatorname{val}_\pi(P_1,\ldots,P_k). & (\text{\cref{fact:hermite-matchings}}) \addtocounter{equation}{1}\tag{\theequation} \label{eq:wick-diagram-expansion}
    \end{align*}

\cref{eq:wick-diagram-expansion} is a ``Feynman diagram expansion'' for the moment $\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} \prod_{a=1}^k p_a$.
When $D$ is odd, $\mathcal D$ is empty, and the expectation is zero as needed.

Call a diagram $\pi$ a \textit{pair diagram} if every connected component
has exactly two vertices. Such a component on vertices $a,b$ is
possible only if $d_a=d_b=d$. Its $d!$ possible matchings all
give $\langle P_a,P_b\rangle_F = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} [p_a p_b]$, and its normalization in
\eqref{eq:wick-diagram-expansion} is $1/d!$. Thus the sum over pair
diagrams equals
\[
  \sum_{\text{pairings }\eta\text{ of }\{1,\ldots,k\}}
    \prod_{\{a,b\}\in\eta}\mathbb E[p_ap_b]
  =\mathbb E\prod_{a=1}^k Z_a.
\]

Every remaining diagram has a connected component with at least
three vertices.
Choose adjacent vertices $a,b$ in this component,
and let $r\ge1$ be the number of edges between them. These edges
cannot be all edges incident to both vertices, so, after interchanging $a,b$ if
necessary, $0<r<d_a$.
As in the proof of \cref{lem:tensor-frob}, form a new tensor network $\pi'$ by contracting the vertices $a,b$ and putting at the contracted vertex the tensor $Q$ which is the contraction of $P_a, P_b$. This defines $\operatorname{val}_\pi(P) = \operatorname{val}_{\pi'}(P, Q)$.
By \cref{lem:tensor-frob},
\[
    |{\operatorname{val}_{\pi'}(P, Q)}| \leq \|Q\|_F \prod_{c \in [k] \setminus \{a,b\}} \|P_c\|_F\,.
\]
We can express $Q$ as the unflattening of a matrix multiplication $(P_a^{(r)})^\intercal P_b^{(r)}$. Use $\|AB\|_F \leq \|A\|\|B\|_F$ on the matrix multiplication to deduce,
\[
    \|Q\|_F \prod_{c \in [k] \setminus \{a,b\}} \|P_c\|_F \leq \|P_a^{(r)}\| \prod_{b \in [k] \setminus \{a\}} \|P_b\|_F \leq \|P_a^{(r)}\| \leq \delta\,.
\]
The second-to-last inequality uses $\|P_b\|_F^2 = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p_b^2 \leq 1$.
Thus $|{\operatorname{val}_\pi(P)}| \leq \delta$ for each non-pair diagram in \cref{eq:wick-diagram-expansion}.
There are at most $(D-1)!!$ matchings of $D$ slots, which proves
\cref{eq:mixed-moment-comparison}.

\noindent (ii) $\Longrightarrow$ (iii). Let $p \in {\cal H}_d(X_1,\dots, X_n)$ have Hermite coefficient tensor $P$.
The fourth cumulant $\kappa_4 := \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^4 - 3(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^2)^2$ can be written as a sum-of-squares expression in terms of tensor contractions as \cite[Proof of Proposition 3, (i) $\Longrightarrow$ (ii)]{nualart2005central},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa4}
    \kappa_4 = \sum_{r=1}^{d-1} \binom{d}{r}^2 \left(\|P \otimes_r P\|_F^2 + \binom{2d-2r}{d-r} \|\operatorname{Sym}(P \otimes_r P)\|_F^2\right)\,.
\end{equation}
Therefore, if $\kappa_4 \rightarrow 0$ as $n \to \infty$ then all of the tensor norms $\|P \otimes_r P\|_F$ must also vanish.

Here is a combinatorial interpretation of \eqref{eq:kappa4}.
Following \cref{eq:wick-diagram-expansion},
\[
    \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^4 = \frac{1}{(d!)^2}\sum_{\pi \in {\cal D}} \operatorname{val}_\pi(P, P, P, P)
\]
where ${\cal D}$ is the set of perfect matchings on four vertices with $d$ incident half-edges such that no half-edges are matched at the same vertex.


Suppose $\pi$ has $r$ edges matched between vertex 1 and 2.
Then there are also $r$ edges matched between vertex 3 and 4 and there are $\left(\binom{d}{r}^2r!\right)^2$ ways to choose this part of $\pi$.
We interpret the edges between 1,2 as contracted in $P \otimes_r P$
and the edges outgoing from 1,2 as the indices of $P \otimes_r P$.

There are $(2d-2r)!$ ways to choose the remaining part of $\pi$.
If the remaining part of $\pi$ matches the half-edges at 1 with the half-edges at 3 (i.e.\ $\pi$ is a cycle graph),
we obtain exactly $\operatorname{val}_\pi(P,P,P,P) = \|P \otimes_r P\|_F^2$.
However, in general, the sum over $\pi$ treats all outgoing edges from $\{1,2\}$
in the same way,
so the average value of $\operatorname{val}_\pi(P,P,P,P)$ is $\|\operatorname{Sym}(P \otimes_r P)\|_F^2$.
In total,
\begin{align}
    \frac{1}{(d!)^2}\sum_{\pi \in {\cal D}} \operatorname{val}_\pi(P, P, P, P) &= \sum_{r=0}^d\frac{(2d-2r)!}{(d!)^2}\left(\binom{d}{r}^2r!\right)^2 \|\operatorname{Sym}(P \otimes_r P)\|_F^2 \notag\\
    &= \sum_{r = 0}^d \binom{d}{r}^2 \binom{2d-2r}{d-r} \|\operatorname{Sym}(P \otimes_r P)\|_F^2
\end{align}

We re-analyze the terms $r=0$ and $r=d$ separately.
The term $r=d$ equals $\|P\|_F^4 = (\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^2)^2$.
All $\pi$ in the term $r = 0$ are cyclic graphs (or disconnected).
Let $s$ be the number of 1-3 edges which equals the number of 2-4 edges.
There are $\binom{d}{s}^4 (s!)^2((d-s)!)^2 = \binom{d}{s}^2 (d!)^2$ choices for $\pi$.
For these cases, $\operatorname{val}_\pi(P,P,P,P) = \|P \otimes_s P\|_F^2$.
Therefore,
\begin{align*}
    \frac{1}{(d!)^2}\sum_{\pi \in {\cal D}} \operatorname{val}_\pi(P, P, P, P) &=  \|P\|_F^4 + \sum_{s = 0}^d \binom{d}{s}^2\|P \otimes_s P\|_F^2 + \sum_{r = 1}^{d-1}\binom{d}{r}^2 \binom{2d-2r}{d-r} \|\operatorname{Sym}(P \otimes_r P)\|_F^2\\
    &= 3\|P\|_F^4 + \sum_{s = 1}^{d-1} \binom{d}{s}^2 \|P \otimes_s P\|_F^2 + \sum_{r = 1}^{d-1}\binom{d}{r}^2 \binom{2d-2r}{d-r} \|\operatorname{Sym}(P \otimes_r P)\|_F^2
\end{align*}
Recall this equals $\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^4$. Subtract $3\|P\|_F^4 = 3(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} p^2)^2$ to obtain $\kappa_4$.

\[
    \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} S^{2\ell} = \frac{1}{(Nr!)^{\ell}}\sum_{M \in {\cal M}(2\ell,r)} \sum_{\varphi: [2\ell] \to [N]} \prod_{uv \in M}\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(\text{group}(u)) = \varphi(\text{group}(v))}\,.
\]
where we have $2\ell$ groups of $r$ vertices each, ${\cal M}(2\ell, r)$ is the set of perfect matchings on the vertices such that no two vertices are matched within a group,
and the indicator function checks whether all matched pairs use the same Gaussian $G_{k}$ on their group. The inner summation is $N^{c(M)}$ where $c(M)$ is the number
of connected components of $M$ when the groups are contracted. Hence,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s1}
    \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} S^{2\ell} = \frac{1}{(Nr!)^{\ell}}\sum_{M \in {\cal M}(2\ell,r)} N^{c(M)} = (2\ell-1)!! + O_\alpha(N^{-1})\,.
\end{equation}
The second equality separates the dominant terms where ${\cal M}$ is a perfect matching between groups from the remaining $O_\alpha(1)$ terms.

Express the Hermite polynomials in the monomial basis and use \cref{eq:s1} to deduce $\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} \operatorname{He}_m(S)^2 = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} \operatorname{He}_m(X)^2 + O_\alpha(N^{-1}) = m! + O_\alpha(N^{-1})$ for standard Gaussian variables $X$. We also have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s2}
    \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} \operatorname{He}_m(S) U_m  = \operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} U_m^2= m! \frac{(N)_m}{N^m} = m! + O_m(N^{-1})\,.
\end{equation}
where $(N)_m$ is the falling factorial. Indeed, the first equality observes that the only component of $\operatorname{He}_m(S)$ which is not orthogonal to $U_m$ is its ``leading monomial'' $U_m$ itself.
The second equality observes that nonzero terms in $\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}} U_m^2$ are perfect matchings between the indices in $U_m$.
Therefore,
\[

\]
Independence across the $i,r$ coordinates implies the same holds for the multivariate case.
