<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{D: k_rigidity}
             We call a probability measure $\Lambda$ on $\mathcal{P}(E)$ $k$-rigid when it is the unique such probability measure that has its $k$th moment measure equal to $\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda)$. Equivalently, $\Lambda$ is $k$-rigid if $\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda) = \mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma)$ and $\Gamma \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(E))$ implies $\Lambda = \Gamma$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
By the de Finetti-Hewitt-Savage Theorem \cite[Theorem 1.1]{kallenberg05}, $k$-rigidity is equivalent to uniqueness of the infinite exchangeable extension of the corresponding $k$-coordinate law.
            Note also that $k$-rigidity of $\Lambda$ implies $k'$-rigidity of $\Lambda$ for all $k' \geq k$. An important consequence of $k$-rigidity is that it is easy to check convergence of measures to a $k$-rigid law. To be precise, suppose $\Lambda$ is $k$-rigid. Then a sequence $\Lambda_n$ of elements of $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(E))$ converges weakly to $\Lambda$ if and only if $\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda_n)$ converges weakly to $\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda)$.

            To justify this assertion, continuity of $\mu\mapsto\mu^{\otimes k}$
            gives the forward implication. For the converse, assume $k\geq1$.
            Marginalization gives tightness of $\mathcal{M}_1(\Lambda_n)$. For
            $\varepsilon>0$, choose compact sets $K_j\subset E$ such that
            $\sup_n\mathcal{M}_1(\Lambda_n)(K_j^c)\leq\varepsilon2^{-j}/j$.
            Markov's inequality and a union bound show that
            $\Lambda_n(\mathcal K_\varepsilon)\geq1-\varepsilon$ for every $n$,
            where
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathcal K_\varepsilon
                :=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E):\mu(K_j)\geq1-1/j\text{ for every }j\geq1\}.
            \end{equation*}
            This is a closed tight subset of $\mathcal{P}(E)$, hence compact by
            Prokhorov's theorem. Thus $(\Lambda_n)$ is tight. Every
            subsequential limit $\Gamma$ satisfies
            $\mathcal{M}_k(\Gamma)=\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda)$ by continuity, and $k$-rigidity
            gives $\Gamma=\Lambda$. Therefore $\Lambda_n$ converges weakly
            to $\Lambda$. The case $k=0$ is immediate, since $0$-rigidity
            forces $\mathcal{P}(E)$ to be a singleton.

We say that $(\nu,P)$ satisfies the completeness property if
            \begin{equation}\label{E: completeness}\tag{C}
                Q_\nu[f] = 0
                \quad
                \text{ implies }
                \quad
                f = 0 \quad \nu\text{-almost everywhere}
            \end{equation}

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The completeness condition~\eqref{E: completeness} is a form of conditional
            completeness familiar from nonparametric identification.
            Newey and Powell~\cite{NeweyPowell2003} use conditional
            completeness to characterize identification in instrumental
            variable models; the precise $L^2$ formulation relevant here
            is discussed by Andrews~\cite[Section~2]{Andrews2017}.
            To make the connection explicit, let $(U,V)$ have joint law
            $\nu(du)P_u(dv)$ on an auxiliary probability space.
            Lemma~\ref{L: Kh_dense} identifies \eqref{E: completeness}
            with conditional $L^2$ completeness of $U$ given $V$.
            Thus completeness concerns the conditional law of the parameter
            $U$ given the observation $V$, and depends on the mixing law
            $\nu$. 
            
            This differs from completeness of the statistical family $(P_y)$
            in the usual sense; see
            \cite[Section~1]{KaganMalinovskyMattner2015}.
            That condition tests functions on the observation space $E$
            through their expectations under every $P_y$, whereas
            \eqref{E: completeness} tests functions on the parameter space
            $Y$ relative to the fixed mixing law $\nu$.

<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
\label{R: completeness_sharpness}
            The number four in
            Theorem~\ref{T: completeness_rigidity} cannot in general
            be replaced by three. Let $E=\{0,1\}$, $Y=[0,1]$, and
            \begin{equation*}
                P_y=(1-y)\delta_0+y\delta_1.
            \end{equation*}
            Define
            \begin{equation*}
                p_\pm=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right),
                \quad
                \nu=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{p_-}
                     +\frac{1}{2}\delta_{p_+},
            \end{equation*}

            on $[0,1]$. The component measures $P_{p_-}$ and $P_{p_+}$
            are linearly independent, so $(\nu,P)$ satisfies
            \eqref{E: completeness}. However,
            \begin{equation*}
                \int_0^1 y^j\,d\nu(y)
                =\int_0^1 y^j\,d\widetilde\nu(y)
                =\frac{1}{j+1},
                \quad j=0,1,2,3.

            $\{0,1\}^3$ is a polynomial in $y$ of degree at most three.

                =\mathcal{M}_3\left(\mathfrak{G}_{\widetilde\nu}\right),
                \quad
                \text{ yet }
                \quad
                \mathfrak{G}_\nu\ne\mathfrak{G}_{\widetilde\nu}.
            \end{equation*}
            Thus $\mathfrak{G}_\nu$ is $4$-rigid but not $3$-rigid.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{L: laplace_support}
            Let $0=\lambda_0<\lambda_1<\cdots$ satisfy
            \begin{equation*}
                \inf_{n\geq0}(\lambda_{n+1}-\lambda_n)>0.
            \end{equation*}
            For a finite signed Borel measure $\sigma$ on $[0,\infty)$,
            we define the Laplace transform
            \begin{equation*}
                \widehat\sigma(\lambda)
                :=\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-\lambda y}\,d\sigma(y).
            \end{equation*}
            The following are equivalent:
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
                            \frac{1}{\lambda_n}=\infty$.
                \item For every finite signed Borel measure $\sigma$
                    on $[0,\infty)$ and every $b\geq0$,
                    \begin{equation}\label{E: laplace_support}
                        \sigma|_{[0,b)}=0
                        \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
                        \sup_{n\geq1}e^{b\lambda_n}
                            |\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)|<\infty.
                    \end{equation}
                \item For every nonzero finite signed Borel measure
                    $\sigma$ on $[0,\infty)$,
                    \begin{equation*}
                        \inf\operatorname{supp}|\sigma|
                        =-\limsup_{n\to\infty}
                            \frac{1}{\lambda_n}
                            \log|\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)|,
                    \end{equation*}
                    where $\log0:=-\infty$.
            \end{enumerate}

\label{P: exponential_completeness}
            Suppose that $d=1$, $Y=(0,\infty)$, and
            \eqref{E: finite_partition_function} holds. Define
            \begin{equation*}
                a:=\min\operatorname{supp}\rho,
                \quad
                \text{ and }
                \quad
                \mathcal S_\rho
                :=\{t-a:t\in\operatorname{supp}\rho,\ t>a\}.
            \end{equation*}
            For $0<u\leq1$, define the weight
            \begin{equation*}
                w_\rho(u)
                :=\int_{[a,\infty)}
                    e^{-(t-a)}u^{t-a}\,d\rho(t)
                =e^a u^{-a}Z(1-\log u),
            \end{equation*}
            and set $w_\rho(0):=\rho(\{a\})$.
            Then $w_\rho$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ and positive
            on $(0,1]$. For every $\lambda\in\mathcal S_\rho$,
            the function $\frac{u^\lambda}{w_\rho(u)}$ extends
            continuously to zero with value zero.

            The following are equivalent:
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item For every $\nu\in\mathcal{P}((0,\infty))$, the pair
                    $(\nu,P)$ satisfies completeness
                    \eqref{E: completeness}.
                \item
                    \begin{equation}\label{E: weighted_muntz_completeness}
                        \overline{\operatorname{span}
                        \left\{
                            \frac{u^\lambda}{w_\rho(u)}:
                            \lambda\in\mathcal S_\rho
                        \right\}}^{\,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_\infty}
                        =
                        \{g\in C[0,1]:g(0)=0\}.
                    \end{equation}
            \end{enumerate}
            Under these equivalent conditions,
            $\mathfrak{G}_\nu$ is $4$-rigid for every
            $\nu\in\mathcal{P}((0,\infty))$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{L: Kh_dense}
            The following conditions are equivalent:
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item $(\nu,P)$ satisfies the completeness
                    property~\eqref{E: completeness}.
                \item The linear span of
                    \begin{equation*}
                        \{K_h:h\text{ is a bounded real-valued
                        Borel measurable map from }E\text{ to }\mathbb R\}
                    \end{equation*}
                    is dense in $L^2(\nu)$.
                \item If $(U,V)$ has joint law $\nu(du)P_u(dv)$, then
                    \begin{equation*}
                        \mathbb E[f(U)\mid V]=0
                        \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
                        f(U)=0\quad\text{almost surely},
                        \quad f\in L^2(\nu).
                    \end{equation*}
            \end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Suppose that $f\in L^2(\nu)$ is orthogonal to every
            $K_h$. Since $\nu$ is a probability measure,
            $f\in L^1(\nu)$. For every bounded Borel measurable map $h$,
            Fubini's theorem gives
            \begin{equation*}
                \int_E h\,dQ_\nu[f]
                =\int_Y f(y)P_y(h)\,d\nu(y)=0,
            \end{equation*}
            so $Q_\nu[f]=0$. Conversely, if $Q_\nu[f]=0$, the same
            identity shows that $f$ is orthogonal to every $K_h$.
            Thus the orthogonal complement of their span is the kernel
            of $Q_\nu$, which proves the equivalence of the first two
            conditions.

            To compare with the third condition, put $q:=Q_\nu[\mathbf1]$,
            the law of $V$. Then $Q_\nu[f]\ll q$: if $q(C)=0$, positivity
            gives $P_y(C)=0$ for $\nu$-almost every $y$, and hence
            $Q_\nu[f](C)=0$. Let $r_f:=dQ_\nu[f]/dq$. For every bounded
            Borel measurable $h:E\to\mathbb R$,
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathbb E[f(U)h(V)]
                =\int_E h\,dQ_\nu[f]
                =\mathbb E[r_f(V)h(V)].
            \end{equation*}
            Consequently,
            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{dQ_\nu[f]}{dQ_\nu[\mathbf1]}(V)
                =\mathbb E[f(U)\mid V]
                \quad\text{almost surely}.
            \end{equation*}
            Therefore $Q_\nu[f]=0$ if and only if
            $\mathbb E[f(U)\mid V]=0$ almost surely, proving the remaining
            equivalence.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{L: moment_isometry}
            Suppose that $(\nu,P)$ satisfies~\eqref{E: completeness}, and
            let $h$ range over bounded Borel measurable maps $E\to\mathbb R$.
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item[(a)] If~\eqref{E: k_moment_identity} holds for
                $k=2$, there is a unique isometric embedding
                $W:L^2(\nu)\to L^2(\mathbb P)$ such that
                \begin{equation}\label{E: WKh}
                    WK_h=\eta(h)
                \end{equation}
                for every $h$. Moreover, $W\1=\1$, and the adjoint
                $W^*:L^2(\mathbb P)\to L^2(\nu)$ satisfies $W^*W=I$ and is
                characterized by
                \begin{equation}\label{E: adjoint_characterization}
                    \left\langle W^*u,K_h\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)}=\mathbb E[u\,\eta(h)],
                    \quad u\in L^2(\mathbb P),
                \end{equation}
                for every $h$.
                \item[(b)] If~\eqref{E: k_moment_identity} holds for
                $k=3$, then
                \begin{equation}\label{E: compressed_multiplication}
                    W^*M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}W=M^\nu_{K_h}.
                \end{equation}
                \item[(c)] If~\eqref{E: k_moment_identity} holds for
                $k=4$, then
                \begin{equation}\label{E: exact_intertwining}
                    M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}W=WM^\nu_{K_h}.
                \end{equation}
            \end{enumerate}

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
(a) Integrating $h\otimes h'$ against both sides of the second
            moment identity gives
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathbb E[\eta(h)\eta(h')]
                =\int_Y K_hK_{h'}\,d\nu
                =\left\langle K_h,K_{h'}\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)}.
            \end{equation*}
            Applying this to $h-h'$ and using linearity,
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathbb E\left[\left(\eta(h)-\eta(h')\right)^2\right]
                =\left\lVertK_h-K_{h'}\right\rVert^2_{L^2(\nu)}.
            \end{equation*}
            Thus $\eta(h)=\eta(h')$ almost surely whenever $K_h=K_{h'}$ in
            $L^2(\nu)$, so $K_h\mapsto\eta(h)$ is a well-defined linear
            isometry on the subspace of functions $K_h$. This subspace is
            dense by Lemma~\ref{L: Kh_dense}, so the map extends uniquely
            by continuity to a linear isometry
            $W:L^2(\nu)\to L^2(\mathbb P)$. Since $K_1=\1$ and $\eta(1)=1$, we
            have $W\1=\1$.

            Being an isometry, $W$ is bounded with $\left\lVertW\right\rVert=1$. For each
            $u\in L^2(\mathbb P)$, the map $g\mapsto\left\langle u,Wg\right\rangle_{L^2(\mathbb P)}$ is
            therefore a bounded linear functional on $L^2(\nu)$, and the
            Riesz representation theorem gives a unique $W^*u\in L^2(\nu)$
            with
            \begin{equation*}
                \left\langle W^*u,g\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)}=\left\langle u,Wg\right\rangle_{L^2(\mathbb P)},
                \quad g\in L^2(\nu).
            \end{equation*}
            This defines the adjoint $W^*:L^2(\mathbb P)\to L^2(\nu)$, a bounded
            linear operator with $\left\lVertW^*\right\rVert=\left\lVertW\right\rVert=1$. Since $W$
            preserves inner products, $W^*W=I$. By~\eqref{E: WKh} and the
            density of the functions $K_h$, $W^*u$ is the unique element of
            $L^2(\nu)$ satisfying~\eqref{E: adjoint_characterization} for
            every $h$.

            (b) The third moment identity implies the second, so (a)
            applies. Since $\eta(h)$ and
            $K_h$ are real-valued and bounded by $\left\lVerth\right\rVert_\infty$, the multiplication operators $M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}$ and
            $M^\nu_{K_h}$ are bounded and self-adjoint. Hence
            both sides of~\eqref{E: compressed_multiplication} are bounded
            operators on $L^2(\nu)$. Integrating $h'\otimes h\otimes h''$
            against both sides of the third moment identity, and using the
            definition of $W^*$ and~\eqref{E: WKh}, we obtain
            \begin{align*}
                \left\langle K_{h'},W^*M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}WK_{h''}\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)}
                &=\left\langle WK_{h'},M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}WK_{h''}\right\rangle_{L^2(\mathbb P)}
                =\mathbb E[\eta(h')\eta(h)\eta(h'')]\\
                &=\int_YK_{h'}K_hK_{h''}\,d\nu
                =\left\langle K_{h'},M^\nu_{K_h}K_{h''}\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)}.
            \end{align*}
            Thus the bounded bilinear forms
            $(f,g)\mapsto\left\langle f,W^*M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}Wg\right\rangle$ and
            $(f,g)\mapsto\left\langle f,M^\nu_{K_h}g\right\rangle$ on $L^2(\nu)$ agree whenever
            both arguments lie in the dense subspace of functions $K_h$.
            By continuity they agree everywhere, which
            proves~\eqref{E: compressed_multiplication}.

            (c) The fourth moment identity implies the second and third, so
            (a) and (b) apply. Abbreviate $M:=M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}$ and
            $N:=M^\nu_{K_h}$. Integrating $h'\otimes h\otimes h\otimes h''$
            against both sides of the fourth moment identity gives, as
            in~(b),
            \begin{align*}
                \left\langle K_{h'},W^*M^2WK_{h''}\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)}
                &=\left\langle WK_{h'},M^2WK_{h''}\right\rangle_{L^2(\mathbb P)}
                =\mathbb E[\eta(h')\eta(h)^2\eta(h'')]\\
                &=\int_YK_{h'}K_h^2K_{h''}\,d\nu
                =\left\langle K_{h'},N^2K_{h''}\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)},
            \end{align*}
            and the density argument of~(b) yields
            \begin{equation*}
                W^*M^2W=N^2.
            \end{equation*}
            For $g\in L^2(\nu)$, self-adjointness of $M$ and $N$, the
            definition of $W^*$, the preceding identities, and the isometry
            property of $W$ give the following calculation, with inner
            products in $L^2(\mathbb P)$ on the first line and in $L^2(\nu)$
            thereafter:
            \begin{align*}
                \left\lVertMWg-WNg\right\rVert^2_{L^2(\mathbb P)}
                &=\left\langle Wg,M^2Wg\right\rangle-2\left\langle Wg,MW(Ng)\right\rangle+\left\lVertW(Ng)\right\rVert^2\\
                &=\left\langle g,W^*M^2Wg\right\rangle-2\left\langle g,W^*MW(Ng)\right\rangle+\left\lVertNg\right\rVert^2\\
                &=\left\langle g,N^2g\right\rangle-2\left\langle g,N^2g\right\rangle+\left\lVertNg\right\rVert^2\\
                &=\left\lVertNg\right\rVert^2-2\left\lVertNg\right\rVert^2+\left\lVertNg\right\rVert^2=0.
            \end{align*}
            Since $g$ was arbitrary, $MW=WN$, which
            is~\eqref{E: exact_intertwining}.

\label{L: point_realization}
            Let $W:L^2(\nu)\to L^2(\mathbb P)$ be a linear isometry with
            $W\1=\1$, and suppose that $W\1_B$ is an indicator for every
            $B$ in a $\pi$-system generating $\mathcal{B}(Y)$. Then there is a
            $Y$-valued random variable $A$ with law $\nu$ such that
            \begin{equation}\label{E: evaluation_isometry}
                Wg=g(A)\quad\text{in }L^2(\mathbb P)
            \end{equation}
            for every $g\in L^2(\nu)$.
            If, in addition, $W$ is the isometry of
            Lemma~\ref{L: moment_isometry}(a), then $\eta=P_A$ almost
            surely.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We first show that $W\1_B$ is an indicator function for any $B \in \mathcal{B}(Y)$.
            Since $W$ is a linear isometry, it preserves the inner product
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathbb E[Wf\,Wg]=\int_Y fg\,d\nu
            \end{equation*}
            for all $f, g \in L^2(\nu)$.
            Let $\mathcal{H}$ be the class of Borel sets $B\subseteq Y$ for
            which $W\1_B$ has an indicator version. This class contains
            $Y$ and is closed under complements because $W\1=\1$ and
            $W\1_{Y\setminus B}=\1-W\1_B$. If
            $B_1,B_2,\ldots\in\mathcal{H}$ are pairwise disjoint, choose
            indicator versions $S_j$ of $W\1_{B_j}$. For $i\neq j$,
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathbb E[S_iS_j]=\nu(B_i\cap B_j)=0.
            \end{equation*}
            Thus, outside a single null set, at most one $S_j$ equals
            one. The partial sums $\sum_{j\leq n}S_j$ therefore
            converge boundedly to an indicator, while
            $\sum_{j\leq n}\1_{B_j}\to\1_{\bigcup_jB_j}$ in
            $L^2(\nu)$. Continuity of $W$ and bounded convergence give
            \begin{equation*}
                W\1_{\bigcup_jB_j}=\sum_{j\geq1}S_j
                \quad\text{in }L^2(\mathbb P).
            \end{equation*}
            Hence $\mathcal{H}$ is a Dynkin system. It contains the generating
            $\pi$-system in the hypothesis, so the $\pi$-$\lambda$
            theorem gives $\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{B}(Y)$.
        
            We now construct the random parameter $A$. Since $Y$ is Polish,
            \cite[Theorem~1.8(ii)]{KallenbergFoundations} provides a Borel
            set $F\subseteq[0,1]$ and a bijection $\iota:Y\to F$ such
            that both $\iota$ and $\iota^{-1}$ are Borel measurable.
            For $q\in\mathbb Q$, put
            \begin{equation*}
                B_q:=\{y\in Y:\iota(y)>q\},
            \end{equation*}
            and choose an indicator version $S_q$ of $W\1_{B_q}$.
            Since $W\1=\1$, the inner-product identity gives
            $\mathbb E[S_q]=\nu(B_q)$. For rational $q<r$,
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathbb E[S_r(1-S_q)]
                =\nu(B_r)-\nu(B_r\cap B_q)=0,
            \end{equation*}
            so $S_r\leq S_q$ almost surely. Moreover, $S_q=1$ for $q<0$
            and $S_q=0$ for $q\geq1$, almost surely. These relations
            hold simultaneously outside a common measurable null set.
            On that null set, replace $S_q$ by $\1_{\{q<0\}}$,
            so the relations hold everywhere. Define
            \begin{equation*}
                R:=\sup\{q\in\mathbb Q:S_q=1\}\in[0,1].
            \end{equation*}
            For each rational $q$, nesting gives
            \begin{equation*}
                \{R>q\}
                =\bigcup_{n\geq1}\{S_{q+1/n}=1\}
                \subseteq\{S_q=1\}.
            \end{equation*}
            In particular, $R$ is measurable. Since
            $B_{q+1/n}\uparrow B_q$, continuity from below gives
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathbb P(R>q)
                =\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb E[S_{q+1/n}]
                =\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu(B_{q+1/n})
                =\nu(B_q)
                =\mathbb E[S_q].
            \end{equation*}
            Consequently, $S_q=\1_{\{R>q\}}$ almost surely, and $R$
            has law $\iota_*\nu$. In particular, $R\in F$ almost
            surely. Fix $y_0\in Y$ and define
            \begin{equation*}
                A:=
                \begin{cases}
                    \iota^{-1}(R),& R\in F,\\
                    y_0,& R\notin F.
                \end{cases}
            \end{equation*}
            Because $F$ is Borel and $\iota^{-1}$ is measurable,
            $A$ is measurable, and its law is $\nu$.
        
            We now identify $W$ with evaluation at $A$. For every
            rational $q$,
            \begin{equation*}
                W\1_{B_q}=S_q
                =\1_{\{R>q\}}
                =\1_{\{A\in B_q\}}
                \quad\text{almost surely}.
            \end{equation*}
            Since $\iota$ is a Borel isomorphism, the sets $B_q$ form
            a $\pi$-system containing $Y$ and generating $\mathcal{B}(Y)$.
            Their indicators span a dense subspace of $L^2(\nu)$:
            the Borel sets whose indicators lie in the closed linear
            span form a Dynkin system containing this $\pi$-system,
            so the span contains all Borel indicators.
            Evaluation $g\mapsto g(A)$ is an isometry because $A$
            has law $\nu$. Its agreement with $W$ on a dense
            subspace therefore proves \eqref{E: evaluation_isometry}.
        
            If $W$ is the isometry of
            Lemma~\ref{L: moment_isometry}(a), then
            \eqref{E: WKh} and \eqref{E: evaluation_isometry} give
            \begin{equation*}
                \eta(h)=WK_h=K_h(A)=P_A(h)
            \end{equation*}
            almost surely for each bounded Borel function $h$.
            Apply this to the indicators of a countable algebra
            generating $\mathcal{B}(E)$ and intersect the resulting
            probability-one events. Then $\eta$ and $P_A$ agree
            on that algebra, and hence on $\mathcal{B}(E)$, almost surely.

\label{L: localization_completeness}
            Strong localization implies weak localization, and weak
            localization implies completeness:
            \begin{equation*}
                \eqref{E: strong_localization}
                \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
                \eqref{E: weak_localization}
                \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
                \eqref{E: completeness}.
            \end{equation*}

The first implication follows by integrating over

            $\pi$-system, uniqueness of finite signed measures gives
            $f\,d\nu=0$. Thus $f=0$ $\nu$-almost everywhere.

                \mathbb E[\1_DS^2(1-S)]=0

            $\mathbb E[\1_DH]=0$ with $H>0$ on $D$, so $\mathbb P(D)=0$. The same
            argument applies to $\{\left|S\right|\leq N,\ H<0\}$. Thus $H=0$
            almost surely on $\{\left|S\right|\leq N\}$. Since these events
            increase to $\Omega$, letting $N\to\infty$ gives $H=0$ almost
            surely.

                \mu_D(C):=\mathbb E[\1_DS^2\eta(C)]

                0\leq Q_\nu[f_D]=\mu_D\leq\mu_\Omega=Q_\nu[\1_B].

                =\int_Yf_D(y)P_y(h)\,d\nu(y)
                =Q_\nu[f_D](h).

                &=\left\langle W\1_B,M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}W\1_B\right\rangle_{L^2(\mathbb P)}
                =\left\langle \1_B,W^*M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}W\1_B\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)}\\
                &=\left\langle \1_B,M^\nu_{K_h}\1_B\right\rangle_{L^2(\nu)}
                =\int_BP_y(h)\,d\nu(y)
                =Q_\nu[\1_B](h).

                &=\left\langle \1_DS^2,W(\1-\1_B)\right\rangle_{L^2(\mathbb P)}\\

            variable $A$ with law $\nu$ such that $\eta=P_A$ almost surely,
            so $\mathcal{L}(\eta)=\mathfrak{G}_\nu$.

\label{C: translation_gaussian_rigidity}
            Let $g\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb R^p)$ have a characteristic function whose zero
            set has empty interior, and let $P_y=g(\,\cdot-y)$.
            Then $\mathfrak{G}_\nu$ is $4$-rigid for every $\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb R^p)$.
            In particular, if the first four coordinates of an infinite
            exchangeable sequence are jointly Gaussian, then the sequence
            is a Gaussian process.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
For Borel $B\subseteq\mathbb R^p$, the map
            $y\mapsto P_y(B)=\int_{\mathbb R^p}\1_B(y+z)\,g(dz)$ is Borel by
            Tonelli's theorem, so $P$ is a measurable probability kernel.
            Fix $\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb R^p)$ and a real $f\in L^2(\nu)\subseteq L^1(\nu)$,
            and put $\sigma:=f\nu$. For a finite signed measure $\tau$ on
            $\mathbb R^p$, write $\widehat\tau(t):=\int e^{i\left\langle t,x\right\rangle}\,\tau(dx)$.
            By Fubini's theorem, $Q_\nu[f]=\sigma*g$, and hence
            \begin{equation*}
                \widehat{Q_\nu[f]}(t)=\widehat\sigma(t)\,\widehat g(t),
                \quad t\in\mathbb R^p.
            \end{equation*}
            If $Q_\nu[f]=0$, then the continuous function $\widehat\sigma$
            vanishes on $\{\widehat g\neq0\}$, which is dense by hypothesis,
            and therefore everywhere. Uniqueness of Fourier transforms of
            finite signed measures gives $\sigma=0$, that is, $f=0$
            $\nu$-almost everywhere. Thus \eqref{E: completeness} holds, and
            Theorem~\ref{T: completeness_rigidity} shows that $\mathfrak{G}_\nu$ is
            $4$-rigid.

            For the Gaussian assertion, let $X$ be an infinite exchangeable sequence with
            $(X_1,\ldots,X_4)$ jointly Gaussian, and put $\mu:=\mathbb E X_1$,
            $b:=\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)$, and
            $a:=\operatorname{Var}(X_1)-b$. By exchangeability,
            $2a=\operatorname{Var}(X_1-X_2)\geq0$ and
            \begin{equation*}
                0\leq\operatorname{Var}\Bigl(\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nX_j\Bigr)
                =\frac an+b
                \quad\text{for all }n\geq1,
            \end{equation*}
            so $b\geq0$. With the convention $\mathcal N(m,0):=\delta_m$,
            apply the first part with $g=\mathcal N(0,a)$, whose
            characteristic function $e^{-at^2/2}$ never vanishes, and
            $\nu=\mathcal N(\mu,b)$; let $\Gamma$ be the resulting
            $4$-rigid directing law. If $A\sim\mathcal N(\mu,b)$ is
            independent of iid standard Gaussian variables
            $G_1,G_2,\ldots$, then $Y_j:=A+\sqrt a\,G_j$ are conditionally
            iid with law $\mathcal N(A,a)$, so
            $\mathcal{L}(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)=\mathcal{M}_n(\Gamma)$ for every $n$. Each such
            block is Gaussian with mean $\mu$ in every coordinate and
            covariance matrix $aI+bJ$, where $J$ is the all-ones matrix.
            The block $(X_1,\ldots,X_4)$ has the same mean and covariance,
            so the directing law $\Lambda$ of $X$ satisfies
            $\mathcal{M}_4(\Lambda)=\mathcal{L}(X_1,\ldots,X_4)=\mathcal{M}_4(\Gamma)$. Hence
            $\Lambda=\Gamma$, and every finite block of $X$ has the
            Gaussian law $\mathcal{M}_n(\Gamma)=\mathcal{L}(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)$. Thus $X$ is a
            Gaussian process.

\label{Ex: uniform_localization}
            Let $E=Y=(0,\infty)$ and let $P_y$ be the uniform
            probability measure on $(0,y)$. For every

            \begin{equation*}
                q_f(x):=\int_{(x,\infty)}\frac{f(y)}{y}\,d\nu(y),

            \end{equation*}

            now gives $f=0$ $\nu$-almost everywhere on $(b,\infty)$,
            proving \eqref{E: strong_localization}.

We prove the cycle (i)$\Rightarrow$(iii)$\Rightarrow$(ii)$\Rightarrow$(i).
            The first implication uses the Arendt--Batty--Hieber--Neubrander
            support theorem for Laplace transforms sampled along a M\"untz
            sequence; the last uses the full M\"untz theorem to construct a
            measure contradicting (ii).

            Assume (i), fix $\sigma\ne0$, and set
            $\delta:=\inf_{n\geq0}(\lambda_{n+1}-\lambda_n)>0$.
            Replacing $\lambda_n$ by $\frac{\lambda_n}{\delta}$ and $\sigma$
            by its image under $y\mapsto\delta y$ multiplies both sides of
            (iii) by $\delta$ and leaves (i) unchanged. For the proof of
            this implication, we may therefore assume
            $\lambda_{n+1}-\lambda_n\geq1$ for all $n\geq0$, so $\lambda_n\geq n$.

            Since $(\lambda_n)_{n\geq1}$ has gaps at least one and divergent
            reciprocal sum, parts (i) and (ii) of
            \cite[Theorem~2.8.3, p.~103]{ArendtEtAl2011}
            together give
            \begin{equation*}
                \inf\operatorname{supp}\left(|H(t)|\,dt\right)
                =-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_n}
                    \log\left|\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda_n t}H(t)\,dt\right|
            \end{equation*}
            for every bounded measurable function $H$ on $[0,\infty)$
            that is not zero almost everywhere.
            Put $F(t):=\sigma([0,t])$, a bounded right-continuous function.
            For every $b>0$, $F=0$ almost everywhere on $[0,b)$ if and only
            if $\sigma|_{[0,b)}=0$. Indeed, right-continuity turns
            almost-everywhere vanishing into pointwise vanishing on $[0,b)$,
            and
            $\sigma(\{0\})=F(0)$ and $\sigma((s,t])=F(t)-F(s)$ determine
            the restriction of $\sigma$ to $[0,b)$. Consequently,
            \begin{equation*}
                \inf\operatorname{supp}\left(|F(t)|\,dt\right)=\inf\operatorname{supp}|\sigma|.
            \end{equation*}
            Each side is the supremum of the $b\geq0$ for which the
            corresponding measure vanishes on $[0,b)$.
            Since $\sigma\ne0$, letting $b\to\infty$ shows that $F$ is not
            zero almost everywhere, as the support theorem requires.

            For $\lambda>0$, the bound
            \begin{equation*}
                \int_{[0,\infty)}\int_y^\infty
                    e^{-\lambda t}\,dt\,d|\sigma|(y)
                \leq\frac{\|\sigma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}}{\lambda}<\infty
            \end{equation*}
            justifies Fubini's theorem, which gives
            \begin{equation*}
                \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}F(t)\,dt
                =\int_{[0,\infty)}\int_y^\infty
                    e^{-\lambda t}\,dt\,d\sigma(y)
                =\frac{\widehat\sigma(\lambda)}{\lambda}.
            \end{equation*}
            We obtain
            \begin{align*}
                \inf\operatorname{supp}|\sigma|
                    &=-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_n}
                        \log\left|\frac{\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)}{\lambda_n}\right|\\
                    &=-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_n}
                        \log|\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)|.
            \end{align*}
            The first equality is the support identity applied to $F$.
            The second follows from
            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{1}{\lambda_n}
                    \log\left|\frac{\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)}{\lambda_n}\right|
                =\frac{1}{\lambda_n}\log|\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)|
                    -\frac{\log\lambda_n}{\lambda_n}.
            \end{equation*}
            Both sides of this identity are $-\infty$ when
            $\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)=0$. Since $\lambda_n\geq n\to\infty$
            and $\frac{\log x}{x}\to0$ as $x\to\infty$, the correction
            tends to zero and does not affect the limsup.
            This proves (i)$\Rightarrow$(iii).

            Suppose (iii) holds, and fix a finite signed measure $\sigma$
            and $b\geq0$. The condition
            $\sigma|_{[0,b)}=0$ gives
            \begin{equation*}
                |\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)|
                \leq\|\sigma\|_{\mathrm{TV}}e^{-b\lambda_n}
                \quad\text{ for all }\quad n\geq1,
            \end{equation*}
            proving the implication $\Rightarrow$ in \eqref{E: laplace_support}.
            If $\sigma=0$, the equivalence is immediate. If $\sigma\ne0$
            and the right-hand side of \eqref{E: laplace_support} holds,
            then $|\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)|\leq Ce^{-b\lambda_n}$ for some
            $C>0$ and all $n\geq1$. Then
            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{1}{\lambda_n}\log|\widehat\sigma(\lambda_n)|
                \leq\frac{\log C}{\lambda_n}-b.
            \end{equation*}
            The gap condition implies $\lambda_n\to\infty$, so the limsup
            of the left-hand side is at most $-b$. By (iii),
            $\inf\operatorname{supp}|\sigma|\geq b$, and hence $\sigma|_{[0,b)}=0$.
            This proves (iii)$\Rightarrow$(ii).

            We prove (ii)$\Rightarrow$(i) by contraposition.
            Suppose $\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{1}{\lambda_n}<\infty$.

            $\mathbb R\mathbf1+V$ onto $V$. It therefore carries density of
            $\mathbb R\mathbf1+V$ in $C[0,1]$ to density of $V$ in $C_0$.

            $\mathcal S$, the summands are positive, so
            $\Delta(\mathcal S)$ is the sum of the series in any
            enumeration of $\mathcal S$. The full M\"untz theorem
            \cite[Theorem~2.1]{BorweinErdelyi1996} characterizes
            density for an arbitrary sequence of distinct positive
            exponents, with no separation or monotonicity condition.
            This generality is needed here, since $\mathcal S_\rho$
            may have finite accumulation points. For countably
            infinite $\mathcal S$, the theorem states that

            if $\Delta(\mathcal S)=\infty$. The same equivalence holds
            for finite $\mathcal S$, including $\mathcal S=\emptyset$,

            $\mathcal V(\mathcal S)$ is finite-dimensional, hence
            closed and proper. Thus, for every countable
            $\mathcal S\subseteq(0,\infty)$, density of
            $\mathcal V(\mathcal S)$ in $C[0,1]$ is equivalent to
            $\Delta(\mathcal S)=\infty$.

            $\Delta(\mathcal S_\rho)=\infty$, choose finite sets
            $F_n\subseteq\mathcal S_\rho$ such that

                \sum_{\lambda\in F_n}\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^2}\geq n

            \end{equation*}

            $\mathcal S_\rho$ is countable. The countable case then
            shows that $\mathcal V(\mathcal S_\rho)$ is not dense.
            This proves the equivalence.

            \begin{equation*}

                    \geq\left[1-\delta(M-1)\right]P\geq0,

            belong to $\mathcal{P}(E)$.
        
            To prove the moment identities, we first verify that the
            weights cancel the constant, linear, and quadratic powers
            of $\vartheta$, while giving $-1$ for the cubic power.
            For each $i\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, construct the cubic polynomial
            \begin{equation*}
                p_i(\vartheta)
                    :=\prod_{k\ne i}
                        \frac{\vartheta-\vartheta_k}
                             {\vartheta_i-\vartheta_k}.
            \end{equation*}

            other three parameters. Consequently, for each
            $j\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, the polynomials $\vartheta^j$ and
            $\sum_{i=0}^3\vartheta_i^jp_i(\vartheta)$ agree at all four
            parameters. Their difference has degree at most three and
            four distinct roots, so
            \begin{equation*}
                \vartheta^j
                    =\sum_{i=0}^3\vartheta_i^jp_i(\vartheta)
                \quad\text{ for all }\quad j\in\{0,1,2,3\}.
            \end{equation*}

                \sum_{i=0}^3a_i\vartheta_i^j&=0
                    \quad\text{ for all }\quad j\in\{0,1,2\},

The exponentially convex representation theorem
            \cite[Chapter~VI, Sections~20--21]{Widder} gives a unique
            positive measure $\mu$ on $\mathbb R$ such that
            \begin{equation*}
                F(t)=\int_\mathbb R e^{-\beta t}\,d\mu(\beta),
                \quad t>0.
            \end{equation*}

            $\mu(\mathbb R)\leq F(0)<\infty$. On $[0,\infty)$, monotone
            convergence applies as $t\downarrow0$; on $(-\infty,0)$,
            dominated convergence applies after fixing $t_0>0$, since
            $e^{-\beta t}\leq e^{-\beta t_0}$ for $0<t\leq t_0$.
            Continuity of $F$ at zero therefore gives $\mu(\mathbb R)=F(0)$,
            and the representation extends to $t=0$.

            Suppose that $F$ is bounded. If
            $\mu\left((-\infty,-\varepsilon]\right)>0$ for some $\varepsilon>0$,
            then
            \begin{equation*}
                F(t)\geq e^{\varepsilon t}
                    \mu\left((-\infty,-\varepsilon]\right),
            \end{equation*}
            contradicting boundedness as $t\to\infty$. Thus $\mu$ is
            supported in $[0,\infty)$. Differentiation under the integral
            for $t>0$ gives complete monotonicity, and
            $\mu(\mathbb R)=F(0)$ proves the last assertion.

Take $E=\mathbb R$, $m(dx)=dx$, and $T(x)=\left|x\right|^q$. Then $T_*m$ has
            a strictly positive density on $(0,\infty)$, so its support is
            $[0,\infty)$, and

                \quad \beta>0.
            \end{equation}

            $\left\lVertx\right\rVert_q=(\sum_jT(x_j))^{1/q}$, the energy profile of
            Equation~\eqref{E: lq_density_intro} is

                F(s)=c_{q,d,\lambda}e^{-\lambda s^\alpha},
                \quad \alpha:=\frac1q.
            \end{equation*}

            assertion to a property of $F$.

                \frac{d^2}{ds^2}e^{-\lambda s^\alpha}
                =e^{-\lambda s^\alpha}\lambda\alpha s^{\alpha-2}
                    \left[\lambda\alpha s^\alpha-(\alpha-1)\right]
            \end{equation*}

            completely monotone, and no infinite exchangeable extension
            exists.

            factorizes into iid Laplace densities with rate $\lambda$.
            The theorem gives uniqueness for every $d\geq2$.
 
            (c) If $q>1$, then $0<\alpha<1$, and there is a nondegenerate
            positive $\alpha$-stable random variable $B_{\alpha,\lambda}$

                \mathbb E\left[e^{-sB_{\alpha,\lambda}}\right]
                =e^{-\lambda s^\alpha},\quad s\geq0;
            \end{equation}

            completely monotone, with representing measure $\mu$ equal to
            $c_{q,d,\lambda}$ times the law $\xi_{\alpha,\lambda}$ of
            $B_{\alpha,\lambda}$. The integral representation of a negative

                \int_{(0,\infty)}u^{-d/q}\,d\xi_{\alpha,\lambda}(u)
                =\frac{1}{\Gamma(d/q)}\int_0^\infty
                    t^{d/q-1}e^{-\lambda t^{1/q}}\,dt
                =\frac{q\,\Gamma(d)}{\Gamma(d/q)\lambda^d}<\infty.
            \end{equation*}
            Equations~\eqref{E: radial_mixing_law}
            and~\eqref{E: lq_partition_function} therefore identify the
            mixing law as the polynomial tilt

                d\nu_d(\beta)
                =\frac{\beta^{-d/q}}
                    {\displaystyle\int_{(0,\infty)}
                        u^{-d/q}\,d\xi_{\alpha,\lambda}(u)}
                    \,d\xi_{\alpha,\lambda}(\beta).
            \end{equation}
            This law is not a point mass, since the stable law is
            nondegenerate and the tilting weight is strictly positive.
            The theorem gives an extension that is unique for $d\geq3$
            and not unique for $d=2$.

<!-- remark-12: body_md -->
\label{R: joint_irreducibility}
            For finitely supported $\nu$,
            \cite[Theorem~4.7]{VandermeulenScott} show that if the
            components are jointly irreducible, then $\mathfrak{G}_\nu$ is
            determined by its second moment measure among all random
            probability measures taking finitely many values. By Lemma~\ref{L: finite_competitor_reduction}, this is equivalent to unrestricted $2$-rigidity. Joint irreducibility is strictly stronger than
            \eqref{E: strong_localization}, under which Theorem~\ref{T: general_rigidity} determines $\mathfrak{G}_\nu$ by its third moment measure among all random probability measures on $E$. Under this weaker assumption the third moment
            cannot in general be replaced by the second; see the end of this remark.

            Let $\nu=\sum_{j=1}^N w_j\delta_{y_j}$ with $w_j>0$ and distinct
            $y_j$, and write $P_j:=P_{y_j}$. The components are
            \emph{jointly irreducible} if every probability measure of the
            form $\sum_jc_jP_j$ has $c_j\geq0$ for all $j$.
            Joint irreducibility implies linear independence: if
            $\sum_jc_jP_j=0$ with some $c_j\neq0$, then
            $P_1+t\sum_jc_jP_j=P_1$ for every real $t$, and a suitable
            choice of $t$ makes some coefficient negative. It also implies
            \eqref{E: strong_localization} for every Borel $B\subseteq Y$,
            hence for any countable generating $\pi$-system. Suppose
            $0\leq Q_\nu[f]\leq Q_\nu[\1_B]$. If $Q_\nu[f]\neq0$, joint
            irreducibility applied to its normalization gives
            $f(y_j)\geq0$ for all $j$. If $Q_\nu[\1_B]-Q_\nu[f]\neq0$, it
            gives $f(y_j)\leq0$ for $y_j\notin B$. If either measure
            vanishes, linear independence gives the same conclusions.
            Hence $f(y_j)=0$ for $y_j\notin B$.

            The converse fails. For the uniform kernel of
            Example~\ref{Ex: uniform_localization} with $y_1=1$ and $y_2=2$,
            the measure $2P_2-P_1$ is the uniform law on $(1,2)$, so
            $P_1,P_2$ are not jointly irreducible. Nevertheless $(\nu,P)$
            is strongly localized for every $\nu$. Here order two does not
            suffice. For $\nu=\frac12\delta_1+\frac12\delta_2$, the random
            measure equal to $\frac34P_1+\frac14P_2$ with probability
            $\frac45$ and to $\frac32P_2-\frac12P_1$ with probability
            $\frac15$ has the same second moment measure as
            $\mathfrak{G}_\nu$ but a different law.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{P: finite_block_bounds}
            Suppose that $P_1^{\otimes r},\ldots,P_m^{\otimes r}$ are
            linearly independent for some integer $r\geq1$.
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item[(a)] If $\Lambda$ has at most $m$ atoms and
                $\mathcal{M}_{2r+1}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{M}_{2r+1}(\Lambda_0)$, then
                $\Lambda=\Lambda_0$.
                \item[(b)] The law $\Lambda_0$ is $(2r+2)$-rigid.
            \end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-33: body_md -->
Let $\eta$ have the competing law $\Lambda$. For a test
            $H:E^r\to\mathbb R$, put $K_H^{(r)}(i)=P_i^{\otimes r}(H)$.
            These functions span $L^2(w)$ by linear independence.
            Testing the order-$2r$ identity against $H\otimes H'$ gives
            \begin{equation*}
                \mathbb E[\eta^{\otimes r}(H)\eta^{\otimes r}(H')]
                =\sum_i w_iK_H^{(r)}(i)K_{H'}^{(r)}(i).
            \end{equation*}
            Consequently $W_rK_H^{(r)}=\eta^{\otimes r}(H)$ defines
            an isometry $W_r:L^2(w)\to L^2(\mathbb P)$ with $W_r\1=\1$.
            For $h:E\to\mathbb R$, set $B_h=M^\mathbb P_{\eta(h)}$ and
            $D_h=M^w_{K_h}$. Testing against $H\otimes H'\otimes h$
            at order $2r+1$, and using the spanning property, gives
            \begin{equation*}
                W_r^*B_hW_r=D_h.
            \end{equation*}

            In (a), realize $\eta$ canonically on the $\ell\leq m$
            atoms of $\Lambda$. The isometry gives $m\leq\ell$, so it
            is onto, and $B_hW_r=W_rD_h$ follows as in
            Proposition~\ref{P: finite_independent_bounds}(a).
            In (b), testing against $H\otimes H'\otimes h\otimes h$
            at order $2r+2$ gives $W_r^*B_h^2W_r=D_h^2$.
            The two operator identities and the isometry imply, for
            every $g\in L^2(w)$,
            \begin{equation*}
                \left\lVertB_hW_rg-W_rD_hg\right\rVert^2
                =\left\lVertD_hg\right\rVert^2-2\left\lVertD_hg\right\rVert^2+\left\lVertD_hg\right\rVert^2=0.
            \end{equation*}
            Thus exact intertwining holds in both cases.

            Choose $h_*$ so that $a_i=P_i(h_*)$ are pairwise distinct.
            Such a test is a linear combination of finitely many tests
            distinguishing the pairs $P_i,P_j$, with coefficients
            outside finitely many proper hyperplanes. Set $Z_i=W_re_i$.
            Intertwining and $W_r\1=\1$ give
            \begin{equation*}
                (\eta(h_*)-a_i)Z_i=0,\quad \sum_i Z_i=1.
            \end{equation*}
            These finitely many identities hold on one event of
            probability one. There, at most one $Z_i$ is nonzero, so
            $Z_i=\1_{\{A=i\}}$ for a measurable random index $A$.
            As before, $\mathbb E[Z_i]=\left\langle W_re_i,W_r\1\right\rangle=w_i$.
            Intertwining also gives $\eta(h)Z_i=P_i(h)Z_i$.
            Intersecting the events for all $i$ and for the indicators
            of a countable generating algebra yields $\eta=P_A$
            almost surely. Thus $\Lambda=\Lambda_0$.

<!-- lemma-16: body_md -->
\label{L: finite_competitor_reduction}
            Let $\Lambda_0$ be finitely supported and $k\geq2$. Then
            $\Lambda_0$ is $k$-rigid if and only if it is the unique
            finitely supported law with moment measure
            $\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda_0)$.
<!-- end lemma-16 -->
<!-- proof-35: body_md -->
Only the reverse implication needs proof. Let $\eta$ have
            law $\Lambda\ne\Lambda_0$ with
            $\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda)=\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda_0)$. We construct a finite
            counterexample. No independence of the target components
            is assumed here.

            Put $V=\operatorname{span}\{P_1,\ldots,P_m\}$, and select
            a basis $R_1,\ldots,R_d$ from these probability measures.
            Choose bounded dual tests $h_j$ with
            $R_i(h_j)=\1_{\{i=j\}}$, and define
            \begin{equation*}
                T\mu=\sum_{j=1}^d\mu(h_j)R_j,\quad
                g_h=h-\sum_{j=1}^dR_j(h)h_j.
            \end{equation*}
            Since $TP_i=P_i$, every $P_i$ annihilates $g_h$.
            The second-moment identity therefore gives
            $\mathbb E[\eta(g_h)^2]=0$. Take $h$ to be the indicators of a
            countable algebra generating $\mathcal{B}(E)$ and intersect the
            resulting events. On that event $\eta$ and $T\eta$ are
            finite signed measures agreeing on the algebra, hence
            $\eta=T\eta\in V$.

            The set $C=V\cap\mathcal{P}(E)$ is compact in the finite-dimensional
            total variation topology: it is closed and every element
            has total variation norm one. Identify it with its compact
            coordinate image in $\mathbb R^d$, using $x_j=\mu(h_j)$; these
            coordinates are Borel measurable. For laws concentrated on
            $C$, equality of $k$th moment measures is equivalent to
            equality of the finitely many coordinate monomials of
            total degree at most $k$. Testing products of the dual
            functions gives one direction; expanding
            $\mu^{\otimes k}=(\sum_jx_jR_j)^{\otimes k}$ gives the other.

            If $\Lambda$ is concentrated on $\{P_1,\ldots,P_m\}$, it
            is already finite. Otherwise, let $x_i$ be the coordinate
            vector of $P_i$ and put
            \begin{equation*}
                q(x)=\prod_{i=1}^m\left\lVertx-x_i\right\rVert^2.
            \end{equation*}
            Its expectation is zero under $\Lambda_0$ and strictly
            positive under $\Lambda$. Let $\Phi$ collect all coordinate
            monomials of degree at most $k$, together with $q$.
            By Carath\'eodory's theorem, the convex hull of the compact
            set $\Phi(C)$ is compact. Thus the mean of $\Phi$ under
            $\Lambda$ belongs to this convex hull and is a finite
            convex combination of values $\Phi(x)$, $x\in C$.
            The corresponding finite law $\Lambda'$ has
            $\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda')=\mathcal{M}_k(\Lambda_0)$ and positive
            $q$-expectation, so $\Lambda'\ne\Lambda_0$.
            The polynomial $q$ is only used to preserve nonuniqueness
            in this construction; it is not an additional observation.
