\label{thm: big buddy} There exist $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ such that $n = ax+by$ and $\gcd(x,y)=1$ if and only if $n \in S_{\left(a,b\right)}$.

\label{prop: at least}
Let $r \geq 1$, $0 \leq k \leq 2^r$, and $a < b$. Then each new element in $Z(a,b)$ (i.e. every element in an odd position) is at least $ra+b$.

<!-- thm-2: body_md -->
\label{thm: relatively prime main}
Let $r \geq 0$ and $0 \leq k \leq 2^r$. Then $\gcd(s(k),s(2^r-k))=1$.
<!-- end thm-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We proceed by induction on $r$. Given that $\left\{s(0),s(1),s(2),s(3),s(4) \right\} = \left\{0,1,1,2,1\right\} $, it is easy to verify that the claim holds for $r=0,1,2$. Suppose now that the claim holds for some $r \geq 2$ and consider $\gcd(s(k),s(2^{r+1}-k) )$ for $0 \leq k \leq 2^{r+1}$. It suffices to consider $0 \leq k \leq 2^r$, for if $k \geq 2^r$, then by letting $l=2^{r+1}-k$, we have
\[ \gcd ( s(k), s(2^{r+1}-k) ) = \gcd (s(2^{r+1}-l),s(l) ) = \gcd (s(l),s(2^{r+1}-l)) \]
where $0 \leq l \leq 2^r$. Then we may assume without loss of generality that $0 \leq k \leq 2^r$, and consequently may put $k = 2^r-m$ for $0 \leq m \leq 2^r$. Then
\[ \gcd (s(k),s(2^{r+1}-k)) =\gcd (s(2^r-m),s(2^{r+1}-2^r+m) ) 
=\gcd (s(2^r-m),s(2^r+m)). \]
By recalling $s(2^r n + k) = s(2^r-k) s(n) + s(k) s(n +1)$ for $0 \leq k \leq 2^r$ \cite{rez2} and setting $n=1,k=m,$ we obtain
\[ s(2^r+m) =s(2^r \cdot 1 +m) = s(2^r-m)s(1)+s(m)s(2) = s(2^r-m)+s(m).\]
Then
\begin{align*}
\gcd (s(k),s(2^{r+1}-k)) &= \gcd (s(2^r-m),s(2^r+m))\\
&= \gcd (s(2^r-m),s(2^r-m)+s(m))\\
&= \gcd (s(2^r-m),s(m))\\
&= 1,
\end{align*}
where the last step follows by the induction hypothesis.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{cor: relatively prime cor}
Let $r \geq 0$ and $0 \leq k \leq 2^r$. Then $\gcd( s(k),s(2^r+k) )=1$.
<!-- end cor-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Since we have seen that $s(2^r+k)=s(2^r-k)+s(k)$ in the proof of Theorem \ref{thm: relatively prime main}, it follows that
\[ \gcd( s(k),s(2^r+k)) = \gcd( s(k),s(2^r-k)+s(k)) = \gcd( s(k),s(2^r-k)) = 1 \]
where the last equality follows directly from Theorem \ref{thm: relatively prime main}.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- rem-1: body_md -->
\label{rem: knuth}
    Let $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Then there exists a unique odd $k$ and $r$ such that $a=s(k)$ and $b=s(2^r+k)$.
<!-- end rem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
$\left(\impliedby\right)$ Let $n \in S_{\left(a,b\right)}$. Then there exists some $r \geq 0$, $0 \leq k \leq 2^r$ such that 
\[n = Z(r,k;a,b) = s(2^r-k)a+s(k)b.\]
By Theorem \ref{thm: relatively prime main}, $\gcd (s(k),s(2^r-k))=1$, so we may take $x = s(k)$ and $y = s(2^r-k)$, which guarantees $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, $\gcd(x,y)=1$, and $n=ax+by$. 

$\left(\implies \right) $ Let there exist $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ such that $n = ax+by$ and $\gcd(x,y)=1$. Because $\gcd(x,y)=1$, we also know $\gcd(y,x+y)=1$, and so there exists a unique odd $k$ and $r$ such that $y=s(k)$ and $x+y=s(2^r+k)$ by Remark \ref{rem: knuth}. As we have seen above, $s(2^r+k)=s(2^r-k)+s(k)$, and since $y=s(k)$,
\[ x+y=s(2^r+k)=s(2^r-k)+s(k) \implies x=s(2^r-k). \]
Then $n = ax+by=s(2^r-k)a+s(k)b$ which implies $n \in S{\left(a,b\right)}$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lem-1: body_md -->
\label{lem: ugh FINE}
For any choice of positive integers $a,b$, there exists a positive integer $r_0$ such that if $r \geq r_0$, then $ab \cdot 2^r < \left(p_1-1\right)\left(p_2-1\right)\cdots\left(p_r-1\right)$, where $p_i$ represents the $i^{th}$ prime.
<!-- end lem-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Note that
\begin{align*}
  &  ab \cdot 2^r < \left(p_1-1\right)\left(p_2-1\right)\cdots\left(p_r-1\right) \\
  \iff & ab < \frac{p_1-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_2-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2} \\
  \iff & ab < \frac{2-1}{2} \cdot \frac{3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2} \\
  \iff & 2ab < \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2}.
\end{align*}
Because $a,b$ are fixed, we know $2ab$ is fixed, whereas the right-hand side of the last inequality grows without bound. Then there is certainly a minimum $r_0$ such that $2ab < \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_{r_0}-1}{2}$, and for all $r \geq r_0$, we have
\[ 2ab < \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_{r_0}-1}{2} \leq \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_{r_0}-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2}. \]
By the above, we may conclude $ab \cdot 2^r < \left(p_1-1\right)\left(p_2-1\right)\cdots\left(p_r-1\right)$ for all $r \geq r_0$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- lem-2: body_md -->
\label{lem: satan's little helper}
Let $\phi(n)$ be Euler's phi function and $\omega(n)$ be the number of distinct primes dividing $n$. Then for any choice of positive integers $a,b$ and all sufficiently large $n$, we have $ ab \cdot 2^{\omega(n)} < \phi(n)$.
<!-- end lem-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Because $a,b$ are positive integers, it follows by Lemma \ref{lem: ugh FINE} that there exists $r_0 \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ such that if $r \geq r_0$, then $ab \cdot 2^r < \left(p_1-1\right)\left(p_2-1\right)\cdots\left(p_r-1\right)$. We set $N=p_1p_2\cdots p_{r_0}$ and note by computing $\phi(N)$ and $\omega(N)$ directly that $ab \cdot 2^{r_0} < \left(p_1-1\right)\left(p_2-1\right)\cdots\left(p_{r_0}-1\right)$ if and only if $ab \cdot 2^{\omega(N)} < \phi(N)$.

Let $m > N$. We divide into two cases.

Case 1: $\omega(m) > \omega(N)$. We set $m=q_1^{l_1}q_2^{l_2}\cdots q_k^{l_k}$, with $q_1 < q_2 < \ldots < q_k$ all prime and each $l_i$ a positive integer. Since $k:=\omega(m)>\omega(N)=r_0$, it follows from Lemma \ref{lem: ugh FINE} that $ab \cdot 2^k < \left(p_1-1\right)\left(p_2-1\right)\cdots\left(p_k-1\right)$. We also observe $q_i \geq p_i$ for $i \leq k$ to obtain the following:
\begin{align*}
    \phi(m) &= \phi\left(q_1^{l_1}q_2^{l_2}\cdots q_k^{l_k}\right)= \prod_{i=1}^k \phi\left(q_i^{l_i}\right) = \prod_{i=1}^k q_i^{l_i-1}\left(q_i-1\right) \geq \prod_{i=1}^k q_i^0(q_i-1) \\&= \prod_{i=1}^k (q_i-1) \geq \prod_{i=1}^k (p_i-1) > ab \cdot 2^k.
\end{align*}

Hence $ ab \cdot 2^{\omega(m)} < \phi(m)$.

Case 2: $\omega(m) \leq \omega(N)$. Again set $m=q_1^{l_1}q_2^{l_2}\cdots q_k^{l_k}$, with $q_1 < q_2 < \ldots < q_k$ all prime and each $l_i$ a positive integer. It is still true that $q_i \geq p_i$ for $i \leq k$, and $q_i \geq p_i$ implies that $1-\frac{1}{q_i} \geq 1-\frac{1}{p_i}$. We also know that $ 1-\frac{1}{p_j} \leq 1$ for $j \leq r_0$, and together with an alternate formula for $\phi$, we see
\[ \phi(m) = m \prod_{i=1}^k \left( 1 - \frac{1}{q_i} \right) \geq m \prod_{i=1}^k \left( 1 - \frac{1}{p_i} \right) \geq m \prod_{i=1}^{r_0} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{p_i} \right) > N \prod_{i=1}^{r_0} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{p_i} \right) = \phi(N).\]

Since $\omega(m) \leq \omega(N)$ and we have already seen above that $ab \cdot 2^{\omega(N)} < \phi(N)$, it follows that
\[  ab \cdot 2^{\omega(m)} \leq ab \cdot 2^{\omega(N)} < \phi(N) < \phi(m). \]
This completes the second case, and so we may conclude that for all $m \geq N$, we have $ ab \cdot 2^{\omega(m)} < \phi(m)$.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- thm-3: body_md -->
\label{thm: ta-da}
For $a,b > 0$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$, we have $g(a,b) < \infty$.
<!-- end thm-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$ be fixed. We show that for sufficiently large $n$,
\[ \left\lvert \left\{ \left(x,y\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : ax+by=n \ \& \ \gcd(x,y)=1 \right\} \right\rvert \geq 1; \]
in particular, the set is non-empty. 

For $\mu\left(d\right)$ the Möbius function, recall $\sum_{d|n} \mu\left(d\right) = 1$ for $n=1$ and 0 otherwise. Then we may count $ \left\lvert \left\{ \left(x,y\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : ax+by=n \ \& \ \gcd(x,y)=1 \right\} \right\rvert$ as
\begin{align*}
     & \hspace{0.19in} \sum_{\substack{x,y \geq 0 \\ ax+by=n}}  \hspace{0.15in} \sum_{d|\gcd(x,y)} \hspace{0.1in} \mu\left(d\right) \\
    &=  \sum_{\substack{x,y \geq 0 \\ ax+by=n}}  \hspace{0.31in} \sum_{d|x, d|y} \hspace{0.26in}  \mu\left(d\right)\\
    &=  \hspace{0.13in} \sum_{d \geq 1} \hspace{0.33in} \sum_{\substack{x,y \geq 0 \\ ax+by=n \\ d|x, d|y}} \hspace{0.13in} \mu\left(d\right)\\
    &=  \hspace{0.13in} \sum_{d \geq 1} \mu\left(d\right)  \sum_{\substack{x,y \geq 0 \\ ax+by=n \\ d|x, d|y}} \hspace{0.2in} 1.
\end{align*}

\noindent Upon setting $x=du, y=dv$ for $u,v \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, we obtain 
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{d \geq 1} \mu\left(d\right) \sum_{\substack{x,y \geq 0 \\ ax+by=n \\ d|x, d|y}} 1 &= \sum_{d \geq 1} \mu\left(d\right) \sum_{\substack{du,dv \geq 0 \\ adu+bdv=n }} 1\\
    &= \sum_{d \geq 1} \mu\left(d\right) \left\lvert \left\{\left(u,v\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : adu+bdv=n \right\}\right)\rvert\\
    &= \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) \left\lvert \left\{\left(u,v\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : au+bv=\frac{n}{d} \right\}\right\rvert.
\end{align*}

We now turn our attention to $\left\lvert \left\{\left(u,v\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : au+bv=\frac{n}{d} \right\}\right\rvert$. Because we know that $\gcd(a,b)=1$ and $1 \mid \frac{n}{d}$ for all divisors $d$ of $n$, it follows that there always exists some solution to $au_0+bv_0=\frac{n}{d}$ for integers $u_0, v_0$ (not necessarily non-negative). All other integer solutions must be of the form $u=u_0+kb, v = v_0-ka$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Since we want to count only those solutions for which $u,v \geq 0$, we count only those integers $k$ for which $u_0+kb \geq 0$ and $v_0-ka \geq 0$. Solving each for $k$, we obtain $\displaystyle \frac{-u_0}{b} \leq k$ and $k \leq \frac{v_0}{a}$. The number of integers $k$ satisfying both inequalities is then
\begin{align*}
    \Big\lfloor \frac{v_0}{a} \Big\rfloor - \Big\lceil \frac{-u_0}{b} \Big\rceil +1 &= \Big\lfloor \frac{v_0}{a} \Big\rfloor + \Big\lfloor \frac{u_0}{b} \Big\rfloor +1\\
    &= \frac{v_0}{a} - \left\{ \frac{v_0}{a} \right\} + \frac{u_0}{b} - \left\{ \frac{u_0}{b} \right\}    +1  \\
    &= \frac{au_0+bv_0}{ab} - \left\{ \frac{u_0}{b} \right\}  - \left\{ \frac{v_0}{a} \right\} +1 \\
    &= \frac{n}{dab} - \left\{ \frac{u_0}{b} \right\}  - \left\{ \frac{v_0}{a} \right\} +1.
\end{align*}

By definition, $\{x\}$, the fractional part of any number $x$, always satisfies $\{x\} \in [0,1)$, so
\[ 1 - \left\{ \frac{u_0}{b} \right\}  - \left\{ \frac{v_0}{a} \right\} \leq 1 -0-0=1 \]
and
\[ 1 - \left\{ \frac{u_0}{b} \right\}  - \left\{ \frac{v_0}{a} \right\} > 1 -1-1=-1. \]
It follows that
\[ \left\lvert \left\{\left(u,v\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : au+bv=\frac{n}{d}  \right\} \right\rvert = \frac{n}{dab}+E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\] 
where $E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)$ represents an error as a function of $n, d$ and $\left\lvert E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) \right\rvert \leq 1$ for all $n, d$. We are justified in treating the error as a function of $\frac{n}{d}$ because $a,b$ are fixed, and $u_0, v_0$ depend on $a,b,\frac{n}{d}$.
With this result, we may write
\begin{align*}
 & \ \ \ \ \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) \left\lvert \left\{\left(u,v\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : au+bv=\frac{n}{d}  \right\} \right\rvert \\
   &= \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) \left( \frac{n}{dab}+E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) \right)\\
    &= \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right)  \frac{n}{dab}+ \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right).
    \end{align*}

The first sum simplifies nicely using an analytic number theory identity, given as Theorem 2.3 in \cite[pg. 26-27]{apostol}:

\[ \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right)  \frac{n}{dab} = \frac{1}{ab} \left( \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right)  \frac{n}{d} \right) = \frac{1}{ab} \left( \phi(n) \right) = \frac{\phi(n)}{ab}.\]

Using the fact that $\left\lvert E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right\rvert\leq1$, we can bound the second sum:

\[ \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) \leq \left\lvert \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) \right\rvert \leq \sum_{d | n} \left\lvert \mu\left(d\right)\right\rvert \left\lvert E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right\rvert \leq \sum_{d | n} \left\lvert \mu\left(d\right) \right\rvert = 2^{\omega(n)}.\]
This last equality follows from the fact that $\left\lvert \mu\left(d\right) \right\rvert=1$ if and only if $d$ is a product of distinct prime factors, and $\left\lvert \mu\left(d\right) \right\rvert=0$ otherwise. Then
\[\sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) \geq -\left\lvert \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) E\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) \right\rvert\geq  -2^{\omega(n)} \]
and so
\[\sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right)  \frac{n}{dab}+ \sum_{d | n} \mu\left(d\right) E\left(\frac{n}{d} \right) \geq  \frac{\phi(n)}{ab} - 2^{\omega(n)}.\]
This shows that
\[ \left\lvert \left\{ \left(x,y\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : ax+by=n \ \& \ \gcd(x,y)=1 \right\} \right\rvert \geq \frac{\phi(n)}{ab} - 2^{\omega(n)} \]
and by Lemma \ref{lem: satan's little helper}, for sufficiently large $n$, 
\[  \phi(n) > ab \cdot 2^{\omega(n)}  \iff \frac{\phi(n)}{ab} > 2^{\omega(n)}  \iff \frac{\phi(n)}{ab} - 2^{\omega(n)} > 0 . \]
Then for sufficiently large $n$,
\[ \left\lvert \left\{ \left(x,y\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : ax+by=n \ \& \ \gcd(x,y)=1 \right\} \right\rvert \geq \frac{\phi(n)}{ab} - 2^{\omega(n)} > 0 \]
and since $\left\lvert \left\{ \left(x,y\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : ax+by=n \ \& \ \gcd(x,y)=1 \right\} \right\rvert$ is necessarily an integer, it follows that for sufficiently large $n$,
\[ \left\lvert \left\{ \left(x,y\right) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} : ax+by=n \ \& \ \gcd(x,y)=1 \right\} \right\rvert \geq 1. \]
Then there exists $N \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that for all $n \geq N$, there is at least one solution to $ax+by=n$ such that $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and $\gcd(x,y)=1$; hence, $g(a,b) < N$, which concludes the proof.

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{prop: onesies}
We have $g(1,b)=b-1$ if $b \neq 2$ and $g(1,2)=0$.
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- prop-4: body_md -->
If $b>1$ is odd, then $g(2,b) = 4b-2$.
\label{prop: twosies}
<!-- end prop-4 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We first claim $4b-2 \notin S_{\left(2,b\right)}$ for any $b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. Suppose for contradiction that $4b-2 \in S_{\left(2,b\right)}$. It follows from Theorem \ref{thm: big buddy} that $4b-2=2x+by$ for some $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ such that $\gcd(x,y)=1$. We divide into four cases based on the possible values of $y$.

Case 1: $y$ is odd. Then $2x+by$ is odd, but since $4b-2$ is even, this contradicts $4b-2=2x+by$.

Case 2: $y=2$. Then $4b-2 = 2x+2b \implies x=b-1$, which implies $x$ is even. This contradicts $\gcd(x,y)=1$.

Case 3: $y \geq 4$. Then $2x+by \geq 2x+4b \geq 4b > 4b-2$; this contradicts $4b-2=2x+by$.

Case 4: $y=0$. Then 
\[ 4b-2=2x \implies x=2b-1 \implies 1= \gcd(x,y) = \gcd(2b-1,0)=2b-1 \implies b=1. \]
This contradicts the assumption that $b > 1$.

These cases account for all possible values of $y$, so it follows that there is no solution to $4b-2=2x+by$ such that $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and $\gcd(x,y)=1$. Then $g(2,b) \geq 4b-2$ for $b>1$ odd.

We now claim that $g(2,b) < 4b-1$ by arguing that for all $n \geq 4b-1$, we have $n \in S_{\left(2,b\right)}$.

First, $4b-1 = b + 3b-1$ where $3b-1$ is even, so $4b-1=2 \cdot \frac{3b-1}{2} + b \cdot 1$ where $\frac{3b-1}{2}, 1 \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and $\gcd \left(\frac{3b-1}{2},1\right)=1$. Then $4b-1 \in S_{\left(2,b\right)}$. Next, $4b = 2b+2b=2 \cdot b + b \cdot 2$ where $b,2 \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and $\gcd(b,2)=1$ because $b$ is odd, so $4b \in S_{\left(2,b\right)}$ as well.

We now consider $4b+k$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$. We divide again into cases, this time based on $k \mod 4$, but will only argue the first explicitly.

Case 1: $k \equiv 1 \mod 4$. Then $4b+k=b+3b+k$ where $3b+k$ is even because $3,b,k$ are odd, so $4b+k= 2 \cdot \frac{3b+k}{2} + b \cdot 1$ where $\frac{3b+k}{2}, 1 \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and $\gcd (\frac{3b+k}{2},1)=1$.

Cases 2, 3, and 4, where $k \equiv 2,3, \text{ or } 0 \mod 4$ respectively, follow similar arguments.

Then for all $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, $4b+k$ is expressible as $2x+by$ where $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and $\gcd(x,y)=1$. Therefore $g(2,b) < 4b-1$ for all odd $b>1$, and together with the above, this proves $g(2,b)=4b-2$ for all odd $b>1$.

<!-- prop-5: body_md -->
\label{prop: fareyexists}
For any choice of $a,b \geq 2$ such that $\gcd(a,b)=1$, there exist some $s,t$ such that $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ are consecutive terms in some Farey sequence.
<!-- end prop-5 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $\gcd(a,b)=1$ with $a,b \geq 2$. Because $a,b$ are relatively prime, there necessarily exists some $s$ satisfying $as \equiv 1 \mod b$ and $1 \leq s \leq b-1$. Let $t=(as-1)/b$; then $t \geq 1$, and \[bt=as-1 \leq a(b-1)-1 = ab-a-1 < ab, \]
so $t < a$. Therefore $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b} \in (0,1)$. Additionally, it follows from $t=(as-1)/b$ that $as-bt=1$ and thus that $\gcd(a,t)=\gcd(b,s)=1$, so $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ are irreducible. It also follows from  $as-bt=1$ that $\frac{s}{b}-\frac{t}{a}=\frac{1}{ab}$ and thus that $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ are consecutive terms in $F_{\max(a,b)}:=F_k$ by \cite{ta2}.
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- prop-6: body_md -->
\label{prop: fairydemons}
If $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ are consecutive terms in the Farey sequence of order $k:=\max(a,b)$, then $d$ appears as a denominator between $ \frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ in some $F_{m}$ with $m>k$ if and only if $d=ax+by$ for some $x,y \in \mathbb{N}$ with $\gcd(x,y)=1$.
<!-- end prop-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Let $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ be consecutive terms in the Farey sequence of order $k:=\max(a,b)$.

$(\implies)$ This direction was proved by Edward Sang in 1879 \cite{sang}. Essentially, every term between $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ must be of the form $\frac{tx+sy}{ax+by}$ for $x,y \in \mathbb{N}$, and if $\gcd(x,y) > 1$, we find $\frac{tx+sy}{ax+by}$ is not in lowest terms. Thus every possible denominator $d$ appearing between $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ must be of the form $ax+by$ with $\gcd(x,y)=1$.

$(\impliedby)$ Let $x,y \in \mathbb{N}$ with $\gcd(x,y)=1$ and $d=ax+by$. Since $\gcd(x,y)=1$, there exist $j,k \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $jx+ky=1$. We know that ``if $ \frac{h}{k}$ and $ \frac{h'}{k'}$ are two successive terms of $F_n$, then $kh'-hk'=1$'' \cite{hardy}, so $as-bt=1$. Observe $(ak-bj),(js-kt) \in \mathbb{Z}$ and that
\begin{align*}
    & (ak-bj)(tx+sy)+(js-kt)(ax+by)\\
    = \ & aktx +aksy -bjtx - bjsy +ajsx +bjsy - aktx - bkty\\
    = \ & ajsx -bjtx + aksy - bkty  \\
    = \ & jx(as-bt) + ky(as-bt)\\
    = \ & jx+ky\\
    = \ & 1,
\end{align*}
and it follows that $\gcd(tx+sy,ax+by)=1$. Furthermore, it follows from $as-bt=1$ that $bt < as$, and so
\[ btx < asx \implies btx+bsy < asx+bsy \implies b(tx+sy) < s(ax+by) \implies \frac{tx+sy}{ax+by} < \frac{s}{b} \]
and
\[ bty < asy \implies atx+bty < atx+asy \implies t(ax+by) < a(tx+sy) \implies \frac{t}{a} <\frac{tx+sy}{ax+by}. \]
Therefore $\frac{tx+sy}{ax+by}=\frac{tx+sy}{d}$ appears somewhere between $\frac{t}{a}$ and $\frac{s}{b}$ in all Farey sequences of order at least $d$.
<!-- end proof-9 -->
<!-- cor-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:immed1}
A number $d$ appears as a denominator between $\frac{t}{a}$ and  $\frac{s}{b}$ inclusive in some Farey sequence if and only if $d \in S_{\left(a,b\right)}$ as defined in Section \ref{sec: def,pr}.
<!-- end cor-2 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
This follows immediately from Theorem \ref{thm: big buddy} and Proposition \ref{prop: fairydemons}.

<!-- cor-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:immed2}
If all $n > N$ appear as denominators between $\frac{t}{a}$ and $\frac{s}{b}$ in some $F_{m}$ with $m>\max(a,b)$, then $g(a,b) \leq N$.
<!-- end cor-3 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
This follows immediately from Corollary \ref{cor:immed1} and the fact that $g(a,b)=\sup\left(\mathbb{Z} \setminus S_{\left(a,b\right)}\right)$.

<!-- thm-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:fareyub}
For $N(a,b) \leq p_1p_2\cdots p_{23} $, we have $g(a,b) \leq ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right) < N(a,b)$.
<!-- end thm-4 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Recall $N(a,b)$ is defined as $p_1p_2 \cdots p_r$ for the minimum $r$ satisfying $ 2ab < \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2} $, so $\omega\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)=r$. For $m \leq N(a,b)$, we know $j(m) \leq H(r)$, and so the maximum length of any string of consecutive integers not relatively prime to $m$, that is, $j(m)-1$, is bounded above by $H(r)-1=h(r)-1=j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)-1$. Since 
\[ j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)-1 < \frac{m}{ab}-1 \iff m > ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right), \]
it follows that if $m \leq N(a,b)$ and $m > ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)$, then $j(m) \leq j(N(a,b)) < \frac{m}{ab}$ and so $m$ appears as a denominator between $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ in Farey sequences of sufficiently high order.

We know $ 2ab < \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2} $, so 
\[ ab < \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2} = \frac{p_1-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_2-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2}. \]
Additionally, $j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right) \leq 2^{\omega\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)} = 2^r$ by \cite{costello}, so
\begin{align*}
   ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right) &< \frac{p_1-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_2-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_3-1}{2} \cdot \frac{p_4-1}{2} \cdots \frac{p_r-1}{2} \cdot 2^r\\
   &= (p_1-1)(p_2-1)\cdots (p_r-1)\\
   &< p_1p_2 \cdots p_r\\
   &= N(a,b).
\end{align*}
It follows that all $m$ satisfying $ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right) < m \leq N(a,b)$ appear as denominators between $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ in Farey sequences of sufficiently high order, and thus that $g(a,b) \leq ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right) < N(a,b)$ by Corollary \ref{cor:immed2}.
<!-- end proof-12 -->
<!-- cor-4: body_md -->
\label{cor:refinement}
For $N(a,b) \leq p_1p_2\cdots p_{23} $, we have \[g(a,b) \leq ab \cdot j\left(\frac{N\left(a,b\right)}{p_{q+1}\cdot p_{q+2}\cdots p_r}\right)\] for $q$ the maximum integer such that $p_1p_2 \cdots p_q \leq ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)$.
<!-- end cor-4 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
We know by Theorem \ref{thm:fareyub} that $ ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right) < N(a,b)$. Then $\omega\left(ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)\right) < r $, as $N(a,b)$ is the smallest positive integer satisfying $\omega\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)=r$. Then for $m \leq ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)$, the maximum length of any string of consecutive integers not relatively prime to $m$ is \[ H(q)-1=h(q)-1=j\left(N\left(a,b\right)/\left(p_{q+1}\cdot p_{q+2}\cdots p_r \right)\right)-1\]
where $q$ is the maximum integer such that $p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdots p_q < ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right) $. Repeating the logic of Theorem \ref{thm:fareyub}, it follows that all $m$ satisfying 
\[ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)/\left(p_{q+1}\cdot p_{q+2}\cdots p_r \right)\right) < m < ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)\] appear as denominators between $\frac{t}{a}, \frac{s}{b}$ in Farey sequences of sufficiently high order, and thus that \[g(a,b) \leq ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)/\left(p_{q+1}\cdot p_{q+2}\cdots p_r \right)\right) < ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)\right)\] by Corollary \ref{cor:immed2}.
<!-- end proof-13 -->

If we can show $g(a,b) \neq n$ for all $n \leq ab \cdot j\left(N\left(a,b\right)/\left(p_{q+1}\cdot p_{q+2}\cdots p_r \right)\right)$ satisfying $\omega(n)=q$ in Corollary \ref{cor:refinement}, we may replace $q$ with $q-1$ in the process above. It may be possible to iterate this step.
