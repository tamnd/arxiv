<!-- lem-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:algorithmicPoisson} 
                \hfill\\ 
                Suppose that a hypergraph is sampled from $H_k(n,p_k)$, where \[ \binom{n}{k}p_k = \lambda_k \frac{2^k}{k}n, \] with $k\geq 5$. Let $j=j(n)$ and set \[ \alpha_n:=\frac{2j(n)}{n}. \] Let $X$ count the $3$-tails appearing after the $j$-th step of AKKT. Then 
                \[ 
                    \mathbb{E}\left[X\right] = \frac{16}{3}\lambda_k(k-1)(k-2)(k-3) \alpha_n^{k-4}(1-\alpha_n)^3+o(1) 
                \] 
                as $n\to\infty$.
<!-- end lem-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
\hfill \\
                Since $X \sim \operatorname{Bin}\left( 2\binom{n-2j-2}{3}\binom{j}{k-4}, p_k\right)$,
                \[
                    \mathbb{E}\left[X\right] = \binom{n-2j-2}{3} \cdot 2\binom{j}{k-4} \cdot p_k
                    \sim \frac{(n-2j-2)^3}{6} \cdot 2 \frac{j^{k-4}}{(k-4)!} \cdot \lambda_k \frac{2^k}{k} \frac{n}{\binom{n}{k}}
                \]
                \[
                    \sim \frac{1}{3}n^3 \left( 1 - \frac{2j}{n} \right)^3 \frac{\left( \frac{2j}{n} \right)^{k-4}}{ (k-4)! } \left( \frac{n}{2} \right)^{k-4} \cdot \lambda_k \frac{2^k}{k} n \cdot \frac{k!}{n^{k}}
                    = \frac{16}{3} \lambda_k \left( 1 - \frac{2j}{n} \right)^{3} \left( \frac{2j}{n} \right)^{k-4} (k-1)(k-2)(k-3)
                \]
                \[
                    = \frac{16}{3} \lambda_k (1-\alpha_n)^3 \alpha_n^{k-4} (k-1)(k-2)(k-3)
                \]
                Note that for $\alpha_n = o(1)$ or $\alpha_n = 1 - o(1)$, the expected count of new 3-tails is $o(1)$. Treating the formula for $\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]$  as a function of $\alpha_n$, differentiation shows that it is unimodal and maximized at $1 - \frac{3}{k-1}$.

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:shortIntervalsAlgorithmic}
                \hfill

                W.h.p., the length of any positive interval is at most $\ln^2 n$. Moreover, at most $\ln^2 n$ tails are created during each positive interval.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
\hfill 

                Recall that the count of new $3$-tails after each round is a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables, as is the total count of new $3$-tails in any interval.

                Suppose that some positive interval has length at least $\ln^2 n$. As in every round at least one active edge is chosen by the algorithm and ceases to be active, at least $\ln^2 n$ new $3$-tails must appear in the first $\ln^2 n$ steps of this interval, as otherwise, the interval would be shorter. The probability of such an event can be bounded using a Chernoff bound. Let $X$ be a random variable counting new $3$-tails in a specific interval of length $\ln^2 n$. Using only the fact that the expected number of new $3$-tails in each step is at most $1-\varepsilon$ we can bound the probability of $X$ being large as follows:
                \[
                    \mathbb{P}\left[ X \geq \ln^2 n \right]
                    \leq \left( \frac{e^{ \frac{\varepsilon}{1 - \varepsilon} }}{ \left( 1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{1 - \varepsilon} \right)^{1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{1 - \varepsilon}} } \right)^{(1-\varepsilon)\ln^2 n}
                    = \left( (1-\varepsilon)e^\varepsilon \right)^{\ln^2 n}
                    \leq \exp \left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} \ln^2 n \right) = o\left( n^{-1} \right)
                \]
                where the last inequality uses $\ln \left(1 - \varepsilon\right) \leq -\varepsilon - \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}$.

                There are at most $n/2$ starting rounds for such an interval, therefore by the union bound, the probability that any interval is of length at least $\ln^2 n$ is $o(1)$. By the same reasoning we can bound the total count of active edges in each interval.

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:simpleIntersectionsAlgorithmic}
                \hfill 

                W.h.p., throughout the execution, every active tail intersects at most one other active tail, and any two tails have at most one common vertex.
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
\hfill 

                Recall that w.h.p. at most $\ln^2 n$ tails appear in each positive interval, and every new tail appears while at least $\frac{1}{2} n^{2/3}$ vertices remain uncolored.

                A given pair of tails shares at least two vertices with probability $\mathcal{O}(n^{-4/3})$. 
                
                Now consider three tails, and their order of appearance. If the first two are disjoint, the third must meet both, with probability $\mathcal{O}(n^{-4/3})$. Otherwise, the first intersection and the third tail meeting their union each contribute a factor $\mathcal{O}(n^{-2/3})$. Therefore, the probability that one of the three tails intersects both others can again be bounded by $\mathcal{O}(n^{-4/3})$.

                Taking a union bound over all pairs and triples in each positive interval, and over at most $n/2$ intervals, completes the proof.

<!-- thm-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:UnboundedWeight} 
                \hfill \\
                For every $c > 0$, there exist a density profile $\left( \lambda_k \right)_{k \geq 1}$ and a distribution $\widehat{H}(n; \mathcal{P})$ satisfying $\mathcal{P}(k) \binom{n}{k} =  \lambda_k \cdot \frac{2^k}{k} n$ and
                \[
                    \sum_k \lambda_k > c
                \]
                for which AKKT succeeds a.a.s. on a hypergraph drawn from $\widehat{H}(n; \mathcal{P})$.
<!-- end thm-7 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
\hfill \\
                Recall that the expected number of new $3$-tails originating from $k$-edges is unimodal and maximized at $\alpha = 1 - \frac{3}{k-1}$, where $\alpha = \frac{2j}{n}$ parametrizes the round number. Let $\left( 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}, 1 - \frac{1}{k^2} \right)$ be the critical interval for edges of arity $k$. We show that the contribution from $k$-edges is negligible whenever $\alpha$ lies outside this interval.

                Substituting $\alpha = 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}$ into the expression from Lemma~\ref{lem:algorithmicPoisson}, we obtain
                \[
                    \frac{16}{3}\lambda_k (k-1)(k-2)(k-3) \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \right)^3 \left( 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \right)^{k-4}
                    < C_1 \cdot k^{3/2} \cdot e^{-\sqrt{k}}
                \]

                Similarly, substituting $\alpha = 1 - \frac{1}{k^2}$ gives
                \[
                    \frac{16}{3}\lambda_k (k-1)(k-2)(k-3) \left( \frac{1}{k^2} \right)^3 \left( 1 - \frac{1}{k^2} \right)^{k-4}
                    < C_2 \cdot k^{-3}
                \]

                Therefore, for sufficiently large $k$, the contribution from $k$-edges outside its critical interval is bounded by $C k^{-3}$, for some $C > 0$. The key to success is to choose a sequence of arities such that their critical intervals are disjoint, for example $k_{i+1} = k_i^4$. Then, every $\alpha$ lies in at most one critical interval, while the combined contribution from all remaining selected arities is bounded by $C \sum_{k \geq k_0} k^{-3}$. Since $\sum_k k^{-3}$ converges, this can be made arbitrarily small by choosing sufficiently large $k_0$.

                Finally, fix a small $\varepsilon > 0$ and assign $\lambda_{k_i} = \lambda > 0$ such that the peak contribution from each selected arity $k_i$ is bounded by $1-2\varepsilon$. By taking $k_0$ sufficiently large, $C \sum_{k \geq k_0} k^{-3} \leq \varepsilon$. Since the total expected count of new $3$-tails is below $1 - \varepsilon$, AKKT succeeds a.a.s. As we can pick arbitrarily many different arities, the proof is complete.

{$\widehat{CSP}_{n, p}(\mathcal{Q})$ model} \hfill \\
            Let $\mathcal{Q}$ be a distribution on $K$-ary constraints. For each ordered $K$-tuple of distinct variables chosen from $n$ variables and each constraint $C \in \operatorname{supp}(\mathcal{Q})$, impose $C$ on the tuple independently with probability $\mathcal{Q}(C)\frac{p}{K!}$.

<!-- prop-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:modelEmbedding}
            \hfill

            For $\mathcal Q$ defined above and
            \[
                p := \frac{n \sum_j\lambda_j 2^j}{\binom{n}{K}},
            \]
            the hypergraph obtained from $\widehat{CSP}_{n, p}(\mathcal{Q})$ has independent edges, and each potential $k$-edge of a permitted size appears with probability $(1+o(1))\mathcal{P}_n(k)$ as $n \to \infty$.
<!-- end prop-4 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
\hfill 

            Fix a potential $k$-edge $e$ with $\lambda_k > 0$. This edge appears if at least one occurrence of $C_k$ has exactly the vertices of $e$ in its first $k$ positions. There are $k!$ ways to order these vertices and $(n-k)!/(n-K)!$ ways to fill the remaining positions with distinct vertices outside $e$. Thus $\frac{k!(n-k)!}{(n-K)!}$ possible constraint occurrences produce $e$. Each is included independently with probability $\mathcal{Q}(C_k)\frac{p}{K!} = \frac{\lambda_k 2^k n}{K!\binom{n}{K}}$. Therefore
            \[
                \mathbb{P}\left[e \text{ appears}\right]
                = 1-\left(1-\frac{\lambda_k2^k n}{K!\binom nK}\right)^{\frac{k!(n-k)!}{(n-K)!}}
                \geq 1 - \exp(-\mathcal{P}_n(k))
                \geq \mathcal{P}_n(k)-\frac{\mathcal{P}_n(k)^2}{2}
            \]
            We used $1-x\leq\exp(-x)$ and $\exp(-x)\leq1-x+x^2/2$. The union bound gives $\mathbb{P}\left[e \text{ appears}\right] \leq \mathcal{P}_n(k)$, so
            \[
                0 \leq \mathcal{P}_n(k) - \mathbb{P}\left[e \text{ appears}\right]
                \leq \frac{\mathcal{P}_n(k)^2}{2}.
            \]
            Since $\mathcal{P}_n(k) \to 0$, this proves the claimed asymptotic.

            To compare the two random hypergraphs, assign each potential edge an independent uniform random number from $[0,1]$. Use the same number in both models, including the edge whenever it is below the respective inclusion probability. A $k$-edge then appears in exactly one model with probability equal to the difference between the two inclusion probabilities, which is at most $\mathcal{P}_n(k)^2/2$.

            By a union bound over all potential edges, the probability that the two hypergraphs differ is at most
            \[
                \frac{1}{2} \sum_k \binom{n}{k} \mathcal{P}_n(k)^2
                = \frac{n^2}{2} \sum_k \frac{(\lambda_k 2^k)^2}{\binom{n}{k}}
                = \mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
                = o(1)
            \]
            Consequently, any hypergraph property, including $2$-colorability, holds in the two models with probabilities differing by $o(1)$.

        Let $\mathcal{H} \sim H(n; \mathcal{M})$ be a random hypergraph. Fix a $2$-coloring $\phi$ with $a$ red and $b := n - a$ blue vertices, and let $p_k$ denote the probability that a random $k$-edge is monochromatic under $\phi$. Then
        \[
            p_k = \frac{ \binom{a}{k} + \binom{b}{k} }{ \binom{n}{k} }
            \geq \frac{ 2 \binom{n/2}{k} }{ \binom{n}{k} }
            = 2 \frac{(n/2)^{\underline{k}}}{n^{\underline{k}}}= 2^{1-k}(1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}))  
        \] 
        where the last equality comes from the fact that the maximal arity $k$ with $\lambda_k>0$ is bounded by some constant. We can put an upper bound on the probability that $\phi$ is proper for $\mathcal{H}$.
        \[
            \mathbb{P}\left[ \phi \text{ is proper for } \mathcal{H} \right]
            \leq \prod_k (1 - p_k)^{\mathcal{M}(k)}
            \leq \prod_k \left( 1 - 2^{1-k} + \mathcal{O}(n^{-1}) \right)^{\mathcal{M}(k)}
            = \prod_k \left( (1 - 2^{1-k}) \left(1 + \mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\right) \right)^{\mathcal{M}(k)}
        \]
        \[
            = \exp\left( \sum_k \mathcal{M}(k) \ln \left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right) \right) \cdot \exp\left( \sum_k \mathcal{M}(k) \ln \left( 1 + \mathcal{O}(n^{-1}) \right) \right)
        \]
        \[
            \leq \exp\left( \sum_k -\mathcal{M}(k) \left( 2^{1-k} + 2^{1-2k} \right) \right) \cdot \exp\left( \sum_k \mathcal{M}(k) \cdot \mathcal{O}(n^{-1}) \right)
        \]
        \[
            \leq \exp\left( \sum_k -2 \lambda_k \cdot n \cdot \left( 1 + 2^{-k} \right) \right) \cdot \mathcal{O}(1)
            = \exp\left( -2n \sum_k \left( 1 + 2^{-k} \right) \lambda_k \right) \cdot \mathcal{O}(1)
        \]

        \[
            2^n \cdot \exp\left( -2n \sum_k \left( 1 + 2^{-k} \right) \lambda_k \right) \cdot \mathcal{O}(1) 
            = \exp\left( n \cdot \left( \ln 2 - 2 \sum_k \left( 1 + 2^{-k} \right) \lambda_k \right) \right) \cdot \mathcal{O}(1)

\hfill \\
        Let $X$ be a random variable  counting the number of proper, equitable colorings of a hypergraph $\mathcal{H} \sim H(n; \mathcal{M})$. To show that the probability that $\mathcal{H}$ is $2$-colorable is positive, we will employ the second moment method. Recall that for nonnegative random variable with finite variance, Paley-Zygmund inequality implies 
        \[
            \mathbb{P}\left[ X > 0 \right] \geq \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[ X \right]^2}{\mathbb{E}\left[ X^2 \right]}.
        \]
        Therefore, it is sufficient to show $\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[ X^2 \right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[ X \right]^2} = \mathcal{O}(1)$. First, we calculate both $\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]$ and $\mathbb{E}\left[X^2\right]$.

        Let $M_k := \lambda_k \cdot 2^k \cdot n$. To simplify the subsequent computation, from now on we pessimistically assume that $\mathcal{H}$ has exactly $M_k$ $k$-edges.
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:EX}
            \mathbb{E}\left[X\right] = \binom{n}{n/2} \prod_k \left( 1 - \frac{2 \binom{n/2}{k}}{ \binom{n}{k} } \right)^{M_k}
        \end{equation}
        \[
            \mathbb{E}\left[X^2\right] = \sum_{ \substack{c_1, c_2\\ \text{equitable 2-colorings of }\mathcal{H}} } \mathbb{P}\left[ \text{Both } c_1 \text{ and } c_2 \text{ are proper} \right]
        \]
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:EX2}
            = \sum_{s=0}^{n/2} \binom{n}{n/2} \binom{n/2}{s}^2 \prod_k \left( 1 - \frac{4\binom{n/2}{k} -2\binom{s}{k} - 2\binom{n/2 - s}{k} }{ \binom{n}{k} } \right)^{M_k},
        \end{equation}
        where $s$ is the number of vertices assigned \emph{RED} in both colorings. By equitability, $s$ is also the number of vertices colored \emph{BLUE} in both colorings. The first coloring can be chosen in $\binom{n}{n/2}$ ways, then the choice of $s$ \emph{RED} and~$s$ \emph{BLUE} vertices determines the second coloring. Given a coloring, there are $2\binom{n/2}{k}$ ways for a $k$-edge to be monochromatic. The rest follows from subtracting double-counted configurations.

        Combining (\ref{eq:EX}) and (\ref{eq:EX2}), we get
        \[
            \frac{ \mathbb{E}\left[X^2\right] }{ \mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^2 }
            = \sum_{s=0}^{n/2} \frac{1}{ \binom{n}{n/2}^2 }  \binom{n}{n/2} \binom{n/2}{s}^2 \prod_k \left( \frac{ 1 - \frac{4\binom{n/2}{k} -2\binom{s}{k} - 2\binom{n/2 - s}{k} }{ \binom{n}{k} } }{ \left( 1 - \frac{2 \binom{n/2}{k}}{ \binom{n}{k} } \right)^2 } \right)^{M_k}
        \]

        After minor simplifications, we can split this expression into two parts. Let
        \[
            A(s) := \frac{1}{ \binom{n}{n/2} } \binom{n/2}{s}^2 
            \text{ \quad and \quad }
            B(s) := \prod_k \left( \frac{ 1 - \frac{4\binom{n/2}{k} -2\binom{s}{k} - 2\binom{n/2 - s}{k} }{ \binom{n}{k} } }{ \left( 1 - \frac{2 \binom{n/2}{k}}{ \binom{n}{k} } \right)^2 } \right)^{M_k}
        \]
        Observe that both $A$ and $B$ are symmetric with respect to $n/4$. Furthermore, $A$ is increasing and $B$ is decreasing for $s \in \{ 0, 1, \ldots, n/4 \}$. Therefore, it suffices to show
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:sumAB}
            \sum_{s=0}^{n/4} A(s)B(s) = \mathcal{O}(1)
        \end{equation}

        We will bound the sum above in two separate intervals. First, recall the following approximation of $\binom{n}{pn}$
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:approxBinom}
            \binom{n}{pn} = \frac{1}{ \sqrt{2\pi n \cdot p(1-p)} } \cdot 2^{n \cdot H(p)} \cdot \left( 1 + o(1) \right),
        \end{equation}
        where $H(p) := -p\log(p) - (1-p)\log(1-p)$ is the binary entropy function. As the approximation follows directly from Stirling's formula, it is valid as long as $pn = \omega(1)$ and $(1-p)n = \omega(1)$. 

        \subsubsection{Initial interval}
            \hfill \\
            First, we analyze the sum (\ref{eq:sumAB}) for $s \in \{0, 1, \ldots, \delta n \}$ for small $\delta > 0$ to be determined later. Note that when $s = 0$ the corresponding summand in (\ref{eq:sumAB}) is exactly $\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^{-1}$. Since $B(s)$ is decreasing we can bound the sum in the \emph{initial interval} as follows
            \begin{equation}
                \label{eq:initialSum}
                \sum_{s=0}^{ \delta n } A(s) B(s) 
                \leq \sum_{s=0}^{ \delta n } A(s) B(0)
                = \mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^{-1} \sum_{s=0}^{ \delta n } \binom{n/2}{s}^2
                < n \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^{-1} \cdot \binom{n/2}{ \delta n }^2
                \leq n \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^{-1} \cdot 2^{n \cdot H(2 \delta)}
            \end{equation}

            Since $\lim_{\delta \rightarrow 0^+} H(2\delta) = 0$, to show that the contribution from the initial interval is $o(1)$, it is sufficient to show that $\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]$ is exponentially large in $n$.

            \begin{restatable}[{$\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]$} is exponentially large]{lem}{exponentiallyManyColorings} 
                \label{lem:exponentiallyManyColorings}
                \hfill \\
                In our regime $\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]$ is exponentially large in $n$.
            \end{restatable}
            \begin{proof} 
                \hfill \\
                This proof is fairly similar to the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:nonUniformUpperBound}. We have
                \begin{align*}
                    \mathbb{E}\left[X\right] & = \binom{n}{n/2} \prod_k \left( 1 - \frac{2 \binom{n/2}{k}}{ \binom{n}{k} } \right)^{M_k}
                    = \binom{n}{n/2} \prod_k \left( 1 - 2\frac{ (n/2)^{\underline{k} } } {n^{\underline{k}}} \right)^{M_k}
                    \\&= \binom{n}{n/2} \prod_k \left( \left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right) \left( 1 + \mathcal{O}\left( n^{-1} \right) \right) \right)^{M_k}
                   \\& = \left( 1 + o(1) \right) \cdot \frac{2^n}{\sqrt{ \frac{1}{2}\pi n }} \cdot \prod_k \left( \left( \left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right) \left( 1 + \mathcal{O}\left( n^{-1} \right) \right) \right)^{\lambda_k \cdot 2^k} \right)^n
                    \\&= \frac{\Theta(1)}{\sqrt{n}} \cdot 2^n \cdot \prod_k \left( \left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right)^{\lambda_k \cdot 2^k} \right)^n
                \end{align*}
 
                Thus,
                \begin{align*}
                    \frac{1}{n}\ln \mathbb{E}\left[X\right]
                    &= \ln 2 + \sum_k \lambda_k 2^k \ln\left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right) + o(1) \\
                    &= \ln 2 - \sum_k \lambda_k \cdot \left( 2 + 2^{1-k} + \mathcal{O}(2^{-2k}) \right) + o(1) \\
                    &= \ln 2 - 2\sum_k \frac{\lambda_k}{1 - 2^{-k}} \left( 1 + \mathcal{O}\left( 2^{-2k} \right) \right) + o(1)
                \end{align*}
                By choosing $k_0 = k_0(\varepsilon)$ sufficiently large, we may ensure that $1 + \mathcal{O}\left( 2^{-2k} \right) \leq 1 + \varepsilon$. This yields
                \begin{align*}
                    \frac{1}{n} \ln \mathbb{E}\left[X\right]
                    &\geq \ln 2 - 2\left(1 + \varepsilon\right)\sum_k \frac{\lambda_k}{1 - 2^{-k}} + o(1) \\
                    &> \ln 2 - \left(1 - \varepsilon^2\right) \ln 2 + o(1)
                \end{align*}
                Therefore, $\mathbb{E}\left[X\right] \geq \exp \left( \frac{\varepsilon^2 \ln 2}{2} n \right)$.
            \end{proof}

            Let $\gamma :=  \frac{\varepsilon^2 \ln 2}{2}$. Choose $\delta_I = \delta_I(\varepsilon)$ sufficiently small that $H(2\delta_I) \ln 2 < \gamma$. Then 
            \[
                \sum_{s = 0}^{\delta_I n} A(s)B(s) = o(1)
            \]
        
        \subsubsection{Middle interval}
            Let $s_I := \lceil \delta_I n \rceil$. We have successfully bounded the contribution to our sum from the initial $s_I$ values of $s$ and we want to do the same for $s \in \{s_I, \ldots, \frac{n}{4}\}$. Note that such $s$' are bounded away from both $0$ and $n/2$, hence we can use Stirling's approximations of binomials (\ref{eq:approxBinom}).

            For $s\in \{s_I, \ldots, \frac{n}{4}\}$ we have
            \begin{align*}
                \label{FUN:A}
                A(s) &= \frac{1}{ \binom{n}{n/2} } \binom{n/2}{s}^2
                = \frac{ \sqrt{\frac{1}{2} \pi n} }{ 2^n } \cdot \frac{1}{ \pi n \cdot \frac{2s}{n} \left( 1 - \frac{2s}{n} \right) } \cdot 2^{H\left( 2s / n \right) n} \cdot \left( 1 + o(1) \right) \\
                &= \frac{ \left( 1 + o(1) \right) }{ \sqrt{2\pi n} \cdot \frac{2s}{n} \left(1 - \frac{2s}{n}\right) } \left( 2^{H\left( 2s / n \right) - 1} \right)^n
            \end{align*}
            and
            \begin{align*}
                B(s) &= \prod_k \left( \frac{ 1 - \frac{4\binom{n/2}{k} -2\binom{s}{k} - 2\binom{n/2 - s}{k} }{ \binom{n}{k} } }{ \left( 1 - \frac{2 \binom{n/2}{k}}{ \binom{n}{k} } \right)^2 } \right)^{M_k} \\
                &= \prod_k \left( \frac{ 1 - \frac{4(n/2)^k - 2s^k - 2(n/2 - s)^k}{n^k} }{ \left( 1 - \frac{2(n/2)^k}{n^k} \right)^2 } \cdot \left( 1 + \mathcal{O}\left( n^{-1} \right) \right) \right)^{\lambda_k \cdot 2^k \cdot n} \\
                &= \prod_k \left( \frac{ 1 - 4(1/2)^k + 2(s/n)^k + 2(1/2 - s/n)^k }{ \left( 1 - 2(1/2)^k \right)^2 } \right)^{\lambda_k \cdot 2^k \cdot n} \cdot \mathcal{O}(1)
            \end{align*}

            Based on these approximations, we define two real functions on the interval $\left[ 0, 1/2 \right]$.
            \[
                a(x) := 2^{H(2x) - 1} 
                \text{\quad and \quad} 
                b(x) := \prod_k \left( 1 + \frac{ -4(1/2)^{2k} + 2x^k + 2(1/2 - x)^k }{ \left( 1 - 2(1/2)^k \right)^2 } \right)^{\lambda_k \cdot 2^k}
            \]

            Note that $A(s) = \mathcal{O}\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \cdot a(s/n)^n \right)$ and $B(s) = \mathcal{O}\left( b(s/n)^n \right)$. Together with the bound on the initial interval we get
            \[
                \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X^2\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^2} = \mathcal{O}\left( \sum_{s = s_I}^{n/4} n^{-1/2} \cdot \left( a(s/n) \cdot b(s/n) \right)^n \right) + o(1)
            \]
            Therefore, from now on we focus on $a$ and $b$.

            Using $1+x \leq e^x$
            \[
                b(x) \leq \prod_k \exp\left( \lambda_k \cdot 2^k \cdot \frac{ -4(1/2)^{2k} + 2x^k + 2(1/2 - x)^k }{ \left( 1 - 2(1/2)^k \right)^2 } \right)
                = \exp\left( \sum_k 2\lambda_k \cdot \frac{ -2^{1-k} + (2x)^k + (1 - 2x)^k }{ \left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right)^2 } \right)
            \]

            Let
            \[
                F(x) := \left( H(x) - 1 \right) \ln 2 + \sum_k 2\lambda_k \frac{ -2^{1-k} + x^k + (1-x)^k }{ \left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right)^2 }
            \]
            Consequently
            \[
                \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[ X^2 \right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[ X \right]^2} 
                = \mathcal{O}\left( n^{-1/2} \sum_{s = s_I}^{n/4} \exp\left( n \cdot F \left( \frac{2s}{n} \right) \right) \right) + o(1)
            \]

            \begin{restatable}[$F$ is uniformly strictly concave]{lem}{uniformConcave} 
                \label{lem:uniformConcave}
                \hfill \\
                For sufficiently large $k_0$, there exists $c > 0$ such that $F''(x) \leq -c$ for every $x \in [2\delta_I, 1/2]$.
            \end{restatable}
            \begin{proof}
                \hfill \\
                We prove this lemma by analyzing $F$'s second derivative
                \[
                    F''(x) = - \frac{1}{x(1-x)} + \sum_k 2\lambda_k \frac{k(k-1)\left( x^{k-2} + (1-x)^{k-2} \right)}{\left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right)^2}
                \]
                Note that
                \[
                    -\frac{1}{x(1-x)} \leq -4
                    \text{\quad and, for } k \geq 2 \text{, }
                    \frac{1}{\left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right)^2} \leq 4
                \]
                Moreover, for $x \in [2\delta_I, 1/2]$
                \[
                    x^{k-2} + (1-x)^{k-2} \leq 2^{2-k} + (1 - 2\delta_I)^{k-2}
                \]
                Therefore
                \begin{align*}
                    \sum_k 2\lambda_k \frac{k(k-1)\left( x^{k-2} + (1-x)^{k-2} \right)}{\left( 1 - 2^{1-k} \right)^2}
                    &\leq 8 \sum_k \lambda_k \cdot \sup_{k \geq k_0} k(k-1)\left( x^{k-2} + (1-x)^{k-2} \right) \\
                    &\leq 8 \sum_k \lambda_k \cdot \sup_{k \geq k_0} k(k-1)\left( 2^{2-k} + (1 - 2\delta_I)^{k-2} \right) \\
                    &\leq 4\ln 2 \sup_{k \geq k_0 }  k(k-1)\left( 2^{2-k} + (1 - 2\delta_I)^{k-2} \right)
                \end{align*}
                The supremum tends to $0$ as $k_0 \to \infty$. Therefore, for sufficiently large $k_0$, there exists some $c > 0$ such that $F''(x) < -c$ throughout the interval.
            \end{proof}

            Note that $F(x) = F(1 - x)$. Therefore, $F'(1/2) = 0$. Direct substitution gives $F(1/2) = 0$ as well. Hence, by Taylor's theorem, for every $x \in [2\delta_I, 1/2]$ there exists some $\xi$ such that
            \begin{align*}
                F(x) &= F(1/2) + F'(1/2)(x - 1/2) + \frac{1}{2}F''(\xi)(x - 1/2)^2 \\
                &= \frac{1}{2}F''(\xi)(x - 1/2)^2 \\
                &\leq -\frac{c}{2}(1/2 - x)^2
            \end{align*}

            Substituting $x = 2s / n$, we get $F(2s / n) \leq -\frac{c}{2}\left( 1/2 - 2s / n \right)^2$. Therefore 
            \begin{align*}
                n^{-1/2} \sum_{s = s_I}^{n/4} \exp \left( n \cdot F(2s / n) \right) 
                &\leq n^{-1/2} \sum_{s = s_I}^{n/4} \exp \left( -\frac{2c}{n} (n/4 - s)^2 \right) \\
                &\leq n^{-1/2} \sum_{d = 0}^{\infty} \exp \left( -\frac{2c}{n} d^2 \right) \\
                &\leq n^{-1/2} \left( 1 + \int_{0}^{\infty} \exp \left( -\frac{2c}{n} t^2 \right) dt \right) \\
                &= n^{-1/2} + \sqrt{\pi / 8c} = \mathcal{O}(1)
            \end{align*}
            Combining this with the bound on the initial interval, we get
            \[
                \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X^2\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^2} = \mathcal{O}(1)
            \]
            and the Paley-Zygmund inequality finishes the proof.
