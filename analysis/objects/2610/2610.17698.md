<!-- lem-1: body_md -->
(\cite[Lemma $2.10$]{ARV})\label{ram1}
 Let $R \subsetneq U \subsetneq W$ be subspaces of $W$, where $\dim R = r$,  $\dim U = r +1$, $\dim W = m$ and $0 \leq r \leq m-2$. Then every simple map $S : R \to W$ has exactly $q^m - q^{r+1}$ simple extensions to $U$. Thus, after fixing $v \in U \setminus R$, exactly $q^{r+1}$ possible values of $S(v)$ (in $W$) produce nonsimple extensions.

<!-- lem-2: body_md -->
(\cite[Lemma $2.7$]{Ra})\label{ram2}
  Every simple map on a hyperplane $H \subset W$ has a unique extension in $\mathcal{C}_h$.

<!-- lem-3: body_md -->
\label{binaryfinalextension}
Suppose $q = 2$, and subspaces $R \subset H \subset W$ such that $\dim R = m -2$, $\dim H = m-1$, and $\dim W = m$. Let $S : R \to W$ be simple, and suppose $S(R) \not \subseteq H$. Write $H = R \oplus \mathbb{F}_2 v$. For any $a \in W$, there is a simple extension $\widetilde{S} : H \to W$ such that $\widetilde{S} (v) \not \in a + H$.
<!-- end lem-3 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For $x \in W$, let $S_x$ extend $S$ by $S_x(v) = x$, and put 
$$
E = \Set{ x \in W : S_x \textrm{ is simple}}, \qquad D = W \setminus E.
$$
By Lemma \ref{ram1}, $\left| E \right| = 2^m -2^{m-1} = 2^{m-1}$. We show that $E$ cannot be an entire affine hyperplane. Suppose not. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, its complement $D$ is also an affine hyperplane. Since $S_0(v) = 0$ by definition, $S_0$ is not simple, hence $0 \in D$ and so $D$ is a \textit{linear} hyperplane. On the other hand, note that $v , S(r), S(r) + r + v \in D$ for all $r \in R$. Indeed, $S_v(v) = v$ so that $S_v$ is not simple, hence $v \not \in E$. Similarly, since $v + r \neq 0$ because $v \not \in R$, we have  $S_{S(r)}(v + r) = S(r) + S(r) = 0$ (we are in characteristic $2$), and $S_{S(r) + r + v} (v + r) = v+r$, so that $S_{S(r)}$ and $S_{S(r)+r + v}$	 cannot be simple. Now, since $D$ is linear, it also contains $r = S(r) + (S(r) + r + v) + v$, so that $R \subset D$, and since $v \in D$ it follows that $H \subset D$. Since both $H$ and $D$ are hyperplanes, $H = D$. But $S(r) \in D$ for every $r \in R$, so that $S(R) \subseteq H$, a contradiction. 

Therefore $E$ is not an affine hyperplane. If $E \subseteq a + H$, then since $\left| E \right| = 2^{m-1}$ and $\left| a + H \right| = 2^{m-1}$ it follows that $E = a + H$ and $E$ is a hyperplane, again a contradiction. Thus, for every $a \in W$, some ``good'' image lies outside every $a + H$.

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{mainprop}
Let $m \geq 1$, $W$ be an $m$-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}_q$,  $A \in \operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}_q}(W)$, and $0 \neq c \in W$. Suppose 
$$
m = 1,\qquad  \textrm{ or } \quad q > 2, \qquad \textrm{ or } \quad m \geq 4.
$$
For every monic irreducible $h$ of degree $m$, there exists $B \in \mathcal{C}_h$ such that $c$ is cyclic for $A - B$.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
If $m =1$, every nonzero vector is cyclic for every operator, in particular for $A - B$. 

For $m \geq 2$, we seek a basis $(v_0 = c, v_1, \ldots, v_{m-1})$ of $W$ such that the maps
$$
S_i : R_{i+1} \to W, \qquad S_i(v_j) = Av_j - v_{j+1} \quad (0 \leq j \leq i),
$$
are simple for $0 \leq i \leq m-2$, where $R_0 = 0$ and $R_j = \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_{j-1})$. Each $S_i$ extends $S_{i-1}$, with $S_{-1} :\Set{0} \to W$. In particular, $S_{m-2}$ is simple on the hyperplane $H = R_{m-1}$. By Lemma \ref{ram2}, it extends uniquely to $B \in \mathcal{C}_h$. Then $(A-B)v_i = v_{i+1}$, for $0 \leq i  \leq m-2$, so that $c$ is cyclic for $A - B$.

For $q > 2$, begin with $v_0 = c$ and the simple zero map $S_{-1} : \Set{0} \to W$ on the zero subspace.  First, we choose $v_1$ (to be decided later) and then assign the image of $v_0$. For any candidate $v_1 \in W$, define $S_0(v_0) = A v_0 - v_1$. This determines $S_0$ on the subspace generated by $v_0$. Now we choose $v_1$ so that $v_1 \not \in \textrm{span}(v_0)$ and such that $S_0$ is simple. These conditions can be checked for each candidate $v_1 \in W$. In particular, at this step, simplicity means $Av_0 - v_1 \not \in \textrm{span}(v_0)$. At the end of this step, $v_1$ is known, but $S_0(v_1)$ \textit{has not been defined}, since the domain of $S_0$ is still the subspace generated by $v_0 = c$. Now, by induction on $r$, we want to extend $S_{r-1}$ on $R_{r} := \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_{r-1})$ to a map $S_{r}$ on $R_{r+1} = \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_r)$, so suppose, for $r \geq 1$, $v_0, \ldots, v_{r-1},v_r$ have been chosen so that the ordered set $(v_0, \ldots, v_{r-1},v_r)$ is linearly independent and $S_{r-1}$ is simple on $R_r := \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_{r-1})$. Choosing $v_{r+1}$ prescribes $S_r(v_r) = A v_r - v_{r+1}$, hence defines an extension of $S_{r-1}$ to $S_r$ by defining on $v_r$. But, by Lemma \ref{ram1}, exactly $q^{r+1}$ choices in $W$ (of $v_{r+1}$) produce a \textit{nonsimple} extension, and furthermore also $q^{r+1}$ choices lie in $R_{r+1}$ making the new vectors dependent; these sets may overlap. Hence at least 
$$
q^m -2 q^{r+1} \geq q^{m-1}(q-2) > 0
$$
choices work for every $0 \leq r \leq m-2$. Thus, finite induction constructs the desired basis and a simple map $S_{m-2}$ on $H$. By Lemma \ref{ram2}, this simple map extends to a unique $B \in \mathcal{C}_h$ satisfying the desired properties. 

Now suppose $q = 2$ and $m \geq 4$. The preceding estimate can become zero, so we take care by keeping a reserved vector $\omega$ outside the chosen span at each induction step. We first describe the induction step, and then the initial choices that must be made to start the induction. Suppose $v_0, \ldots, v_{r-1},v_r$ are independent,
$$
\omega \not \in R_{r+1} = \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_{r-1},v_r),
$$
and $S_{r-1} : R_r \to W$ is simple. In choosing $v_{r+1}$, we want to avoid the nonsimple choices and also the set $R_{r+1} + \mathbb{F}_2 \omega$, having sizes $2^{r+1}$ and $2^{r+2}$ respectively. Indeed, the set $R_{r+1}+ \mathbb{F}_2 \omega$ is the disjoint union
$$
R_{r+1} + \mathbb{F}_2 \omega = R_{r+1} \sqcup (\omega + R_{r+1})
$$
If we choose $v_{r+1} \in R_{r+1}$, the enlarged set of vectors is linearly dependent. Moreover, if $v_{r+1} \in \omega + R_{r+1}$, so that $v_{r+1} = \omega  + u$ for some $u \in R_{r+1}$, then $\omega = u + v_{r+1}$. Hence $\omega$ belongs to $\textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_r, v_{r+1})$. Thus avoiding $R_{r+1} + \mathbb{F}_2 \omega$ for the choice of $v_{r+1}$ keeps the chosen vectors independent and keeps $\omega$ outside their span. Put together,  for $r \leq m-3$, since
$$
2^{r+1} + 2^{r+2} \leq 3 \cdot 2^{m-2} < 2^m	
$$ 
an allowable choice always exists. We make the initial choices needed to begin the construction. We split into three cases. 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item 
  Suppose $Ac \not \in \mathbb{F}_2 c$. Put $\omega = Ac$, and choose $v_1 \not \in \textrm{span}(c, \omega)$. For $R_1 = \mathbb{F}_2 c$, defining $S_0(c) = \omega + v_1$ gives a simple map $S_0 : R_1 \to W$, and $\omega \not \in \textrm{span}(c,v_1)$. Applying the construction above for $r = 1, \ldots, m-3$, we get at the end 
  $$
  R_{m-2} = \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_{m-3}), \qquad H = R_{m-1} = \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_{m-2})
  $$
  with $S_{m-3} : R_{m-2} \to W$ simple and $\omega \not \in H$. Since $v_1 \in H$, we have $S_{m-3}(c) = \omega + v_1 \not \in H$, and hence $S_{m-3}(R_{m-2}) \not \subseteq H$. Applying Lemma \ref{binaryfinalextension} with $v = v_{m-2}$, and $a = A v_{m-2}$, we obtain a simple extension $S_{m-2} : H \to W$ satisfying $S_{m-2}(v_{m-2}) \not \in A v_{m-2} + H$. Set
  $$
  v_{m-1} := Av_{m-2} + S_{m-2}(v_{m-2}).
  $$
  Then $v_{m-1} \not \in H$, and $(v_0, \ldots, v_{m-1})$ is a basis for $W$ and $S_{m-2}$ satisfies $S_{m-2}(v_i) = Av_i - v_{i+1}$ for $0 \leq i \leq m-2$. Now, applying Lemma \ref{ram2} we obtain a unique extension of $S_{m-2}$ to a $B \in \mathcal{C}_h$.
  \item
  Suppose $Ac \in \mathbb{F}_2c$, and the induced operator on  $W/\mathbb{F}_2c$ is nonscalar. This case starts with a two-dimensional map. Let $Ac = \alpha c$, where $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_2$. Choose $v_1$ such that $Av_1 \not \in \textrm{span}(c,v_1)$, and put $\omega = A v_1$; such a vector exists because the induced operator on $W/\mathbb{F}_2 c$ is nonscalar. Since $m \geq 4$ (here we use the hypothesis that $m \geq 4$), choose $v_2 \not \in \textrm{span}(c,v_1, \omega)$. Define $S_1 : R_2 \to W$ by
  $$
  S_1(c) = \alpha c + v_1, \qquad S_1(v_1) = \omega + v_2.
  $$
  This map on $U = \textrm{span}(c,v_1)$ is simple. Indeed, the kernel of $U \xrightarrow{S_1} W \to W/U$ is precisely $\mathbb{F}_2c$. Every invariant subspace in $U$ must therefore lie in $\mathbb{F}_2 c$, while that line is not invariant because $S_1(c) \not \in \mathbb{F}_2 c$.
  
  Also $\omega \not \in \textrm{span}(c,v_1, v_2)$.  If $m > 4$, apply the above induction for $r = 2, \ldots, m-3$; if $m =4$, no induction step is needed. In both cases, put 
  $$
  R_{m-2} = \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_{m-3}), \qquad H = \textrm{span}(v_0, \ldots, v_{m-2})
  $$
  Then $S_{m-3} : R_{m-2} \to W$ is simple, $\omega \not \in H$, and $v_1 \in R_{m-2}$. Since $v_2 \in H$, $S_{m-3}(v_1) = \omega + v_2 \not \in H$. Thus $S_{m-3}(R_{m-2}) \not \subseteq H$. Now Lemma \ref{binaryfinalextension} completes the construction as in the first case.
  
  
  \item
  Suppose $Ac \in \mathbb{F}_2 c$, and the induced operator on $W/\mathbb{F}_2 c$ is scalar. Then $A = a I + c \phi$ for some $a \in \mathbb{F}_2$ and $\phi \in W^*$. Indeed, since the induced operator on $W/\mathbb{F}_2c$ is scalar, $(A- aI)W \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2 c$, so that $A = aI + c \phi$ for some $\phi \in W^*$. Choose any $B \in \mathcal{C}_h$. Irreducibility of $h$ implies that every nonzero vector is cyclic for $B$, because the subspace generated by $B^i c$, with $i \geq 0$, is a nonzero  invariant subspace, which must equal $W$ since $h$ is irreducible.  Put 
  $$
  T = A - B = a I + B + c \phi.
  $$
  For every $j \geq 1$, induction gives
  $$
  T^j c - B^j c \in \textrm{span}(c, Bc, \ldots, B^{j-1}c).
  $$
  Indeed, $T (B^ic) = B^{i+1}c + a B^i c + \phi(B^ic)c$. Consequently, $\textrm{span}(c, Tc, \ldots, T^j c) = \textrm{span}(c, Bc, \ldots, B^jc)$. Taking $j = m-1$ proves that $c$ is cyclic for $A-B$. These three cases exhaust all possibilities and complete the proof. 

\end{enumerate}

<!-- lem-4: body_md -->
\label{mulchar}
Let $g$ be a generator of $\mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}$ and $\chi: \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times} \to \mathbb{C}^{\times}$ be the multiplicative character defined by 
$$
\chi(g^j) := \exp \left( \frac{2\pi j}{e} \cdot i \right)
$$
For $v \neq 0$,
$$
\sum_{j = 0}^{e-1} \chi(v)^j = \begin{cases} e & \textrm{ if } v \textrm{ is an } e\textrm{th power} \\ 0 & \textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}
$$
Moreover, $\chi(v) = 1$ if and only if $v$ is an $e$th power. Furthermore, $\sum_{j =0}^{e-1} \chi(v)^j$ is equal to the number of solutions of the equations $x^e = v$.
<!-- end lem-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Fix a generator $g$, and put $\zeta := \exp( \frac{2 \pi}{e} i )$. Define $\chi(g^r) = \zeta^r$. This is well-defined: if $g^r = g^s$, then $Q-1 \mid r-s$, and hence $e \mid r -s$, so $\zeta^r = \zeta^s$. Since $\chi(g) = \zeta$, the character $\chi$ has order $e$.

Let $v := g^r$. Then $v$ is an $e$-th power if and only if $e \mid r$. Indeed, if $v = u^e$ for some $u \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}$, then $u = g^s$ for some $s \in \mathbb{Z}$, so that $g^r = g^{es}$, hence $r \equiv es \pmod{Q-1}$. Since $e \mid Q-1$, it follows that $e \mid r$. The converse is clear. Because $\zeta$ has order $e$, $\chi(v) = \zeta^r = 1$ if and only if $e \mid r$. Thus $\chi(v) = 1$ if and only if $v$ is an $e$th power. 

Now consider the sum $S = 1 + \chi(v) + \chi(v)^2 + \ldots + \chi(v)^{e-1}$. If $v$ is an $e$th power, then $\chi(v) = 1$, so $S = e$. Otherwise, let $z = \chi(v)$. Then $z \neq 1$, but $z^e = 1$, so that $(z-1)S = (z + z^2 + \ldots z^e) - (1 + z + \ldots + z^{e-1}) = z^e -1 = 0$, hence $S = 0$. 

For the final assertion, consider the homomorphism $\mathbb{F}_Q^{\times} \to \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}$, defined by $u \mapsto u^e$, which has kernel of size $e$.

<!-- lem-5: body_md -->
\label{gauss}
Let $\theta : (\mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}, \times) \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ be a nontrivial multiplicative character, and $\Phi : (\mathbb{F}_Q, +) \to \mathbb{C}^{\times}$ be a nontrivial additive character. Put 
$$
G := \sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}} \theta(x) \Phi(x).
$$
Then $\left| G \right| = \sqrt{Q}$.
<!-- end lem-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Indeed, 
\begin{align*}
\left| G \right|^2 &= \sum_{x, y \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}} \theta(x) \overline{\theta(y)} \Phi(x) \overline{\Phi(y)} = \sum_{x,y \neq 0} \theta \left( \frac{x}{y} \right)\Phi(x-y) = \sum_{v \neq 0} \theta(v) \cdot \sum_{y \neq 0} \Phi((v-1)y),
\end{align*}
where, in the third equality we used the substitution $x = vy$. When $v = 1$, the inner sum is $Q-1$, and $-1$ otherwise since $\sum_{u \in \mathbb{F}_Q} \Phi(u) = 0$. Therefore 
$$
\left| G \right|^2 = (Q- 1) - \sum_{v \neq 0,1} \theta(v).
$$
Since $\theta$ is nontrivial, $\sum_{v \neq 0} \theta(v) = 0$, while $\theta(1) = 1$, so that $\sum_{v \neq 0,1} \theta(v) = -1$. Hence $\left| G \right|^2 = Q$, and the desired result follows.

<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{rtlemma}
We have 
$$
\left| R_t - q^{d-1} \right| \leq (e-1)q^{d/2}.
$$
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $\psi : (\mathbb{F}_q, +) \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ be a nontrivial additive character, so that $\psi(x+y) = \psi(x) \psi(y)$ for all $x,y \in \mathbb{F}_q$	 and $\psi(x) \neq 1$ for some $ x\in \mathbb{F}_q$. Recall the additive-character orthogonality identity: 
$$
\frac{1}{q} \sum_{a \in \mathbb{F}_q} \psi(av) = \begin{cases} 1, & \textrm{ if } v =0 \\ 0, & \textrm{ if } v \neq 0. \end{cases}
$$
Applying this identity with $v := \textrm{Tr}(u^e) -t$, we obtain 
\begin{align*}
R_t &= \sum_{u \in \mathbb{F}_Q} \frac{1}{q} \sum_{a \in \mathbb{F}_q} \psi \left( a (\textrm{Tr}(u^e) -t )\right) \\
&= \frac{1}{q} \sum_{a \in \mathbb{F}_q} \psi(-at) \cdot \sum_{u \in \mathbb{F}_Q} \psi(a \textrm{Tr}(u^e)).
\end{align*}
Define a new additive character on $\mathbb{F}_Q$, $\Psi : (\mathbb{F}_Q, +) \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ by $\Psi(v) = \psi(\textrm{Tr}(v))$. Since $\psi$ is nontrivial, and the trace map is surjective, it follows that $\Psi$ is nontrivial too. Moreover, since the trace map is $\mathbb{F}_q$-linear, we have $\psi(a \textrm{Tr}(u^e)) = \Psi(a u^e)$. The term corresponding to $a = 0$ contributes $Q/q = q^{d-1}$ to the sum, hence 
$$
R_t - q^{d-1 } = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{a \in \mathbb{F}_q^{\times}} \psi(-at) S(a), \quad \textrm{ where } S(a) := \sum_{u \in \mathbb{F}_Q} \Psi(au^e).
$$
Now observe that, for $a \neq 0$
\begin{align*}
S(a) &= 1 + \sum_{u \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}} \Psi( a u^e) \\
&= 1+ \sum_{v \neq 0} \sum_{\substack{u \neq 0,\\ u^e = v}} \Psi(av) \\
&= 1 + \sum_{v \neq 0} \# \Set{u \in \mathbb{F}_Q : u^e = v} \Psi(av) \\
&= 1 + \sum_{v \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}} \left( \sum_{j = 0}^{e-1} \chi(v)^j \right) \Psi(av) \\
&= 1 + \sum_{v \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}} \Psi(av) + \sum_{j = 1}^{e-1} \left( \sum_{v \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}} \chi(v)^j \right)	 \cdot \Psi(av)
\end{align*}
where the  second equality follows by noting that if $v$ is not $e$-th power the weight is $0$, and the third equality follows from Lemma \ref{mulchar}.

Since $a \neq 0$ and $\Psi$ is nontrivial, $\sum_{v \in \mathbb{F}_Q} \Psi(av) = 0$, so that $\sum_{v \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}} \Psi(av) = -1$. Hence $S(a) = \sum_{j =1}^{e-1} G_j(a)$, where $G_j(a) = \sum_{v \in \mathbb{F}_Q^{\times}} \chi(v)^j \Psi(av)$. Thus each $G_j(a)$ is a Gauss sum involving the nontrivial multiplicative character $\chi^j$ and the nontrivial additive character $v \mapsto \Psi(av)$. By Lemma \ref{gauss} and the triangle inequality, we have
$$
\left| S(a) \right| \leq \sum_{j = 1}^{e-1} \left| G_j(a) \right| = (e-1) \sqrt{Q}
$$
so that 
$$
\left| R_t - q^{d-1} \right| \leq \frac{1}{q} \sum_{a \in \mathbb{F}_q^{\times}} \left| S(a) \right|\leq \frac{q-1}{q} \cdot (e-1) \sqrt{Q} = (e-1)q^{d/2}.
$$

\label{mainlemma}
Let $k \geq 1$ and $d \geq 2$ and suppose that $q^{d/2 -1} > 3k$. Then, for every $t \in \mathbb{F}_q$, there is a $k$th power $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_Q$ satisfying 
$$
[\mathbb{F}_q(\alpha) : \mathbb{F}_q] = d, \qquad \textrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_Q/\mathbb{F}_q}(\alpha) = t.
$$

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The $e$th powers and $k$th powers in $\mathbb{F}_Q$ coincide, including zero. Consider
$$
R_t = \# \Set{u \in \mathbb{F}_Q : \textrm{Tr}(u^e) = t}.
$$
By Proposition \ref{rtlemma}, $\left| R_t  - q^{d-1} \right| \leq (e-1)q^{d/2}$. We want to discard $u$ for which $u^e$ lies in a proper subfield of $\mathbb{F}_Q$. The union of these subfields has size at most 
$$
\sum_{\substack{r \mid d,\\ r < d}} q^r \leq \sum_{r = 1}^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor} q^r \leq 2 q^{d/2}.
$$
Each element has at most $e$ preimages under the map $u \mapsto u^e$. Therefore, the number of suitable $u$ is at least 
$$
q^{d-1} -(e-1)q^{d/2} - 2 e q^{d/2} = q^{d/2}( q^{d/2-1} - (3e-1)).
$$
By hypothesis $q^{d/2-1} > 3k$, and since $3k - (3e-1) = 3(k-e) + 1 > 0$, the above expression is positive.

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
First suppose that $n \geq 3$ and $q^n > D_k$.  Since $n \geq 3$, 
$$
q^{\frac{n}{2} - 1 } = (q^n)^{\frac{n-2}{2n}} \geq (q^n)^{(1/6)} > (4k)^{4/3} > 3k.
$$
Let $z \in M_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$ be nonscalar, and put $m = n-1$. Let $u \in \mathbb{F}_{q^m}^{\times}$ be a primitive element and set $\beta = u^k$. Then $\beta$ has degree $m$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$. Indeed, if $\beta \in \mathbb{F}_{q^r}$ for a proper divisor $r \mid m$, then $q^{m} -1 \mid k(q^r-1)$. But $r \leq m/2$ and from $q^{n/2 -1} > 3k$ it follows that $q^{m/2}>k$ so that $k(q^r-1) \leq k(q^{m/2} - 1) < q^m-1$, a contradiction.

Let $b = \textrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^m}/\mathbb{F}_q}(\beta)$. Applying Lemma \ref{mainlemma}, with $d = n$ and $t = \textrm{Tr}(z)-b$, we obtain an $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_{q^n}$ of degree $n$, which is a $k$th power with trace $t$. For a finite extension of $E/\mathbb{F}_q$, fix an $\mathbb{F}_q$-basis and let $M_\gamma$ denote the matrix of the multiplication operator $x \mapsto \gamma x$ for $\gamma \in E$. Let $X$ be the conjugacy class of $M_{\alpha}$ and $Y$ be the conjugacy class of $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & M_{\beta} \end{pmatrix}$. These classes have exactly the required properties:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item
  $X$ has irreducible characteristic polynomial of degree $n$.
  \item
  $Y$ has characteristic polynomial $T h(T)$ where $h(T)$ is irreducible of degree $n-1$ and (certainly) $h(0) \neq 0$. 
  \item
  $\textrm{Tr}(X) + \textrm{Tr}(Y) = \textrm{Tr}(z)$. 
  \item
  Every element of $X$ and $Y$ is a $k$th power, since $M_{v^k} = M_{v}^k$ and conjugation preserves a matrix being a $k$th power. 
\end{enumerate}
Now, if $q>2$ we can apply Theorem \ref{x+ythm} directly to conclude that $z = x + y$, where $x \in X$ and $y \in Y$. If $q = 2$, then $2^n > D_k = (4k)^8 \geq 2^{16}$ so that $n \geq 17$. In particular $n \geq 5$ and the hypothesis of Theorem \ref{x+ythm} holds again. Thus in every case $z = x + y$ with $ x \in X$ and $y \in Y$.

Scalar matrices are handled separately. If $z = a I_n$ for $a \in \mathbb{F}_q$, consider the embedding 
$
\mathbb{F}_{q^n} \hookrightarrow M_n(\mathbb{F}_q)
$
by multiplication matrices $\alpha \mapsto M_{\alpha}$. By \cite[Theorem $2.8$]{KVZ}, every nonzero $a$ is a sum of two $k$th powers, when $q^n > (k-1)^4$. This proves the result when $q^n > D_k$ for $n \geq 3$. 

Now we treat the case $n = 2$. Recall that $D_k = (4k)^8$ and assume that $q^n > D_k$. When $n = 2$, this becomes $q^2 > (4k)^8$ so that $q > (4k)^4$, and since $(4k)^4 > (k-1)^4 + 6k$, it follows \cite[Theorem $1.4$]{KVZ} that for every $Z \in M_2(\mathbb{F}_q)$, there exist $A, B \in M_2(\mathbb{F}_q)$ such that $Z = A^k + B^k$. Notice that we treated $n = 2$ separately, because the condition $q^{n/2 - 1} > 3k$ does not hold for $n = 2$, so that we cannot apply Lemma \ref{mainlemma}.

For the remaining case $n = 1$, the hypothesis is $q > D_k = (4k)^8$. Clearly $D_k$ is strictly greater than $(k-1)^4$, and therefore Theorem $2.8$ \cite{KVZ} applies.

Thus, if $q^n > (4k)^8$ then $M_n(\mathbb{F}_q) = \Set{A^k +B^k : A , B \in M_n(\mathbb{F}_q)}$. The passage to the $q^{n^2} > C_k$ result is straightforward. Indeed, if $q^n \leq D_k$ then since $q \geq 2$ it follows that $n \leq \log_2 D_k \leq L_k$ and so $q^{n^2} = (q^n)^n \leq D_k^{L_k} = C_k$. Therefore if $q^{n^2} > C_k$ then $q^n > D_k$, and the result follows.
