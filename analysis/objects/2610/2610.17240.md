\label{IBP_lemma}
	Let $\Gamma$ be a subgroup of $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ commensurable with $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, let $k\geq 2$, and let $h\in \mathcal{M}_{2-k}^S(\Gamma)$. Let $\alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{Q}\cup \{\infty\}$, and suppose that $h$ is analytic at these two cusps. Then, for $0\leq i\leq k-2$,
	$$\int_\alpha^\beta \left( \frac{d^{k-1}}{d\tau^{k-1}} h(\tau) \right) \tau^i d\tau = B_h(\beta,i) - B_h(\alpha,i),$$ where
	$$B_h(\alpha,i) = \begin{cases}0 & \alpha = \infty,0\leq i\leq k-3, \\ (-1)^{k-2} (k-2)! \times a_0(h) & \alpha = \infty, i=k-2 \\
	\alpha^i (k-2)! (-c)^{k-2} \times a_0(h|_{2-k}\gamma) & \alpha\neq \infty, \gamma = { 
		\tiny\arraycolsep=0.3\arraycolsep\ensuremath{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}}}\in \text{SL}_2(\mathbb{R}) \text{ such that }\alpha = \gamma\infty.\end{cases}$$

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
First we claim that, for $\alpha\neq \infty$, with $\gamma = { 
		\tiny\arraycolsep=0.3\arraycolsep\ensuremath{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}}}\in \text{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ such that $\alpha = \gamma\infty$, we have
$$\lim_{\tau\to \alpha} \frac{d^i}{d\tau^i} h(\tau) = \begin{cases}0 &\text{if } 0\leq i\leq k-3 \\ (k-2)! (-c)^{k-2} a_0(h|_{2-k}\gamma) &\text{if } i=k-2
\end{cases}$$
Write $g(\tau) = (h|_{2-k}\gamma)(\tau)$ and $g(\tau) = A + O(e^{-\varepsilon \operatorname{Im}(\tau)})$ as $\tau\to \infty$. Then $$h = g|_{2-k} \gamma^{-1} = (-c\tau + a)^{k-2} g(\gamma^{-1}\tau) = (-c\tau+a)^{k-2}\left(A+O(e^{-\varepsilon \operatorname{Im}(\gamma^{-1}\tau)})\right), \qquad \tau\to \alpha.$$
The term $e^{-\varepsilon \operatorname{Im}(\gamma^{-1}\tau)}$ and all of its derivatives tend to $0$ exponentially as $\tau\to \alpha$. Also note that $\gamma\infty = \alpha \iff -c\alpha+a = 0$, so the only nonvanishing derivative corresponds to $i=k-2$. Moreover,
$$\left.\frac{d^{k-2}}{d\tau^{k-2}} (-c\tau+a)^{k-2}\right|_{\tau=\alpha} = (-c)^{k-2}(k-2)!,$$
and $A = a_0(h|_{2-k}\gamma)$. This proves the claim. \par
Repeated integration by parts gives, for $0\leq i\leq k-2$, 
$$\int \tau^i h^{(k-1)}(\tau) d\tau = \sum_{j=0}^i (-1)^j i(i-1)\cdots (i-j+1) \tau^{i-j} h^{(k-2-j)}(\tau).$$
It remains to show $$C_h(\alpha,i) := \sum_{j=0}^i (-1)^j i(i-1)\cdots (i-j+1) \lim_{\tau\to \alpha}\left(\tau^{i-j} h^{(k-2-j)}(\tau)\right)$$
equals $B_h(\alpha,i)$ as defined in the lemma. We split into the cases $\alpha = \infty$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}$.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item $\alpha = \infty$: then $h^{(\geq 1)}(\tau)$ decays exponentially as $\tau\to \infty$, so only the term with $j=k-2$ can contribute. Thus $C_h(\alpha,i) = (-1)^{k-2}i(i-1)\cdots(i-k+3) \lim_{\tau \to \infty} (\tau^{i-(k-2)} h(\tau))$. This is nonzero only when $i=k-2$; in this case, it is $(-1)^{k-2} (k-2)! a_0(h)$.
\item $\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}$: in this case
$$C_h(\alpha,i) = \sum_{j=0}^i (-1)^j i(i-1)\cdots (i-j+1) \alpha^{i-j}\lim_{\tau\to \alpha}\left( h^{(k-2-j)}(\tau)\right).$$
By the previous observation, the summand is non-zero only when $k-2-j = k-2\iff j=0$, so $C_h(\alpha,i) = \alpha^i \lim_{\tau\to\alpha} h^{(k-2)}(\tau)$, completing the proof.
\end{itemize}

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lemma:weight_two_residue_relation}
	Let $\Gamma \subset \operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ be any subgroup commensurable with $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$. For each cusp $\beta$ of $\Gamma$, choose $\gamma_\beta\in\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ with $\gamma_\beta\infty=\beta$, and let $w_\beta>0$ be the corresponding cusp width, so that $f|_2\gamma_\beta$ is $w_\beta$-periodic. Then, for $f\in\mathcal{M}_2^!(\Gamma)$, one has
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:weight_two_residue_relation}
		\sum_{\beta}w_\beta\,a_0(f|_2\gamma_\beta)=0.
	\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Since $f$ has weight $2$, $2\pi i f(\tau)\,d\tau$ is a meromorphic differential on the compact Riemann surface $X(\Gamma)$. Near the cusp $\beta$, put $q_\beta=e^{2\pi i\tau/w_\beta}$ and write
	$$(f|_2\gamma_\beta)(\tau)=\sum_{m\in \mathbb{Z}}a_{\beta,m}q_\beta^m.$$
	Since $2\pi i\,d\tau=w_\beta\,dq_\beta/q_\beta$, the residue of the differential at $\beta$ is $w_\beta a_{\beta,0}$. The result now follows from the fact that the sum of the residues of a meromorphic differential on a compact Riemann surface is zero.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:gamma16_denom_type}

Assume that $F$ is $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}$-integral at the cusps $\frac12$ and $\frac13$. Then there exists a positive integer $\nu$, depending only on $k$ and $\mathfrak{B}$ and independent of $F$, such that
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item $\nu  \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} h$ is $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}$-integral at all cusps.
\item $\nu  \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} c_f \in \overline{\mathbb{Z}}$.
\end{itemize}
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Note that it suffices to prove the statement for one $\mathfrak{B}$: choosing any other basis will only alter the constant $\nu$. Fix an effective divisor $\mathcal{D} = A_0(\frac12)+B_0(\frac13)$ such that $\mathfrak{B}\subset \mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathcal{D})$. We proceed in steps. We will use $\nu_1,\nu_2,\ldots \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ to denote integers that depend only on $k$ and $\mathfrak{B}$ (i.e., are independent of $F$).\\
	\underline{Step 1: the origin of the LCM $ \left[1,\cdots,M\right] $.}  Let $\gamma \in \{W_2,W_3\}$ and write
	\begin{equation}\label{aux_30}h|_{2-k}\gamma := a_0 + \sum_{n\neq 0} \frac{a_n}{n^{k-1}} q^n.\end{equation}
	Then $$ \sum_{n\neq 0} a_n q^n = F|_k \gamma -\sum_{f\in \mathfrak{B}} c_f f|_k \gamma.$$
	By assumption, $F$ is integral at the two cusps $\frac12$ and $\frac13$. Hence, $a_n \in\overline{\mathbb{Z}}$ for $n<-B_0$ when $\gamma=W_2$ and for $n<-A_0$ when $\gamma=W_3$. Any nonzero coefficient in these ranges has $n\geq -M$. Using $ \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}$ to cancel the denominators in \eqref{aux_30}, we see that $\mathrm{pp}_{>\mathcal{D}}( \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}h)$ has coefficients in $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}$. \\
	\underline{Step 2.} Take $g=c(u+8v)^{k-2}$, where the positive integer $c$ is chosen so that $g$ is integral at all cusps. Its divisor is $(k-2)(\frac13)$, so $g$ has no zeros in $\mathbb{H}$ and both $g$ and $g^{-1}$ have bounded denominators at every cusp. For the fixed effective divisor $\mathcal{D}_1=\mathcal{D}+\operatorname{div} g$, the principal part $\mathrm{pp}_{>\mathcal{D}_1}( \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}gh)$ has coefficients in $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}$, and the poles of $ \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}gh$ are supported on $\{\frac12,\frac13\}$. Lemma \ref{lemma:gamma16_reduction} therefore gives $G$, $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(2)}$-integral at all cusps, and $\kappa\in\mathcal{L}_0(\mathcal{D}_1)$ such that $$ \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}g h = G + \kappa.$$

		&= \sum_{f\in \mathfrak{B}}  \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} c_f f + \sum_{j=1}^r \theta_j D^{k-1}(\kappa_j g^{-1}),
	\end{aligned}$$

After applying $W_3$, the left-hand side has Fourier coefficients in $\nu_2^{-1}\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(2)}$, since $F$ is integral at $\frac12$, $G$ is $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(2)}$-integral there, and $g^{-1}|_{2-k}W_3$ has bounded denominators. By definition of $\mathfrak{B}$, the forms $\{f\mid f\in \mathfrak{B}\}$ and $\{D^{k-1}(\kappa_1g^{-1}),\ldots,D^{k-1}(\kappa_rg^{-1})\}$ are linearly independent. Also, writing $ \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} h = Gg^{-1} + \kappa g^{-1}$, we see that $\nu_4 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} h$ is $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(2)}$-integral at all cusps. 	\underline{Step 4.} Using part (b) of Lemma \ref{lemma:gamma16_reduction}, one can repeat the previous step with $G$ being $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(3)}$-integral. Since $2$ and $3$ are coprime, this implies the conclusion.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lemma_k_2-k_dimension}
		For $k\geq 2$, we have \begin{equation}\label{aux_1}\deg (\lfloor \operatorname{div} \theta_k \rfloor) -  \deg (\lfloor \operatorname{div} \theta_{2-k} \rfloor) =  \dim \mathcal{E}_k(\Gamma) + 2\dim \mathcal{S}_k(\Gamma) - \delta_{k,2}.\end{equation}

The proof consists only of substituting various formulas from Shimura \cite[Chapter~2]{shimura1971introduction}. Let $e_1,\ldots, e_r$ be the orders of the inequivalent elliptic points of $\Gamma\backslash \mathbb{H}^\ast$. First, we have\footnote{Here the even case is found at the end of page 45 of \cite{shimura1971introduction}, and the odd case in the middle of page 47.}
		$$\deg (\lfloor \operatorname{div} \theta_k \rfloor) = \begin{cases}
			\frac{k}{2}(2g-2 +\varepsilon_\infty) + \sum_{j=1}^r \lfloor \frac{k(e_j-1)}{2e_j}\rfloor, & \qquad k\in 2\mathbb{Z},\\
			\frac{k}{2}(2g-2) + \frac{k}{2}\varepsilon^{\text{reg}}_\infty + \frac{k-1}{2}\varepsilon^{\text{irr}}_\infty + \sum_{j=1}^r \lfloor \frac{k(e_j-1)}{2e_j}\rfloor, & \qquad k\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1.
		\end{cases}$$ 
		Now we recall various dimension formulas for modular forms \cite[Theorems~2.24,2.25]{shimura1971introduction}. When $k=2$, we have $\dim \mathcal{E}_k(\Gamma) = \varepsilon_\infty-1$ and $\dim \mathcal{S}_k(\Gamma) = g$. It is then easy to check that \eqref{aux_1} holds. When $k\geq 4$ is even, $$\dim \mathcal{E}_k(\Gamma) = \varepsilon_\infty,\quad \dim \mathcal{S}_k(\Gamma) = (k-1)(g-1) + (\frac{k}{2}-1)\varepsilon_\infty +\sum_{j=1}^r \lfloor \frac{k(e_j-1)}{2e_j}\rfloor.$$
		When $k\geq 3$ is odd, $$\dim \mathcal{E}_k(\Gamma) = \varepsilon^{\text{reg}}_\infty,\quad \dim \mathcal{S}_k(\Gamma) = (k-1)(g-1) + (\frac{k}{2}-1)\varepsilon^{\text{reg}}_\infty + \frac{k-1}{2}\varepsilon^{\text{irr}}_\infty + \sum_{j=1}^r \lfloor \frac{k(e_j-1)}{2e_j}\rfloor.$$
		Note that when $k$ is odd, the $e_j$ must also be odd \cite[Corollary~1.21]{shimura1971introduction}. The following elementary observation about the floor function then completes the proof:
		\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
			\item For $k\in 2\mathbb{Z}$, $e\in \mathbb{N}$, we have $ \lfloor \frac{k(e-1)}{2e}\rfloor +  \lfloor \frac{(2-k)(e-1)}{2e}\rfloor = 0$.
			\item For $k\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1$, $e\in 2\mathbb{N}+1$, we have $ \lfloor \frac{k(e-1)}{2e}\rfloor +  \lfloor \frac{(2-k)(e-1)}{2e}\rfloor = 0$.
		\end{itemize}

If $e_x = 1$, i.e., $x$ is not an elliptic point, there is nothing to prove. Thus, assume $e := e_x >1$. Introduce the coordinate
		$$w=\frac{\tau-x}{\tau-\overline{x}},\qquad
		\tau=\frac{x-\overline{x}w}{1-w}.$$
		If $\gamma=\begin{psmallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{psmallmatrix}$ generates the stabilizer of $x$ modulo scalar matrices, put
		$$j_x:=cx+d,\qquad \zeta_x:=j_x^{-2}.$$
		One easily checks that $w(\gamma\tau)=\zeta_x w(\tau)$. Thus, $\zeta_x$ is a primitive $e$-th root of unity. Consider $$\widetilde{f}(w) := (1-w)^{-k}f\left(\frac{x-\overline{x}w}{1-w}\right).$$
		Since $f$ has weight $k$, $$\widetilde f(\zeta_x w)=j_x^k\widetilde f(w).$$
		If $\widetilde f(w)=\sum_{m\in \mathbb{Z}} a_mw^m$, comparison of coefficients shows that
		$$a_m\neq 0 \implies \zeta_x^m=j_x^k \iff j_x^{2m+k} = 1.$$
		When $k$ is even, the fact that $j_x^2$ is a primitive $e$-th root of unity implies $m+k/2 \equiv 0 \pmod{e}$. When $k$ is odd, $e$ is odd and $j_x$ is a primitive $e$-th root of unity. <!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{lemma_D_L}
		Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a divisor of $X(\Gamma)$ supported on $S$. Then, \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
			\item $D^{k-1}(\mathcal{L}_{2-k}(\mathcal{D})) \subset \mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathcal{D} + (k-1)S^\circ)$.
			\item Assume that all coefficients of $\mathcal{D}$ at points of $S$ are positive. If $f\in \mathcal{M}_{2-k}^S(\Gamma)$ is such that $D^{k-1}(f) \in \mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathcal{D} + (k-1)S^\circ)$, then $f\in \mathcal{L}_{2-k}(\mathcal{D})$. 
		\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-12 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Let $f\in\mathcal{L}_{2-k}(\mathcal{D})$. Bol's identity shows that $D^{k-1}(f)$ is a meromorphic modular form of weight $k$. We first consider the cusp $\infty$. Write $f = \sum_{n\in \mathbb{Z}} a_n e^{2\pi i n\tau/w}$, where $w$ is the width at $\infty$. Then
		\begin{equation}\label{aux_2}D^{k-1}(f) =  \frac{1}{w^{k-1}} \sum_{\substack{n\in \mathbb{Z} \\ n\neq 0}} a_n n^{k-1} e^{2\pi i n\tau/w}.\end{equation}
		Thus $v_\infty(D^{k-1}(f)) \geq v_\infty (f)$, and equality holds if and only if $v_\infty(f)\neq 0$. The situation at other cusps can be translated to that of $\infty$ by using Bol's identity $D^{k-1}(f|_{2-k} \gamma) = (D^{k-1}f)|_k \gamma$; at an irregular cusp, the same calculation is made in the corresponding half-integral local parameter. At an interior point $x$, differentiation of a local Laurent expansion gives
		$$v_x(D^{k-1}f)\geq v_x(f)-\frac{k-1}{e_x}.$$
		Moreover, if $f$ is holomorphic at $x$, then so is $D^{k-1}f$. It follows that $D^{k-1}f$ has no poles outside $S$ and that
		$$\operatorname{div}(D^{k-1}f)\geq-\mathcal{D}-(k-1)S^\circ,$$
		which proves (1).

		For (2), let $x\in S\cap(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})$. If $f$ has a pole at $x$, differentiation of its leading Laurent term gives the equality
		$$v_x(D^{k-1}f)=v_x(f)-\frac{k-1}{e_x}.$$
		The assumed lower bound for $v_x(D^{k-1}f)$ therefore implies $v_x(f)\geq-\mathcal{D}(x)$. If $f$ is holomorphic at $x$, the same conclusion follows from the positivity of $\mathcal{D}(x)$. At a cusp $s\in S$, equation \eqref{aux_2} and Bol's identity similarly show that
		$$v_s(D^{k-1}f)=v_s(f)$$
		whenever $f$ has a pole at $s$; the holomorphic case again follows from the positivity of $\mathcal{D}(s)$. Since $f$ has no poles outside $S$, we conclude that $\operatorname{div}(f)\geq-\mathcal{D}$, proving (2).

For $N\in\mathbb{N}$, let
	$$\mathcal{D}_N:=\sum_{s\in S}Ns.$$
	For sufficiently large $N$, the divisor $\mathcal{D}_N-(k-1)S^\circ$ has support $S$. Lemma~\ref{lemma_D_L} gives
	$$D^{k-1}\left(\mathcal{L}_{2-k}(\mathcal{D}_N-(k-1)S^\circ)\right)\subset\mathcal{L}_k(\mathcal{D}_N).$$ We may therefore consider the quotient

	The kernel of $D^{k-1}$ on $\mathcal{M}_{2-k}^S(\Gamma)$ is zero when $k\geq 3$ and consists of the constants when $k=2$. It follows that

	-\ell_0\left(\mathcal{D}_N+\left\lfloor\operatorname{div}\theta_{2-k}-(k-1)S^\circ\right\rfloor\right)+\delta_{k,2}.$$

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{Dt^n_continuation}
	Let $k\geq 2$ and $h\in \mathcal{L}_{2-k}(\mathcal{D})$ for a divisor $\mathcal{D}$ supported on $S$. Let $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ be a basis of $\mathcal{L}_k(\mathcal{D}+(k-1)S^\circ)$. Then there exist polynomials $p_1,\ldots,p_r\in \mathbb{C}[n]$ such that, for every $n\in \mathbb{Z}$, we have
	\begin{equation}\label{aux_16}\frac{D^{k-1}(t^n h)}{t^n} = p_1(n) f_1 + \cdots + p_r(n) f_r.\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
Let $\mathcal{D}_0 = -\operatorname{div} t$, which is supported on $S$ by assumption. Then $t^n h \in \mathcal{L}_{2-k}(n\mathcal{D}_0 + \mathcal{D})$. Part (1) of Lemma \ref{lemma_D_L} implies that $D^{k-1}(t^n h) \in \mathcal{L}_k(n\mathcal{D}_0 + \mathcal{D}+(k-1)S^\circ)$, and hence $$\frac{D^{k-1}(t^n h)}{t^n} \in \mathcal{L}_k(\mathcal{D}+(k-1)S^\circ),\qquad \forall n\in \mathbb{Z}.$$
	Therefore, there exist functions $p_i\colon \mathbb{Z}\to \mathbb{C}$ such that \eqref{aux_16} holds. It remains to show that these $p_i$ are polynomials in $n$. To do this, we consider the $q$-expansion at the cusp $\infty$. Without loss of generality, we assume that this cusp has width $1$, so $t, h \in \mathbb{C}(\!(q)\!)$. When $t$ and $h$ are interpreted as formal Laurent series, we claim that
	\begin{equation}\label{aux_15}\frac{D^{k-1}(t^n h)}{t^n} \in \mathbb{C}[n](\!(q)\!),\end{equation}
	i.e., the $q$-coefficients of the left-hand side are polynomials in $n$. Indeed, write $a = \frac{Dt}{t} \in \mathbb{C}(\!(q)\!)$. Then
	$$\frac{D(t^nh)}{t^n} = Dh + n \frac{Dt}{t}h = (D+na)h.$$
	Note that $D$ and multiplication by $a$ need not commute. Nonetheless, we have $$\frac{D^{k-1}(t^nh)}{t^n} = (D+na)^{k-1}h.$$
	Thus, each individual coefficient is a polynomial in $n$ of degree at most $k-1$. The left-hand side of \eqref{aux_16}, when expanded as a $q$-series, has coefficients in $\mathbb{C}[n]$, while each $f_i$ has $q$-coefficients in $\mathbb{C}$. Hence, the $p_i$ must be polynomials in $n$.

<!-- proof-22: body_md -->
Write $d:= \dim \frac{\mathcal{M}_k^S(\Gamma)}{D^{k-1}(\mathcal{M}_{2-k}^S(\Gamma))} - \delta_{k,2}$. Consider the $d+1$ forms $g,tg,\ldots,t^d g$. Choose an integral divisor $\mathcal{D}$ supported on $S$, with $\deg \mathcal{D}$ sufficiently large, such that they all lie in $\mathcal{L}_k(\mathcal{D}+(k-1)S^\circ)$. <!-- lemma-13: body_md -->
\label{lemma_valence_bound}
	Assume that $\infty$ is a regular cusp. For a divisor $\mathcal{D}$, put
	$$B_{k,\mathcal{D}}:=\left\lfloor \deg(\operatorname{div}\theta_k) +\deg\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}(\infty)\right\rfloor.$$
	If $f=\sum_n a_nq_\infty^n\in\mathcal{L}_k(\mathcal{D})$ and $a_n=0$ for every $n\leq B_{k,\mathcal{D}}$, then $f=0$.
<!-- end lemma-13 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
Suppose that $0\neq f\in\mathcal{L}_k(\mathcal{D})$. By the definition of $\mathcal{L}_k(\mathcal{D})$, we have $v_x(f)\geq-\mathcal{D}(x)$ for every $x\in X(\Gamma)$. Therefore
	\begin{align*}
	 \deg(\operatorname{div}\theta_k) =\deg(\operatorname{div} f)
	&=v_\infty(f)+\sum_{x\neq\infty}v_x(f)\\
	&\geq v_\infty(f)-\sum_{x\neq\infty}\mathcal{D}(x)=v_\infty(f)-\deg\mathcal{D}+\mathcal{D}(\infty).
	\end{align*}
	It follows that
	$$v_\infty(f)\leq \deg(\operatorname{div}\theta_k) +\deg\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}(\infty).$$
	Since $\infty$ is regular, $v_\infty(f)$ is an integer. Thus every nonzero $f\in\mathcal{L}_k(\mathcal{D})$ satisfies $v_\infty(f)\leq B_{k,\mathcal{D}}$. If all coefficients $a_n$ with $n\leq B_{k,\mathcal{D}}$ vanished, then $v_\infty(f)>B_{k,\mathcal{D}}$, a contradiction, forcing $f=0$.

Since $t$ has a simple pole at $\tau_0$, the right-hand side has a pole of order $l$ at $\tau_0$, and hence $v_{\tau_0}(R_n)\geq 3-l$. Since $R_n$ has weight $-2$ and is holomorphic away from $S$, Lemma~\ref{lemma_elliptic_order_congruence} gives
	$$v_\rho(R_n)\geq\frac13,\qquad v_i(R_n)\geq\frac12,
	\qquad \rho=e^{2\pi i/3},\quad i=\sqrt{-1}.$$
	The valence formula \eqref{degXk} therefore implies
	$$-\frac16=\deg\operatorname{div}(R_n)\geq(3-l)+\frac13+\frac12,
	\qquad\text{hence }l\geq4.$$
	When $l=4$, equality is forced throughout, so
	$$\operatorname{div}(R_n)=\frac{(\rho)}3+\frac{(i)}2-(\tau_0)=\operatorname{div}(R).$$
	Consequently, $R_n/R\in\mathbb{C}(n)$, and rescaling the telescoping identity makes its certificate equal to $R$, independent of $n$. We will see a similar argument again in Lemma \ref{four_cusp_cert} below.

<!-- lemma-16: body_md -->
\label{lemma:constant_term_generating_function}
		Let $t,g\in\mathbb{C}((q))$ be Laurent series convergent in a neighbourhood of $q=0$, and suppose that $v_\infty(t)=1$ and $g$ is holomorphic at $q=0$. Then, for $\tau$ sufficiently close to $i\infty$,
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:constant_term_generating_function}
			\sum_{n\geq0}a_0(t^{-n}g)t(\tau)^n=\frac{t(\tau)g(\tau)}{Dt(\tau)}.
		\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-16 -->
<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
Since $t$ has a simple zero at $q=0$, it is a local coordinate there. Let $q=q(t)$ be its local inverse and put
		$$F(t):=\frac{t\,g(q(t))}{Dt(q(t))}.$$
		Changing variables from $q$ to $t$ in the residue gives
		\begin{align*}
			a_0(t^{-n}g)
			&=\underset{q=0}{\operatorname{Res}}\,t(q)^{-n}g(q)\frac{dq}{q}\\
			&=\underset{t=0}{\operatorname{Res}}\,t^{-n}\frac{g(q(t))}{Dt(q(t))}\,dt
			=[t^n]F(t).
		\end{align*}
		Summing over $n\geq0$ proves \eqref{eq:constant_term_generating_function}.

<!-- proof-31: body_md -->
\underline{Step 1.} We have $a_m(g^\sharp)=0$ if $\chi(m)=1$. By the quadratic Gauss sum $\sum_{r=1}^6\chi(r)e^{2\pi ir/7}=i\sqrt7$, this is equivalent to the identity
$$\begin{aligned}
g^\sharp(\tau)
&+\frac1{i\sqrt7}\sum_{r=1}^6\chi(r)g^\sharp\!\left(\tau+\frac r7\right)-\frac17\sum_{r=0}^6g^\sharp\!\left(\tau+\frac r7\right)=0.
\end{aligned}$$
Indeed, the coefficient of $q^m$ on the left is
$(1+\chi(m)-\mathbf{1}_{7\mid m})a_m(g^\sharp)$.
The left-hand side belongs to $\mathcal{M}_3^!(\Gamma_0(49),\chi)$. Thus, by the valence formula on $\Gamma_0(49)$, it suffices to show that the left-hand side is $O(q^{22})$. \\
\underline{Step 2.} For each $m\geq1$, there is a unique $F_m\in\mathcal{S}_3^!(\Gamma_0,\chi)\cap\mathbb{Z}((q))$ that vanishes at the cusp $0$ and satisfies
$$F_m = q^{-m} + O(q^2).$$
For uniqueness, the difference of two such forms is a cusp form that is $O(q^2)$, hence zero because $\mathcal{S}_3(\Gamma_0,\chi)=\mathbb{C}g$ and $a_1(g)=1$. For existence, consider $t^{-m-1}g\in q^{-m}+q^{-m+1}\mathbb{Z}[[q]]$. Subtract integer multiples of the previously constructed forms $F_{m-1},\ldots,F_1$ to remove the negative powers, then an integer multiple of $g/t=1+O(q)$ to remove the constant term, and finally an integer multiple of $g=q+O(q^2)$ to remove the coefficient of $q$. Every form used vanishes at $0$. This also constructs the initial case $F_1=g^\sharp$. \\
\underline{Step 3.} For $p$ such that $\chi(p) = 1$, we claim that $$T_{p^a}g^\sharp = p^{2a} F_{p^a}.$$
Indeed, since $g^\sharp=q^{-1}+O(q^2)$, equation \eqref{aux_24} implies that their difference is $O(q)$. Moreover,
$$a_1(T_{p^a}g^\sharp - p^{2a} F_{p^a}) = a_{p^a}(g^\sharp) = 0$$
by Step 1, since $\chi(p^a)=1$. Their difference is therefore $O(q^2)$. Both forms vanish at $0$, so their difference is a cusp form and must be zero. \\
\underline{Step 4.} Finally, taking the $m$-th coefficient, with $p\nmid m$, gives
$$a_m(T_{p^a} g^\sharp) = a_{p^a m} (g^\sharp) = p^{2a} a_m(F_{p^a}),$$
so $p^{2a}$ divides $a_{p^a m} (g^\sharp)$, as desired.

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
A set $\mathcal{R} = \{f_1,\ldots,f_r\} \subset \mathcal{M}_0^!(\Gamma)$ is called an $N$-\textit{reduction system} with respect to a subset of cusps $S \subset \Gamma\backslash\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$ if
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item the $q$-expansions of each $f_i$ at all cusps are units in $\mathcal{P}_{(N)}$;
\item each $f_i$ has no pole outside $S$;
\item there exists $M_0 \in \mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}$ such that, whenever $M\geq M_0$ and $s\in S$, there exists $f$ in the monoid generated by $\mathcal{R}$ such that the $s$-component of $\operatorname{div} f$ is $-M$; for every $s'\in S\setminus\{s\}$, the $s'$-component of $\operatorname{div} f$ is at least $-M_0$.
\end{itemize}

<!-- lemma-20: body_md -->
\label{reduction_lemma}
	Let $\mathcal{R} \subset \mathcal{M}_0^!(\Gamma)$ be an $N$-reduction system with respect to the cusps in $S$. Then there exists a nonnegative cuspidal divisor $\mathcal{D}_0$ supported on $S$ such that, for every $X\in\mathcal{M}_0^!(\Gamma)$ whose poles are supported on $S$ and for which $\mathrm{pp}_{>\mathcal{D}_0}(X)$ has coefficients in $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(N)}$, we can write $X = G + \kappa$, where $G$ is $N$-integral at all cusps and $\kappa\in \mathcal{L}_0(\mathcal{D}_0)$.
<!-- end lemma-20 -->
<!-- proof-32: body_md -->
We take $\mathcal{D}_0 = \sum_{s\in S} M_0(s)$, where $M_0$ is as in the definition of a reduction system. Write $$\operatorname{div} X = -\sum_{s\in S} x_s (s) + \cdots,\qquad \delta(X) := \sum_{s\in S} \max(x_s-M_0,0).$$
	We proceed by induction on $\delta(X)$. The base case is $\delta(X) = 0$. In this case, $x_s\leq M_0$ for every $s\in S$, and hence $X\in \mathcal{L}_0(\mathcal{D}_0)$, so we can take $(G,\kappa) = (0,X)$.\par
	We therefore assume that $x_s > M_0$ for some $s$. By the definition of a reduction system, we can find $f$ in the monoid generated by $\mathcal{R}$ such that $$\operatorname{div} f = -x_s(s) + \sum_{s' \in S\setminus\{s\}} c_{s'} (s') + \cdots, \qquad c_{s'}\geq -M_0.$$
	Moreover, the Fourier expansions of $f$ at all cusps have coefficients in $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(N)}$. There exists a unique constant $a$ such that the order of the pole of $X-af$ at the cusp $s$ is less than that of $X$. By comparing the leading coefficients, we see that $a\in \overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(N)}$. Write $\operatorname{div} (X-af) = -\sum_{s\in S} y_s(s) + \cdots$. We just saw that $y_s < x_s$. For $s'\in S\setminus\{s\}$, we also have $\max(x_{s'}-M_0,0) = \max(y_{s'}-M_0,0)$. Hence $\delta(X-af) < \delta(X)$. By the induction hypothesis, we can write $X-af = G' + \kappa'$, where $G'$ is $N$-integral at all cusps and $\kappa'\in \mathcal{L}_0(\mathcal{D}_0)$. Setting $G = G'+af$ and $\kappa = \kappa'$ completes the proof.

<!-- proposition-8: body_md -->
\label{N_reduction_integrality}
	Let $k\geq 3$ and $F\in \mathcal{M}_k^!(\Gamma)$, and write $$(\operatorname{div} F)_\infty = \sum_{s\in S} M_s(s), \qquad M:= \max_s\{M_s\}.$$
	Assume that \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
	\item $F$ is integral at all cusps and all cusps in $S$ are regular;
	\item there exists a quasi-unit $g\in\mathcal{M}_{k-2}^!(\Gamma)$;
	\item for some $N$, the group $\Gamma$ has an $N$-reduction system with respect to $S$.
	\end{enumerate} 
Then, for $c_f$ and $h$ in \eqref{F_basis_expansion}, there exists an integer $\nu$, depending only on $k$, $\Gamma$, and $\mathfrak{B}$ (i.e., independent of $F$), such that \begin{itemize}[leftmargin = *]
		\item $\left( \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} \nu\right)c_f\in \overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(N)}$;
		\item $\left( \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} \nu\right)h$ is $N$-integral at all cusps.
\end{itemize}
<!-- end proposition-8 -->
<!-- proof-33: body_md -->
The proof is largely parallel to the three steps in the proof of Proposition \ref{prop:gamma16_denom_type}. We may enlarge each $M_s$ to a fixed sufficiently large bound, at the cost of changing $\nu$ by a fixed factor. We will use $\nu_1,\nu_2,\ldots \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ to denote integers that depend only on $k$, $\Gamma$, and $\mathfrak{B}$ (i.e., are independent of $F$).\\
\underline{Step 1.} Fix an effective cuspidal divisor $\mathcal{D}$ such that $\mathfrak{B}\subset \mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathcal{D})$. Let $\gamma \in \text{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, set $s=\gamma\infty$, and write \begin{equation}\label{aux_5}h|_{2-k}\gamma := a_0 + \sum_{\substack{n\geq -M_s\\ n\neq 0}} \frac{a_n}{(n/w)^{k-1}} q^{n/w},\end{equation}
where $w$ is the width of the cusp $s$. Then $$ \sum_{\substack{n\geq -M_s\\ n\neq 0}} a_n q^{n/w} = F|_k \gamma -\sum_{f\in \mathfrak{B}} c_f f|_k \gamma.$$
Since $F$ is integral at all cusps, we have $a_n \in \overline{\mathbb{Z}}$ for every negative $n$ for which $q^{n/w}$ does not appear in any $f|_k \gamma$ with $f\in\mathfrak{B}$. Using $ \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}$ to cancel the denominators in \eqref{aux_5} and absorbing the cusp widths into a constant $\nu_1$, we see that $\mathrm{pp}_{>\mathcal{D}}(\nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}h)$ has coefficients in $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}$. \\
\underline{Step 2.} By hypothesis (2), we can choose a quasi-unit $g\in \mathcal{M}_{k-2}^!(\Gamma)$. After enlarging $\nu_1$ by a fixed factor, $\mathrm{pp}_{>\mathcal{D}_1}(\nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}gh)$ has coefficients in $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}$ for some effective cuspidal divisor $\mathcal{D}_1$. Lemma \ref{reduction_lemma} then gives $G$, which is $N$-integral at all cusps, and $\kappa\in \mathcal{L}_0(\mathcal{D}_1)$ such that $$\nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1}g h = G + \kappa.$$
\underline{Step 3.} Multiply both sides of the above equation by $g^{-1}$, apply $D^{k-1}$, and then use \eqref{F_basis_expansion} to obtain
$$\begin{aligned}\nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} F - D^{k-1}(Gg^{-1}) &= \sum_{f\in \mathfrak{B}} \nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} c_f f + D^{k-1}(\kappa g^{-1}) \\ 
&= \sum_{f\in \mathfrak{B}} \nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} c_f f + \sum_{j=1}^r \theta_j D^{k-1}(\kappa_j g^{-1}),
\end{aligned}$$ 
where, in the last line, we chose $\kappa_1,\ldots,\kappa_r$ as a basis of $\mathcal{L}_0(\mathcal{D}_1)$ and wrote $\kappa = \sum_j \theta_j \kappa_j$. Since $F$ and $G$ are $N$-integral at all cusps, the left-hand side has Fourier coefficients in $\nu_2^{-1}\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(N)}$. By definition of $\mathfrak{B}$ and since $k\geq 3$, the forms $\{f\mid f\in \mathfrak{B}\}$ and $\{D^{k-1}(\kappa_1g^{-1}),\ldots,D^{k-1}(\kappa_rg^{-1})\}$ are linearly independent. Lemma \ref{linear_comb_int_lemma} shows that there exists $\nu_3\in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ such that $$\nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} c_f \in \overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(N)}/\nu_3, \qquad \theta_j \in  \overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{(N)}/\nu_3.$$
This proves the first bullet after absorbing $\nu_1\nu_3$ into $\nu$. Also, writing $\nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} h = Gg^{-1} + \kappa g^{-1}$ and recalling that $g$ is a quasi-unit, we see that $\nu_4\nu_1 \left[1,\cdots,M\right] ^{k-1} h$ is $N$-integral at all cusps.

Consider the case $k=2$ and $\Gamma = \Gamma_0(15)$, with the same $t$ as in the preceding example. The curve has genus one, and its unique cusp form is $f = \eta(\tau)\eta(3\tau)\eta(5\tau)\eta(15\tau)$.
Let
$$\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{-E_2(\tau)+21E_2(3\tau)-35E_2(5\tau)+15E_2(15\tau)}{24}
= (1+4t)f \in \mathcal{E}_2(\Gamma),\\
f^\sharp &= t(t+5)f \in \mathcal{S}_2^!(\Gamma).
\end{aligned}$$
Note that $E$ is the unique Eisenstein series vanishing at $0$ and $\infty$. Therefore, for $n\geq 0$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{aux_22}t^n f = A_n E + B_n f + C_n f^\sharp + D(h_n), \qquad h_n\in \mathcal{M}_0^!(\Gamma).\end{equation}
All elements are in the minus space of the Fricke involution, so $h_n$ is uniquely determined\footnote{Constant functions have Fricke eigenvalue $+1$, so the minus space contains no nonzero constants.}. A familiar argument shows that $A_n$ has trivial denominator type. By Proposition \ref{N_reduction_integrality}, $B_n$, $C_n$, and $a_0(h_n)$ have denominator type $ \left[1,\cdots,n\right] $. By integrating \eqref{aux_22} from $0$ to $i\infty$, we obtain
$$(2\pi i)\int_0^{i\infty} t^n f\,d\tau = A_n \log\left(\frac{81}{125}\right) - B_n L(f,1) - C_n L(f^\sharp,1) + 2a_0(h_n).$$
Here we use the Mellin normalization $L(F,1)=2\pi\int_0^\infty F(iy)\,dy$ for $F=f,f^\sharp$; both integrals converge since these forms vanish at $0$ and $\infty$.
Since $t(\frac{i}{\sqrt{15}}) = \frac{-11+5\sqrt{5}}{2}$, at least one of the above numbers is irrational. This conclusion is rather uninteresting, since the first one is already known to be irrational.

Let $\Gamma = \Gamma_0(30)$ and $k=6$. It has genus $3$ and eight cusps
$$\left\{0,\infty,\frac12,\frac13,\frac15,\frac16,\frac1{10},\frac1{15}\right\}.$$
Pick any $g\in \mathcal{M}_6(\Gamma)$. Let
$$t(\tau) = \frac{\eta (\tau )^9 \eta (30 \tau )^9}{\eta (2 \tau )^5 \eta (3 \tau )^3 \eta (5 \tau ) \eta (6 \tau ) \eta (10 \tau )^3 \eta (15 \tau )^5} \sim 6(0)+6(\infty)-2(\frac12)-2(\frac13)-2(\frac15)-2(\frac16)-2(\frac1{10})-2(\frac1{15}).$$
Choose a $\mathbb{Q}$-basis $\mathfrak{B}$ of the subspace of $\mathcal{M}_6^!(\Gamma)$ modulo $D^5(\mathcal{M}_{-4}^!(\Gamma))$ represented by forms vanishing at $0$ and $\infty$. In the expansion
$$t^ng = \sum_{f\in \mathfrak{B}} c_{f,n} f + D^5(h_n), \qquad h_n\in \mathcal{M}_{-4}^!(\Gamma),\quad n\geq 1,$$
there are a total of $\dim \mathcal{E}_6 + 2\dim \mathcal{S}_6 -2 = 58$ forms $f$. The above proposition implies that each $c_{f,n}$ has denominator type $ \left[1,\cdots,2n\right] ^5$. For our chosen $t$, the integral $(2\pi i)^5 \int_0^{i\infty} t^n f\,d\tau$ decays very rapidly, since
$$t(\frac{i}{\sqrt{30}}) = \left(3 \sqrt{\frac{5}{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 \approx 1.65 \times 10^{-5}.$$
The inequality $e^{10} t(\frac{i}{\sqrt{30}}) < 1$ implies that at least one of 
\begin{equation}\label{irrational_list_30}\left\{(2\pi i)^5 \int_0^{i\infty} f(\tau)\,d\tau : f\in \mathfrak{B}\right\}\end{equation}
is irrational. In particular, $\zeta(5)$ belongs to this list by considering an Eisenstein series. Although the above list contains $58$ real numbers, the number of $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent numbers is much smaller because most of the forms in $\mathfrak{B}$ are oldforms. For example, if $F\in \mathcal{S}_6(\Gamma_0(3))$, the elements $\{F(m\tau) : m\mid 10\}$ give the same value of $(2\pi i)^5 \int_0^{i\infty} F(m\tau)\,d\tau$ up to rational multiples. A further reduction of \eqref{irrational_list_30} can be obtained by considering the $W_{30}$-eigenspaces. We do not pursue this point further here.
