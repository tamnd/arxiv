<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{Prop:MEF-implies-compatible}
Let $(X,\alpha, G)$ be a minimal dynamical system, and let $\pi:(X,\alpha, G) \to (X_{eq}, \beta, G)$ be the factor map onto its maximal equicontinuous factor.
Then:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\pi$ is compatible with ${\rm N}(X,\alpha)$;
        \item $\hat{\pi}({\rm N}(X,\alpha,\psi))\subseteq {\rm N}(X_{eq},\beta,\psi)$ for every $\psi\in {\rm Aut}(G)$;
        \item If $\pi$ is almost $1$-$1$, then the map $\hat{\pi}:{\rm N}(X,\alpha)\to {\rm N}(X_{eq},\beta)$ is injective.
    \end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $\phi \in {\rm N}(X,\alpha)$. Observe that $\pi \circ \phi \colon X \to X_{eq}$ is a factor map onto the system defined by the action $\beta'^{g} = \beta^{j(\phi)(g)}$, $g \in G$, and note that the family of maps $\beta(G)= \beta'(G)$ is equicontinuous. By maximality, there is a factor map $\zeta\colon  (X_{eq}, \beta, G) \to (X_{eq}, \beta', G)$ such that $\pi\circ \phi = \zeta \circ \pi$. It follows that $\pi$ is compatible with every $\phi \in {\rm N}(X,\alpha)$, proving (1).

Moreover, for any $\phi\in {\rm N}(X,\alpha,\psi)$ we have $$\hat{\pi}(\phi)(\beta^g\pi(x))=\hat{\pi}(\phi)(\pi(\alpha^g x))=\pi(\phi(\alpha^g x))=\alpha^{\psi(g)}(\pi(\phi(x)))=\alpha^{\psi(g)}\hat{\pi}(\phi)(\pi(x)),$$
which shows that $\hat{\pi}(\phi)\in {\rm N}(Y,\beta,\psi)$, proving (2).

For (3), assume $\pi$ is an almost $1$-$1$ factor map with $\pi(x)\in X_{eq}$ an element with a single pre-image, and suppose $\hat{\pi}(\phi)={\rm Id}_{X_{eq}}$ for some $\phi\in {\rm N}(X,\alpha,\psi)$, $\psi\in {\rm Aut}(G)$.
For every $g\in G$ we have $$\pi(\alpha^g x)=\hat{\pi}(\phi)(\pi(\alpha^g x))=\pi(\phi(\alpha^g x)).$$
Since $\pi(x)$ has a single pre-image, so does $\alpha^g \pi(x)$; consequently, $\alpha^gx=\phi(\alpha^g x)$ for every $g\in G$.
Since, by minimality, the orbit $O_\alpha(x)$ is dense in $X$, this gives $z=\phi(z)$ for every $z\in X$, i.e., $\phi={\rm Id}_X$, which proves (3).

<!-- proposition-7: body_md -->
\label{Lemma: decomposition-odometers}
    Let $(G_n)_{n\geq 0}$ be a decreasing sequence of finite index of ${\mathbb Z}$ such that $G_n=m(n){\mathbb Z}$ with $m(n)\in {\mathbb Z}^+$.
    Then,  as abstract groups,
    \begin{align*}
        {\mathbb Z}_{(G_n)}\cong \prod_{\substack{p\in \mathcal{P}_{(G_n)}\\r(p)=\infty}}{\mathbb Z}_p\times \prod_{\substack{p\in\mathcal{P}_{(G_n)}\\r(p)<\infty}}{\mathbb Z}/p^{r(p)}{\mathbb Z},
    \end{align*}
    where ${\mathbb Z}_p$ denotes the $p$-adic integers.

    Moreover, ${\mathbb Z}_{(G_n)}$ is a torsion-free group if and only if $r(p)=\infty$ for every $p\in \mathcal{P}_{(G_n)}$.
<!-- end proposition-7 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
For every $p\in \mathcal{P}_{(G_n)}$, consider $G_n^p:=p^{r_n(p)}{\mathbb Z}$.
Using the fundamental theorem for finitely generated torsion abelian groups, we have that ${\mathbb Z}/G_n\cong \prod_{p\in \mathcal{P}}{\mathbb Z}/G_n^p$.
Note that for every $n\geq 0$, there exist only finitely many $p\in \mathcal{P}_{(G_n)}$ such that $G_{n}^p\neq {\mathbb Z}$.
Observe that for $p\in \mathcal{P}_{(G_n)}$ we have that 
\begin{align*}
    \varprojlim_n({\mathbb Z}/G_n^p, \varphi_n^p)\cong\begin{cases}
    {\mathbb Z}/p^{r(p)}{\mathbb Z} &\mbox{ if }r(p)<\infty\\
    {\mathbb Z}_p&\mbox{ if }r(p)=\infty.
    \end{cases}
\end{align*}
    
As inverse limits and products are particular cases of limits in the category of abelian groups, they commute (see, for instance, \cite[Proposition 6.2.8]{Le14}) and we obtain the claimed isomorphism.

The second part of the Lemma follows since the components ${\mathbb Z}_p$ are torsion-free and we have only these components exactly when $r(p)=\infty$ for every $p\in \mathcal{P}_{(G_n)}$.

That is, $r_{(G_n)}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^d\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{(G_n)}^i}(p)$.
Similarly, define $r_{(H_n)}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^d\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{(H_n)}^i}(p)$.

Using \cite[Lemma 2.9.4]{RiZa10}, we know that the Pontryagin duals $\hat{X}$ and $\hat{Y}$ of $X$ and $Y$, respectively, are given by 
\begin{align*}
    \hat{X}\cong \bigoplus_{\substack{p\;{\rm prime}\\r_{(G_n)}(p)>0}} ({\mathbb Z}(p^\infty))^{r_{(G_n)}(p)}, \mbox{ and }    \hat{Y}\cong \bigoplus_{\substack{p\;{\rm prime}\\r_{(H_n)}(p)>0}} ({\mathbb Z}(p^\infty))^{r_{(H_n)}(p)}.
\end{align*}
where ${\mathbb Z}(p^\infty)$ is the Prüfer $p$-group and recalling that $\hat{{\mathbb Z}_p}\cong {\mathbb Z}(p^\infty)$ (see, for instance, \cite[Example 2.9.5]{RiZa10}).

As $\psi\in {\rm GL}_d(\mathbb{Q})$, we have that $\psi({\mathbb Z}^d)\leqslant H^*_\infty$ is also a free group in $H^*_\infty$.
Therefore, using \cite[Chapter 12, Page 414]{Fu15}, we deduce that $r_{(H_n)}(p)$ are invariants if we quotient $H^*_\infty$ by any free subgroup of $H^*_\infty$, namely ${\mathbb Z}^d$ or $\psi({\mathbb Z}^d)$.

Thus, we conclude the proof.

For each $n\in {\mathbb N}$, consider the finite index normal subgroups $G_n = 2^n{\mathbb Z}^2\times \{0\}\triangleleft G$ and $H_n = 2^n{\mathbb Z}^2\times \{0\}\triangleleft H$.
The  sequences $(G_n)_{n\geq 0}$ and $(H_n)_{n\geq 0}$ are scales.
It is clear that $G_0\cong H_0$ with the identity map as the group isomorphism, and under this isomorphism $G_n$ is sent to $H_n$.
Moreover, notice that $[G:G_0] = [H:H_0] =4$.
Therefore, Theorem \ref{Theorem: coe-iff-structure} implies that the odometers $(G_{(G_n)},\alpha, G)$ and $(H_{(H_n)},\beta, H)$ are continuously orbit equivalent.


To compare the normalizer groups of $ G_{(G_n)}$ and $H_{(H_n)}$ we first describe ${\rm Aut}(G)$ and ${\rm Aut}(H)$.

 We begin with ${\rm Aut}(G)$: 
Any element in $G$ is of the form $({n},r)$, with ${n}\in {\mathbb Z}^2$ and $r\in {\mathbb Z}/4{\mathbb Z}$.
Moreover, for every ${v}\in {\mathbb Z}^2$ the following relation holds:
\begin{align}\label{eq: 2-G}
({0},1)({v},0) = (A{v}, 0)({0},1).
\end{align}
Notice that $G_0$ is the only  torsion free normal subgroup of minimal index $4$ of $G$.
Therefore, any isomorphism $\psi\in {\rm Aut}(G)$ preserves this subgroup $\psi(G_0) = G_0$.
So, every $\psi\in {\rm Aut}(G)$ is characterized by $r\in ({\mathbb Z}/4{\mathbb Z})^*$, ${w}\in {\mathbb Z}^2$ and $R\in {\rm GL}_2({\mathbb Z})$ such that
\begin{align*}
    \psi({0},1) = ({w},r),\;
    \psi({v},0) = (R{v},0), \mbox{ for any ${v}\in {\mathbb Z}^2$.}
\end{align*}
Actually, the linear map  $R$ has some restrictions.
Applying $\psi$ on \eqref{eq: 2-G} provides  $    ({w},r)(R{v},0) = (RA{v},0)({w},r)$,
and consequently, $({w}+A^rR{v},r) = ({w}+RA{v},r)$.
This implies that $R$ belongs to the subgroup ${\rm N}_{{\rm GL}_2({\mathbb Z})}(\langle A\rangle)$, the normalizer of the group $\langle A\rangle$ in ${\rm GL}_2({\mathbb Z})$.
A standard calculation shows that ${\rm N}_{{\rm GL}_2({\mathbb Z})}(\langle A\rangle) = \langle A, C\rangle$ is isomorphic to the dihedral group,  where $C = \begin{pmatrix}
    -1&0\\0&1
\end{pmatrix}$.
More precisely, it is possible to check that 
\begin{align*}
    \psi({0},1) = ({w},r),\;
    \psi({v},0) = (R{v},0)      \mbox{ for any } {v}\in {\mathbb Z}^2,
\end{align*}
where
\begin{align*}
\begin{cases}
        R\in \langle A\rangle , &\mbox{ if }r=1\\
        R\in {\rm N}_{{\rm GL}_2({\mathbb Z})}(\langle A\rangle)\setminus\langle A\rangle, &\mbox{ if }r=3.
    \end{cases}
\end{align*}

This description implies that ${\rm Aut}(G)$ is a semidirect product of the normal subgroup $N$ and the subgroup $M$, where
\begin{align*}
    N =& \{\psi\in {\rm Aut}(G) : \psi({v},0) = ({v},0), \forall{v}\in {\mathbb Z}^2  \},\\
    M =& \{\psi\in {\rm Aut}(G) : \psi({0},1) = ({0},1) \}\cup \{\psi\in {\rm Aut}(G) : \psi({0},1) = ({0},3) \}. 
\end{align*}
In fact, observe that $\psi \in {\rm Aut}(G)$, with $\psi(0,1)=(w,r)$, $\psi(v,0)=(Rv,0)$, can be decomposed as $\psi=\psi_R\circ \psi_w$, where $\psi_R(0,1)=(0,r)$, $\psi_R(v,0)=(Rv,0)$ , and $\psi_w(0,1)=(R^{-1}w,1)$, $\psi_w(v,0)=(v,0)$.
It is clear that $\psi_R\in M$ and $\psi_w\in N$.
Moreover, we have that $M\cap N=\{{\rm Id}_G\}$.

On the other hand, $M=\langle \psi_A,\psi_C\rangle$, where $\psi_A(0,1)=(0,1),\psi_A(v,0)=(Av,0)$ and $\psi_C(0,1)=(0,3),\psi_C(v,0)=(Cv,0)$.
Moreover, $\psi_A^4=\psi_C^2=(\psi_A\circ\psi_C)^2=id_G$, hence, $M\cong D_4$.

Observe that for every $i\geq 0$ and $\psi\in {\rm Aut}(G)$, $\psi(G_i) = G_i$.
Thus, Lemma \ref{lemma: if-only-Norm} implies that ${\rm GL}(G,\alpha) = {\rm Aut}(G)$, and ${\rm N}( G_{(G_n)},\alpha)$ is a semidirect product between $ G_{(G_n)}$ and ${\rm Aut}(G)$.

Let us describe ${\rm Aut}(H)$: 
Analogous to the case with $G$,  $H_0$ is the only free torsion normal subgroup of minimal index $4$.
Therefore, $\psi(H_0) = H_0$ for every element $\psi\in {\rm Aut}(H)$.
Again, we have that every $\psi\in {\rm Aut}(H)$ is characterized by $R\in {\rm GL}_2({\mathbb Z})$, $r\in ({\mathbb Z}/4{\mathbb Z})^*$ and ${w}\in {\mathbb Z}^2$ such that
\begin{align*}
    \psi({0},1)=({w},r),\psi({v},0)=(R{v},0), \mbox{ for every }{v}\in {\mathbb Z}^2.
\end{align*}
The additional relation given to the presentation of $H$ by the semidirect product reads as
\begin{align*}
    ({0},1)({v},0) = (B{v},0)({0},1),\mbox{ for each }{v}\in {\mathbb Z}^2.
\end{align*}
Since $B$ commutes with any element in ${\rm GL}_2({\mathbb Z})$, there is no  restriction on the choice of $R$.
Thus, ${\rm Aut}(H)$ is a semidirect product of the normal subgroup $L$ and the subgroup $K$ given by
\begin{align*}
L =& \{\psi\in {\rm Aut}(H): \psi({v},0) = ({v},0), \forall {v}\in {\mathbb Z}^2\}\cong {\mathbb Z}^2\times {\mathbb Z}/2{\mathbb Z},\\
    K =& \{\psi\in {\rm Aut}(H): \psi({0},1) = ({0},1)\}\cong {\rm GL}_2 ({\mathbb Z}). 
\end{align*}
This implies that ${\rm Aut}(G)$ and ${\rm Aut}(H)$ are not commensurable since, for example, ${\rm Aut}(G)$ is amenable, whereas ${\rm Aut}(H)$ is not.
Moreover, any $\psi\in {\rm Aut}(H)$ satisfies  $\psi(H_i) = H_i$ for each $i\geq 0$.
Consequently, ${\rm GL}(H,\beta) = {\rm Aut}(H)$ and by Proposition \ref{coro:splitsequence}, ${\rm N}(H_{(H_i)},\beta)$ is a semidirect product of $H_{(H_i)}$ by ${\rm Aut}(H)$. 
Furthermore, we have that ${\rm N}( G_{(G_n)},\alpha)$ and ${\rm N}(H_{(H_i)},\beta)$ are not commensurable for the same reason: one is amenable, and the other is not.

\label{example: Heisenberg}
    Let ${\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z})$ be the discrete Heisenberg group.
    That is, the group defined on $\mathbb{Z}^3$ with group multiplication given by
    \begin{align*}
        (x,y,z)\cdot(x',y',z') = (x+x',y+y',z+z'+xy').
    \end{align*}
    The center of this group is ${\rm Z}({\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z})) = \{(0,0,z): z\in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
    An element in ${\rm Aut}({\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z}))$ is completely characterized by the information given in  $\{(1,0,0), (0,1,0)\}$, as this set generates ${\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z})$. Recall that these isomorphism preserves the center  ${\rm Z}({\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z}))$.
    The relation between these elements is given by 
    \begin{align}\label{eq: natural-Heisenberge-eq}
        (1,0,0)\cdot(0,1,0) = (0,1,0)\cdot(1,0,0)\cdot(0,0,1).
    \end{align}
    Let $\varphi\in {\rm Aut}({\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z}))$ be such that $\varphi(1,0,0)=(a,b,s)$, $\varphi(0,1,0) = (c,d,t)$. 
    Applying $\varphi$ to \eqref{eq: natural-Heisenberge-eq} provides 
    \begin{align*}
        (a,b,s)\cdot(c,d,t) = (c,d,t)\cdot(a,b,s)\cdot(0,0,\varepsilon), \;\varepsilon\in \{-1,1\}.
    \end{align*}
    The previous relation implies that $ad-bc = \pm 1$.
    On the other hand any element $(x,y,z)\in {\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z})$ is written as 
    \begin{align*}
        (x,y,z) = (0,1,0)^y\cdot(1,0,0)^x\cdot(0,0,1)^z,
    \end{align*}
    where $(a,b,c)^d$ denotes the product of $(a,b,c)$ with itself $d$ times in the group.
    This gives that
    \begin{align}\label{eq: Heisenberg-eq-2}
        \nonumber\varphi(x,y,z) &= (c,d,t)^y \cdot (a,b,s)^x \cdot(0,0,\varepsilon)^z\\
        &= (yc+ax, yd+bx, yt+xs+\frac{y(y-1)}{2}cd+\frac{x(x-1)}{2}ab+\varepsilon z).
    \end{align}
   Recall from \cite{LiSaUg14} that
   for each $n\in \mathbb{N}$,  the subgroup  $G_n\leqslant {\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z})$ defined by 
    \begin{align*}
        G_n = \{(s_n x, s_n y, s_n z)\in {\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z})\mid x,y,z\in \mathbb{Z}\}, \mbox{ for some fix } s_n\in \mathbb{N},
    \end{align*}
    is normal and has finite index.
    If $(s_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is a sequence of odd numbers such that $s_n\mid s_{n+1}$ (for instance, $s_n = 3^n$), then $(G_n)_{n\geq 0}$ is a scale.
    Moreover, \eqref{eq: Heisenberg-eq-2} implies that $\varphi(G_n)\subseteq G_n$ and consequently, $\varphi(G_n)=G_n$ as they have the same finite index.
    Using that ${\rm Aut}({\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z}))\cong \mathbb{Z}^2\rtimes {\rm GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ (see, for instance, \cite{Os15}) and Corollary \ref{coro: splitsequence of Xf- Aut}, we obtain that ${\rm N}(X_f,\sigma_{X_f})\cong {\mathbb H}_3({\mathbb Z})\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}^2\rtimes {\rm GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}))$.
