<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:nondensity}
Let $Q=(0,1)^2$. There exist $u\in C(\overline Q;\mathbb R^2)\cap\mathrm{BD}(Q)$,
functions $H_1,H_2\in C(\overline Q)$, and singular nonatomic probability
measures $\nu_1,\nu_2$ on $(0,1)$ such that, with
$\mu=\nu_1\otimes\nu_2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-strain}
 Eu=\begin{pmatrix}H_1&0\\0&H_2\end{pmatrix}\mathcal L^2
   +\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\mu,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-gap}
 \inf_{v\in\mathrm{BV}(Q;\mathbb R^2)}|E(u-v)|(Q)\ge\sqrt2.
\end{equation}
Consequently, BV is not dense in BD in the norm topology of the latter. More generally,
for every $n\ge2$ and every nonempty open $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^n$, there is
$U\in C_c(\Omega;\mathbb R^n)\cap\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)$ whose distance
from $\mathrm{BV}(\Omega;\mathbb R^n)$ in the BD norm is positive.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:riesz}
There exist nonatomic probability measures $\nu_1,\nu_2$ on $\mathbb T$,
both singular with respect to $\mathcal L^1$, such that
\[
 F_k\mathcal L^2\overset{*}{\rightharpoondown}\nu_1\otimes\nu_2=:\mu
 \quad\text{on }\mathbb T^2.
\]

Represent points of $\mathbb T$ by numbers in $[0,1)$; since $\nu_1(\{0\})=0=\nu_2(\{0\})$,
                                                          
we may regard
$\nu_1$, $\nu_2$ as probability measures on $(0,1)$. There are Borel sets
$A,B\subset(0,1)$ such that
\[
 \mathcal L^1(A)=\mathcal L^1(B)=0,
 \quad \nu_1(A)=\nu_2(B)=\mu(A\times B)=1.
\]

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For a finite measure $\nu$ on $\mathbb T$, the Fourier coefficients are
\[
 \widehat\nu(q)=\int_{\mathbb T}e^{-2\pi iqt}\,d\nu(t),
 \quad q\in\mathbb Z.
\]
                                                                                       
                          
                                              
                                                                  
              

\emph{Step 1: Uniqueness of signed frequency sums.}
                                                       
                                             
                                                                       
                                                           
                

We first prove a uniqueness result: suppose two finitely supported sequences
$(\varepsilon_j)$ and $(\varepsilon'_j)$ with entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$
satisfy $\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_jN_j=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon'_jN_j$.
We claim that $(\varepsilon_j)$ and $(\varepsilon'_j)$ necessarily coincide; suppose instead that they
are distinct, and let
$p=\max\{j:\varepsilon_j\ne\varepsilon'_j\}$.
Subtracting the sums cancels all terms with $j>p$. Taking absolute
values gives
\[
 N_p\le |\varepsilon_p-\varepsilon'_p|N_p
 =\left|\sum_{j<p}(\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon'_j)N_j\right|
 \le2S_{p-1}<N_p\quad\textrm{by }\eqref{eq:dissociation},
\]
a contradiction.
This proves the claim.
                                                        
In particular, zero has only the all-zero representation.\\

                                                                                    
                                                                     
                                   
   
                                                              
                                                                      
   
                                                                          
                                                                        
                                                                     
        
                                         
 
                       
                                                                                     

\emph{Step 2: Construction and Fourier coefficients of $\nu_1$, $\nu_2$.}\\
Let $(M_j)_{j\ge1}$ denote either $(N_{2j-1})_{j\ge1}$ or
$(N_{2j})_{j\ge1}$, and set $R_0=1$ and
\[
 R_r(t)=\prod_{j=1}^r\bigl(1+a\cos(2\pi M_jt)\bigr),\quad r\in\mathbb{N}.
\]
We have
\[
 1+a\cos(2\pi M_jt)
 =1+\frac a2 e^{2\pi iM_jt}+\frac a2 e^{-2\pi iM_jt}.
\]
When we expand the product, we choose one of the three terms from each factor. For
$j=1,\ldots,r$, the number $\varepsilon_j\in\{-1,0,1\}$ records this
choice in the $j$-th factor: $\varepsilon_j=0$ selects $1$,
$\varepsilon_j=1$ selects $(a/2)e^{2\pi iM_jt}$, and
$\varepsilon_j=-1$ selects $(a/2)e^{-2\pi iM_jt}$.
Thus
                                                                                           
the product expands as
\[
 R_r(t)=\sum_{\varepsilon_1=-1}^{1}\ldots
         \sum_{\varepsilon_r=-1}^{1}
 \left(\frac a2\right)^{\sum_{j=1}^r|\varepsilon_j|}
 e^{2\pi it\sum_{j=1}^r\varepsilon_jM_j}.
\]
                                                                   
                                               
                                                         
For $q\in\mathbb Z$, we get the Fourier coefficient
\begin{align*}
 \widehat{R_r\mathcal L^1}(q)
 &=\int_0^1 R_r(t)e^{-2\pi iqt}\,dt\\
 &=\sum_{\varepsilon_1=-1}^{1}\ldots
 \sum_{\varepsilon_r=-1}^{1}
   \left(\frac a2\right)^{\sum_{j=1}^r|\varepsilon_j|}
   \int_0^1 e^{2\pi it(\sum_{j=1}^r\varepsilon_jM_j-q)}\,dt.
\end{align*}
                           
   
                               
                
                
                
              
                          
   
Only terms satisfying $\sum_{j=1}^r\varepsilon_jM_j=q$
contribute to this Fourier coefficient, and
by Step 1, there is at most one finitely supported sequence $(\varepsilon_j)$
satisfying the equality.                                 
Thus $\widehat{R_r\mathcal L^1}(0)=1$
and for every fixed $q$, as we increase $r$, $\widehat{R_r\mathcal L^1}(q)$ will eventually remain the same.
                                                 
Since $R_r\ge0$, the measures $R_r\mathcal L^1$
are probability measures. Weak compactness on $\mathbb T$, together
with the fact that Fourier coefficients determine a measure, gives
convergence of the full sequence to a probability measure $\nu$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nu Fourier}
 \widehat\nu(q)=
 \begin{cases}
  (a/2)^{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\varepsilon_j|},
   &q=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_jM_j
     \text{ with finitely supported }\varepsilon_j\in\{-1,0,1\},\\
  0,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
These coefficients are real. Write $\nu_1=\nu$ and $\nu_2=\nu$ for the cases of
$(N_{2j-1})_{j\ge1}$ and $(N_{2j})_{j\ge1}$, respectively.\\

\emph{Step 3: Convergence to the product measure.}\\
Write $R_r^{(1)}$ and $R_r^{(2)}$ for the products of Step 2 associated
with the odd and even subsequences. Then
\[
 F_k(x_1,x_2)=R_{\lceil k/2\rceil}^{(1)}(x_1)
          R_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}^{(2)}(x_2).
\]
For $\varphi,\psi\in C(\mathbb T)$, Fubini's theorem therefore gives
\begin{align*}
 \int_{\mathbb T^2}\varphi(x_1)\psi(x_2)F_k(x_1,x_2)\,d\mathcal L^1(x_1)\,d\mathcal L^1(x_2)
 &=\left(\int_{\mathbb T}\varphi R_{\lceil k/2\rceil}^{(1)}\,d\mathcal L^1\right)
   \left(\int_{\mathbb T}\psi R_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}^{(2)}\,d\mathcal L^1\right)\\
 &\to
   \left(\int_{\mathbb T}\varphi\,d\nu_1\right)
   \left(\int_{\mathbb T}\psi\,d\nu_2\right)\quad\textrm{as }k\to\infty.
\end{align*}
Finite sums of these product functions are uniformly dense in
$C(\mathbb T^2)$. Since all the measures have mass one, uniform approximation
extends the convergence to every continuous test function. Thus
$F_k\mathcal L^2\overset{*}{\rightharpoondown}\nu_1\otimes\nu_2$.\\

\emph{Step 4: Singularity of $\nu_1$, $\nu_2$.}\\
Fix $\nu$ to be either $\nu_1$ or $\nu_2$, and consider the frequency sequence $(M_j)$
that is $(N_{2j-1})_{j\ge1}$ or $(N_{2j})_{j\ge1}$, respectively.
By equation \eqref{eq:nu Fourier}, and noting that e.g. $M_j$ is represented uniquely
(by Step 1) with the choice
$\varepsilon_j=1$ and other coefficients zero, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:widehat nu}
 \widehat\nu(M_j)=a/2,\qquad\widehat\nu(-M_j)=a/2,\qquad
 \widehat\nu(M_j\pm M_\ell)=a^2/4\quad(j\ne\ell).
\end{equation}
We also have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2Mj}
\widehat\nu(2M_j)=0;
\end{equation}
indeed, note that $M_j$ is $N_p$ for some $p\in\mathbb{N}$, and then for any choice of
$(\varepsilon_j)$ we have
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\varepsilon_j N_j\le N_p+S_{p-1}<2N_p\quad\textrm{by }\eqref{eq:dissociation},
\]
                                                 
while if $\varepsilon_{\ell}\neq 0$ for a given $\ell>p$,  we get
\[
\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\varepsilon_j N_j\Big|
\ge N_\ell-S_{\ell-1}>3S_{\ell-1}\ge3N_p.
\]
Thus $2M_j$ has no representation as a finite sum $\sum_{j}\varepsilon_j N_j$.

Consider the continuous functions
\[
 X_j(t)=\cos(2\pi M_jt),\quad
 Z_r(t)=\frac1r\sum_{j=1}^rX_j(t).
\]
On the second and third lines below, we use the identities $\cos^2 s=\tfrac 12 (1+\cos(2s))$
and $\cos s \cos t=\tfrac 12(\cos(s-t)+\cos(s+t))$.
Thus
\begin{align*}
 \int_{\mathbb T} X_j\,d\nu&=\int_{\mathbb T}\tfrac 12 (e^{2\pi iM_jt}+e^{-2\pi iM_jt}) \,d\nu(t)=
 \frac 12 (\widehat\nu(-M_j)+\widehat\nu(M_j))=a/2\quad \textrm{by }\eqref{eq:widehat nu};\\
 \int_{\mathbb T} X_j^2\,d\nu&
 =\tfrac12\int_{\mathbb T}(1+\cos(2\pi (2M_j)t)) \,d\nu(t)
 =\tfrac12\bigl(1+\tfrac 12 (\widehat\nu(-2M_j)+\widehat\nu(2M_j))
 \bigr)=\tfrac12\quad\textrm{by }\eqref{eq:2Mj};\\
 \int_{\mathbb T} X_jX_\ell\,d\nu
 &=
 \tfrac12\int_{\mathbb T}(\cos(2\pi (M_j-M_{\ell})t)+\cos(2\pi (M_j+M_{\ell})t))\,d\nu(t)\\
 &=\tfrac12\bigl(\widehat\nu(M_j-M_\ell)
                 +\widehat\nu(M_j+M_\ell)\bigr)=a^2/4
 \quad(j\ne\ell),\quad\textrm{by }\eqref{eq:widehat nu}.
\end{align*}
Hence 
\[
\int_{\mathbb T}(X_j-a/2)(X_{\ell}-a/2)\,d\nu=0\quad\textrm{for }j\ne\ell.
\]
                                                                            
                                            
It follows that
\[
 \int_{\mathbb T}|Z_r-a/2|^2\,d\nu
 =\frac{1}{r^2}\sum_{j=1}^r\int_{\mathbb T}(X_j-a/2)^2\,d\mathcal \nu
 =\frac{1/2-a^2/4}{r}.
\]
                                                                      
                                         
Moreover, since cosine functions with distinct frequencies are orthogonal with respect to the usual
Lebesgue measure, we have
\[
 \int_{\mathbb T}|Z_r|^2\,d\mathcal L^1
 =\frac{1}{r^2}\sum_{j=1}^r \int_{\mathbb T}X_j^2\,d\mathcal L^1=\frac1{2r}.
\]
                                                              
For every $\varepsilon>0$, Chebyshev's inequality at the dyadic indices
$r=2^s$ gives
                    
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\nu\bigl(|Z_{2^s}-a/2|>\varepsilon\bigr)
 &\le\frac{1/2-a^2/4}{\varepsilon^2}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}2^{-s}<\infty,\\
 \sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\mathcal L^1\bigl(|Z_{2^s}|>\varepsilon\bigr)
 &\le\frac1{2\varepsilon^2}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}2^{-s}<\infty.
\end{align*}
Apply the first Borel--Cantelli lemma for each threshold
$\varepsilon=1,1/2,1/3,\ldots$. By the lemma, for both measures we have that outside a null set,
every such threshold is exceeded only finitely often, and so
\[
 Z_{2^s}\to a/2\quad\nu\text{-a.e.},
 \quad Z_{2^s}\to 0\quad\mathcal L^1\text{-a.e.}
\]
The set
\[
 E_\nu=\{t\in\mathbb T:\lim_{s\to\infty}Z_{2^s}(t)=a/2\}
\]
is Borel because the $Z_{2^s}$ are continuous. It satisfies
$\nu(E_\nu)=1$ and $\mathcal L^1(E_\nu)=0$, since $a/2\ne0$. This proves
$\nu\perp\mathcal L^1$.
                                                        
Recalling that $\nu$ is either $\nu_1$ or $\nu_2$, we get the two $\mathcal L^1$-null sets
$E_1,E_2$ which carry $\nu_1,\nu_2$, respectively.\\

\emph{Step 5: Absence of atoms and null carriers in $(0,1)$.}\\
The two measures cannot have nonzero Fourier coefficients at the same
nonzero frequency $q\in\mathbb{Z}$; otherwise, that frequency would have two
finite sum representations $\sum_{j}\varepsilon_j N_j$,
one using odd $j$ and the other using even $j$, contradicting Step 1. The Fourier coefficients of the
convolution $\nu_1*\nu_2$ are products $\widehat{\nu_1}(q)\widehat{\nu_2}(q)$, so they equal
one at $q=0$ and vanish for other $q\in\mathbb{Z}$. These are exactly the coefficients
of the Lebesgue measure; hence $\nu_1*\nu_2=\mathcal L^1$.

Suppose we had $\nu_1(\{t_0\})=c>0$ for some $t_0\in\mathbb T$. Since
$\nu_2(E_2)=1$, we would have
\[
\nu_1*\nu_2(t_0+E_2)\ge c.
\]
                                           
                                     
This set is Lebesgue-null, whereas the convolution equals the Lebesgue measure, giving a contradiction.
The same argument applies to $\nu_2$, so neither measure has atoms.

Represent torus points by numbers in $[0,1)$ and remove $0$ from the
Borel null sets $E_1,E_2$ to obtain $A,B\subset(0,1)$. Since neither
measure charges $0$, removing it loses no mass. Thus
$\nu_1(A)=\nu_2(B)=1$, and consequently $\mu(A\times B)=1$,
as required.

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
\emph{Step 1: Preliminary definitions and estimates.}\\
                            
Let $i_k=1$ for odd $k$ and $i_k=2$ for even $k$.
Set
\[
 g_k=F_k-F_{k-1}
     =a\cos(2\pi N_kx_{i_k})F_{k-1}.
                         
\]
Here $x_{i_k}$ is the coordinate of the new oscillation, and $x_{3-i_k}$ is the
other coordinate. For a trigonometric polynomial $p$ on
$\mathbb T^2$, write
\[
 \widehat p(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb T^2}p(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi\cdot x}\,d\mathcal L^2(x),
 \quad
 \|p\|_{\mathcal A}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb Z^2}|\widehat p(\xi)|.
\]
The finite Fourier expansion and the convolution formula for a product give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A norm}
 \|p\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb T^2)}\le\|p\|_{\mathcal A},
 \quad
 \|pq\|_{\mathcal A}\le\|p\|_{\mathcal A}\|q\|_{\mathcal A}.
\end{equation}
For each factor $1+a\cos(2\pi N_jx_{i_j})$, the sum of the absolute Fourier
coefficients is $1+a$; for $a\cos(2\pi N_kx_{i_k})$, it is $a$. Hence,
using the latter inequality of \eqref{eq:A norm}, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wiener}
 \|g_k\|_{\mathcal A}\le a(1+a)^{k-1}.
\end{equation}

Let $e_1,e_2$ be the coordinate unit vectors.
By the Fourier expansion of $g_k$,
every frequency $\xi$ with $\widehat g_k(\xi)\ne0$ has the form
\[
 \xi=\sigma N_ke_{i_k}+\sum_{j<k}\varepsilon_jN_je_{i_j},
 \quad \sigma\in\{-1,1\},\quad \varepsilon_j\in\{-1,0,1\}.
\]
The new frequency therefore dominates in the $i_k$ coordinate, while
the other coordinate contains only earlier frequencies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frequency bounds}
 |\xi_{i_k}|\ge N_k-S_{k-1}>0,
 \quad
 |\xi_{3-i_k}|\le S_{k-1}.
\end{equation}
                                                                  
                                                            

                                                                   
Define a periodic trigonometric polynomial $q_k$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:primitive}
 \widehat q_k(\xi)=
 \begin{cases}
  \widehat g_k(\xi)/(2\pi i\xi_{i_k}),&\xi_{i_k}\ne0,\\
  0,&\xi_{i_k}=0.
 \end{cases}
 \quad \partial_{i_k}q_k=g_k.
\end{equation}
Differentiation multiplies the coefficient at $\xi$ by
$2\pi i\xi_{i_k}$, while $\widehat g_k(\xi)=0$ when $\xi_{i_k}=0$.
Since $g_k$ is real, its coefficients are conjugate symmetric. The
definition therefore gives $\widehat q_k(-\xi)=\overline{\widehat q_k(\xi)}$,
so $q_k$ is real.

                                                                     
Set
\[
 w_k=
 \begin{cases}
  (0,2q_k),&k\text{ odd},\\
  (2q_k,0),&k\text{ even},
 \end{cases}
 \quad h_k=2\partial_{3-i_k}q_k.
\]
For a smooth $w\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb T^2;\mathbb R^2)$,
let $e(w)=(\nabla w+\nabla w^T)/2$ denote the
density of $Ew=e(w)\mathcal L^2$. For odd $k$, the identity
$\partial_1q_k=g_k$ gives
\[
 e(w_k)_{1}^2=\tfrac12\partial_1(2q_k)=g_k,
 \quad e(w_k)_{2}^2=\partial_2(2q_k)=h_k,
 \quad e(w_k)_{1}^1=0.
\]
For even $k$, the coordinates are interchanged. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-strain}
 Ew_k=
 \begin{cases}
  \begin{pmatrix}0&g_k\\g_k&h_k\end{pmatrix}\mathcal L^2,
       &k\text{ odd},\\[2mm]
  \begin{pmatrix}h_k&g_k\\g_k&0\end{pmatrix}\mathcal L^2,
       &k\text{ even}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:primitive}, \eqref{eq:frequency bounds}, and
\eqref{eq:wiener}, we get
\begin{align*}
 \|q_k\|_{\mathcal A}
 &\le\frac{\|g_k\|_{\mathcal A}}{2\pi(N_k-S_{k-1})}
 \le\frac{a(1+a)^{k-1}}{2\pi(N_k-S_{k-1})},\\
 \|\partial_{3-i_k}q_k\|_{\mathcal A}
 &=\sum_{\xi:\,\widehat g_k(\xi)\ne0}
       \frac{|\xi_{3-i_k}|}{|\xi_{i_k}|}\,|\widehat g_k(\xi)|
 \le\frac{a(1+a)^{k-1} S_{k-1}}{N_k-S_{k-1}}.
\end{align*}
Using the first inequality of \eqref{eq:A norm},
                                                     
we obtain
\begin{align}
 \|w_k\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb T^2)}
 &\le\frac{a(1+a)^{k-1}}{\pi(N_k-S_{k-1})},\label{eq:w-bound}\\
 \|h_k\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb T^2)}
 &\le\frac{2a(1+a)^{k-1}S_{k-1}}{N_k-S_{k-1}}.\label{eq:h-bound}
\end{align}
By the growth condition \eqref{eq:growth},
\[
 \|w_k\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb T^2)}+\|h_k\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb T^2)} \le 2^{-k}.
\]
                                                                  
                              

\emph{Step 2: The symmetric gradient of the limit.}\\
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:u-series}
 u(x_1,x_2)=(x_2,x_1)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_k(x_1,x_2),
 \quad
 H_1=\sum_{k\ {\rm even}}h_k,\quad
 H_2=\sum_{k\ {\rm odd}}h_k.
\end{equation}
All three series converge uniformly by Step 2. In particular,
$u\in C(\overline Q;\mathbb R^2)$, hence $u\in L^1(Q;\mathbb R^2)$, and $H_1,H_2$
are continuous periodic functions.

Consider the finite sums
\[
 u^{(m)}=(x_2,x_1)+\sum_{k=1}^m w_k,\quad
 H_1^{(m)}=\sum_{\substack{k\le m\\k\ {\rm even}}}h_k,\quad
 H_2^{(m)}=\sum_{\substack{k\le m\\k\ {\rm odd}}}h_k.
\]
The affine function $(x_2,x_1)$ has symmetric gradient
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mathcal L^2$.
Since $F_0=1$, the off-diagonal terms telescope:
\[
 1+\sum_{k=1}^m g_k
 =1+\sum_{k=1}^m(F_k-F_{k-1})=F_m.
\]
Together with \eqref{eq:block-strain}, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-strain}
 Eu^{(m)}
 =\begin{pmatrix}H_1^{(m)}&F_m\\F_m&H_2^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}\mathcal L^2.
\end{equation}

To pass to the distributional limit, fix $\varphi\in C_c^\infty(Q)$.
For the diagonal entries, integration by parts at the finite stages and
uniform convergence give
\begin{align*}
                                 
 -\int_Q u_1\partial_1\varphi\,dx
   &=-2\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_Q\sum_{\substack{k\le m\\k\ {\rm even}}} q_k\partial_1\varphi\,dx
   =\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_QH_1^{(m)}\varphi\,dx
   =\int_QH_1\varphi\,dx,\\
 -\int_Q u_2\partial_2\varphi \,dx
                                    
   &=-2\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_Q\sum_{\substack{k\le m\\k\ {\rm odd}}} q_k\partial_2\varphi\,dx
   =\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_QH_2^{(m)}\varphi\,dx
   =\int_QH_2\varphi\,dx.
\end{align*}
For the off-diagonal entry, we similarly get
\begin{align*}
                                                                        
 -\frac12\int_Q
       (u_2\partial_1\varphi+u_1\partial_2\varphi)\,dx
       &= -\frac12\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_Q\Big(
       \big(x_1+2\sum_{\substack{k\le m\\k\ {\rm odd}}} q_k\big)\partial_1\varphi+\big(x_2+2\sum_{\substack{k\le m\\k\ {\rm even}}} q_k\big)\partial_2\varphi\Big)\,dx\\
 &=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_QF_m\varphi\,dx\\
   &=\int_Q\varphi\,d\mu.
\end{align*}
The last equality follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:riesz} after extending
$\varphi$ by zero to $\mathbb T^2$. The extension is smooth because
$\varphi$ vanishes near the boundary of $Q$.

These identities give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Eu formula}
 Eu=\begin{pmatrix}H_1&0\\0&H_2\end{pmatrix}\mathcal L^2
       +\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\mu,
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{eq:main-strain}. The total variation is finite, since
\[
 |Eu|(Q)\le\|H_1\|_{L^1(Q)}+\|H_2\|_{L^1(Q)}+\sqrt2\,\mu(Q)<\infty.
\]
Thus $u\in\mathrm{BD}(Q)$. Note that this argument identifies the symmetric sum
$\partial_1u_2+\partial_2u_1$ as a measure without requiring either
individual cross derivative to be a measure.\\

\emph{Step 3: Separation from BV functions.}\\
Let $A,B\subset (0,1)$ be the sets of Lebesgue measure zero from Lemma~\ref{lem:riesz}, set
$F=A\times B$, and write
\[
 P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad |P|=\sqrt2.
\]
The notation $\mu\!\mathbin{\vrule height 1.6ex depth 0pt width
		0.13ex\vrule height 0.13ex depth 0pt width 1.1ex}\! F$
denotes the restriction of $\mu$ to $F$, that is,
\[
\mu \!\mathbin{\vrule height 1.6ex depth 0pt width
		0.13ex\vrule height 0.13ex depth 0pt width 1.1ex}\! F(H):=\mu(F\cap H),\quad H\subset Q(0,1)\textrm{ is Borel}.
\]
Since $\mathcal L^2(F)=0$ and $\mu(F)=1$, the absolutely continuous part of
$Eu$ vanishes on $F$, giving
\[
 Eu\!\mathbin{\vrule height 1.6ex depth 0pt width
		0.13ex\vrule height 0.13ex depth 0pt width 1.1ex}\! F=P\mu.
\]
For $v=(v_1,v_2)\in\mathrm{BV}(Q;\mathbb R^2)$, apply
\eqref{eq:BV-null-product} to both components $v_j$. All four measures
$D_kv_j$ vanish on $F$, so $Dv\!\mathbin{\vrule height 1.6ex depth 0pt width
		0.13ex\vrule height 0.13ex depth 0pt width 1.1ex}\! F=0$ and $Ev\!\mathbin{\vrule height 1.6ex depth 0pt width
		0.13ex\vrule height 0.13ex depth 0pt width 1.1ex}\! F=0$.
Subtracting gives
\[
 E(u-v)\!\mathbin{\vrule height 1.6ex depth 0pt width
		0.13ex\vrule height 0.13ex depth 0pt width 1.1ex}\! F=P\mu.
\]
Thus
\[
 |E(u-v)|(Q)\ge |E(u-v)|(F)=|P|\mu(F)=\sqrt2.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:main-gap}.\\
                     
                               
                                       

\emph{Step 4: Higher dimensions.}\\
Fix $n\ge2$ and let $Q=(0,1)^n$, and
                                                     
               
                                                             
           
\[
 \widetilde u(x)=(u_1(x_1,x_2),u_2(x_1,x_2),0,\ldots,0),
\]
and
\[
 \widetilde\mu=\mu\otimes
      (\mathcal L^{n-2}\!\mathbin{\vrule height 1.6ex depth 0pt width
		0.13ex\vrule height 0.13ex depth 0pt width 1.1ex}\!(0,1)^{n-2}),\quad
 \widetilde F=A\times B\times(0,1)^{n-2}.
\]
Let
\[
H'(x)=\operatorname{diag}(H_1(x_1,x_2),H_2(x_1,x_2),0,\ldots,0),
\]
and define the $n\times n$ matrix $P$ so that
$(P)_{1}^2=(P)_{2}^1=1$ and all other entries are zero. 
The first two components of $\widetilde u$ are independent of the remaining coordinates, so
\eqref{eq:Eu formula} gives
\[
 E\widetilde u=H'\mathcal L^n+P\widetilde\mu,\qquad 
\]
In
particular, $|P|=\sqrt2$, $\widetilde u\in\mathrm{BD}(Q)$, and
$\widetilde\mu$ is a probability measure carried by the Lebesgue-null
set $\widetilde F$.

                                                      
                 
                       
Recall from \eqref{eq:projection} the orthogonal projections $\pi_j$.
               
                                               
                                                                 
                                             
We have
\[
 \mathcal L^{n-1}\bigl(\pi_j(\widetilde F)\bigr)=0
 \quad\text{for every }j=1,\ldots,n.
\]
                                                                    
Thus from the one-dimensional slicing \eqref{eq:slice representation for total variation}, we get
$|Dw|(\widetilde F)=0$ for any $w\in\mathrm{BV}(Q,\mathbb R^n)$.
                                
                                                                       

Next, choose a compact set $K\subset \widetilde F$ with
$\widetilde\mu(K)>0$, which exists by inner regularity. Choose
$\eta\in C_c^\infty(Q)$ with $0\le\eta\le1$ and $\eta=1$ on $K$.
          
   
                              
                               
   
Let $v=\eta\widetilde u$ on $Q$, extended by zero to $\mathbb R^n$.
We have $v\in C_c(Q)$.
For vectors $a,b\in\mathbb R^n$, we write
$a\odot b=(a\otimes b+b\otimes a)/2$. 
The distributional product rule gives
\[
 Ev=\eta E\widetilde u+
       (\widetilde u\odot\nabla\eta)\mathcal L^n.
\]
                                                                 
These are finite measures, so $v\in\mathrm{BD}(\mathbb R^n)$.
                          
                                                                      
                                  
On $K$ the
absolutely continuous terms vanish, leaving
\[
 Ev\!\mathbin{\vrule height 1.6ex depth 0pt width
		0.13ex\vrule height 0.13ex depth 0pt width 1.1ex}\! K=P\widetilde\mu.
\]
                                                                               
                                                    
                                                                
                                                       
                                           
                                                                      
                                                                              
   
                                 
                                                 
   
                                                                
                                          
   
                   
   
                                                                                
 
                                                 
   
                                           
   
                                                                    
                                                                  
                   
   
                
                                         
   
                                                                    
                                 
Hence for every $w\in\mathrm{BV}(Q;\mathbb R^n)$,
\[
 |E(v-w)|(Q)\ge |E(v-w)|(K)=|Ev|(K)
 = \sqrt2\,\widetilde\mu(K)>0.
\]
The generalization from $Q$ to a general open set $\Omega\subset \mathbb R^n$ is straightforward. This completes
the proof of the theorem.
