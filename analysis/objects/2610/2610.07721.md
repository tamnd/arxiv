<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:calibration}
Let $X$ have full column rank and $A_0\succ0$. For $1\le s\le m$,
a calibrated $R_s\succ0$ exists if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:feasibility}
 s>d_{\mathrm{eff}}(A_0):=\operatorname{tr}\{G(G+A_0)^{-1}\}.
\end{equation}
It is unique among all positive definite matrices and depends only on
$G,A_0,m,s$. At $s=m$, $R_m=A_0$. For every feasible $s<m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:penalty-upper}
 0\prec R_s\preceq \frac{s}{m}A_0\prec A_0.
\end{equation}
If $d\le s<m$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:penalty-sandwich}
 \frac{s-d+1}{m-d+1}A_0\preceq R_s\preceq\frac{s}{m}A_0.
\end{equation}
Both inequalities in \eqref{eq:penalty-sandwich} are strict when
$d>1$. If $A_0$ is scalar, $R_s$ commutes with $G$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:mean-identity}
For $R\succ0$,
\begin{align}
 \sum_{s=0}^m t^sZ_s(G,R)
   &=(1+t)^m\det\left(R+\frac{t}{1+t}G\right),\label{eq:partition-generating}\\
 \mathbb E_{s,R}w_S
   &=\{\nabla_G\log Z_s(G,R)\}X^\top y.\label{eq:mean-identity}
\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Expand the determinant into distinct rank-one row contributions. A term
using $k$ rows belongs to $\binom{m-k}{s-k}$ size-$s$ subsets. Summing in
$s$ proves \eqref{eq:partition-generating}. For the mean, allow nonsymmetric
rank-one contributions and replace $x_ix_i^\top$ by
$x_i(x_i+t y_i v)^\top$. On a selected subset the derivative at zero is
$\det(G_S+R)v^\top(G_S+R)^{-1}X_S^\top y_S$. On the total Gram matrix
the derivative is $v^\top(\nabla_G Z_s)X^\top y$. Equating these
derivatives and dividing by $Z_s$ proves \eqref{eq:mean-identity}.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:moment-map}
Let $1\le s\le m$ and $m\ge d$. The gradient of
$A_s(z)=\log P_s(e^z)$, with $P_s$ in \eqref{eq:cal-partition}, is a
bijection onto
$\{\rho:0<\rho_j<1,\ \sum_j\rho_j<s\}$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The distribution on $J\subseteq[d]$ with mass proportional to
$c_{|J|}\exp(\sum_{j\in J}z_j)$ has support all zero-one vectors with at
most $s$ ones. Its convex hull is
$\mathcal C_s=\{\rho:0\le\rho_j\le1,\ \sum_j\rho_j\le s\}$.
To check the hull, any vertex with two fractional coordinates admits a
feasible perturbation; a vertex with one fractional coordinate cannot
satisfy an active integral sum constraint and also admits a perturbation.
Thus every vertex is a stated zero-one vector.

The gradient of $A_s$ is the mean indicator, and its Hessian is the
indicator covariance. A zero-variance linear combination must agree on
the empty set and every singleton, so all its coefficients are zero.
The Hessian is therefore positive definite. Every finite parameter gives
positive probability to every support point, so its mean is interior.
Conversely, for an interior $\rho$, a ball of radius $\delta>0$ about
$\rho$ lies in $\mathcal C_s$. The support function then gives
\[
 A_s(z)-\rho^\top z\ge\delta\lVert z\rVert+
             \min_{k:c_k>0}\log c_k.
\]
This function is coercive and strictly convex. Its unique minimizer
solves $\nabla A_s(z)=\rho$. This also proves injectivity. When a sum
constraint is redundant, the displayed strict inequality remains true
through $0<\rho_j<1$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Use coefficient coordinates $\widetilde w=G^{1/2}w$ and design
$\widetilde X=XG^{-1/2}$, whose Gram matrix is $I$. Write
\[
 C=G^{-1/2}RG^{-1/2},\qquad
 D=G^{1/2}(G+A_0)^{-1}G^{1/2},\qquad 0\prec D\prec I.
\]
If $C=U\operatorname{diag}(c_j)U^\top$, put $q_j=1/c_j$. The
determinant expansion gives $Z_s(I,C)=\det(C)P_s(q)$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:mean-identity} gives the mean operator
\[
 \Phi_s(C)\widetilde X^\top,\qquad
 \Phi_s(C)=U\operatorname{diag}(\rho_j(q))U^\top.
\]
The desired operator is $D\widetilde X^\top$. Full row rank of
$\widetilde X^\top$ makes calibration equivalent to $\Phi_s(C)=D$.
Thus every solution $C$ commutes with $D$. In a simultaneous eigenbasis,
Lemma~\ref{lem:moment-map} gives exactly one positive eigenvalue vector
$q$ if and only if $\operatorname{tr}D<s$. Equal target eigenvalues
force equal $q$ coordinates, since swapping them preserves the unique
moment-map solution. There is no freedom to rotate a different penalty
inside a repeated eigenspace. This proves all-matrix uniqueness and
\eqref{eq:feasibility}. Equivalently, the exact identity
\[
 \rho_i-\rho_j=\frac{q_i-q_j}{P_s(q)}
 \sum_{J\subseteq[d]\setminus\{i,j\}}c_{|J|+1}\prod_{k\in J}q_k
\]
has a strictly positive final factor, whose empty term is $c_1>0$.
Unwhitening gives $R_s=G^{1/2}CG^{1/2}$. For scalar $A_0$, $D$ is a
spectral function of $G$, so $R_s$ commutes with $G$.

For the bounds, write $q_J=\prod_{k\in J}q_k$ and split
$P_s=B_j+q_jA_j$. Then
\[
 \frac{\rho_j}{1-\rho_j}=q_j\frac{A_j}{B_j},\qquad
 \frac{A_j}{B_j}
 =\frac{\sum_{J\not\ni j}c_{|J|}q_J (s-|J|)/(m-|J|)}
        {\sum_{J\not\ni j}c_{|J|}q_J}.
\]
Terms with zero coefficient are omitted. At $s<m$ all denominators here
are positive; this weighted average is at most $s/m$. For $s\ge d$ it lies between
$(s-d+1)/(m-d+1)$ and $s/m$. The eigenvalues of the whitened target
penalty are $(1-\rho_j)/\rho_j$, and the ratio of the calibrated to
target eigenvalues is $A_j/B_j$. Congruence proves
\eqref{eq:penalty-upper} and \eqref{eq:penalty-sandwich}. If $d>1$ and
$d\le s<m$, the positive weights at $|J|=0$ and $|J|=d-1$ have distinct
ratios, so both sandwich inequalities are strict. At $s=m$ the fit is deterministic; equality
of its operator with the target and full rank give $R_m=A_0$ directly.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:calibration-closed}
If $s=1$ is feasible, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:singleton-penalty}
 R_1=\frac{1-d_{\mathrm{eff}}(A_0)}{m}(G+A_0).
\end{equation}
For $d=1$ and $A_0=\lambda_0>0$, every budget has
$r_s=s\lambda_0/m$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For $s=1$, $P_1(q)=m+\sum_jq_j$. Hence
$q_j=m\rho_j/(1-\sum_k\rho_k)$.
In the whitened coordinates of the theorem, this says
$C=(1-\operatorname{tr}D)D^{-1}/m$.
Congruence by $G^{1/2}$ proves \eqref{eq:singleton-penalty}.
For $d=1$, $P_s(q)=c_0+c_1q$ and $c_1/c_0=s/m$.
The odds equation in the theorem's proof gives $r_s=s\lambda_0/m$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:zero-variance}
Let $d\ge2$, $1\le s<m$, and $R\succ0$. Under any law positive on
every size-$s$ subset, the selected ridge fits satisfy
\[
 \operatorname{Cov}(w_S)=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_i y_i=0\quad\text{for every }i.
\]
Thus the zero-variance responses are exactly those supported on zero rows.
This statement does not require calibration.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Zero covariance makes all fits the same vector $w$. Set
$v_i=x_i(y_i-x_i^\top w)$. The normal equations give
$\sum_{i\in S}v_i=Rw$ for every size-$s$ subset.
For any two indices, choose $s-1$ other indices and exchange the remaining
index. This shows that all $v_i$ agree. Their common value lies in every
row line. Those lines have zero intersection because the rows span at
least two dimensions. Hence $v_i=0$ and $Rw=0$, so $w=0$ and $x_iy_i=0$.
The converse follows because every selected right-hand side is then zero.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:common-energy}
Let $d\ge2$. At every feasible $1\le s<m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:common-energy-bound}
 0<\underline c_s\le\overline c_s<\infty,\qquad
 \underline c_s\mathcal E_0(y)\le\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_s(y)
 \le\overline c_s\mathcal E_0(y).
\end{equation}
Consequently, for any two feasible interior budgets $s,t$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total-comparison}
 \frac{\underline c_s}{\overline c_t}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_t(y)
 \le\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_s(y)
 \le\frac{\overline c_s}{\underline c_t}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_t(y).
\end{equation}
For a fixed $W\succ0$, the same comparison holds for
$\operatorname{tr}(W\Sigma_s)$ after multiplying the lower constant by
$\lambda_{\min}(W)/\lambda_{\max}(W)$ and the upper constant by its
reciprocal. The energy $\mathcal E_0$ vanishes exactly on the zero-row
response space.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Set $\delta_S=w_S-w_0$. The selected normal equation gives
\[
 (G_S+R_s)\delta_S=\sum_{i\in S}b_i-R_sw_0=:t_S.
\]
Let $U_s$ be the uniform law on size-$s$ subsets. Write
$t_S=\sum_{i\in S}(b_i-\bar b)+[(s/m)A_0-R_s]w_0$.
Uniform inclusion probabilities are $s/m$ for one index and
$s(s-1)/[m(m-1)]$ for two distinct indices. Since
$\sum_i(b_i-\bar b)=0$, expansion yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-energy}
 \mathbb E_{U_s}\lVert t_S\rVert^2
 =a_s\mathcal E_0+\lVert[(s/m)A_0-R_s]w_0\rVert^2.
\end{equation}
For a nonzero row, $b_i$ belongs to its row line. Projection onto the
orthogonal complement of that line therefore gives
\[
 \lVert b_i-\bar b\rVert^2\ge
 \frac{\lVert x_i\rVert^2\lVert\bar b\rVert^2-(x_i^\top\bar b)^2}{L_x^2}.
\]
For a zero row the right side is zero. Summing and using the largest
eigenvalue of $G$ gives
\[
 \mathcal E_0\ge\mu_X\lVert\bar b\rVert^2,\qquad
 \lVert A_0w_0\rVert^2\le\frac{m^2}{\mu_X}\mathcal E_0.
\]
Thus \eqref{eq:uniform-energy} lies between $a_s\mathcal E_0$ and
$(a_s+m^2\lVert \mathcal D_s\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^2/\mu_X)\mathcal E_0$.

The order bounds $R_s\preceq G_S+R_s\preceq G+R_s$ imply
\[
 \frac{\lVert t_S\rVert}{\Lambda_s}\le\lVert\delta_S\rVert
 \le\frac{\lVert t_S\rVert}{r_{\min,s}}.
\]
Every determinant weight lies between $\det R_s$ and $\det(G+R_s)$.
Its ratio to the average weight therefore lies between
$\kappa_s^{-1}$ and $\kappa_s$, so
$\kappa_s^{-1}\le p_{s,R_s}(S)/U_s(S)\le\kappa_s$.
Calibration gives $\mathbb E_p\delta_S=0$ and hence
$\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_s=\mathbb E_p\lVert\delta_S\rVert^2$.
Combining the bounds proves \eqref{eq:common-energy-bound}.
Combining budgets proves \eqref{eq:total-comparison}; the extreme
eigenvalues of $W$ prove its query version.
Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-variance} identifies the common zero space.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:loo-envelope}
Under the preceding assumptions, $\rho>0$. The risk satisfies
$h_{m-1}(X,W)\le\rho$.
The equality kernel at this envelope is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loo-kernel}
 \ker(\rho K-C_{m-1})
 =\{y:X^\top y=0,\ y_i=0\text{ for }i\notin M\}.
\end{equation}
Thus $h_{m-1}(X,W)=\rho$ if and only if the rows $X_M$ are linearly
dependent. When this holds, \eqref{eq:loo-kernel} is the complete
maximizing space, of dimension $|M|-\operatorname{rank}(X_M)$.
If those rows are independent, $h_{m-1}(X,W)<\rho$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Every deleted normal matrix $G+R-x_ix_i^\top$ is positive definite.
The determinant lemma and the rank-one inverse identity give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loo-deletion}
 0\le\ell_i<1,\qquad p_i=\frac{1-\ell_i}{D},\qquad
 w_{-i}=BX^\top y-\frac{Bx_i(Ly)_i}{1-\ell_i}.
\end{equation}
Also $D=m-\operatorname{tr}(GB)>m-d\ge1$.
The mean of the deleted correction is $D^{-1}BX^\top Ly$.
Since $X^\top L=RBX^\top$, all-response calibration and full row rank
of $X^\top$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loo-calibration-identity}
 (G+A_0)^{-1}=B-D^{-1}BRB,\qquad
 K-L=D^{-1}XBRBX^\top.
\end{equation}
The second matrix is positive semidefinite with kernel $\ker(X^\top)$.
No commutation is used here.

Taking the correction's second moment in \eqref{eq:loo-deletion} and
subtracting its mean square gives the centered response operator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loo-centered-operator}
 C_{m-1}=L\operatorname{diag}(\omega_i)L
       -D^{-2}LXBWBX^\top L.
\end{equation}
Woodbury gives $L=(I+XR^{-1}X^\top)^{-1}$, so $0\prec L\preceq I$.
Spectral calculus for this matrix gives $L-L^2\succeq0$. Therefore

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{Calibration and the centered operator.}
Assume the ETF conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:etf-query}. Put
$T=XX^\top$, $P=T/g$, $P_\perp=I-P$, $q=\mu^2=(g-1)/(m-1)$,
and $h=g-1$. Here $q$ is the squared ETF coherence, not a group mean.
Let $k=m-d\ge2$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etf-retained-positive}
 h^2-q=\frac{k\{k(m-1)-d\}}{d^2(m-1)}>0,
 \qquad k(m-1)-d=(k-1)m>0.
\end{equation}
The Gram matrix after deleting two rows has eigenvalues $g$ with
multiplicity $d-2$, and $h+\mu,h-\mu$. All are positive.

For a positive raw penalty $r$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etf-basic-coefficients}
 b=(g+r)^{-1},\quad a=1-b,\quad \ell=1-bg=br,\quad
 \delta=a^2-b^2q,\quad N=\binom m2,\quad
 L=I-bT=P_\perp+\ell P.
\end{equation}
For a deleted pair $D_0=\{i,j\}$ let $E_{D_0}$ select its two
coordinates, $X_{D_0}=E_{D_0}X$, and $t=T_{ij}$. Its deletion matrix is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etf-pair-inverse}
 Q_{D_0}=I_2-bE_{D_0}TE_{D_0}^\top
 =\begin{pmatrix}a&-bt\\-bt&a\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 Q_{D_0}^{-1}=\delta^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}a&bt\\bt&a\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The deletion matrix $Q_{D_0}$ is positive definite. Every retained determinant is
$(g+r)^d\delta$, so the actual law is uniform on the $N$ original
pairs. This is the pair geometry associated with ETFs
\citep{bodmann2005}; it does not use a rotation of the response coordinates.
The fit can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etf-correction}
 w_S=bX^\top y-z_{D_0}(y),\qquad
 z_{D_0}(y)=bX_{D_0}^\top Q_{D_0}^{-1}E_{D_0}Ly.
\end{equation}
Counting diagonal and off-diagonal pair occurrences gives
\begin{align}
 \sum_{D_0}E_{D_0}^\top Q_{D_0}^{-1}E_{D_0}
  &=\delta^{-1}\{[(m-1)a-b]I+bT\},\nonumber\\
 e&=(m-1)a+b(g-1),\qquad
 \eta=\frac{b\ell e}{N\delta}>0,\nonumber\\
 \mathbb E z_{D_0}(y)&=\eta X^\top y,\qquad
 \mathbb E w_S=f(r)X^\top y,\quad f(r)=b-\eta.\label{eq:etf-mean}
\end{align}
To identify $f$, apply this mean identity to $y=Xv$ and take traces
of the mean fit map. The retained spectrum above gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etf-calibration}
 f(r)=\frac1m\left\{
 \frac{(d-2)g}{g+r}+
 \frac{h+\mu}{h+\mu+r}+\frac{h-\mu}{h-\mu+r}\right\}.
\end{equation}
It decreases continuously and strictly from $1/g$ to zero. Hence every
prescribed $\lambda_0>0$ has exactly one positive scalar solution of
$f(r)=1/(g+\lambda_0)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:calibration} gives uniqueness
among all SPD penalties because $s=m-2\ge d$ is feasible. At this
calibration,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etf-full-loss}
 k_F=1-gf=\frac{\lambda_0}{g+\lambda_0}=\ell+g\eta>0,
 \qquad K=P_\perp+k_FP.
\end{equation}
Thus this construction reaches a prescribed target, not just a target
chosen after the raw penalty.

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{The operator for any symmetric query.}
Let $H=XWX^\top$, $h_i=H_{ii}$, and $D_h=\operatorname{diag}(h_i)$.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etf-operator-coefficients}
 \alpha=a^2(m-2)+2ab(g-2)-2b^2q,\quad
 \beta=b^2q,\quad \gamma=ab,\quad \theta=a^2+b^2q.
\end{equation}
The coefficient covariance is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etf-actual-covariance}
 \operatorname{Cov}(w_S(y))=
 \frac1N\sum_{D_0}z_{D_0}(y)z_{D_0}(y)^\top
   -\eta^2X^\top yy^\top X.
\end{equation}
For a pair $D_0=\{i,j\}$, signed multiplication in
\eqref{eq:etf-pair-inverse} gives
\begin{align}
 (Q_{D_0}^{-1}H_{D_0D_0}Q_{D_0}^{-1})_{11}
  &=\frac{a^2h_i+2ab\,tH_{ij}+b^2q h_j}{\delta^2},\nonumber\\
 (Q_{D_0}^{-1}H_{D_0D_0}Q_{D_0}^{-1})_{12}
  &=\frac{ab\,t(h_i+h_j)+\theta H_{ij}}{\delta^2}.
 \label{eq:etf-signed-product}
\end{align}
The second diagonal entry swaps $i,j$. Since $TH=HT=gH$, summing the
first line over $j\ne i$ has numerator

This is the response operator, not the coefficient covariance matrix.
The last subtraction centers at the actual mean in \eqref{eq:etf-mean}.

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{Flat-query deficit, strict signs, and converse.}

 \nu_e=\frac{(m-1)n_e}{2},\quad N_e=\frac{m(m-1)n_e}{6}.
\end{equation}
Both classes occur, since
\[
 (m-2)^2q-(g-2)^2=\frac{m^2(d-1)(k-1)}{d^2(m-1)}>0.
\]
With $N=\binom m3$, summing \eqref{eq:triple-det} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:triple-law}
 Z_s(r)=Nz^{d-3}D,\qquad
 \mathbb P(S=D_0^c)=\frac{\delta_e}{ND}.
\end{equation}
All triples in one class have the same individual probability.
This probability differs between the two classes.

Every retained Gram is positive definite even at $r=0$. For $k\ge4$,
\[
 (g-1)^2-4q=\frac{k[(k-4)d+k(k-1)]}{d^2(m-1)}>0.
\]
For $k=3$, complete the columns of $X/\sqrt g$ to an orthogonal matrix
and multiply its remaining columns by $\sqrt{m/k}$. This gives a unit-row
ETF in dimension three. Its row projectors have positive definite
Frobenius Gram matrix $(1-q')I+q'\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top$, with $q'<1$,
so they are linearly independent in the six-dimensional space of
symmetric $3$ by $3$ matrices. Therefore $m\le6$. Since $d\ge3$, the
only possible pair is $d=k=3$, where $(g-1)^2-4q=1/5$.
Thus $g-1>2\mu$ in every actual case. In particular $z$,
$h\pm2\mu$, $\delta_e$, $D$, and $h^2-4q$ are positive.
This is the usual Naimark-complement and projector-dimension argument;
it does not assert existence at other formal parameter pairs.

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{Calibration with the target fixed first.}
Expanding the selected determinants into original-row minors and counting
the supersets of each minor gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:triple-partition}
 Z_s(X,r)=\sum_{j=0}^d\binom{m-j}{s-j}r^{d-j}e_j(X^\top X).
\end{equation}
At $X^\top X=gI$, the matrix derivative of $\log Z_s$ with respect to
the Gram is scalar. Differentiating instead with respect to each original
row identifies the entire mean map as $f(r)X^\top$. Taking the trace of
its product with $X$ gives
\[
 mf(r)=\mathbb E\operatorname{tr}[G_S(G_S+rI)^{-1}]
       =d-rZ_s'(r)/Z_s(r)=M(r).
\]
Every coefficient in \eqref{eq:triple-partition} is positive, because
$s=m-3\ge d$. Under the distribution on $j$ proportional to those
terms, $M=\mathbb E j$ and
\[
 \frac{dM}{d\log r}=-\operatorname{Var}(j)<0,\qquad
 M(0+)=d,\quad M(+\infty)=0.
\]
Thus $M/m=1/(g+\lambda_0)$ has a unique positive solution for every
fixed positive target. Theorem~\ref{thm:calibration} gives uniqueness
among SPD penalties. In particular $k_F=1-M/d=rH_1/d>0$.

\subsection{The covariance quadratics}
Conditional on all counts, the selected contrast sums are independent
across groups, have mean zero, and variance
$K_j(n-K_j)E_j/[n(n-1)]$. Thus, with
$\omega(k)=k(n-k)/[n(n-1)]$,
\begin{align}
 \alpha(t)&=\mathbb E[\omega(K_1)h_t(K_1)^2],\nonumber\\
 \beta(t)&=\frac{\mathbb E[(g_t(K_1)-g_t(K_2))^2]}{2b},\qquad

 \operatorname{Cov}(w(t))
 &=\alpha(t)\operatorname{diag}(E_j)
      +b\beta(t)\operatorname{diag}(q_j^2)-b\tau(t)qq^\top.\nonumber
\end{align}
This calculation includes the covariance between the ridge and
inclusion-weighted estimates on their shared subset. Their cross
contrast coefficient is $c_H\mathbb E[\omega(K_1)h(K_1)]$;
their mean cross covariance is

Exchangeability makes that cross covariance symmetric. No independence
between the two estimates is assumed.

\[

nondiagonal PSD queries and all ties. For a finite grid of budgets,

Let $A=\lambda_{\max}(U^\top M_1U)$,
$D=\lambda_{\max}(Q^\top M_1Q)$ and $b_1=\|Q^\top M_1U\|$.
Compression and the variational principle give $A-D\ge g$ and $b_1\le B$.
For a unit vector $x=Uu+Qv$,
\[
 x^\top M_1x\le A\|u\|^2+D\|v\|^2+2b_1\|u\|\|v\|.
\]
The largest eigenvalue of the corresponding two-by-two matrix, minus
$A$, is $2b_1^2/(\sqrt{(A-D)^2+4b_1^2}+A-D)$.
It decreases with the gap and increases with the cross norm. This proves
\eqref{eq:enlarged-regret}, the classical block Rayleigh bound
\citep{lili2005}. Weyl's inequality proves the alternative bound.
Here $A=h_{1,I}$ because the trial space is $K_1^{1/2}\mathcal E_I$.

For leakage put $t=\|v\|$. Since $h_1\ge A$,
\[
 h_1-x^\top M_1x\ge(A-D)t^2-2b_1t\sqrt{1-t^2}\ge gt^2-2Bt.
\]
Solving this quadratic proves \eqref{eq:enlarged-leakage}.
For a top eigenvector, its complementary eigen-equation gives
$(h_1-D)t\le b_1\sqrt{1-t^2}$, proving the sharper bound, including ties.
A surrogate deficit at most $r$ implies true deficit at most $r+2e$.
Thus the leakage statement also has an evaluable surrogate test.

For the uniform claim, Appendix~\ref{app:enlarged} proves
$\|M_1-M_0\|\le Lh$ and $e\le\bar e(h)=O(h^2)$, with
$h=\epsilon+\|P_1-P_0\|$ and constants independent of normalized $W$.
It bounds the moving-space projector by $f(h)=O(h)$ and proves
\[
 g\ge\gamma-(\alpha_s+\gamma)f(h)^2-2Lh-4\bar e(h),\quad
 B\le Lh+2\alpha_sf(h)+2\bar e(h).
\]
The explicit dyadic choice \eqref{eq:enlarged-dyadic} makes
$g\ge\gamma/2>0$ on a whole ball. The full-space gap is
$\gamma=\alpha_s-\beta_s$, or use the threshold gap above.
Exact calibration sensitivity gives $h\le(1+L_{X,s})\epsilon$.
The finite minimum \eqref{eq:enlarged-feature-radius} then proves the
common feature radius, quadratic regret and leakage orders.
