<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
When $\mathcal{X} = [0,1]$ and $T^{j}(x) = x^j$ these are the usual moments of $\nu_0$, which is where the terminology comes from. The moment functions are defined on the whole of $\mathbb{R}$ because the same family $\mathcal{T}$ will be used on several spaces $\mathcal{X}$, in particular on finite ones (Definition~\ref{def:adapted}); nothing below depends on the structure of $\mathbb{R}$, which could be replaced by any infinite set.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The picture is the one familiar from information geometry: the constraints defining $M^{\mathcal{T}}_{\nu_0}$
		are linear in the density, making it a mixture-flat (\(m\)-flat) affine set, whereas the
		log-densities of $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ are affine in $\mathcal{T}$, making it an exponential-flat (\(e\)-flat) set. A
		one-parameter exponential family ($k=1$) is literally an \(e\)-geodesic; for general $k$ it is an
		\(e\)-flat submanifold, and it is in this loose sense that we will speak of $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ as the
		maximum-entropy ``geodesic''.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:pyth}
		For every $\nu_\theta \in \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{KL}}$,
		\begin{equation}\label{eq:pythagore}
			\mathrm{KL}(\nu_0, \nu_\theta) \;=\; \mathrm{KL}(\nu_0, \nu_{\bar\theta}) + \mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\bar\theta}, \nu_\theta)\,.
		\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
Concretely, (P1) is nothing but a finite-dimensional system of $k$ scalar moment equations in the
		unknown $\theta$, namely $\nu_\theta(\mathcal{T}) = \nu_0(\mathcal{T})$. What could have been an optimization over an
		infinite-dimensional space of measures becomes the resolution of $k$ scalar equations.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:fdiv}
		A function $f : \mathbb{R}^*_+ \to \mathbb{R}$ defines an \emph{$f$-divergence} if it is strictly
		convex on $\mathbb{R}^*_+$ and $f(1) = 0$. The associated divergence is
		\[
		D_f(\nu, \mu) =
		\begin{cases}
			\int_{\mathcal{X}} f\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\,\mathrm{d}\nu}{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}\Bigr) \,\mathrm{d}\mu, & \text{if } \nu \ll \mu, \\[4pt]
			+\infty, & \text{otherwise.}
		\end{cases}
		\]
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:eqfdiv}
		We  will say that $f$ is the \emph{generator} of $D_f$, and that functions $f, g$ are \emph{equivalent}, written $f \sim g$, if they define the same $f$-divergence up to a multiplicative constant, that is if $D_f$ is proportional to $D_g$.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rem:equiv}
		Adding an affine term leads to an equivalent generator: for $A>0$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}$, if $g(x) = Af(x) + B(x-1)$ then $g \sim f$.
<!-- end remark-4 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:adapted}
		Let $f$ be a $C^2$ $f$-divergence and $\mathcal{T} = (T^{0}, \dots, T^{k})$ a free family with $T^{0} \equiv 1$
		and $k \geq 1$. We say that $f$ is \emph{$\mathcal{T}$-entropy-adapted} if, for every finite space $\mathcal{X} = \{x_1,\dots,x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}$
		with reference probability measure $\lambda$ of full support and every $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$ equivalent to $\lambda$, if $\mathcal{E}_f \cap M^{\mathcal{T}}_{\nu}$ is non-empty its (necessarily unique) element $\nu_{\bar\theta}$ is such that
		\[
		D_f(\mu, \nu_\theta) \;\ge\; D_f(\mu, \nu_{\bar\theta})
		\qquad \text{for all } \mu \in M^{\mathcal{T}}_{\nu} \text{ and all } \nu_\theta \in \mathcal{E}_f\,.
		\]
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
\label{rem:finite-spaces}
		We restrict Definition~\ref{def:adapted} to finite spaces for two reasons. First, the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} only ever uses the hypothesis on finite spaces (Remark~\ref{rem:finite}), so a weaker hypothesis makes the ``only if'' direction stronger. Second, on infinite spaces the moment matcher may fail to exist even for KL: take $\mathcal{X} = [0,\infty)$, $T(x) = x$ and $\lambda(\,\mathrm{d} x) \propto e^{-x}(1+x^3)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d} x$. Then $e^{\theta x}$ is $\lambda$-integrable only for $\theta \leq 1$, so the means of the elements of $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ are bounded by some $m^* < \infty$, while measures $\nu \sim \lambda$ with finite mean larger than $m^*$ exist. A definition quantifying over all spaces would therefore exclude KL itself. Finally, on a finite space all integrals are finite sums, and $\mathcal{E}_f$ is simply parametrized by $\{\theta \in \Theta_f : \sum_i \lambda_i (f')^{-1}(\theta^\top \mathcal{T}(x_i)) = 1\}$. The definition could nevertheless be extended to
		infinite spaces under additional assumptions guaranteeing the existence of the moment matcher, for
		instance bounded moment functions $\mathcal{T}$, or more generally measures $\nu$ whose moment vector
		lies in the image of the moment map of $\mathcal{E}_f$; Theorem~\ref{thm:main} would remain
		valid, since finite spaces are still included.
<!-- end remark-5 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
		Let $\mathcal{T} = (T^{0}, \dots, T^{k})$ be a free family with $T^{0} \equiv 1$ and $k \geq 1$, and let $f$ be
		a $C^2$ $f$-divergence. Then, we have:
		\[
		\text{$f$ is $\mathcal{T}$-entropy-adapted iff up to equivalence, }  D_f = \mathrm{KL}\,.
		\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:const}
		Let $f$ be a $C^2$ $f$-divergence and set $h(x) = f(x) - x f'(x)$, so that $h'(x) = -x f''(x)$.
		If $h'$ is constant on $\mathbb{R}^*_+$ (equivalently, if $x \mapsto x f''(x)$ is constant) then,
		up to equivalence, $D_f = \mathrm{KL}$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Write $h'(x) = -x f''(x) = -A$ for all $x > 0$. If $A = 0$ then $f'' \equiv 0$ and $f$ is affine,
		contradicting strict convexity; convexity of $f$ then forces $A > 0$. Integrating
		$f''(x) = A/x$ twice, there exist $C, D \in \mathbb{R}$ such that
		\[
		f(x) = A\, x \ln x - A x + C x + D\,,
		\]
		and the normalization $f(1) = 0$ gives $D = A - C$, that is
		\[
		f(x) = A\, x \ln x + (C - A)(x - 1)\,.
		\]

From now on we normalize $f'(1) = 0$, which by Remark~\ref{rem:equiv} changes neither $D_f$, nor
	$\mathcal{E}_f$, nor $f''$. We write
	\[
	Z \mathrel{\overset{\text{def}}{=}} \{x > 0 : f''(x) = 0\}\,.
	\]
	By strict convexity, $Z$ is closed with empty interior (on any interval where $f'' \equiv 0$ the
	function $f$ would be affine). The derivative $f'$ is continuous and strictly increasing, hence a
	homeomorphism from $\mathbb{R}^*_+$ onto the \emph{open} interval $I \mathrel{\overset{\text{def}}{=}} f'(\mathbb{R}^*_+)$, which contains
	$0 = f'(1)$; consequently $f'(Z)$ is closed in $I$, and the inverse
	$(f')^{-1} : I \to \mathbb{R}^*_+$ is continuous, increasing, and $C^1$ on the open set
	$I \setminus f'(Z)$, with
	\[
	\bigl((f')^{-1}\bigr)' \;=\; \frac{1}{f'' \circ (f')^{-1}}
	\]
	there, by the inverse function theorem. Finally, by Sard's theorem in dimension
	one\footnote{Elementary in dimension one: fix a compact interval $[a,b] \subset \mathbb{R}^*_+$ and
		$\varepsilon > 0$. By uniform continuity of $f''$ on $[a,b]$, there is $\delta > 0$ such that
		$|f''| \le \varepsilon$ at every point of $[a,b]$ at distance $\le \delta$ from $Z$ (recall
		$f'' = 0$ on $Z$). Split $[a,b]$ into at most $(b-a)/\delta + 1$ intervals of length $\le \delta$:
		each one meeting $Z$ is made of such points, so $f'$ has oscillation $\le \varepsilon\delta$ on
		it, and $f'(Z \cap [a,b])$ has outer measure at most $\varepsilon(b - a + \delta)$. Let
		$\varepsilon \to 0$, then exhaust $\mathbb{R}^*_+$ by compact intervals.}, the set $f'(Z)$ of critical
	values of $f'$ is Lebesgue-negligible. Note right away that when $f''(1) \neq 0$ none of this
	machinery will be needed: it only serves to handle the degenerate case $f''(1) = 0$.

\label{lem:anchor}
		Let $y_1, \dots, y_N \in \mathbb{R}$ be distinct points, $j \in \{1, \dots, k\}$, and fix two indices
		$\kappa, \Lambda$ with $T^j(y_\kappa) < T^j(y_\Lambda)$. There exist $\theta^\ast_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ and
		$\theta^\ast_j > 0$ such that, setting all the other coordinates of $\theta^\ast$ to zero,
		$m_i \mathrel{\overset{\text{def}}{=}} \theta^\ast_0 + \theta^\ast_j T^j(y_i)$ and $p_i \mathrel{\overset{\text{def}}{=}} (f')^{-1}(m_i)$:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item[(i)] $m_i \in I \setminus f'(Z)$ for every $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$; in particular
			$f''(p_i) > 0$, and $\theta \mapsto p_{\theta,i} \mathrel{\overset{\text{def}}{=}} (f')^{-1}\bigl(\theta_0 + \sum_l \theta_l T^l(y_i)\bigr)$
			is $C^1$ in a neighbourhood of $\theta^\ast$;
			\item[(ii)] $p_\kappa < 1 < p_\Lambda$.
		\end{itemize}
		Moreover, let $\mathcal{X}$ be a subset of $\{y_1, \dots, y_N\}$ containing $y_\kappa$ and $y_\Lambda$, and
		split its other indices into two disjoint sets $A$ and $B$. There exists $\delta > 0$ such that, for
		all $(\epsilon, \eta) \in [0, \delta]^2$, the solution $(\lambda_\kappa, \lambda_\Lambda)$ of
		\[
		\lambda_\kappa + \lambda_\Lambda = 1 - \epsilon\, |A| - \eta\, |B|\,, \qquad
		\lambda_\kappa\, p_\kappa + \lambda_\Lambda\, p_\Lambda = 1 - \epsilon \sum_{i \in A} p_i - \eta \sum_{i \in B} p_i
		\]
		has positive coordinates and depends continuously on $(\epsilon, \eta)$. In particular, for
		$(\epsilon, \eta) \in (0, \delta]^2$, the weights $\lambda_i = \epsilon$ ($i \in A$),
		$\lambda_i = \eta$ ($i \in B$), $\lambda_\kappa$, $\lambda_\Lambda$, together with $\theta^\ast$,
		turn $\mathcal{X}$ into an anchored configuration.

Write $\theta_0$, $\theta_1$ for $\theta^\ast_0$, $\theta^\ast_j$, and $T_i$ for $T^j(y_i)$. Fix
		$\theta_1 > 0$, to be shrunk below. Since $(f')^{-1}$ is increasing with $(f')^{-1}(0) = 1$,
		condition (ii) reads $m_\kappa < 0 < m_\Lambda$, i.e.
		\[
		\theta_0 \in J \mathrel{\overset{\text{def}}{=}} \bigl(-\theta_1 T_\Lambda,\, -\theta_1 T_\kappa\bigr)\,,
		\]
		a non-empty open interval, of length $\theta_1 (T_\Lambda - T_\kappa)$. For $\theta_0 \in J$ one has
		$|\theta_0| \le \theta_1 \max(|T_\kappa|, |T_\Lambda|)$, hence
		\[
		\max_i |m_i| \;\le\; \theta_1 \Bigl( \max(|T_\kappa|, |T_\Lambda|) + \max_i |T_i| \Bigr)\,;
		\]
		since $I$ is open and contains $0$, taking $\theta_1$ small enough guarantees $m_i \in I$ for
		every $i$ and every $\theta_0 \in J$. With $\theta_1$ now fixed, the failure of the first part of

		Lebesgue-null sets, hence a null set; since $J$ has positive measure, we may pick $\theta_0 \in J$

		\[
		(\lambda_\kappa, \lambda_\Lambda) \;=\;
		\Bigl( \frac{p_\Lambda - 1}{p_\Lambda - p_\kappa},\; \frac{1 - p_\kappa}{p_\Lambda - p_\kappa} \Bigr)\,,
		\]
		whose coordinates are positive by (ii); by continuity, they remain positive on $[0, \delta]^2$ for
		some $\delta > 0$. The two equations are exactly $\sum_i \lambda_i = 1$ and
		$\sum_i \lambda_i\, p_i = 1$, which, together with (i), is the definition of an anchored
		configuration.

The measure $\nu_\ast$ belongs to $\mathcal{E}_f \cap M^{\mathcal{T}}_{\nu_\ast}$, so the uniqueness part of
		Definition~\ref{def:adapted}, applied with $\nu = \nu_\ast$, gives $\nu_{\bar\theta} = \nu_\ast$:

		for $\mu \mathrel{\overset{\text{def}}{=}} r\nu_\ast \in M^{\mathcal{T}}_{\nu_\ast}$,
		\[
		D_f(\mu, \nu_\theta) \;\ge\; D_f(\mu, \nu_\ast) \qquad \text{for all } \nu_\theta \in \mathcal{E}_f\,.
		\]
		The density of $\mu$ with respect to $\lambda$ being $r p_\ast$, this says that
		$\theta^\ast$ minimizes
		\[
		\theta \;\longmapsto\; \sum_i \lambda_i\, f\!\Bigl(\frac{r_i\, p_i}{p_{\theta,i}}\Bigr)\, p_{\theta,i}
		\qquad \text{subject to} \qquad \sum_i \lambda_i\, p_{\theta,i} = 1\,,
		\]
		with $p_{\theta,i} = (f')^{-1}\bigl(\theta_0 + \sum_l \theta_l T^l_i\bigr)$, over a neighbourhood of
		$\theta^\ast$ in $\mathbb{R}^{k+1}$ on which every $\theta_0 + \sum_l \theta_l T^l_i$ stays in the open set
		$I \setminus f'(Z)$. Since $f''(p_i) > 0$ and $f''$ is continuous, both the objective and the
		constraint are $C^1$ near $\theta^\ast$, with
		\[
		\frac{\partial p_{\theta,i}}{\partial \theta_l} = \frac{T^l_i}{f''(p_{\theta,i})}
		\qquad (l = 0, \dots, k,\ T^0 \equiv 1)\,.
		\]
		Moreover, for any fixed $q > 0$,
		\[
		\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d} p}\Bigl[ f\!\Bigl(\frac{q}{p}\Bigr)\, p \Bigr]
		= f'\!\Bigl(\frac{q}{p}\Bigr)\Bigl(-\frac{q}{p^2}\Bigr)\, p + f\!\Bigl(\frac{q}{p}\Bigr)
		= f\!\Bigl(\frac{q}{p}\Bigr) - \frac{q}{p}\, f'\!\Bigl(\frac{q}{p}\Bigr)
		= h\!\Bigl(\frac{q}{p}\Bigr)\,.
		\]

		$p_{\theta^\ast, i} = p_i$ (so that $q/p = r_i$), the partial derivatives in $\theta_l$ of the
		objective and of the constraint at $\theta^\ast$ are
		\[
		\sum_i \lambda_i\, T^l_i\, \frac{h(r_i)}{f''(p_i)}
		\qquad \text{and} \qquad
		\sum_i \lambda_i\, T^l_i\, \frac{1}{f''(p_i)}\,, \qquad l = 0, \dots, k\,.
		\]
		The constraint gradient is non-zero (its $\theta_0$-coordinate is
		$\sum_i \lambda_i / f''(p_i) > 0$) so the Lagrange multiplier rule applies: there exists
		$\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ such that, for $l = 0, \dots, k$,
		\[
		\sum_i \lambda_i\, T^l_i\, \frac{h(r_i)}{f''(p_i)} = \alpha \sum_i \lambda_i\, T^l_i\, \frac{1}{f''(p_i)}\,.
		\]

		equation with index $l = j \geq 1$ reads

		\sum_i \nu^\ast_i\, w_i\, T^j_i\, h(r_i)
		\;=\; \alpha \sum_i \nu^\ast_i\, w_i\, T^j_i
		\;=\; \alpha\, W \tau_j \;=\; 0\,,
		\]
		by the standing convention $\tau = 0$. (The equation with index $l = 0$ merely determines $\alpha$,
		which we do not need.)

\label{lem:points}
		Let $F = \operatorname{span}(1, T^1, \dots, T^k)$, viewed as a space of functions on $\mathbb{R}$, let $F_0 \subset F$ be
		the subspace of its finitely supported elements, and let $B$ be the union of the supports of the
		elements of $F_0$. Then $B$ is finite. Let $F'$ be the space of restrictions to $\mathbb{R} \setminus B$ of
		the elements of $F$, and $d \mathrel{\overset{\text{def}}{=}} \dim F' \ge 1$. Then:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item[(a)] for every finite $C \subset \mathbb{R} \setminus B$, the restriction map from $F'$ to functions
			on $\mathbb{R} \setminus (B \cup C)$ is injective;
			\item[(b)] for every finite $C \subset \mathbb{R} \setminus B$, there exist $d$ points
			$y_1, \dots, y_d \in \mathbb{R} \setminus (B \cup C)$ such that $g \mapsto (g(y_1), \dots, g(y_d))$ is a

		$T^j(x_\ell) \neq \tau_j$; after the standing translation, $T^j_\ell \neq 0$.
		
		Since $\mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R} \setminus B$ and evaluation on $Q$ is injective on $F'$, $\dim S_\mathcal{X} = d$ and
		the restriction maps $S_\mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^P$ and $S_\mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^Q$ are bijective. Prescribe
		\[
		r_i = s \quad (i \in P)\,, \qquad r_\ell = u\,,
		\]
		and let $g^1, \dots, g^d$ be a basis of $S_\mathcal{X}$. The condition $r \in \mathcal{A}(\nu_\ast)$ is the
		linear system
		\[
		\sum_{i \in Q} \nu^\ast_i\, g^m_i\, (r_i - 1) \;=\; - \sum_{i \in P \cup \{\ell\}} \nu^\ast_i\, g^m_i\, (r_i - 1)
		\qquad (m = 1, \dots, d)
		\]
		in the unknowns $(r_i - 1)_{i \in Q}$. As $\epsilon \to 0$, its matrix converges to
		$\bigl(\nu^0_i\, g^m_i\bigr)_{m,\, i \in Q}$, which is invertible since $(g^m_i)_{m,\, i \in Q}$ is,
		while its right-hand side is $O(\epsilon)$. Hence the solution exists for $\epsilon$ small and
		satisfies $r_i \to 1$ for $i \in Q$; in particular $r_i > 0$ and $r \in \mathcal{R}(\nu_\ast)$. We
		fix such an $\epsilon$; by Step~\ref{step:affine}, $\zeta^j(r) \in S_\mathcal{X}$. We can now conclude.
		
		\bigskip
		\noindent\emph{Case 1: $f''(1) = 0$.}
		\smallskip
		
		We show that this case cannot occur. Here $h'(1) = -f''(1) = 0$; take $s = 1$ in the
		construction above. At the indices $i \in P$, where $r_i = 1$, the vector
		$\zeta^j(r) = \bigl(w_i\, T^j_i\, h'(r_i)\bigr)_i$ vanishes; as $S_\mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^P$ is injective,
		$\zeta^j(r) = 0$. At the index $\ell$ this reads
		\[
		w_\ell\, T^j_\ell\, h'(u) = 0\,,
		\]
		and $w_\ell > 0$, $T^j_\ell \neq 0$ give $h'(u) = 0$. As $u > 0$ was arbitrary, $h' \equiv 0$ on
		$\mathbb{R}^*_+$, so $f''(x) = -h'(x)/x$ vanishes identically and $f$ is affine (contradicting strict
		convexity). A $\mathcal{T}$-entropy-adapted $C^2$ divergence therefore satisfies $f''(1) \neq 0$,
		and Case~2 proves the theorem.
		
		\bigskip
		\noindent\emph{Case 2: $f''(1) \neq 0$.}
		\smallskip
		
		The anchor is the trivial one of Remark~\ref{rem:trivialanchor}: $\nu_\ast = \lambda$ and the
		weights are constant, $w_i \equiv w = 1/f''(1) > 0$. Since $T^j|_\mathcal{X} \in S_\mathcal{X}$, the vector
		\[
		\zeta^j(r) - w\, h'(s)\, T^j|_\mathcal{X} \;=\; \bigl(w\, T^j_i\, (h'(r_i) - h'(s))\bigr)_{1 \le i \le n}
		\]
		belongs to $S_\mathcal{X}$ and vanishes on $P$, where $r_i = s$; it is therefore zero. At the index $\ell$
		this gives
		\[
		w\, T^j_\ell\, \bigl(h'(u) - h'(s)\bigr) = 0\,,
		\]
		and $T^j_\ell \neq 0$ gives $h'(u) = h'(s)$. As $s, u > 0$ were arbitrary, $h'$ is constant on
		$\mathbb{R}^*_+$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:const} concludes: up to equivalence, $D_f = \mathrm{KL}$.
