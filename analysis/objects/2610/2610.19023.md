\label{thm:scalar-expectation}
Let $\Gamma$ be a countable discrete group and let
$\sigma$ be a pointwise weakly mixing trace-preserving action of $\Gamma$ on a tracial von Neumann algebra $(Q,\tau_Q)$. If

\label{thm:main}
Let $\Gamma$ be a countable discrete group and let
$\sigma$ be a trace-preserving action of $\Gamma$ on a tracial von Neumann
algebra $(Q,\tau_Q)$ such
that
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] the action is pointwise weakly mixing relative to every proper $\Gamma$-factor of $(Q,\tau_Q)$, including
the trivial factor;
\item[\textup{(b)}] for every non-trivial $\Gamma$-factor $B$ of $(Q,\tau_Q)$,
the inclusion $B\subseteq Q$ is centrally $\Gamma$-free.
\end{enumerate}
Let ${\mathcal P}\subseteq Q\rtimes\Gamma$ be a $\Gamma$-invariant von
Neumann subalgebra. Then
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] if ${\mathcal P}\cap Q\neq{\mathbb C}1$, then
${\mathcal P}=({\mathcal P}\cap Q)\rtimes N$ for some $N\trianglelefteq\Gamma$;
\item[\textup{(ii)}] if $\mathbb{E}({\mathcal P})={\mathbb C}1$, then
${\mathcal P}\subseteq L(\Gamma)$; if moreover $\Gamma$ has the ISR property,
then ${\mathcal P}=L(N)$ for some $N\trianglelefteq\Gamma$.
\end{enumerate}

A $\Gamma$-invariant inclusion $B\subseteq Q$ is \emph{centrally

\label{lem:central-free-expectation}
Suppose that $\sigma$ is a trace-preserving  action  of a countable group $\Gamma$ on a tracial von Neumann algebra $Q$. Let $B$ be a $\Gamma$-invariant subalgebra of $Q$ such that  $B\subseteq Q$ is centrally $\Gamma$-free. Then
$\mathbb{E}(x)\in W^*(B,x)$ for every $x\in Q\rtimes_\sigma\Gamma$,
and $B'\cap(B\rtimes_\sigma\Gamma)=Z(B)$. Here, $W^*(B, x)$ denotes the von Neumann subalgebra of $Q\rtimes_{\sigma}\Gamma$ generated by $B$ and $x$.

For the first assertion, we follow the argument in
\cite[Theorem~4.6]{Suz}.

$x=\sum_{t\in\Gamma}x_tu_t$, where
$x_t=\mathbb{E}(xu_t^*)\in Q$. Fix $s \in \Gamma, s\ne e$, and choose projections
$p_j\in B^\omega\cap Q'\subseteq D^\omega$ as in Definition~

These projections are pairwise orthogonal. 
For $y\in M^\omega$, define $\Phi(y)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty p_jyp_j$.
The partial sums have norm at most $\|y\|$, and
\[
\sum_j\|p_jyp_j\|_2^2\le\sum_j\|p_jy\|_2^2=\|y\|_2^2.

Since the summands are orthogonal in $L^2(M^\omega)$, the partial sums
are $\|\cdot\|_2$-Cauchy. Therefore $\Phi(y)\in M^\omega$, with
$\|\Phi(y)\|\le\|y\|$ and $\|\Phi(y)\|_2\le\|y\|_2$.
Let $\Psi=\mathbb{E}_M\circ\Phi$, with $\mathbb{E}_M$ as in fact~(2) above.
Then $\Psi$ is contractive in both the operator norm and the $L^2$-norm. For $y\in D$, the partial
sums defining $\Phi(y)$ belong to $D^\omega$ and have norm at most
$\|y\|$, so their $\|\cdot\|_2$-limit also belongs to $D^\omega$.
Since $\mathbb{E}_M(D^\omega)=D$, we get $\Psi(D)\subseteq D$.

We shall now compute the Fourier coefficients of $\Psi(y)$ for $y=\sum_{t\in \Gamma}y_tu_t\in M$.
For $t\in\Gamma$, fact~(3) gives $u_tp_j=\sigma_t^\omega(p_j)u_t$, so
$\Phi(y_tu_t)=c_tu_t$, where
$c_t=\sum_{j=1}^\infty p_jy_t\sigma_t^\omega(p_j)$.
The partial sums defining $c_t$ belong to $Q^\omega$, have norm at most
$\|y_t\|$, and converge in $\|\cdot\|_2$. Thus $c_t\in Q^\omega$.
Bimodularity gives $\Psi(y_tu_t)=\mathbb{E}_M(c_t)u_t$, where
$\mathbb{E}_M(c_t)\in Q$. By linearity and $\|\cdot\|_2$-contractivity,
$\Psi(y)=\sum_{t\in \Gamma}\mathbb{E}_M(c_t)u_t$ in $L^2(M)$.
Since the projections $p_j$ are pairwise orthogonal,
\[
\|\mathbb{E}_M(c_t)\|_2^2\le\|c_t\|_2^2
=\sum_{j=1}^\infty\|p_jy_t\sigma_t^\omega(p_j)\|_2^2
\le\sum_{j=1}^\infty\|p_jy_t\|_2^2=\|y_t\|_2^2.
\]
Since $p_j$ commutes with $y_t\in Q$, we have $c_e=y_e$ and
$c_s=y_s\sum_{j=1}^\infty p_j\sigma_s^\omega(p_j)=0$.
Thus $\Psi$ maps $D$ into $D$, fixes the Fourier coefficient at $e$,
annihilates the coefficient at $s$, and does not increase the
$\|\cdot\|_2$-norm of any Fourier coefficient.

$\Psi_i$ be the map constructed above for $s=s_i$. Set $x^{(0)}=x$ and
$x^{(i)}=\Psi_i(x^{(i-1)})$ for $1\le i\le k$. Then $x^{(k)}\in D$ and
$\|x^{(k)}\|\le\|x\|$. Its Fourier coefficient at $e$ is $x_e$, its
coefficients at the elements of $F$ vanish, and its coefficient at any
$t$ has $\|\cdot\|_2$-norm at most $\|x_t\|_2$. Hence
\[
\|x^{(k)}-\mathbb{E}(x)\|_2^2\le\sum_{t\notin F\cup\{e\}}\|x_t\|_2^2.
\]
Taking an increasing sequence of finite sets $F$ exhausting
$\Gamma\setminus\{e\}$, we obtain a sequence in $D$, bounded in norm by
$\|x\|$, which converges to $\mathbb{E}(x)$ in $\|\cdot\|_2$.
Since the ball of radius $\|x\|$ in $D$ is $\|\cdot\|_2$-closed in $M$,
we conclude that $\mathbb{E}(x)\in D$.

\label{lem:boundary-covariance}
Write $(g\cdot f)(x)=f(g^{-1}x)$ for $g\in\Gamma$, $f\in C(Z)$ and
$x\in Z$. Then
$v_sm_fv_s^*=m_{s\cdot f}$ and $u_tm_fu_t^*=m_{t\cdot f}$ for $s,t\in\Gamma$,
equivalently $u_t^*m_f=m_{t^{-1}\cdot f}u_t^*$ with $(t^{-1}\cdot f)(x)=f(tx)$. Moreover,
$C(Z)$ commutes with $\mathbb{C}1\bar{\otimes}Q$ inside $\mathbb B(L^2(\widetilde{M}))$.

For the first relation we have
\begin{align*}
v_s m_f v_s^*(\delta_r\otimes\eta\otimes\delta_h)
&=v_s m_f(\delta_r\otimes\eta\otimes\delta_{s^{-1}h})\\
&=f(s^{-1}hrx_0)\delta_r\otimes\eta\otimes\delta_h\\
&=(s\cdot f)(hrx_0)\delta_r\otimes\eta\otimes\delta_h
=m_{s\cdot f}(\delta_r\otimes\eta\otimes\delta_h),
\end{align*}
and for the second,
\begin{align*}
u_t m_f u_t^*(\delta_r\otimes\eta\otimes\delta_h)
&=u_t m_f(\delta_{h^{-1}t^{-1}hr}\otimes\eta\otimes\delta_h)\\

\label{def: relative weak mixing}
Let $\alpha$ be a trace-preserving automorphism of $(Q,\tau_Q)$ and let
$B\subseteq Q$ be an $\alpha$-invariant von Neumann subalgebra.
The automorphism $\alpha$ is \emph{weakly mixing relative to $B$} if
$\frac1m\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\lVert\mathbb{E}_B(\zeta^*\alpha^n(\xi))\rVert_2^2\to0$ as $m\to\infty$
for all $\xi,\zeta\in Q$ with
$\mathbb{E}_B(\xi)=\mathbb{E}_B(\zeta)=0$.
For $B={\mathbb C}1$, this is \emph{weak mixing}.
A trace-preserving action $\sigma$ of $\Gamma$ on $(Q,\tau_Q)$ is
\emph{pointwise weakly mixing relative to $B$} if $B$ is
$\Gamma$-invariant and $\sigma_g$ is weakly mixing relative to $B$
for every $g\ne e$. For $B={\mathbb C}1$, we say that $\sigma$ is
\emph{pointwise weakly mixing}.

\label{remark: relative weak mixing for diagonal action wrt a coordinate factor}
    For an ergodic $T \in {\rm Aut}(X,\nu)$, relative weak mixing is equivalent to ergodicity of the diagonal automorphism $T\times T\in {\rm Aut}(X\times_YX,\nu\times_{\mu}\nu)$, where $X\times_YX=\{(x_1,x_2)\in X\times X\mid\pi(x_1)=\pi(x_2)\}$ denotes the fibred product space and $\pi$ denotes the factor map from $(X,\nu)$ to $(Y,\mu)$ (see e.g.\ \cite{Furman_imrn}). For a reference, see, e.g.,\ \cite[Proposition~7.30 in Chapter~7]{EMW_book} or \cite[Theorem~7.5]{Furstenberg_1977}. In particular, a diagonal automorphism of two weakly mixing automorphisms, say $T\times S\in{\rm Aut}(X\times Z, \nu\times \mu)$, is weakly mixing relative to $L^{\infty}(X, \nu)\bar{\otimes}\mathbb{C}1_{L^{\infty}(Z,\mu)}$. Indeed, this follows since the automorphism $ (T\times S)\times (T\times S)\in {\rm Aut}((X\times Z)\times_X(X\times Z), (\nu\times \mu)\times_{\nu}(\nu\times \mu))$ is conjugate to  $T\times S\times S\in{\rm Aut}(X\times Z\times Z, \nu\times \mu\times \mu)$, which is ergodic. For further discussion, see \cite{BergelsonGorodnik}.

\label{lem:relative-pairwise-wm-selection}
Let $\alpha$ be a trace-preserving automorphism of $(Q,\tau_Q)$ which
is weakly mixing relative to an $\alpha$-invariant von Neumann subalgebra
$B\subseteq Q$, and let $\Xi\Subset Q$ be finite. Then there exists a strictly increasing sequence
$(m_k)_{k\ge1}\subseteq{\mathbb N}$ such that
$\|\mathbb{E}_B(\eta^*\alpha^{m_\ell-m_k}(\xi))\|_2\to0$ as
$\max\{k,\ell\}\to\infty$ with $k\neq\ell$, for all
$\xi,\eta\in\Xi$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}_B(\xi)=\mathbb{E}_B(\eta)=0$.
<!-- end lem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $\Xi_0=\{\xi\in\Xi\mid\mathbb{E}_B\xi=0\}$. If $\Xi_0$ is empty, any
strictly increasing sequence works. Otherwise, for $\xi,\eta\in\Xi_0$, set
$q_{\xi,\eta}(n)=\|\mathbb{E}_B(\eta^*\alpha^n(\xi))\|_2$.
For each positive integer $r$, relative weak mixing implies that
$\{n\ge1\mid q_{\xi,\eta}(n)\ge1/r\}$ has density zero, since its proportion
in $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ is bounded by
$r^2N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^Nq_{\xi,\eta}(n)^2$.
Equivariance, preservation of adjoints and invariance of the $L^2$-norm give
$q_{\xi,\eta}(-n)=q_{\eta,\xi}(n)$. Hence
$S_r=\{n\ge1\mid\max_{\xi,\eta\in\Xi_0}\max_{\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}} q_{\xi,\eta}(\varepsilon n)<1/r\}$
has density one. Choose $m_1\ge1$ and, recursively, choose
$m_k>m_{k-1}$ in $\bigcap_{j<k}(m_j+S_k)$; this finite intersection has
density one. If $\ell>k$, then $m_\ell-m_k\in S_\ell$; if $k>\ell$,
use the negative-time inequality from $S_k$. The required norm is thus
less than $1/\max(k,\ell)$ for every unequal pair of indices.

\label{lem:weak-zero-from-pairwise}
Let $\widehat{\mathcal H}$ be a Hilbert space and let $(\eta_k)_{k\in{\mathbb N}}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal H}$ be a bounded sequence. Assume that $\langle\eta_k,\eta_\ell\rangle\to0$ as
$\max\{k,\ell\}\to\infty$, $k\neq\ell$. Then $\eta_k\to0$ weakly as $k$ tends to infinity.
<!-- end lem-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
As closed balls in $\widehat{\mathcal H}$ are weakly sequentially compact, it suffices to show that every weak cluster point of $(\eta_k)_{k \in {\mathbb N}}$ equals $0$. Let $\eta\in\widehat{\mathcal H}$ and suppose that $\eta_{k_\ell}\to\eta$
weakly as $\ell$ tends to infinity. Fix $j\in {\mathbb N}$. Since $\langle\eta_{k_j},\eta_{k_\ell}\rangle\to0$ as $\ell\to\infty$, we see that $\langle\eta_{k_j},\eta\rangle=0$. Letting $j\to\infty$ and
using again the assumed weak convergence gives $\|\eta\|^2=0$. Thus $\eta=0$.

A group $\Gamma$ satisfies the ECI property if for every
$e\neq g\in \Gamma$ and every pair of
finite sets $F\Subset \Gamma\setminus C_\Gamma(g)$ and $R \Subset \Gamma$ there exists $N \in {\mathbb N}$ such that

<!-- lem-6: body_md -->
\label{lemma: unique roots -> ECI}
Let $\Gamma$ be a countable ICC group. Then it satisfies the ECI property if and only if it satisfies    the unique  roots property.
<!-- end lem-6 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We may assume that $\Gamma$ is non-trivial.

Assume that $\Gamma$  satisfies    the unique roots property.    Fix $g\in \Gamma\setminus \{e\}$ and let $f\in \Gamma\setminus C_\Gamma(g)$. Assume that there exist $n,m\in\mathbb N$, $n<m$, such that $g^nfg^{-n}=g^mfg^{-m}$. Then we have
    $(f^{-1}gf)^{m-n}=f^{-1}g^{m-n}f=g^{m-n}$. The unique roots property implies that $f^{-1}gf=g$, which contradicts the fact that $f$ does not belong to the centralizer of $g$. Thus, the elements $g^nfg^{-n}$, $n\in\mathbb N$, are pairwise distinct.

   Now let $F\Subset \Gamma\setminus C_\Gamma(g)$ and $R \Subset \Gamma$ be finite sets.
For each $f\in F$ and $r\in R$, there is at most one $n\in{\mathbb N}$ such that $g^nfg^{-n}=r$.
Thus there are only finitely many such integers. Choose $N\geq1$ larger than all of them,
taking $N=1$ if there are none. Then $g^nFg^{-n}\cap R=\emptyset$ for every $n>N$, proving ECI.

     Assume then that $\Gamma$ has the ECI property and does not have the unique  roots property. Then there exist $g\neq h\in \Gamma$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ such that $g^n=h^n$. Assume first $g,h\neq e$. If $h\notin C_\Gamma(g)$, set $F=\{g^ihg^{-i}\mid i=0,\ldots,n-1\}$. Clearly, $F\subseteq \Gamma\setminus C_\Gamma(g)$, but $gFg^{-1}=F$, which contradicts the ECI property. Let then $h\in C_\Gamma(g)$. Then $(h^{-1}g)^n=e$. Thus, we arrive at the case $g^n=e$ for some $g\neq e$. As $\Gamma$ is assumed to be ICC, $g$ cannot belong to the centre of $\Gamma$. Take $h$ not commuting with $g$ and set $F$ as above. We then again obtain a contradiction to the ECI property, which finishes the proof.

<!-- lem-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:commuting-square}
Let $(M,\tau)$ be a tracial von Neumann algebra, let $A,{\mathcal P}\subseteq M$ be von Neumann
subalgebras, and let $B={\mathcal P}\cap A$. The following conditions are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] $\mathbb{E}_A({\mathcal P})\subseteq{\mathcal P}$;
\item[\textup{(ii)}] $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(A)\subseteq A$;
\item[\textup{(iii)}] $\mathbb{E}_A\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}\mathbb{E}_A$.
\end{enumerate}
In this case $\mathbb{E}_A\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}\mathbb{E}_A=\mathbb{E}_B$. In particular,
$\mathbb{E}_A({\mathcal P})=B=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(A)$ and $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(a)=\mathbb{E}_B(a)$ for every $a\in A$,
and the diagram
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
M \arrow[r,"\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}"] \arrow[d,"\mathbb{E}_A"'] & {\mathcal P} \arrow[d,"\mathbb{E}_A"]\\
A \arrow[r,"\mathbb{E}_B"'] & B={\mathcal P}\cap A
\end{tikzcd}
\]
commutes in the sense that $\mathbb{E}_A\circ\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}=\mathbb{E}_B\circ\mathbb{E}_A$.

\label{lem:groupoid-to-normal}
Let $\sigma$ be a trace-preserving action of a countable group $\Gamma$ on a tracial
von Neumann algebra $(B,\tau_B)$. Suppose that $B'\cap(B\rtimes\Gamma)=Z(B)$ and that
$\sigma_s$ is ergodic for every $s\neq e$. If $B\subseteq{\mathcal P}\subseteq B\rtimes\Gamma$
is a $\Gamma$-invariant intermediate von Neumann algebra, then ${\mathcal P}=B\rtimes N$ for the
normal subgroup $N=\{s\in\Gamma\mid u_s\in{\mathcal P}\}$.
<!-- end lem-8 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Recall that the canonical conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}$ is $\Gamma$-equivariant, that is,
$\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_gxu_g^*)=u_g\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(x)u_g^*$ for all $g\in\Gamma$ and $x\in B\rtimes\Gamma$.

Fix $e\neq s\in \Gamma$ and let $v_s=u_s^*\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)$. We claim that
$v_s\in{\mathcal P}'\cap(B\rtimes \Gamma)$. Indeed, for $m\in{\mathcal P}$ let
$\tilde{m}=u_s^*mu_s\in{\mathcal P}$. Using ${\mathcal P}$-bimodularity of $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}$ twice, we obtain
\[
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)\tilde{m}=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s\tilde{m})=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(mu_s)=m\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s),
\]
whence $v_s\tilde{m}=u_s^*m\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)=\tilde{m}v_s$. As $m\mapsto \tilde{m}$ is a bijection of ${\mathcal P}$, the
claim follows. Since $B\subseteq{\mathcal P}$, our assumption gives
$v_s\in{\mathcal P}'\cap(B\rtimes\Gamma)\subseteq B'\cap(B\rtimes\Gamma)=Z(B)$.

                                      
By equivariance of $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}$ we have
$\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)=u_s\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)u_s^*$, that is,
$u_s^*\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)u_s^*$, and therefore $\sigma_s(v_s)=u_sv_su_s^*=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)u_s^*=v_s$.
Since $\sigma_s$ is ergodic on $B$, we get $v_s=c_s1$ for some $c_s\in{\mathbb C}$, that is,
$\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)=c_su_s$. Applying $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}$ once more and using idempotence yields
$c_s^2=c_s$, so $c_s\in\{0,1\}$. Thus for every $s\in \Gamma$, either $u_s\in{\mathcal P}$ or
$\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)=0$.

Let $N=\{s\in \Gamma\mid u_s\in{\mathcal P}\}$. Then $N$ is a subgroup, and it is normal because
$u_gu_su_g^*=u_{gsg^{-1}}$ for all $s, g \in \Gamma$ and ${\mathcal P}$ is $\Gamma$-invariant. Clearly
$B\rtimes N\subseteq{\mathcal P}$. Conversely, let $x\in{\mathcal P}$ and $s\in \Gamma \setminus  N$. For every $b\in B$,
bimodularity gives $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(bu_s)=b\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(u_s)=0$, hence
$\langle x,bu_s\rangle=\langle x,\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal P}}(bu_s)\rangle=0$.
Thus the $s$-th Fourier coefficient of $x$ vanishes. Hence $x\in B\rtimes N$, and
${\mathcal P}=B\rtimes N$.

$\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\nu)$ be a p.m.p.~action on a nonatomic
probability space. Then $L^\infty(X,\nu)\rtimes\Gamma$ admits a $\Gamma$-invariant von Neumann subalgebra that contains
$L(\Gamma)$ and is not of the form $B\rtimes\Lambda$ in the canonical embedding.

Indeed, let $p \in Z(L(\Gamma))$ be a non-trivial projection. 
                                                         
                                                          
                                                                     
                                   
Set ${\mathcal P}=p(L^\infty(X,\nu)\rtimes\Gamma)p\oplus p^{\perp}L(\Gamma)$. Then                                     
                 
${\mathcal P}$ is a $\Gamma$-invariant von Neumann subalgebra which contains $L(\Gamma)$.                            
                                   

We claim that ${\mathcal P}\cap L^\infty(X,\nu)={\mathbb C}1$. If $a\in{\mathcal P}\cap L^\infty(X,\nu)$, then $a$
commutes with $p^{\perp}$ and $p^{\perp}ap^{\perp}$ belongs to $p^{\perp}L(\Gamma)$. Bimodularity of the
trace-preserving expectation onto $L(\Gamma)$ gives
$p^{\perp}ap^{\perp}=\mathbb{E}_{L(\Gamma)}(p^{\perp}ap^{\perp})=\nu(a)p^{\perp}$.
Thus, $(a-\nu(a)1)p^{\perp}=0$. Applying $\mathbb{E}$ to
$(a-\nu(a)1)p^{\perp}(a-\nu(a)1)^*$ gives
$\tau(p^{\perp})|a-\nu(a)1|^2=0$, proving the claim.

$p=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}p_g u_g$ and choose a measurable set $D$ with
$0<\nu(D)<\tau(p)$. Then
\[
 \mathbb{E}(p1_Dp)=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}|p_g|^2\sigma_g(1_D)
 \geq |p_e|^2\sigma_e(1_D)=\tau(p)^2 1_D.
\]

They converge to $p$ in $L^2(L^\infty(X,\nu)\rtimes\Gamma)$, so $p_F1_Dp_F^*$ converges to
$p1_Dp$ in $L^1(L^\infty(X,\nu)\rtimes\Gamma)$ by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality. Apply the $L^1$-contractive
expectation and note that the series on the right converges in
operator norm, since $\sum_{g\in \Gamma}|p_g|^2=\tau(p)$.
If this expectation were scalar, trace preservation would imply that it equals
$\tau(p)\nu(D)1$, contrary to the lower bound on $D$.

embedding would force $\Lambda=\Gamma$, since $L(\Gamma)\subseteq{\mathcal P}$,
and $B={\mathbb C}1$, since ${\mathcal P}\cap L^\infty(X,\nu)={\mathbb C}1$. This contradicts
$\mathbb{E}({\mathcal P})\ne{\mathbb C}1$.

$r_hr_{ha}=q_hq_{ha}^*\in B$.

${\mathcal P}=B\rtimes N$

$q_e=r_eu_a\in{\mathcal P}$
has Fourier support at $a$, we must have $a\in N$. Hence
$u_a\in B\rtimes N={\mathcal P}$.
Therefore $r_e=q_eu_a^*\in{\mathcal P}\capL^\infty(X,\nu)=B$.

${\mathcal P}=L(K)$,

$(\bigoplus_{\Gamma}\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)\rtimes\Gamma$ to $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$
by $\chi(\eta,g)=|\eta|+\operatorname{exp}_a(g)\pmod2$ for
$\eta\in\bigoplus_{\Gamma}\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ and $g\in\Gamma$. Here $|\eta|$ denotes the number of nonzero coordinates of $\eta$, and $\operatorname{exp}_a(g)$ is the $a$-exponent sum of $g$, namely the image of $g$ under the surjective homomorphism $\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}$ sending $a$ to $1$ and $b$ to $0$. For every generator $(\delta_h,c_h)$ of $K$, we have
$|\delta_h|=1$, and
$\operatorname{exp}_a(c_h)=1$,
and hence $\chi(\delta_h,c_h)=0$.
Thus,
$K\subseteq\ker\chi$. But
$\chi(\delta_e,e)=1$, so 
$(\delta_e,e)\notin K$.
Equivalently,
$r_e\notin B$.
This contradicts the conclusion above. Hence ${\mathcal P}\neq B\rtimes N$ for every normal subgroup $N\trianglelefteq\Gamma$.

$c_e=c_{a^n}=a$
for every $n\ge1$, we have $q_e q_{a^n}^*
=
r_e r_{a^n}\in B$.
Moreover, from the description of $B$ above,
$\mathbb{E}_B(r_e)=0$. But
$\sigma_{a^n}(r_e)=r_{a^n}$. Therefore,
$\mathbb{E}_B\bigl(r_e^*\sigma_{a^n}(r_e)\bigr) = \mathbb{E}_B(r_e r_{a^n}) = r_e r_{a^n}.$
These functions have $L^2$ norm $1$ for every $n\ge1$, so $\sigma_a$ is not weakly mixing relative to $B$.

\label{thm:gen}
Let $\Gamma$ be a countable discrete group with the ECI property, and let $\sigma$
be a trace-preserving action of $\Gamma$ on $(Q,\tau_Q)$. Let
${\mathcal P}\subseteq Q\rtimes\Gamma$ be a $\Gamma$-invariant von Neumann subalgebra, and let

<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
Equivariance of $\mathbb{E}$ implies that $B$ is $\Gamma$-invariant. Fix $x\in{\mathcal P}$
with $\|x\|_\infty\le1$ and write $f_g=\mathbb{E}(xu_g^*)$ for $g\in\Gamma$.
We have $f_e\in B$. Fix $t_0\neq e$ and $\epsilon>0$. Choose a finite set
$F\subseteq\Gamma$ containing $e$ and $t_0$ such that $\|x-x_F\|_2<\epsilon$,
where $x_F=\sum_{g\in F}f_gu_g$, and let $F_1=F\cap C_\Gamma(t_0)$.
By ECI, there is $n_0\in{\mathbb N}$ such that
$t_0^n(F\setminus F_1)t_0^{-n}\cap F=\varnothing$ for every $n\ge n_0$.
For such $n$, let $c_n=\mathbb{E}(u_{t_0}^nxu_{t_0}^{-n}x^*)$.
Since ${\mathcal P}$ is invariant, $c_n\in B$ and $\|c_n\|_\infty\le1$.
Multiplying the finite Fourier sums gives
\[
 \mathbb{E}(u_{t_0}^nx_Fu_{t_0}^{-n}x_F^*)
 =\sum_{g\in F_1}\sigma_{t_0}^n(f_g)f_g^*.
\]
By equation~\eqref{eq:basic}, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L1A}
 \left\|c_n-\sum_{g\in F_1}\sigma_{t_0}^n(f_g)f_g^*\right\|_1
 \le\|x-x_F\|_2\|x\|_2+\|x_F\|_2\|x-x_F\|_2<2\epsilon.
\end{equation}

Let $\xi\in Q$ satisfy $\mathbb{E}_B(\xi)=0$ and $\|\xi\|_\infty\le1$.
Apply the functional $a\mapsto\tau_Q(\xi^*\sigma_{t_0}^n(\xi)a)$, whose norm on
$L^1(Q,\tau_Q)$ is at most one, to equation~\eqref{eq:L1A}.
Since $c_n\in B$ and $\|c_n\|_2\le1$, we have
\[
 |\tau_Q(\xi^*\sigma_{t_0}^n(\xi)c_n)|
 =|\tau_Q(c_n\mathbb{E}_B(\xi^*\sigma_{t_0}^n(\xi)))|
 \le\|\mathbb{E}_B(\xi^*\sigma_{t_0}^n(\xi))\|_2.
\]
Relative weak mixing and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality show that the Ces\'aro averages of these
absolute values tend to zero. For each term in the finite sum, traciality gives
\[
 \tau_Q(\xi^*\sigma_{t_0}^n(\xi)\sigma_{t_0}^n(f_g)f_g^*)
 =\tau_Q((\xi f_g)^*\sigma_{t_0}^n(\xi f_g)).
\]
By the mean ergodic theorem, the Ces\'aro averages on the right converge to the
squared norm of the orthogonal projection of $\xi f_g$ onto the invariant vectors
in $L^2(Q,\tau_Q)$. This limit is nonnegative and at least
$|\tau_Q(\xi f_g)|^2$, since the invariant subspace contains the constants.
Averaging the scalar inequality obtained from equation~\eqref{eq:L1A} and passing
to the limit therefore gives
$\sum_{g\in F_1}|\tau_Q(\xi f_g)|^2\le2\epsilon$.
Since $t_0\in F_1$ and $\epsilon$ was arbitrary, $\tau_Q(\xi f_{t_0})=0$.
By scaling, this holds for every $\xi\in Q$ with $\mathbb{E}_B(\xi)=0$.
Taking $\xi=(f_{t_0}-\mathbb{E}_B(f_{t_0}))^*$ gives
$\|f_{t_0}-\mathbb{E}_B(f_{t_0})\|_2^2=0$, so $f_{t_0}\in B$.
As $t_0\neq e$ was arbitrary, every Fourier coefficient of $x$ belongs to $B$,
and hence $x\in B\rtimes\Gamma$.

\label{prop:direct-augmentation}
Let ${\mathbb F}_n$ be the nonabelian free group on $2\leq n\leq\infty$ generators,
and let $V=\mathbb Z{\mathbb F}_n$, regarded as an additive group.
There is a subgroup $H<V\times{\mathbb F}_n$ with the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $H$ is invariant under $(v,s)\mapsto(gv,gsg^{-1})$ for every $g\in{\mathbb F}_n$;
\item $\{0\}\neq H\cap V\neq V$;
\item $H\neq H_1\times H_2$ for all subgroups $H_1\leq V$ and $H_2\leq{\mathbb F}_n$;
\item in the tensor coordinates $L(V\times{\mathbb F}_n)\cong L({\mathbb F}_n)\bar\otimes L(V)$,
$L(H)\neq B_2\bar\otimes B_1$ for all von Neumann subalgebras
$B_1\subseteq L(V)=L^\infty(\mathbb T^{{\mathbb F}_n})$ and $B_2\subseteq L({\mathbb F}_n)$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
Let $\epsilon$ be the augmentation homomorphism from $V$ to $\mathbb Z$, given by
$\epsilon(\sum_s c_ss)=\sum_s c_s$, and let $I=\ker\epsilon$.
Choose a free generator $s_1$ and let $\phi$ be the homomorphism from ${\mathbb F}_n$ to $\mathbb Z$ that sends
$s_1$ to $1$ and every other free generator to $0$. Define the homomorphism $D$ from $V\times{\mathbb F}_n$ to $\mathbb Z$ by $D(v,s)=\epsilon(v)-\phi(s)$. Its kernel is $H=\{(\phi(s)e+x,s)\mid x\in I,s\in{\mathbb F}_n\}$,
where $e$ denotes the basis element of $V$ at the group identity.
Since $\epsilon(gv)=\epsilon(v)$ and
$\phi(gsg^{-1})=\phi(s)$, the kernel is invariant under the given
diagonal action. Its intersection with $V$ is $I$, which is nonzero
because $s_1-e\in I$ and is proper because $\epsilon(e)=1$.

The projection of $H$ to ${\mathbb F}_n$ is onto. Its projection to $V$ is also
onto, since for $v\in V$ we can choose $s$ with $\phi(s)=\epsilon(v)$.
But $(0,s_1)\notin H$. If $H=H_1\times H_2$, the two projections would
force $H_1=V$ and $H_2={\mathbb F}_n$, contradicting this last fact. This proves (3).

Suppose that $L(H)=B_2\bar\otimes B_1$. For each $(v,s)\in H$, the unitary
$\lambda_s\otimes\lambda_v$ belongs to $B_2\bar\otimes B_1$.
Applying the normal slice maps defined by
$z\mapsto\tau_{{\mathbb F}_n}(\lambda_s^*z)$ and
$z\mapsto\tau_V(\lambda_v^*z)$ gives $\lambda_v\in B_1$ and $\lambda_s\in B_2$.
Surjectivity of the two group projections therefore implies
$B_1=L(V)$ and $B_2=L({\mathbb F}_n)$. It follows that
$\lambda_{(0,s_1)}\in L(H)$, which is impossible because the Fourier expansion of every
element of $L(H)$ is supported in $H$. This proves (4).

<!-- lem-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:disjointization}
Let $P_1,\ldots,P_k\in{\rm Aut}(X,\nu)$, and let $D\subseteq X$ be a Borel set
such that $P_1(x),\ldots,P_k(x)$ are pairwise distinct for every $x\in D$.
If $0\leq a<\nu(D)$, then there are pairwise disjoint Borel sets
$A_1,\ldots,A_M\subseteq D$ such that
$\nu(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_M)>a$ and, for every $r=1, \ldots,M$, the sets
$P_1(A_r),\ldots,P_k(A_r)$ are pairwise disjoint.
<!-- end lem-11 -->
<!-- proof-22: body_md -->
Because $X$ is a standard Borel space, there is a countable Boolean algebra
$\mathcal A$ of Borel sets which separates points. For example, after
identifying $X$ with a Borel subset of $[0,1]$, one may pull back the algebra
of finite unions of intervals with rational endpoints.

Fix $x\in D$. Since $P_1(x),\ldots,P_k(x)$ are distinct, for every pair of
these points choose a member of $\mathcal A$ which contains one and not the
other. The finitely many chosen sets generate a finite Boolean algebra, and
the points $P_1(x),\ldots,P_k(x)$ lie in distinct atoms of that finite
algebra.                                                                
               
Hence there exist pairwise disjoint sets
$V_1,\ldots,V_k\in\mathcal A$ with $P_i(x)\in V_i$ for every $i=1, \ldots,k$.

For every pairwise disjoint $k$-tuple
$\mathbf V=(V_1,\ldots,V_k)$ from $\mathcal A$, let
 $U_{\mathbf V}=D\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^k P_i^{-1}(V_i)$.
There are only countably many such tuples, these sets cover $D$, and
$P_i(U_{\mathbf V})\subseteq V_i$; therefore the images of each
$U_{\mathbf V}$ are pairwise disjoint Borel sets. Enumerate the nonempty sets in this
cover as $U_1,U_2,\ldots$ and let $B_1=U_1$ and
$B_r=U_r\setminus\bigcup_{j<r}U_j$ for $r\geq2$.
Then $D=\bigsqcup_{r\geq1}B_r$, and the images
$P_1(B_r),\ldots,P_k(B_r)$ are pairwise disjoint for every $r$. Since
$\nu(\bigcup_{r=1}^N B_r)\to\nu(D)>a$, a sufficiently large finite initial
union has measure greater than $a$. Discarding its null members gives the
required sets.

<!-- lem-12: body_md -->
\label{lem:local-modification}
Let $R\in{\rm Aut}(X,\nu)$, let $D\subseteq X$ be Borel, and let
$\mathcal C$ be a finite family of Borel sets. There is an involution
$P\in{\rm Aut}(X,\nu)$ such that
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $\text{supp}(P)=D$ modulo null sets;
\item $P$ preserves every member of the finite Boolean algebra generated by
\[
 \{D\}\cup\mathcal C
 \cup\{R(C)\mid C\in\mathcal C\}
 \cup\{R^{-1}(C)\mid C\in\mathcal C\}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, for both $R_+=RP$ and $R_-=PR$ one has
 $R_\pm(C)=R(C)$,
 $R_\pm^{-1}(C)=R^{-1}(C)$ for every $C\in\mathcal C$.
<!-- end lem-12 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
The displayed family is finite, hence it generates a finite Boolean algebra.
Atoms of this algebra arise as intersections obtained by choosing, for each generator, either the set itself or its complement. Since $D$ is one of the generators, every atom
is either contained in $D$ or disjoint from $D$.

Let $E$ be a positive-measure atom contained in $D$. The restriction of
$\nu$ to $E$ is nonatomic, so we can choose a Borel partition
$E=E^0\sqcup E^1$ with $\nu(E^0)=\nu(E^1)$. The two standard nonatomic
finite measure spaces $E^0$ and $E^1$ are measure-isomorphic; this follows,
for example, from the standard isomorphism theorem recalled in
\cite[Section~1(A)]{kechris2010global}. Choose a measure-preserving Borel
isomorphism $\psi_E$ from $E^0$ to $E^1$ modulo null sets. Define $P$ to be
$\psi_E$ on each $E^0$, $\psi_E^{-1}$ on each $E^1$, and the identity outside the
union of the positive-measure atoms contained in $D$. Then $P$ is a
measure-preserving involution which has no fixed point on $D$ modulo null
sets.                                                                 
                                                                        
                                         
Furthermore, it preserves every atom, hence
every member, of the finite Boolean algebra in question. In particular, for
$C\in\mathcal C$, we have
$ P(C)=C$, $P(R(C))=R(C)$, $P(R^{-1}(C))=R^{-1}(C)$.
Hence $R_+(C)=R(P(C))=R(C)$ and
$R_+^{-1}(C)=P(R^{-1}(C))=R^{-1}(C)$, and likewise
$R_-(C)=P(R(C))=R(C)$ and $R_-^{-1}(C)=R^{-1}(P(C))=R^{-1}(C)$.
The claim follows.

\label{lem: non-prime Z-actions with all factor maps controlled}
For every integer $k\geq1$, there are mixing prime transformations
$T_i\in{\rm Aut}(X_i,\nu_i)$ on standard nonatomic probability spaces,
$1\leq i\leq k$, such that every factor algebra of
$T_1\times\cdots\times T_k$ is a coordinate algebra
$\mathcal A_I=\pi_I^{-1}\bigl(\bigotimes_{i\in I}\mathcal B_i\bigr)$
for some $I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}$, where $\mathcal B_i$ is the measure

<!-- proof-28: body_md -->
Choose distinct positive integers $r_1<\cdots<r_k$, let
$(X_i,\nu_i)=(X_0,\nu_0)$ and $T_i=T^{r_i}$, and write
$R=T_1\times\cdots\times T_k$ and
$(X,\nu)=(X_0^k,\nu_0^{\otimes k})$. Each $T_i$ is mixing, and so is $R$.
Indeed, mixing of a finite product follows first for measurable rectangles
and then for arbitrary measurable sets by approximation.

Let $\mathcal A$ be an $R$-invariant factor algebra and let
$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}$ be the corresponding conditional expectation, acting on $L^2(X,\nu)$.
We consider the \emph{relatively independent self-joining} $\lambda$ over
$\mathcal A$, which is a measure on $X^2=X_0^{2k}$. Both marginals of $\lambda$ are $\nu$, and for
$f,h\in L^2(X,\nu)$ we have
\[
 \int_{X\times X}f(x)\overline{h(y)}d\lambda(x,y)
 =\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}f,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}h\rangle
 =\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}f,h\rangle.
\]
Since $\mathcal A$ is $R$-invariant, we have
$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}(f\circ R)=(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}f)\circ R$
for every $f\in L^2(X,\nu)$. It follows that
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \int_{X\times X}f(Rx)\overline{h(Ry)}d\lambda(x,y)
 &=\int_X\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}(f\circ R)(x)
       \overline{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}(h\circ R)(x)}d\nu(x)\\
 &=\int_X(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}f)(Rx)
       \overline{(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}h)(Rx)}d\nu(x)\\
 &=\int_X(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}f)(x)
       \overline{(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}h)(x)}d\nu(x)\\
 &=\int_{X\times X}f(x)\overline{h(y)}d\lambda(x,y).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Here the third equality uses that $R$ preserves $\nu$.
Thus, $\lambda$ is $R\times R$-invariant. Let
$\lambda=\int\lambda_\alpha d\eta(\alpha)$ be its ergodic decomposition.

Almost every component $\lambda_\alpha$ has marginal $\nu_0$ on each
of the $2k$ coordinates. Indeed, let $\pi_i$ be the projection onto the
$i$-th coordinate of either copy of $X$. Since
$\pi_i\circ(R\times R)=T^{r_i}\circ\pi_i$, we have
\[
 (T^{r_i})_*(\pi_i)_*\lambda_\alpha
 =(\pi_i)_*(R\times R)_*\lambda_\alpha
 =(\pi_i)_*\lambda_\alpha.
\]
Thus, $(\pi_i)_*\lambda_\alpha$ is $T^{r_i}$-invariant, and
$\nu_0=\int(\pi_i)_*\lambda_\alpha d\eta(\alpha)$.
Since $T^{r_i}$ is ergodic, $\nu_0$ is an extreme point of the convex
set of $T^{r_i}$-invariant probability measures. Hence
$(\pi_i)_*\lambda_\alpha=\nu_0$ for almost every $\alpha$.

We now apply the minimal power joining property to the $2k$ coordinates
of $\lambda_\alpha$ for almost every $\alpha$. The exponents are $r_1,\ldots,r_k,r_1,\ldots,r_k$, and
each one-dimensional marginal is $\nu_0$. Since the $r_i$ are distinct,
every block is either a singleton or the pair $\{i,k+i\}$ for some $i$.
Identifying $X\times X$ with $\prod_{i=1}^k(X_i\times X_i)$ by pairing
the corresponding coordinates, we obtain
$\lambda_\alpha=\bigotimes_{i=1}^k\theta_{i,\alpha}$, where
$\theta_{i,\alpha}$ is a measure on $X_i\times X_i$.
If the two coordinates form singleton blocks, then
$\theta_{i,\alpha}=\nu_0\otimes\nu_0$. Otherwise $\theta_{i,\alpha} =
(T^a,T^b)_*\nu_0=(\mathrm{id},T^{b-a})_*\nu_0$ for some $a,b\in{\mathbb Z}$.
Thus, each $\theta_{i,\alpha}$ is either product measure or a graph joining
$(\mathrm{id},T^m)_*\nu_0$ with $m\in{\mathbb Z}$. In particular, both marginals of
$\lambda_\alpha$ are $\nu$.

To use this description, we associate an operator with each joining.
If $\theta$ is a probability measure on $X\times X$ with both marginals
$\nu$, then the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
$\left|\int_{X\times X}f(x)\overline{h(y)}d\theta(x,y)\right|
\leq\|f\|_2\|h\|_2$ for $f,h\in L^2(X,\nu)$. Hence there is a unique contraction $K_\theta$
on $L^2(X,\nu)$ satisfying
$\langle K_\theta f,h\rangle=\int f(x)\overline{h(y)}d\theta(x,y)$.
In particular, $K_\lambda=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}$. 


We can also `localise' this construction to measures on $X_0 \times X_0$ with both marginals equal to $\nu_0$.
Let $U_Tf=f\circ T^{-1}$ and
$\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb C}1}f=(\int f d\nu_0)1$ on $L^2(X_0,\nu_0)$.
The product measure gives the operator $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb C}1}$, since its matrix
coefficient is $(\int f d\nu_0)\overline{(\int h d\nu_0)}$.
The graph joining $(\mathrm{id},T^m)_*\nu_0$ gives $U_T^m$, since
\[
 \int f(z)\overline{h(T^mz)}d\nu_0(z)
 =\langle f,h\circ T^m\rangle
 =\langle U_T^mf,h\rangle.
\]
For elementary tensors $f=f_1\otimes\cdots\otimes f_k$ and
$h=h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_k$, the product description of
$\lambda_\alpha$ therefore gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \langle K_{\lambda_\alpha}f,h\rangle
 &=\prod_{i=1}^k\int f_i(x_i)\overline{h_i(y_i)}d\theta_{i,\alpha}(x_i,y_i)\\
 &=\prod_{i=1}^k\langle K_{i,\alpha}f_i,h_i\rangle,
 \end{aligned}
\]
where $K_{i,\alpha}\in\{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb C}1}\}\cup\{U_T^m\mid m\in{\mathbb Z}\}$.
Since the linear span of elementary tensors is dense, this proves
$K_{\lambda_\alpha}=K_{1,\alpha}\otimes\cdots\otimes K_{k,\alpha}$.

The ergodic decomposition gives
$\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}f,h\rangle
=\int\langle K_{\lambda_\alpha}f,h\rangle d\eta(\alpha)$
for every $f,h\in L^2(X,\nu)$. There are only countably many possible
operators $K_{\lambda_\alpha}$. For each such operator $K$, the set
$\{\alpha\mid K_{\lambda_\alpha}=K\}$ is measurable. Indeed, the matrix
coefficients are measurable, and equality of bounded operators on the
separable space $L^2(X,\nu)$ can be tested on a countable dense subset.
Let $p_K$ be the $\eta$-measure of this set. Then $p_K\geq0$,
$\sum_Kp_K=1$ and $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}=\sum_Kp_KK$.
The series converges in operator norm because
$\sum_Kp_K\|K\|\leq1$.

If $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}f=f$, contractivity of the operators $K$ gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \sum_Kp_K\|Kf-f\|_2^2
 &\leq\sum_Kp_K\bigl(2\|f\|_2^2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle Kf,f\rangle\bigr)\\
 &=2\|f\|_2^2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A}f,f\rangle
 =0.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus, $Kf=f$ whenever $p_K>0$. Conversely, a vector fixed by every such
$K$ is fixed by their convex combination. Consequently,
$L^2(\mathcal A)=\bigcap_{p_K>0}\operatorname{Fix}(K)$.

It remains to identify the fixed space of each $K$. Write
$K=K_1\otimes\cdots\otimes K_k$, and let
$J_K=\{i\mid K_i\neq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb C}1}\}$ and
$I_K=\{i\mid K_i=\mathrm{id}\}$. For $i\in J_K$, write $K_i=U_T^{m_i}$.
Here $m_i=0$ exactly when $i\in I_K$, since $T$ is mixing on a nonatomic
space. Let $V_K$ be the Koopman operator of the transformation which
applies $T^{m_i}$ in coordinate $i\in J_K$ and fixes the other coordinates.
Then $K=V_K\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A_{J_K}}$. Indeed, with the coordinates in
their original order,
\[
 \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A_{J_K}}(f_1\otimes\cdots\otimes f_k)(x)
 =\left(\prod_{i\notin J_K}\int f_i d\nu_0\right)
   \prod_{i\in J_K}f_i(x_i).
\]
Applying $V_K$ replaces $f_i(x_i)$ by $f_i(T^{-m_i}x_i)$ for $i\in J_K$,
which gives exactly $K(f_1\otimes\cdots\otimes f_k)$. The identity follows
by density.

Suppose $Kf=f$. Since $V_K$ is unitary,
$\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A_{J_K}}f\|_2=\|Kf\|_2=\|f\|_2$.
The operator $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A_{J_K}}$ is an orthogonal projection,
so $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A_{J_K}}f=f$. Thus, $f$ depends only on the
coordinates in $J_K$ and is fixed by $V_K$.
By Fubini, for almost every value of the coordinates in $I_K$, the
corresponding section of $f$ is invariant under
$\prod_{i\in J_K\setminus I_K}T^{m_i}$.
Each exponent in this product is nonzero, so each transformation is
mixing. Their finite product is therefore mixing and hence ergodic.
Thus, each such section is constant almost everywhere, which shows that
$f\in L^2(\mathcal A_{I_K})$.
If $J_K\setminus I_K$ is empty, this conclusion already follows from
$f\in L^2(\mathcal A_{J_K})$.
Conversely, $K$ fixes every function of the coordinates in $I_K$, since
each of its other tensor factors fixes the constant function $1$.
Therefore $\operatorname{Fix}(K)=L^2(\mathcal A_{I_K})$.

Let $I=\bigcap_{p_K>0}I_K$. For coordinate conditional expectations we
have $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A_J}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A_L}
=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal A_{J\cap L}}$.
This follows by testing on elementary tensors, since both sides retain
precisely the coordinates in $J\cap L$ and integrate the others.
                                                                   
                                                            
Only finitely many different subsets $I_K$ can occur, so
$L^2(\mathcal A)=\bigcap_{p_K>0}L^2(\mathcal A_{I_K})=L^2(\mathcal A_I)$.
Equality of these spaces implies equality of the factor algebras modulo
null sets. Indeed, the indicator of a set in either algebra is measurable
with respect to the other algebra up to a null set.
Thus, $\mathcal A=\mathcal A_I$. Conversely, every coordinate algebra is
$R$-invariant, so these are exactly its factor algebras.

Finally, applying the same argument with $k=1$ and $r_1=r$ shows that,
for every $r\geq1$, the only factors of $T^r$ are the trivial factor and
the whole space. Hence every $T^r$ is prime. Each $T_i$ is also mixing,
which proves the lemma.
