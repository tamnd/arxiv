<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{th:FPE_analysis}
	Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be a Lipschitz domain; let $\rho_0,\rho_\infty \in \mathcal{P}_2(\Omega)$ satisfy
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:hypotheses_FPE}
		\inf_{x\in \Omega} \rho_\infty(x) > 0,
		\quad
		{\rm and} \quad
		\nabla \rho_\infty \in {\rm Lip}(\Omega, \mathbb{R}^d).
	\end{equation}
	Then, the following hold.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item
		There exists a unique weak solution $\rho \in C(\mathbb{R}_+; \mathcal{P}_2(\Omega))$ to \eqref{eq:FPequation}.
		\item
		The solution to \eqref{eq:FPequation} converges to $\rho_\infty$ in $L^1(\Omega)$; that is,
		$\lim_{t\to \infty} \| \rho(\cdot, t) - \rho_\infty  \|_{L^1(\Omega)} = 0$.
		\item
		If   $X_1^0,\ldots,X_N^0 \overset{\rm iid}{\sim} \rho_0$, and $\Phi$ is defined as in \eqref{eq:FPflow}, we have that
		$\Phi(X_i^0,t) \overset{\rm iid}{\sim} \rho(\cdot, t)$ for all $t>0$.
	\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
In the absence of the driving term (that is, when \(S_\infty\) is constant in \(\Omega\)), Eq.~\eqref{eq:FPequation} reduces to the linear heat equation for the density $\rho$.
	The velocity $u$ then becomes the osmotic velocity associated with the diffusion process
	\[
	u = -\nabla S = -\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}.
	\]
	The flow map induced by such osmotic velocity transforms the initial density $\rho(x,0)$ into the density $\rho(x,t)$, which solves the heat equation.
	This observation is at the core of a particle method for the heat equation based on the direct reconstruction of the osmotic velocity from the particle distribution
	\cite{russo1990deterministic}.
	If, on the other hand, one includes the potential $S_\infty(x)$, then the flow map asymptotically transforms the initial density $\rho(x,0)$ into the target density $\rho_\infty$.
	This is consistent with the convergence result stated in Theorem \ref{th:FPE_analysis}.
	The connection between particle methods based on osmotic velocity and the diffusion equation has been studied in
	\cite{russo1990particle} and \cite{russo1990deterministic}.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The MLS method presented here suffers from ill-conditioning for higher-order approximations due to the use of a shifted monomial basis. The Local Anisotropic Basis Function Method (LABFM) provides a promising alternative \cite{king_high_2020,king_high-order_2022}. It employs better-conditioned basis functions and does not rely on the solution to a least-squares method to determine the coefficients. In our numerical experiments, LABFM performed well for basic test cases such as those in Section \ref{section:DiffusionTestCase}, but exhibited boundary instabilities in the test cases from Sections \ref{section:gaussianAcrossCylinder} and \ref{section:PSRAcrossCylinder}. We therefore use the quadratic MLS discretization described above throughout this work. Developing a robust boundary treatment for LABFM is left for future investigation.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
Reflective boundary conditions can alternatively be enforced by imposing zero normal velocity directly in the least-squares problem \cite{suchde_conservation_2018}. We tested this constrained formulation in preliminary numerical experiments; for the configurations considered here, however, the ghost-particle approach exhibited greater numerical robustness. We therefore employ the ghost-particle treatment throughout this work and do not investigate the constrained formulation further.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{theorem:velocity}
	Given \(\mathbf{x} \in \Omega\), suppose that the density \(p\) is of class \(C^3\) and \(p(\mathbf{x}) > 0\). Then, the mean-shift vector \(V(\mathbf{x},r)\)
	defined in \eqref{eq:scoreVelocity} satisfies
	\begin{align*}
		V(\mathbf{x}, r) = \frac{r^2}{4} \frac{\nabla p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x})} + \mathcal{O}(r^4),
	\end{align*} or equivalently, \begin{align}
		\frac{\nabla p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x})} = \frac{4}{r^2} V(\mathbf{x}, r) + \mathcal{O}(r^2). \label{eq:scoreEq}
	\end{align}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Making the change of variables \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x}\) gives \begin{align}
		m(\mathbf{x}, r) = \int_{\abs{\mathbf{z}} \leq r} p(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{z})d\mathbf{z}, \quad I(\mathbf{x}, r) = \int_{\abs{\mathbf{z}} \leq r} \mathbf{z} p(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{z}) d\mathbf{z}. \label{eq:numAndDenum} 
	\end{align} We perform a Taylor expansion of \(p\) at \(x\), \begin{align*}
		p(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{z}) = p(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla p(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{z} + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{z}^\top H_p(\mathbf{x}) \mathbf{z} + \mathcal{O}(\abs{\mathbf{z}}^3),
	\end{align*} where \( H_p(\mathbf{x})\) denotes the Hessian. Then, the integrands in Equation \eqref{eq:numAndDenum} can be expanded. For the mass \(m(\mathbf{x}, r)\), we find \begin{align}
		m(\mathbf{x}, r) &= \int_{\abs{\mathbf{z}} \leq r} \left[p(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla p(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{z} + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{z}^\top H_p(\mathbf{x}) \mathbf{z} + \mathcal{O}(\abs{\mathbf{z}}^3)\right] d\mathbf{z} = \pi r^2 p(\mathbf{x}) + \mathcal{O}(r^4), \label{eq:scoreMass}	\end{align} where the linear term vanishes due to symmetry. Expansion of the numerator yields \begin{align*}
		I(\mathbf{x}, r) = \int_{\abs{\mathbf{z}} \leq r} \mathbf{z} \left[p(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla p(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{z} + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{z}^\top H_p(\mathbf{x}) \mathbf{z} + \mathcal{O}(\abs{\mathbf{z}}^3) \right] d\mathbf{z}. 	\end{align*} The odd terms in \(z\) vanish due to symmetry. The remaining quadratic term yields \begin{align*}
		\int_{\abs{\mathbf{z}} \leq r} \mathbf{z} \left( \nabla p(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{z}\right) d\mathbf{z} = \left( \int_{\abs{\mathbf{z}} \leq r} \mathbf{z} \mathbf{z}^\top d\mathbf{z} \right) \nabla p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\pi r^4}{4} \nabla p(\mathbf{x}), 
	\end{align*} such that \begin{align}
		I(\mathbf{x}, r) = \frac{\pi r^4}{4} \nabla p(\mathbf{x}) + \mathcal{O}(r^6). \label{eq:scoreNumerator}
	\end{align} Combining Equations \eqref{eq:scoreMass} and \eqref{eq:scoreNumerator}, we find \begin{align*}
		V(\mathbf{x}, r) = \frac{I(\mathbf{x}, r)}{m(\mathbf{x}, r)} = \frac{\frac{\pi r^4}{4} \nabla p(\mathbf{x}) + \mathcal{O}(r^6)}{\pi r^2 p(\mathbf{x}) + \mathcal{O}(r^4)} = \frac{r^2}{4} \frac{\nabla p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x})}\left(1 + \mathcal{O}(r^2)\right) = \frac{r^2}{4} \frac{\nabla p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x})} + \mathcal{O}(r^4). 
	\end{align*} This completes the proof.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
Alternatively to the procedure above, one could recompute the density using the new radius and iterate to obtain a more self-consistent relation between the local density and the optimal radius.

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
The same particle ensemble is used both to estimate the local density in \eqref{eq:densityEst} and to evaluate the mean-shift estimator in \eqref{eq:meanShift}. Consequently, the adaptive radius and the score estimate are statistically dependent. This dependence can be reduced by sample splitting: one independent particle ensemble determines the radius, while a second ensemble evaluates the score. Independence is not required for consistency as the radius is guaranted to stay bounded; and sample splitting requires additional particles and thus increases computational cost. In the point-set registration experiment, the shared- and split-ensemble formulations produced nearly identical paths. Thus, for the particle counts considered here, the effect of this statistical dependence appears negligible.
<!-- end remark-5 -->
