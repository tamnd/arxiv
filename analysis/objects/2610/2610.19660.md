<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:integrable}
	A convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ is \emph{algebraically integrable}
	if there is a nonzero polynomial
	$P(\eta,s,w)\in\mathbb R[\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_n,s,w]$ such that
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:cutoff-algebraic}
		P\bigl(\eta,s,\operatorname{vol}_n(K\cap\{x:\eta\cdot x\le s\})\bigr)=0
	\end{equation}
	whenever $\eta\in\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}$ and the hyperplane
	$\eta\cdot x=s$ intersects the interior of $K$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
	Let $n\ge3$ be odd, and let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be a convex body
	with $C^\infty$ boundary. Then $K$ is algebraically integrable
	if and only if $K$ is an ellipsoid.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:all-dimensions}
	Let $n\ge2$, and let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be a convex body
	with $C^\infty$ boundary. Then $K$ is algebraically integrable
	if and only if $n$ is odd and $K$ is an ellipsoid.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:section}
Let $n\ge3$ be odd, and let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be a convex body with $C^\infty$ semialgebraic boundary. If $A_K$ satisfies a joint algebraic relation, then $K$ is an ellipsoid.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:algebraization}
If a convex body $K$ with $C^\infty$ boundary is algebraically integrable, then $K$ and $\partial K$ are semialgebraic. Moreover, $A_K$ satisfies a joint algebraic relation.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The boundary is semialgebraic by
	\cite[Remark~1(2)]{Vassiliev2015}.
	Every point of $K$ lies on a segment with endpoints in
	$\partial K$, and every such segment is contained in $K$.
	Thus $K$ is the image of the semialgebraic set
	$\partial K\times\partial K\times[0,1]$ under the polynomial
	map $(x,y,u)\mapsto(1-u)x+uy$. It is therefore semialgebraic
	by \cite[Proposition~2.2.7]{BCR}.
	
	It remains to deduce from the algebraic relation for $V_K$
	a polynomial relation for its derivative $A_K=\partial_tV_K$.
	Let $P$ satisfy \eqref{eq:cutoff-algebraic}. Although $P$
	is nonzero, its restriction to $|\eta|=1$ might be
	identically zero. We  choose another polynomial relation
	to avoid this.
	
	Expand
	\[
	P(c\eta,cs,w)=\sum_{j=0}^D c^jP_j(\eta,s,w)
	\]
	as a polynomial in $c$. For $\xi\inS^{n-1}$ and
	$t\in I_K(\xi)$, multiplying the inequality
	$x\cdot\xi\le t$ by $c>0$ leaves the cutoff volume
	unchanged. Thus
	\[
	\sum_{j=0}^D c^jP_j(\xi,t,V_K(\xi,t))=0
	\qquad\text{for all }c>0.
	\]
	Each coefficient is therefore zero, so
	$P_j(\xi,t,V_K(\xi,t))=0$ for every $j$.
	At least one $P_j$ is not identically zero on
	$S^{n-1}\times\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$. Otherwise,
	$P(c\xi,ct,w)=0$ for all $c>0$, $\xi\inS^{n-1}$,
	and $t,w\in\mathbb R$, contradicting $P\ne0$. We replace $P$ by such a $P_j$.
	
	We regard $P(\xi,t,w)$ as a polynomial in $w$. Its
	coefficients are polynomial functions of $(\xi,t)$ restricted
	to $S^{n-1}\times\mathbb R$. We work over the fraction field
	\[
	\mathbb F
	=\operatorname{Frac}\!\left(
	\mathbb R[\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n,t]\Big/
	\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i^2-1\Big)
	\right).
	\]
	The quotient ring is an integral domain, since
	$\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i^2-1$ is irreducible for $n\ge2$.
	Thus $\mathbb F$ consists of ratios of polynomial functions
	on $S^{n-1}\times\mathbb R$.
	
	The degree of $P$ in $w$ is positive. Otherwise, for each
	fixed $\xi\inS^{n-1}$, the polynomial $P(\xi,t)$ in $t$
	would vanish on the nonempty interval $I_K(\xi)$ and hence
	for every $t\in\mathbb R$. This contradicts the choice of $P$.
	
	Factor $P$ in $\mathbb F[w]$ as
	\[
	P=a\,f_1^{m_1}\cdots f_r^{m_r},
	\]
	where $a\in\mathbb F\setminus\{0\}$, the integers $m_i$
	are positive, and $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ are distinct monic
	irreducible polynomials of positive degree in $w$. Define
	\[
	B(\xi,t,w)=f_1(\xi,t,w)\cdots f_r(\xi,t,w).
	\]
	Thus $B$ is monic and square-free.
	
	Let $E\subsetS^{n-1}\times\mathbb R$ be the union of the zero sets
	of the denominators in $a$ and in the coefficients of the
	$f_i$, together with the zero set of the numerator of $a$.
	None of these polynomial functions vanishes identically on
	$S^{n-1}\times\mathbb R$, so $E$ is a proper algebraic subset.
	For $(\xi,t)\notin E$ with $t\in I_K(\xi)$, the identity
	\[
	0=P(\xi,t,V_K(\xi,t))
	=a(\xi,t)\prod_{i=1}^r
	f_i(\xi,t,V_K(\xi,t))^{m_i}
	\]
	implies $B(\xi,t,V_K(\xi,t))=0$.
	This identity holds on an open set of internal parameters.
	Differentiating with respect to $t$, with $\xi$ fixed, gives
	\[
	B_w(\xi,t,V_K(\xi,t))A_K(\xi,t)
	+B_t(\xi,t,V_K(\xi,t))=0.
	\]
	
	Introduce an independent variable $z$ and define
	\[
	Q(\xi,t,z)
	=\operatorname{Res}_w\!\left(B,B_wz+B_t\right)\in\mathbb F[z],
	\]
	where $\operatorname{Res}_w$ denotes the resultant with respect to $w$.
	The coefficients of $B$, $B_w$, and $B_t$ are defined
	outside $E$, and the resultant is a polynomial in these
	coefficients. For $z=A_K(\xi,t)$, its two arguments have
	the common root $w=V_K(\xi,t)$. Hence
	\[
	Q(\xi,t,A_K(\xi,t))=0
	\]
	for $(\xi,t)\notin E$ with $t\in I_K(\xi)$.
	
	This relation is nontrivial. Since $\mathbb F$ has
	characteristic zero and $B$ is square-free, $B$ and $B_w$
	are relatively prime. Therefore $\operatorname{Res}_w(B,B_w)\ne0$.
	If $d=\deg_w B$, the coefficient of $z^d$ in $Q$ is
	$\operatorname{Res}_w(B,B_w)$, because $B$ is monic.
	Thus $Q\ne0$ in $\mathbb F[z]$.
	
	Multiplying $Q$ by a common denominator of its coefficients
	gives a real polynomial in $(\xi,t,z)$ whose restriction
	to $S^{n-1}\times\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ is not identically zero.
	This polynomial vanishes at $z=A_K(\xi,t)$ for internal
	parameters outside $E$.
	The set $E$ has empty interior, since a nonzero polynomial
	function on the connected real analytic manifold
	$S^{n-1}\times\mathbb R$ cannot vanish on a nonempty open set.
	Its complement is therefore dense.
	Every internal hyperplane meets $\partial K$ transversely,
	since a tangent hyperplane of a smooth convex body is
	supporting. Consequently, $A_K$ is continuous on the open
	set of internal parameters, and the polynomial relation
	extends to all $\xi\inS^{n-1}$ and $t\in I_K(\xi)$.
	This proves the last assertion.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:nash-boundary}
Let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be a convex body with $C^\infty$ semialgebraic boundary. Then $\partial K$ is real analytic. There is a real polynomial $F$, irreducible over $\mathbb C$, such that
\[
 \partial K\subset X:=\{F=0\}\subset\mathbb C^n,
\]
and $\partial K$ is Zariski dense in $X$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Local graph representations of $\partial K$ are $C^\infty$
semialgebraic functions, hence real analytic by
\cite[Definition~2.9.3 and Proposition~8.1.8]{BCR}.
Thus $\partial K$ is a connected Nash hypersurface.
Its real Zariski closure is irreducible of dimension $n-1$
by \cite[Proposition~8.4.1(i)]{BCR}.
The ideal $I\subset\mathbb R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ of polynomials
vanishing on $\partial K$ is therefore prime of height one.
Since $\mathbb R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is a unique factorization domain,
$I=(F)$ for a real irreducible polynomial $F$.
	
For $G\in\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, write $G=G_1+iG_2$
with $G_1,G_2\in\mathbb R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$.
If $G$ vanishes on $\partial K$, then both $G_1$ and $G_2$
vanish there and are therefore divisible by $F$.
Hence $F$ divides $G$ in $\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, so the ideal
of complex polynomials vanishing on $\partial K$ is $(F)$.
	
	This complex ideal is prime. To see this, suppose that
	$GH$ vanishes on $\partial K$ and that $G$ does not
	vanish identically there. Then $G\ne0$ on a nonempty
	relatively open subset of $\partial K$, so $H=0$ on
	that subset. The real analytic identity theorem on the
	connected manifold $\partial K$ gives $H=0$ on all of
	$\partial K$. Thus $(F)$ is prime over $\mathbb C$, and $F$
	is irreducible over $\mathbb C$.
Every complex polynomial vanishing on $\partial K$ is
divisible by $F$ and hence vanishes on $X$.
Thus $\partial K$ is Zariski dense in $X$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:real-analytic-sections}
For a convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ with real analytic boundary and a fixed $\xi\inS^{n-1}$, the function $A_K(\xi,\cdot)$ is real analytic on $I_K(\xi)$. If $\partial K$ is only $C^\infty$ and $0\in\operatorname{int}K$, then $A_K(\xi,t)$ is $C^\infty$ as a function of $(\xi,t)$
in a neighborhood of $S^{n-1}\times\{0\}$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
A hyperplane meeting $\operatorname{int}K$
	cannot be tangent to $\partial K$. Equivalently, the
	differential of $x\mapsto x\cdot\xi$ is nonzero on
	$T_x\partial K$ at every point of an internal section
	boundary. Thus all internal sections are transverse.
	
	First assume that $\partial K$ is real analytic. Fix
	$\xi\inS^{n-1}$ and $t_0\in I_K(\xi)$, and choose orthonormal
	coordinates with $\xi=e_n$. Put
	\[
	C_t=\partial K\cap\{x_n=t\}.
	\]
	Let $\nabla_{\partial K}$ denote the tangential gradient
	for the induced Euclidean metric. On the open subset $\{x\in\partial K:x_n\in I_K(\xi)\}$, the vector field
	\[
	Y=\frac{\nabla_{\partial K}x_n}
	{|\nabla_{\partial K}x_n|^2}
	\]
	is well defined and real analytic, and satisfies $dx_n(Y)=1$.
	Let $\Phi_s$ be the local flow of $Y$, characterized by
	$\partial_s\Phi_s(p)=Y(\Phi_s(p))$ and $\Phi_0(p)=p$.
	In local analytic coordinates, the coefficients of $Y$
	extend holomorphically to a complex neighborhood.
	The Picard iteration argument in
	\cite[proof of Theorem~4.2]{Teschl}, applied to the resulting
	system, shows that $(s,p)\mapsto\Phi_s(p)$ is real analytic.
	
	Choose a closed interval about $t_0$ contained in $I_K(\xi)$.
	The part of $\partial K$ over this interval is compact,
	so the flow is defined there for uniformly small positive
	and negative times. Since $dx_n(Y)=1$,
	\(
	\frac{d}{ds}x_n(\Phi_s(x))=1,
	\)
	and hence $x_n(\Phi_s(x))=x_n(x)+s$.
	Therefore $\Phi_s$ maps $C_{t_0}$ onto $C_{t_0+s}$,
	with inverse $\Phi_{-s}$, for sufficiently small $|s|$.
	
	Orient each section hyperplane by
	$dx_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{n-1}$, and give $C_t$ the
	induced boundary orientation. Consider the polynomial
	$(n-2)$-form
	\[
	\beta=x_1\,dx_2\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{n-1},
	\qquad
	d\beta=dx_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{n-1},
	\]
	with $\beta=x_1$ when $n=2$.
	Stokes' theorem \cite[Theorem~16.11]{Lee} gives
	\[
	A_K(\xi,t)
	=\int_{C_t}\beta
	=\int_{C_{t_0}}\Phi_{t-t_0}^{*}\beta
	\]
	for $t$ sufficiently close to $t_0$; the flow preserves
	the boundary orientations for small times.
	
	This last integral is over a fixed compact manifold.
	Choose a finite analytic coordinate cover of $C_{t_0}$
	and a subordinate smooth partition of unity, independent
	of $t$, with compact supports in the coordinate charts.
	After shrinking the neighborhoods, the coefficients of
	the pulled-back forms extend holomorphically in $t$ to
	a common complex disk about $t_0$ and are uniformly bounded
	on the supports of the partition of unity.
	Integration in the fixed real coordinates therefore gives
	a holomorphic function of $t$. Its restriction to real $t$
	is $A_K(\xi,t)$, proving real analyticity near $t_0$.
	Since $t_0$ was arbitrary, this holds on $I_K(\xi)$.
	
	Now assume only that $\partial K$ is $C^\infty$ and that
	$0\in\operatorname{int}K$. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ with
	$\varepsilon B_2^n\subset\operatorname{int}K$, and set
	\[
	\mathcal E=
	\{(x,\xi,t)\in\partial K\timesS^{n-1}\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon):
	x\cdot\xi=t\}.
	\]
Since $\partial_t(x\cdot\xi-t)=-1$, $\mathcal E$ is a
smooth manifold. Consider the projection
	$\pi(x,\xi,t)=(\xi,t)$.
	Fix $(x,\xi,t)\in\mathcal E$ and take any
	$(v,a)\in T_\xiS^{n-1}\times\mathbb R$.
	A vector $(h,v,a)$ is tangent to $\mathcal E$ precisely
	when $h\in T_x\partial K$ and
	$\xi\cdot h+x\cdot v=a$.
	By transversality, the linear map
	$h\mapsto\xi\cdot h$ from $T_x\partial K$ to $\mathbb R$
	is nonzero and hence surjective. Thus such an $h$ exists
	for every $(v,a)$.
	Since $d\pi_{(x,\xi,t)}(h,v,a)=(v,a)$, the differential
	of $\pi$ is surjective. Hence $\pi$ is a submersion.
	It is surjective because $|t|<\varepsilon$ gives an internal
	section, and proper because $\partial K$ is compact.
	Ehresmann's theorem \cite[Lemma~9.2]{KolarMichorSlovak}
	therefore supplies smooth local trivializations of this
	family of section boundaries.
	

	
	Locally in $\xi$, choose smoothly varying orthonormal
	coordinates on $\xi^\perp$, translate them to
	$t\xi+\xi^\perp$, and define $\beta_\xi$ as above.
	Over a sufficiently small parameter neighborhood, the
	preceding trivialization can be chosen to give a smooth
	family of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms
	$\psi_{\xi,t}:C_*\to\partial K\cap\{x:x\cdot\xi=t\}$,
	where $C_*$ is a fixed section boundary.
	By Stokes' theorem and change of variables,
	$A_K(\xi,t)=\int_{C_*}\psi_{\xi,t}^*\beta_\xi$.
	The pulled-back form depends smoothly on $(\xi,t)$, and
	compactness of $C_*$ allows repeated differentiation
	under the integral. Since this argument applies near every
	parameter, $A_K$ is $C^\infty$ on
	$S^{n-1}\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:infinity-model}

	
	There is a nonempty Zariski open set of directions
	$[\xi]\in\mathbb P^{n-1}$ such that
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:incidence}
		\mathcal M=\{(p,t)\in M\times\mathbb C:s_1(p)=t s_0(p)\},
		\qquad f(p,t)=t,
	\end{equation}
	is smooth of dimension $d+1$, and $f:\mathcal M\to\mathbb C$ is
	projective. The reduced boundary of $\mathcal M$,
	\[
	\mathcal D=\bigl(\mathcal M\cap(D_\infty\times\mathbb C)\bigr)_{\mathrm{red}},
	\]
	is a relative simple normal-crossing divisor over all of $\mathbb C$.
	In particular, $f$ is smooth near $\mathcal D$ for every finite
	complex parameter, and all intersections of components of
	$\mathcal D$ are smooth over $\mathbb C$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The sections $\pi^*z_1,\ldots,\pi^*z_n$ have no common zero:
	such a zero would map to $[1:0:\cdots:0]$, the affine origin,
	which is excluded by \eqref{eq:origin}. Their restrictions to
	each stratum of $D_\infty$ are also base-point-free.
	Apply Bertini's theorem in characteristic zero to these
	restrictions, treating irreducible components separately when
	necessary; see \cite[Corollary~5]{Kleiman}.  For a general $s_1$, its restriction to each
	positive-dimensional stratum defines a smooth divisor or has
	no zeros. Thus, at each zero $p$ on a stratum $S$, the
	differential of a local representative satisfies
	$ds_1|_{T_pS}\ne0$.  We also choose $s_1$ nonzero at every
	zero-dimensional stratum. There are only finitely many
	strata, so these conditions hold on a common nonempty
	Zariski open set of directions. This form of Bertini requires
	no projective embedding by the chosen linear system.
	
On $M\setminus D_\infty$, the section $s_0$ does not vanish.
Thus $\mathcal M\setminus\mathcal D$ is the graph of the regular function
$s_1/s_0$ on $M\setminus D_\infty$, so $\mathcal M$ is smooth
there. On $D_\infty\times\mathbb C$, the incidence equation
reduces to $s_1=0$; in particular, as reduced varieties,
	\[
	\mathcal D=(D_\infty\cap\{s_1=0\})\times\mathbb C.
	\]
	We must also check the map in a neighborhood of this boundary.
	
	
	Let $(p,t_0)\in\mathcal D$. Trivialize $L$ near $p$, using
	$s_0,s_1$ also for their local representatives, and choose
	normal-crossing coordinates for $D_\infty$, with the
	components through $p$ given by $u_1=0,\ldots,u_r=0$.
	The stratum $S$ through $p$ is locally
	$\{u_1=\cdots=u_r=0\}$. Since $s_1(p)=t_0s_0(p)=0$
	and $s_1$ was chosen nonzero on all zero-dimensional strata,
	$\dim S=d+1-r\ge1$, so $r\le d$.
	Since $ds_1(p)|_{T_pS}\ne0$, the covectors
	$du_1(p),\ldots,du_r(p),ds_1(p)$ are linearly independent.
	Keeping $u_1,\ldots,u_r$ unchanged, the holomorphic inverse
	function theorem gives coordinates $(y,u_1,\ldots,u_d)$
	with $y=s_1$. The boundary components are therefore still
	given by $u_i=0$, $1\le i\le r$.
	

	
	The zero divisor of $s_0$ is supported on $D_\infty$.
	Consequently, in this trivialization,
	\[
	s_0=g(y,u)u_1^{a_1}\cdots u_r^{a_r},\qquad s_1=y,
	\]
	where $u=(u_1,\ldots,u_d)$, $g$ is a nowhere-vanishing
	holomorphic function, and each positive integer $a_i$ is the
	vanishing order of $s_0$ along $u_i=0$.
	The incidence equation is therefore
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-model}
		y=t\,g(y,u)u_1^{a_1}\cdots u_r^{a_r}.
	\end{equation}
	At $(p,t_0)$, the product $u_1^{a_1}\cdots u_r^{a_r}$
	vanishes, so the derivative with respect to $y$ of the left
	side minus the right side is $1$.
	The holomorphic implicit function theorem solves uniquely
	for $y$ as a holomorphic function of $(t,u)$ near this point.
	Thus $(t,u_1,\ldots,u_d)$ are coordinates on $\mathcal M$,
	$f$ is projection to $t$, and $\mathcal D$ is given by
	$u_1\cdots u_r=0$. This proves the required local description at every finite
	$t_0$. For each irreducible component $E$ of $D_\infty$,
	the reduced intersection
	$(\mathcal M\cap(E\times\mathbb C))_{\mathrm{red}}$ is locally given
	by a single coordinate equation $u_i=0$.
	It is therefore smooth.
	Consequently, the irreducible components of $\mathcal D$ are smooth.
	Each nonempty intersection of distinct components is locally
	defined by setting some of $u_1,\ldots,u_r$ equal to zero.
	The restriction of $f$ is still projection to the free
	coordinate $t$, so every such intersection is smooth over $\mathbb C$. 
	

	
Finally, \eqref{eq:incidence} defines a closed subvariety of
$M\times\mathbb C$. Since $M$ is projective, the projection
$M\times\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ is projective. Its restriction
$f:\mathcal M\to\mathbb C$ is therefore projective.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:dual}
	Let $X\subset\mathbb C^n$ be the complex Zariski closure of the
	$C^\infty$ semialgebraic boundary of a convex body
	$K\subset\mathbb R^n$, as in Lemma~\ref{lem:nash-boundary}.
	The projective dual $\overline X^*\subset(\mathbb P^n)^*$ of its
	projective closure is an irreducible hypersurface.
	Outside a proper algebraic subset of $\overline X^*$, a
	hyperplane has exactly one point of tangency on the smooth
	locus of $\overline X$. This point lies in $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$,
	and the contact is an ordinary quadratic tangency.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $\nu_K:\partial K\toS^{n-1}$ be the outward Gauss map.
	It 
	is a Nash map because $\partial K$ is a Nash hypersurface.
	A value is regular if $d\nu_K$ is surjective at every point
	of its inverse image. The critical values form a compact set,
	since $\partial K$ is compact, and have measure zero by Sard's
	theorem \cite[Theorem~6.10]{Lee}. Thus the regular values form
	an open dense subset of $S^{n-1}$. With the convention
	$\mathrm{II}_p(v,w)=d\nu_K|_p(v)\cdot w$, convexity makes
	$\mathrm{II}_p$ positive semidefinite. If $\nu_K(p)$ is a regular
	value, then $d\nu_K|_p$ is invertible, so $\mathrm{II}_p$
	is positive definite.
	
	We also need a support point outside $\operatorname{Sing} X$.
	Since $\partial K$ is Zariski dense in $X$ and $\operatorname{Sing} X$ is a
	proper algebraic subset defined over $\mathbb R$, there is a real
	polynomial vanishing on $\operatorname{Sing} X$ but not identically on
	$\partial K$. Its restriction is a nonzero Nash function on
	the connected Nash manifold $\partial K$.
	By \cite[Proposition~8.1.10 and Theorem~2.8.8]{BCR}, its zero
	set has dimension at most $n-2$, and the Gauss map does not
	increase semialgebraic dimension. Hence
	$\nu_K(\partial K\cap\operatorname{Sing} X)$ is compact and semialgebraic
	of real dimension at most $n-2$.
Choose a regular value $\xi$ outside this image and a support
point $p$ with $\nu_K(p)=\xi$. Then $p\in X_{\mathrm{reg}}$
and $d\nu_K|_p$ is invertible.

Let $\gamma$ denote the projective Gauss map of $\overline X$.

Let $G=\pi_1(U,\tau)$ be the fundamental group of based loops
modulo homotopy, acting by monodromy. Since $\langle v,v\rangle=0$,
formula \eqref{eq:pl-open} gives, for every $g\in G$ and
$k\in\mathbb Z$,
\[
 T^k(g\delta)=g\delta+
 k\epsilon\langle j(g\delta),v\rangle\delta.
\]
Taking periods gives an identity of holomorphic germs at $\tau$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-values}
 I_{T^kg\delta}=I_{g\delta}+
 k\epsilon\langle j(g\delta),v\rangle I_\delta.
\end{equation}
For each fixed $g$, finite branching allows only finitely many
germs on the left as $k$ varies. Since $I_\delta$ is a nonzero
germ, the coefficient of $k$ must vanish. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:orbit-orthogonal}
 \langle j(g\delta),v\rangle=0\qquad(g\in G).
\end{equation}

\[
 W=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}\{j(g\delta):g\in G\}\subset\mathsf H.
\]
Inclusion commutes with transport, as explained after \eqref{eq:fibres}, so $j(ga)=g(ja)$. Hence $W$ is a $G$-invariant subspace. By \eqref{eq:pl-compact} and \eqref{eq:orbit-orthogonal}, $T$ acts as the identity on $W$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:infinity-model} and \eqref{eq:base}, the restriction $f^{-1}(U)\to U$ is a smooth projective morphism over the algebraic curve $U$. Dualizing gives semisimplicity of the representation on $\mathsf H$. Thus there is a $G$-invariant complement
\begin{equation}\label{eq:semisimple-splitting}
 \mathsf H=W\oplus W'.
\end{equation}
The complement $W'$ is invariant under all monodromy transformations, not only under $T$. Let $\mathrm{Id}$ denote the identity on $\mathsf H$. By \eqref{eq:pl-compact}, $(T-\mathrm{Id})\mathsf H\subset\mathbb Q v\subset W$. Since $W'$ is $T$-invariant,
\[
 (T-\mathrm{Id})W'\subset W\cap W'=0.
\]
Therefore $T$ is the identity on all of $\mathsf H$. If $v\ne0$, nondegeneracy of the compact intersection pairing gives $h\in\mathsf H$ with $\langle h,v\rangle\ne0$, contradicting \eqref{eq:pl-compact}. Hence $v=0$.

Let $X$ be given by Lemma~\ref{lem:nash-boundary}, and make the translation \eqref{eq:origin}.
Remove from the set $\mathcal G$ of
Lemma~\ref{lem:good-real-directions} the directions for which
\eqref{eq:section-algebraic} specializes to the zero polynomial.
The excluded directions are the common zeros on $S^{n-1}$ of
the coefficient polynomials of $Q$ when expanded in $t,w$.
Since $Q$ is not identically zero on $S^{n-1}\times\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$,
this is a proper real algebraic subset and has empty interior.
The remaining set is dense, and we continue to denote it
by $\mathcal G$.
Fix $\xi\in\mathcal G$ and use the associated pencil
$f:\mathcal M\to\mathbb C$ with regular base $U=\mathbb C\setminus\Sigma$.
The dependence of the pencil and of $\Sigma,U$ on $\xi$
is suppressed in the notation.

Choose oriented orthonormal coordinates $y_1,\ldots,y_{d+1}$ on $\xi^\perp$, extend them to $\mathbb R^n$ by orthogonal projection onto $\xi^\perp$, and then extend them complex-linearly to $\mathbb C^n$. Together with $t=\xi\cdot x$, they form linear coordinates on the ambient space. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:primitive}
 \Omega_\xi=dy_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{d+1},
 \qquad
 \lambda_\xi=y_1\,dy_2\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{d+1}.
\end{equation}
Thus $d\lambda_\xi=\Omega_\xi$. Pull these forms back along the composite $\mathcal Z\to M\setminus D_\infty\to X\subset\mathbb C^n$. They are holomorphic there, including along the affine exceptional set, since the map to the affine ambient space is regular. On every smooth complex $d$-dimensional fiber, $\lambda_\xi$ is closed.
We use the same notation for these forms and their pullbacks,
and take $\lambda=\lambda_\xi$ in the period construction of
Section~\ref{sec:semisimplicity}.

Let $t_+=b_K(\xi)$. For real $t<t_+$ sufficiently close to $t_+$, the entire section
\[
 C_{\xi,t}=K\cap\{x:\xi\cdot x=t\}
\]
lies in a small neighborhood of the unique support point. Indeed, otherwise a sequence of points outside that neighborhood would converge to another point in the support face. The real analytic Morse lemma gives coordinates $u_1,\ldots,u_{d+1}$ on the boundary near the support point in which the height is $t_+-\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}u_i^2$. At the support point, $dF\ne0$. The real and holomorphic implicit
function theorems identify $\partial K$ and $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$
locally with the real and complex graphs of the same analytic
function. The real analytic Morse coordinate change extends
holomorphically, and its nonzero Jacobian makes the extension
a local holomorphic coordinate change on $X_{\mathrm{reg}}$. For $t<t_+$ close enough, the entire boundary of $C_{\xi,t}$ is therefore the real sphere $\sum_i u_i^2=t_+-t$, with $u\in\mathbb R^{d+1}$, in this local complex fiber. It represents the vanishing cycle described in Section~\ref{sec:semisimplicity}. The resolution is an isomorphism on this neighborhood, so we denote the lifted sphere, with its boundary orientation, by $\delta_t$. Applying Stokes' theorem in the real section hyperplane and identifying its boundary with the lifted vanishing sphere gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-period}
 A_K(\xi,t)=\int_{C_{\xi,t}}\Omega_\xi
 =\int_{\delta_t}\lambda_\xi>0.
\end{equation}
The specialized equation has positive degree in $w$, since otherwise a nonzero polynomial in $t$ would vanish on an interval. For the branching argument, let $B_\xi(t,w)$ be its monic square-free part in $w$ over $\mathbb C(t)$. Let $\mathcal E_\xi$ consist of its coefficient poles and the zeros, away from these poles, of $\operatorname{Res}_w(B_\xi,\partial_wB_\xi)$. This resultant is a nonzero rational function of $t$ by square-freeness, so $\mathcal E_\xi$ is finite; outside it the equation has only simple roots. Choose a real base point $\tau\in U\setminus\mathcal E_\xi$ in the above interval, and let $\delta$ be the homology class of $\delta_\tau$. The period $I_\delta$ is nonzero by \eqref{eq:real-period}.

The local triviality of the pair established after \eqref{eq:fibres} supplies homological transport along every path in $U$. The holomorphy of periods established in Section~\ref{sec:semisimplicity} then supplies analytic continuation of $I_\delta$ along each such path. Every returning germ still satisfies the original specialized
polynomial relation. Taking the square-free part over $\mathbb C(t)$
does not change its solutions as meromorphic germs, so each
returning germ also satisfies $B_\xi(t,w)=0$.
At $\tau$, this equation has only finitely many simple local
roots, so there are only finitely many such germs. Thus $I_\delta$ has finite branching over $U$. The set $\mathcal E_\xi$ is used only to choose the base point; it need not be removed from the geometric base $U$. Corollary~\ref{cor:entire-vanishing} now gives an entire extension.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:real-analytic-sections}, the actual section function is real analytic on the whole interval $I_K(\xi)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:real-period} and the identity theorem therefore give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-identification}
 A_K(\xi,t)=I_\delta(t)
 \qquad(t\in I_K(\xi)).
\end{equation}
This argument does not require transporting the actual real cycle through singular fibers of the complex algebraic family.

We next obtain a degree bound valid for all the directions under consideration. For this purpose, return to the original joint equation, rather than the square-free equation chosen for one direction. Write it in the form
\[
 Q(\xi,t,w)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}p_j(\xi,t)w^j,
 \qquad N_0=\max_{0\le j\le r}\deg_t p_j.
\]
Zero coefficients are omitted when taking the maximum defining $N_0$. This number is independent of $\xi$. By the identity theorem, the entire extension still satisfies $Q(\xi,t,I_\delta(t))=0$ for every $t\in\mathbb C$. For a fixed $\xi\in\mathcal G$, discard coefficients which vanish identically in $t$, and let $p_{r_\xi}(\xi,t)$ be the highest remaining coefficient in $w$. As noted above, $r_\xi\ge1$. The root bound for a polynomial gives, for all sufficiently large $|t|$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:growth}
 |I_\delta(t)|
 \le 1+\max_{j<r_\xi}
 \left|\frac{p_j(\xi,t)}{p_{r_\xi}(\xi,t)}\right|
 \le C_\xi(1+|t|)^{N_0}.
\end{equation}
Here $C_\xi>0$ may depend on $\xi$. The leading coefficient has no zeros for sufficiently large $|t|$, and each quotient in \eqref{eq:growth} has growth at most $|t|^{N_0}$. Cauchy's estimates show that the entire function $I_\delta$ is a polynomial of degree at most $N_0$.

It follows from \eqref{eq:real-identification} that
\[
 \partial_t^j A_K(\xi,0)=0
 \qquad(\xi\in\mathcal G,\ j>N_0).
\]
For each fixed $j$, Lemma~\ref{lem:real-analytic-sections}
makes $\xi\mapsto\partial_t^jA_K(\xi,0)$ continuous.
Density of $\mathcal G$ therefore extends these identities
to every $\xi\inS^{n-1}$. For each such $\xi$, real analyticity
in $t$ implies that $A_K(\xi,t)$ agrees near $0$ with its
Taylor polynomial of degree at most $N_0$. The identity
theorem extends this equality to all of $I_K(\xi)$.
Thus $K$ is polynomially integrable, with a uniform degree bound.
In particular, all central derivatives of every integer order
at least $N=\max\{n,N_0+1\}$ vanish in every direction.
Theorem~3.7 of \cite{KMY} now shows that $K$ is an ellipsoid.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:special-projection}
There is a smooth affine surface with a polynomial projection having a nondegenerate critical point and a nonzero algebraic vanishing period which is not polynomial, although the vanishing cycle is zero in the first homology of a smooth projective compactification of a general fiber.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Consider the smooth graph
\[
 X_0=\{(u,v,w)\in\mathbb C^3:w=(1+u^2+v^2)^2\}
\]
and the projection $f_0=w$. In its global coordinates $(u,v)$,
\[
 f_0=1+2u^2+2v^2+(u^2+v^2)^2,
\]
so $(0,0)$ is a nondegenerate critical point with critical value $1$. The other critical locus is $1+u^2+v^2=0$, with critical value $0$. For real $t>1$ close to $1$, consider the disk in the real coordinate plane
\[
 \{(u,v)\in\mathbb R^2:u^2+v^2\le\sqrt t-1\}.
\]
Its boundary lies in $f_0^{-1}(t)$ and is the local vanishing sphere.
With $\lambda_0=u\,dv$, its boundary period is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sqrt-example}
 I(t)=\pi(\sqrt t-1).
\end{equation}
It has two branches and is not a polynomial.

For $t\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}$, the affine fiber is the disjoint union of the two smooth conics
\[
 u^2+v^2=-1+\sqrt t,
 \qquad u^2+v^2=-1-\sqrt t.
\]
Each has smooth projective compactification $\mathbb P^1$, so the image of the vanishing cycle in compact first homology is zero. These compactifications are taken separately on the two
components, and each affine conic acquires two points at infinity. Around $t=0$, analytic continuation exchanges the two
components and induces a nontrivial permutation of their
four points at infinity. The local system formed by the
zeroth homology of these points therefore cannot extend
across $t=0$ as a locally constant system. Thus this special projection cannot have the relative boundary property in Proposition~\ref{prop:boundary-entire}. It does not contradict the generic-direction construction in Lemma~\ref{lem:infinity-model}.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
The odd-dimensional hypothesis in Theorem~\ref{thm:section} is essential. In the plane, the convex body
\[
 \{(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb R^2:x_1^4+x_2^4\le1\}
\]
has smooth semialgebraic boundary and is not an ellipse. For $\xi=(\xi_1,\xi_2)\in S^1$, parametrize a line by
\[
 x=t\xi+u(-\xi_2,\xi_1).
\]
Substitution in $x_1^4+x_2^4=1$ gives a polynomial in $u$ with coefficients polynomial in $(\xi,t)$ and leading coefficient $\xi_1^4+\xi_2^4>0$. The chord length is the difference of its two real endpoint roots. To obtain a joint equation, take the monic polynomial whose roots are all ordered differences of the four complex roots. Its coefficients are symmetric polynomials in those roots, hence rational functions of the original coefficients. Clearing denominators gives a polynomial equation that is nontrivial on $S^1\times\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ and is satisfied by every internal chord length. This does not give algebraicity of the cutoff area and does not contradict the even-dimensional obstruction of \cite{Vassiliev2015}.
