\label{thm:two}
For sufficiently small $\delta$, every function $g$ holomorphic in $\{z\in\mathbb D_\delta\setminus[\ell,L]:\delta<u_\delta(z)<1\}$, continuous on its two boundaries, and normalized by $g(0)=1$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-constants}
 B(g)^{1-\alpha_\delta}V(g)^{\alpha_\delta}\ge1.
\end{equation}
For every integer $K\ge1$, the function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gK}
 g_K(z)=\frac{\cosh(Kq_\delta(z))}{\cosh(Kq_\delta(0))}
\end{equation}
has a holomorphic extension on a neighborhood of the closure of $\mathbb{D}_{\delta}$, satisfies $g_K(0)=1$, and obeys
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gK-bound}
      \begin{aligned}
            B(g_K)&\le4\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi K(1-\delta)\mathbf K(k^2)}
            {\mathbf K(1-k^2)}\alpha_\delta\right),\\
            V(g_K)&\le4\exp\left(\frac{2\pi K(1-\delta)\mathbf K(k^2)}
            {\mathbf K(1-k^2)}(1-\alpha_\delta)\right).
           \end{aligned}      
\end{equation}
Consequently $1\le B(g_K)^{1-\alpha_\delta}V(g_K)^{\alpha_\delta}\le4$.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The above trade-off \eqref{eq:two-constants} is essentially the two-constants theorem (see \citet[Theorem 4.3.7]{ransford1995potential}). The key insight why $u_0(0)$ implies the minimax exponent is because the extremal case of \eqref{eq:two-constants} is achieved by the harmonic measure by the two-constants theorem. In fact, the equality in \eqref{eq:two-constants} is achieved by $\exp(K(q_\delta(z)-q_\delta(0)))$. However, it has different values on the two sides of the slit $[\ell, L]$ so that it cannot be holomorphically extended to a neighborhood of the closure of $\mathbb{D}_{\delta}$. The hyperbolic cosine function in \eqref{eq:gK} symmetrizes the optimal exponential function, and makes it holomorphic on the whole disc. As is shown in Theorem~\ref{thm:two}, although $g_K$ does not attain the equality in \eqref{eq:two-constants}, it still achieves the constant upper bound of the left-hand side of  \eqref{eq:two-constants}, which is enough to achieve the optimal rate.

We first compare the logarithmic boundary norms by the maximum principle, then verify holomorphic extension of the reflected function, and finally bound its two norms.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{The logarithmic boundary constraint.}\enspace
For $z\in\mathbb D_\delta\setminus[\ell,L]$ with $u_\delta(z)>\delta$, define
\[
 \mathfrak h(z)=\left(1-\frac{u_\delta(z)-\delta}{1-\delta}\right)\log B(g)
       +\frac{u_\delta(z)-\delta}{1-\delta}\log V(g).
\]
If either $B(g)$ or $V(g)$ is zero, first use its value plus $\varepsilon>0$ and then let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ in the comparison below; $g(0)=1$ rules out that case. Thus both logarithms are finite. This harmonic function equals $\log B(g)$ on $\Lambda_\delta$ and $\log V(g)$ on $\Gamma_\delta$. The function $\log|g|^2-\mathfrak h$ is subharmonic and has nonpositive boundary upper limits. The subharmonic maximum principle therefore gives $\log|g(0)|^2\le \mathfrak h(0)$; see \citet[Chapter~6, Section~4.1]{Ahlfors1979}. Since $g(0)=1$, we have $0\le(1-\alpha_\delta)\log B(g)+\alpha_\delta\log V(g)$, which proves \eqref{eq:two-constants}. At zeros, $\log|g|=-\infty$ is interpreted in the usual subharmonic sense; alternatively apply the comparison to $\max(\log|g|,-M)$ and then let $M\to\infty$.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Extension of the reflected function.}\enspace
Lemma~\ref{lem:geometry} gives an increment $2\pi\mathrm i$ for $q_\delta$ around the slit. Thus integer $K$ makes $\cosh(Kq_\delta)$ single-valued on $\mathbb D_\delta\setminus[\ell,L]$. Its two traces on the open slit satisfy
\[
 q_{\delta,-}(x)=-q_{\delta,+}(x)\pmod{2\pi\mathrm i},\qquad
 g_{K,-}(x)=g_{K,+}(x),\qquad \ell<x<L.
\]
Fix $x\in(\ell,L)$ and choose $r<\min(x-\ell,L-x)$. On the disk $|z-x|<r$, assign the common trace to $g_K$ on the real diameter. This defines a continuous function. For a triangle crossing that diameter, apply Cauchy's theorem to the portions at imaginary distance at least $\epsilon$ above and below it, and then let $\epsilon\downarrow0$. The integrals along their horizontal cuts converge to opposite integrals of the same continuous trace. They cancel, so the integral along the triangle is zero. Morera's theorem yields a holomorphic function on the disk; see \citet[Chapter~4, Section~2.3, p.~122]{Ahlfors1979}. Varying $x$ gives a holomorphic extension to $\mathbb D_\delta\setminus\{\ell,L\}$.

The identity $|\cosh(x+\mathrm i y)|\le\cosh x$ therefore bounds $g_K$ in punctured neighborhoods of both endpoints, including the values just assigned on the open slit. Consequently $(z-\ell)g_K(z)\to0$ and $(z-L)g_K(z)\to0$. The removable-singularity theorem in \citet[Chapter~4, Section~3.1, Theorem~7]{Ahlfors1979} extends $g_K$ through both endpoints.

Schwarz reflection is the following extension principle: a holomorphic function continuous up to a real interval and real-valued there extends to the other side by $f(z)=\overline{f(\bar z)}$; see \citet[Chapter~4, Section~6.5, Theorem~24]{Ahlfors1979}. On the outer circle $\operatorname{Re}q_\delta$ is constant. Subtract that constant, multiply by $\mathrm i$, and take a local fractional-linear coordinate sending the circle to a real interval. The principle then continues the local determination of $q_\delta$ across the circle. The period-invariant function $g_K$ has consistent continuations on overlaps. Compactness of the circle supplies an exterior neighborhood. Since $q_\delta(0)>0$ is real, its denominator is nonzero and $g_K(0)=1$.

 C\left(n^{-1/6+\epsilon_0/3}(\log n)^{2/3}
       +n^{-1/4}(\log n)^{3/4}
       +n^{-1/2+\epsilon_0}\log n\right)
\]
of the matching deterministic free spectrum, with any fixed polynomial failure probability. This error is smaller than every fixed power of $\delta$. The hypotheses of these results concern independent Hermitian summands and their listed parameters; they impose no regularity condition on individual sample-covariance edges.

To identify the deterministic free resolvent, expand it in inverse powers of the spectral parameter outside a fixed large disk. The semicircular moment formula pairs entries in noncrossing pairs. Isolating the pair containing the first random entry gives the matrix self-consistency equation; the independent top--bottom entry coefficients make its diagonal blocks diagonal. In the covariance eigenbasis the resulting equations are $g_j(z)=(z-\lambda_jg_0(z))^{-1}$ and $g_0(z)=(z-n^{-1}\sum_j\lambda_jg_j(z))^{-1}$. The convergent expansions establish these identities initially for large arguments; analytic continuation and the Stieltjes sign determine their solution elsewhere. Substitution $g_0(z)=-zm(z^2)$ gives \eqref{eq:MP}. The trace in the deterministic free model is faithful, so its support identifies the operator spectrum. Only these deterministic equations are diagonalized; a rotation of the non-Gaussian independent entries is never made.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Excluding an additional small companion eigenvalue.}\enspace
The ordinary dilation has $p-n$ structural zero modes, so confinement alone would not exclude a small additional singular value. Apply the same two comparison theorems to
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}I_p&\mathcal X\\\mathcal X^\top&-I_n\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its eigenvalues are $\pm\sqrt{1+s_j(\mathcal X)^2}$ and $p-n$ copies of $+1$. The deterministic bottom covariance is bounded below by the identity-covariance bottom model multiplied by $\ell$, whose lower edge is $(\sqrt\gamma-1)^2$. The shifted deterministic model therefore has a fixed gap around $-1$. Spectral confinement of the shifted model excludes small sample singular values. The deterministic shift does not alter any summand or variance parameter. This proves $cI_n\preceq\widehat S\preceq CI_n$ with the stated probability. Together with Lemma~\ref{lem:calibration-control}, it provides the needed resolvent neighborhoods, including calibrated values equal to zero.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Smooth matrix functions and their expectations.}\enspace
We use the generalized resolvent \eqref{eq:linearization}. Its top block equals $X^{\top}(\widehat S-zI_n)^{-1}X/n-I_p$, and its bottom block is $(\widehat S-zI_n)^{-1}$. The following construction specifies the smooth versions used on exceptional spectral events, including a spectral argument equal to zero.

Fix a small neighborhood of one deterministic spectral argument. The nonzero spectrum of the ordinary dilation is separated from all square roots of arguments in this neighborhood. Choose an even $C^\infty$ function $\chi$ of the real variable which equals one on a smaller neighborhood of that nonzero spectrum, vanishes near zero and outside a fixed bounded set, and also vanishes near every such real pole. The fixed positive gap at zero and the separation in Lemma~\ref{lem:calibration-control} allow the transition widths to be fixed powers of $\delta$. Convolution of an indicator of a spectral neighborhood with a fixed smooth mollifier constructs such a function with $\lVert\chi^{(j)}\rVert_\infty\le C_j\delta^{-C_j}$; this bound is independent of the number of spectral intervals. A finite cover of the spectral-argument curve suffices.

The smooth version of $G(z)$ in \eqref{eq:smooth-G} is
\[
 \widetilde G(z) = P_1f_{2,z}(\mathscr H)P_1-P_1
 +P_2f_{0,z}(\mathscr H)P_2
 +P_1f_{1,z}(\mathscr H)P_2
 +P_2f_{1,z}(\mathscr H)P_1.
\]
In the expectation and cumulant calculations below, $G$ denotes this smooth version; it equals the exact generalized resolvent on the spectral event. Algebraic resolvent identities and their entry derivatives below are first applied to the exact matrix on this event and on its one-entry perturbations. When taking expectations of the smooth versions, the resulting discrepancy is $O(n^{-D})$ for any prescribed fixed $D$, by their polynomial derivative bounds and the arbitrarily high probability exponent. Such terms are included in the stated remainders. Its normalized bottom trace is denoted by $\widehat m$ in those calculations. In particular, its top nullspace value is $-I$ and its bottom block has no zero mode. Each scalar function is analytic in $z$ and has every fixed Sobolev norm bounded by a fixed power of $\delta^{-1}$. This also bounds fixed matrix-entry derivatives: write the scalar function by its Fourier integral, differentiate the matrix exponential using
\[
 \frac{d}{ds}e^{\mathrm i t(A+sE)}\bigg|_{s=0}
 =\mathrm i t\int_0^1 e^{\mathrm i t(1-u)A}E e^{\mathrm i tuA}\,du,

The operator-valued smooth comparison in \citet[Theorem~2.10]{Brailovskaya2024} bounds the non-Gaussian/Gaussian expectation difference by
$C(R\sigma^2+R^3\log(p+n))\lVertf\rVert_{W^{5,1}}$, in that theorem's notation. With the summand parameters above it is $O(n^{-1/2+\epsilon_0}\delta^{-C})$. The matching Gaussian/free comparison follows from the matrix-valued resolvent bound in \citet[Theorem~2.8]{Bandeira2023}. Lemma~\ref{lem:smooth-calculus} integrates that bound and gives $O(n^{-1}\lVertf\rVert_{W^{6,1}})$ for these compactly supported functions. \par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Resampling bounds.}\enspace
Write a normalized entry as $x_{ki}$, and replace it by an independent copy with difference $d$. On the spectral event, the rank-two resolvent identity and its second-order expansion give, with $s=s_k$,
\[
 |G'_{vw}-G_{vw}|
 \le C|d|\bigl(|G_{vs}G_{iw}|+|G_{vi}G_{sw}|\bigr)
 +C|d|^2\delta^{-C}\bigl(|G_{vs}G_{sw}|+|G_{vi}G_{iw}|\bigr).
\]
All entries on the right can be taken from the unperturbed matrix; the remaining rank-two inverse is uniformly bounded because $|d|\le Cn^{-1/2+\epsilon_0}$ is smaller than the spectral margin. Squaring and summing over $(k,i)$, using $\sum_ks_ks_k^\top=\Sigma$, bounds the discrete squared gradient by $Cn^{-1+4\epsilon_0}\delta^{-C}$. The first-order products have a bounded summed square before the factor $\max d^2$; the second-order same-block products have at most one additional factor $n+p$ before $\max d^4$.

For the normalized trace, differentiation gives $\partial_{ki}\widehat m=-2(GP_2G)_{s_ki}/n$, where $P_2$ is the observation-block projection from Appendix~\ref{app:notation}. The summed squared first derivatives are at most $C\delta^{-C}/n$, using the Frobenius bound on $GP_2G$. Second-order trace coefficients are at most $C\delta^{-C}/n$. Its discrete squared gradient is therefore $Cn^{-2+4\epsilon_0}\delta^{-C}$. The smooth versions have polynomial bounds on the complementary spectral events. H\"older's inequality and an arbitrarily small exceptional probability make their contribution negligible. All one-entry perturbations and their intervening segments are covered by the rank-two perturbation bound; a finite polynomial union over replacements does not change the conclusion.

Apply \citet[Theorem~2]{Boucheron2005} to the positive and negative parts of each centered real statistic, and then to its imaginary part. The theorem uses the conditional expectation of the squared replacement differences, which is bounded by the same estimates. Its moment conclusion yields fluctuations of sizes $n^{-1/2+2\epsilon_0}\delta^{-C_r}$ and $n^{-1+2\epsilon_0}\delta^{-C_r}$. Combining with the preliminary mean comparison and choosing $\epsilon_0$ sufficiently small proves \eqref{eq:coarse-moments}. The derivative bound in \eqref{eq:Cauchy-derivative} supplies fixed-order spectral derivatives. The refinement is proved next and does not enter this preliminary argument.

 \partial_{ki}^2G_{iw}

For cumulant order five, four derivatives of the original mixed-block entry leave at least one mixed-block factor in every term. Lemma~\ref{lem:cumulant}, with a sufficiently large fixed order, gives a smaller remainder.

The coefficient equals $-m^{-1}(1-m^2\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^2D^{-2})/n)$, whose modulus is at least $c\delta$ by the deterministic stability estimate. This argument remains valid when $z=0$; no division by $z$ is made. Reducing the preliminary proof exponent establishes \eqref{eq:refined-mean}.

Lemmas~\ref{lem:preliminary} and~\ref{lem:mean}, a polynomial-size net on slightly smaller spectral neighborhoods from Lemma~\ref{lem:calibration-control}, and sufficiently high fixed moments give
\[
 \sup_z|\widehat m(z)-m(z)|\le Cn^{-3/4}\delta^{-c}

The moment order and failure exponent are chosen after the mesh size. The event includes the spectral gap and upper norm from Lemma~\ref{lem:preliminary}.

The empirical perturbation is smaller than $c_2r$ for large $n$, so Rouch\'e's theorem \citep[Chapter~4, Section~5.2, corollary to Theorem~18]{Ahlfors1979} gives exactly one empirical zero in this disk, also counted with multiplicity. It is therefore simple. The same Taylor bound at that zero yields displacement at most $Cn^{-3/4}\delta^{-c}$, uniformly in $\mu$.

The displacement is $o(r)$. By the analytic implicit-function theorem, each selected root continues locally in $\mu$. In a sufficiently small neighborhood of a given contour point, this continuation stays inside both the original isolating disk and the disk about the nearby deterministic root. The latter has exactly one zero, so the two selections coincide. This proves continuity on the circle. At the repeated endpoint of a parametrization the deterministic disk is the same and contains the same unique zero; hence the selection is a function on the closed circle.

At $\mu_0=L-R_\delta<0$, the deterministic root $\zeta(\mu_0)$ is below $\min\operatorname{supp}\varrho$. To see this, follow the real map from $\mu=-\infty$, where $\zeta(\mu)\to-\infty$; its derivative remains positive and its image remains outside $\operatorname{supp}\varrho$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:calibration}. The quantitative gap and sample spectral confinement keep its isolating disk below $\lambda_{\min}(\widehat S)$ for large $n$. The disk and its equation are conjugation invariant, so its unique root is real. It is the unique solution below the smallest sample eigenvalue, since $\widehat m$ increases there from zero to infinity. Thus it has the required initial value.

Spectral separation and the displacement bound imply the resolvent estimate in \eqref{eq:empirical-control}. Conjugating the root construction and using uniqueness gives the stated symmetry. All events involve only $X$, with uniform constants over the model family.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:recursion}
For each fixed $N\ge2$, uniformly on the deterministic tubes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment-recursion}
 \mathbb E\prod_{j=1}^N \mathcal F_j
 =\sum_{r=2}^N C_{1r}\mathbb E\prod_{j\notin\{1,r\}}\mathcal F_j
 +O_N\left(n^{-(N+1)/2+\epsilon_N}\delta^{-C_N}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_N>0$ can be chosen arbitrarily small by choosing the preliminary exponent sufficiently small. Thus, for even $N$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Wick}
 \mathbb E\prod_{j=1}^N\mathcal F_j
 =\sum_{\mathfrak P}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\mathfrak P}C_{ij}
 +O_N(n^{-N/2-1/4}\delta^{-C_N}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathfrak P$ ranges over pair partitions. For odd $N$ the whole expectation has the order of the remainder in \eqref{eq:Wick}. Constants are allowed to depend on fixed $N$; no bound for $N$ growing with $n$ is claimed.

Here the spectral points are on the explicitly varying contour and the error retains its dependence on $\delta$. The fourth cumulant is kept in the coefficient rather than absorbed into an unspecified covariance. We apply the cumulant expansion to the first factor, identify terms that pair it with one of the remaining factors, and bound every other derivative allocation by an additional negative power of $n$. Iterating the resulting recursion proves the pair-partition formula.

Put $t=D_1^{-1}v_1$ and $c_k=t^\top s_k$. Multiply \eqref{eq:master} by $\mathscr P=\prod_{j=2}^N\mathcal F_j$, and expand in each independent normalized entry. \par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Second cumulant: one pair is removed.}\enspace
The derivative on $G^1_{iw_1}$ cancels the last term of \eqref{eq:master}, except for
\[
 -\mathbb E\bigl((\widehat m_1-m_1)G^1_{\Sigma t,w_1}\mathscr P\bigr)
 -\frac1n\mathbb E\bigl((G_1P_2G_1)_{\Sigma t,w_1}\mathscr P\bigr).
\]
The refined mean, trace fluctuation, and the $N-1$ centered factors bound these terms by the required error. A derivative on $\mathcal F_r$ instead gives, after carrying out the latent-coordinate sum exactly,
\begin{align}\label{eq:pair-summed}
 -\frac1n\mathbb E\Big[&\bigl(
 (G_r)_{v_r,\Sigma t}(G_1P_2G_r)_{w_1w_r}
 +(G_r)_{\Sigma t,w_r}(G_1P_2G_r)_{w_1v_r}\bigr)\prod_{j\notin\{1,r\}}\mathcal F_j\Big].
\end{align}
There is no free coordinate sum left in this expression. The exact identity

 G_1P_2G_r=\frac{G_1-G_r}{z_1-z_r}

has the spectral derivative interpretation at coincidence. On a small disk in a tube, the derivative bound in \eqref{eq:Cauchy-derivative} applied to the coarse error gives the same moment bound for its derivative, with an additional fixed power of $\delta^{-1}$. For separated arguments, direct division gives that bound. Hence the deterministic equivalent of the top block of \eqref{eq:resolvent-difference} is $S_{1r}M_{1r}$, with $L^s$ error $n^{-1/2+\epsilon}\delta^{-C_s}$. The identity $S_{1r}=(m_1-m_r)/(z_1-z_r)$ follows by subtracting \eqref{eq:MP}. Replace also the individual top entry in \eqref{eq:pair-summed} by its deterministic equivalent. The result is the first line of \eqref{eq:leading-covariance} times the expectation with factors $1,r$ removed. Every replacement error has one extra coarse fluctuation, giving $n^{-(N+1)/2+C_N\epsilon}\delta^{-C_N}$.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Third cumulant: the row sum is essential.}\enspace
When two derivatives hit at most one external factor, the allocations are $(2,0),(1,1),(0,2)$ between the first Green factor and that factor. For $(2,0)$ use the explicit second derivative in Lemma~\ref{lem:mean}. The term $2G^1_{ii}G^1_{s_ks_k}G^1_{iw_1}\mathscr P$ is potentially largest. Replace its two diagonal entries by deterministic equivalents, which are independent of $i$, and sum the last mixed entry as $\sqrt nG^1_{\bar e,w_1}$. This gains $\sqrt n$ relative to an absolute sum. A diagonal replacement error supplies a second coarse fluctuation, providing the same saving. Other terms in this allocation have a bounded endpoint contraction against $c_k$ or at least three mixed entries.

For $(1,1)$ the only terms with one mixed entry have, up to permutation of the external endpoints, the form $G^1_{ii}G^1_{s_kw_1}G^r_{v_rs_k}G^r_{iw_r}$. Replace the first three factors by deterministic ones and use the same $i$-sum. The top coefficients have a bounded absolute sum over $k$. A replacement error either gives an additional fluctuation while retaining this contraction, or replaces both top endpoints and thus gives two additional small factors before a crude $\sqrt p$ sum is needed. For $(0,2)$ the zero-mixed part of the external second derivative is $2G^r_{v_rs_k}G^r_{ii}G^r_{s_kw_r}$; its product with $G^1_{iw_1}$ has precisely the same structure. All other terms have at least three mixed entries.

There is one further allocation: the two derivatives hit distinct external factors. It has three mixed entries, the original one and one from each external derivative. Its top endpoints contract against $c_k$. Before multiplication by the $N-3$ remaining centered factors its size is at most $Cn^{-3/2}\,n\,(n^{-1/2+\epsilon})^3\delta^{-C}=Cn^{-2+3\epsilon}\delta^{-C}$. Thus every third-cumulant contribution is bounded by the error in \eqref{eq:moment-recursion}. The common entry cumulant is independent of $i$, as required by the row-sum argument. This is where the iid assumption, rather than symmetry, is used.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Fourth cumulant: the non-Gaussian covariance term.}\enspace
The exact first derivative is
\[
 \partial_{ki}G^1_{iw_1}
 =-G^1_{ii}G^1_{s_kw_1}-G^1_{is_k}G^1_{iw_1}.
\]
For a differentiated external factor, we have
\begin{align*}
 \partial_{ki}^2G^r_{v_rw_r}
 ={}&2G^r_{v_rs_k}G^r_{ii}G^r_{s_kw_r}
 +2G^r_{v_rs_k}G^r_{is_k}G^r_{iw_r}
 +2G^r_{v_ri}G^r_{s_ks_k}G^r_{iw_r}
 +2G^r_{v_ri}G^r_{s_ki}G^r_{s_kw_r}.
\end{align*}
Only the first summand in each display has no mixed-block entry. Taking these two summands corresponds to one derivative on $G^1_{iw_1}$ and two derivatives on the same external factor. The multiplicity three and the factor two cancel the $3!$ in the cumulant coefficient. Replacing these factors by their deterministic equivalents and summing over $i$ yields the second line of \eqref{eq:leading-covariance}. The derivative's minus sign and the three negative top deterministic Green factors give a positive sign. The remaining $N-2$ centered factors are undifferentiated.

A replacement error adds a coarse fluctuation while preserving the summable top endpoints. The other allocations either leave all $N-1$ centered factors, contain at least two mixed entries after touching at most two external factors, or touch three external factors and contain at least four mixed entries. Each has at least one additional factor $n^{-1/2+C_N\epsilon}$ relative to the leading order $n^{-N/2}$.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Higher cumulants and a complete power count.}\enspace
For a cumulant of order $j\ge5$, let $s_0$ derivatives hit the original mixed entry, let $d$ distinct external factors be differentiated with positive orders $s_1,\ldots,s_d$, and let $t$ be the number of mixed entries in a resulting product. The rank-two derivative switches blocks, so
\[
 s_0+\sum_{a=1}^d s_a=j-1,
 \qquad t\ge\mathbf1_{\{s_0\text{ is even}\}}+
                         \#\{a:s_a\text{ is odd}\}.
\]
There are $N-1-d$ undifferentiated centered factors. If a fixed top endpoint is attached to $s_k$, its coefficient contracts with $c_k$ by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality. All other factors may be bounded uniformly, with arbitrarily small powers of $n$ obtained from fixed higher moments and a finite polynomial union. The total power is at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:word-count}
 n^{-j/2}\,n\,n^{-t/2}n^{-(N-1-d)/2}
 n^{C_N\epsilon}\delta^{-C_N}
 =n^{-N/2+(3-j+d-t)/2+C_N\epsilon}\delta^{-C_N}.
\end{equation}
An even positive $s_a$ is at least two; hence the integer $j-d+t$ is at least $(j+1+s_0)/2+\mathbf1_{\{s_0\text{ is even}\}}\ge4$. Therefore \eqref{eq:word-count} has an extra factor $n^{-1/2}$. If no such top endpoint contraction is available, each differentiated external factor has both fixed top endpoints attached to bottom indices, giving $t\ge1+2d$. The remaining latent sum costs at most $\sqrt p$, since $\lVertc\rVert_2\le C$. Replacing $3$ by $4$ in \eqref{eq:word-count} still gives a smaller order, because $4-j+d-t\le3-j-d\le-2$.

These counts also cover the nonleading fourth-cumulant terms after the allocation just extracted is removed. For the third cumulant, the exceptions to the crude count are exactly the one-mixed-entry terms handled by the row-sum calculation. The contractions used throughout are $\sum_k|(s_k^\top v)(s_k^\top w)|\le L\lVertv\rVert_2\lVertw\rVert_2$, $\sum_k|c_k|\le\sqrt p\lVertc\rVert_2$, and $\sum_iG_{iw}=\sqrt nG_{\bar e,w}$. When endpoint vectors involve the resolvent, the first contraction uses their operator-norm bounds; the remaining coarse fluctuations are bounded uniformly before contracting. H\"older's inequality involves only finitely many moments at fixed $N$.

This proves \eqref{eq:moment-recursion}. Iteration removes pairs, with each leading coefficient bounded by $Cn^{-1}\delta^{-C}$. All errors retain the extra factor $n^{-1/2+C'_N\epsilon}$ relative to $n^{-N/2}$. Choose the preliminary exponent so that $C'_N\epsilon<1/4$. Odd moments terminate at a centered factor and have no leading term. This proves \eqref{eq:Wick}.

We express each leading covariance as a sum of products of analytic functions, compare its principal kernel with the Hardy-space kernel, and then calculate its contour integral by Fourier coefficients.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Hardy space and the kernel comparison.}\enspace
Write $\Sigma=\sum_j\lambda_j o_jo_j^{\top}$ with an orthonormal eigenbasis $(o_j)$, and set $b_j=\lambda_j/L$. The disk coordinate $w=\sqrt\gamma L/(L-z)$ takes the observation contour to $|w|=(1+\delta)^{-1}$. Define
\[
 f_j(w)=\frac1{\sqrt p}\frac{b_jw}{1-(1-b_j)w/\sqrt\gamma},
 \qquad
 K_f(w,\xi)=\frac1{1-\sum_j f_j(w)\overline{f_j(\xi)}}.
\]
Since $1-(1-b_j)/\sqrt\gamma\ge b_j$, we have
$\sup_{|w|<1}\sum_j|f_j(w)/w|^2\le1$.

The Hardy space $H^2(\mathbb D)$ is the Hilbert space of holomorphic functions $h(w)=\sum_{a\ge0}h_aw^a$ with $\lVerth\rVert_{H^2}^2=\sum_{a\ge0}|h_a|^2<\infty$. Its inner product is linear in the first argument. Evaluation at $\xi$ is represented by $k_\xi(w)=(1-w\bar\xi)^{-1}$, as follows by summing the coefficient inner product. For two kernels $K_1,K_2$, the notation $K_1\preceq K_2$ means
\[
 \sum_{a,b=1}^N c_a\bar c_b
 (K_2(w_a,w_b)-K_1(w_a,w_b))\ge0
\]
for every finite choice of points and complex coefficients. This is a positive-semidefinite-kernel order, not a pointwise order of complex numbers.

Let $\mathcal M_j$ be multiplication by $f_j$, let $S$ be multiplication by $w$, and let $\mathsf P_0$ project onto constants. For any finite collection $h_j\in H^2$, the pointwise Cauchy--Schwarz inequality on circles, followed by the limit as their radii tend to one, gives
$\lVert\sum_j(f_j/w)h_j\rVert_{H^2}^2\le\sum_j\lVerth_j\rVert_{H^2}^2$.
It follows that $\sum_j\mathcal M_j\mathcal M_j^*\preceq SS^*=I-\mathsf P_0$. For the positive map $\mathcal T(A)=\sum_j\mathcal M_jA\mathcal M_j^*$, telescoping gives
\[
 \sum_{a=0}^N\mathcal T^a(\mathsf P_0)
 \preceq\sum_{a=0}^N\mathcal T^a(I-\mathcal T(I))
 =I-\mathcal T^{N+1}(I)\preceq I.
\]
The kernel of the left side is the partial geometric series in $\sum_jf_j(w)\overline{f_j(\xi)}$. Letting $N\to\infty$ proves
\[
 K_f(w,\xi)\preceq\frac1{1-w\bar\xi}.
\]
If $A(w)$ is analytic and bounded by $C$, multiplication by $A$ on $H^2$ has norm at most $C$, by the same circle argument. Multiplying both kernel arguments by $A$ and $\bar A$ therefore preserves this comparison with an additional factor $C^2$. These elementary Hardy-space facts have been proved here to fix the conventions used in the integral.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{The covariance factors and the contour parameter.}\enspace
After diagonalizing only the deterministic covariance matrices, the signal covariance is a sum of $K_f/n$ multiplied by
\[
 A_{jk}(z)\overline{A_{jk}(z')},\qquad

\[
 b(t)=\frac{z'(t)}{\mathrm i}\,\psi(z(t))A(z(t)),

\]
The factor $z'(t)/\mathrm i$ is exactly the change from $dz/(2\pi\mathrm i)$ to $dt/(2\pi)$ in the contour integral. Expanding the geometric series and using Parseval's identity (\citealp[Section~9.5, pp.~180--182]{WhittakerWatson1920}) gives
\[
 \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{2\pi}

 \frac{dt\,ds}{(2\pi)^2}
 =\sum_{j\ge0}(1+\delta)^{-2j}|\widehat b(j)|^2
 \le\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|b(t)|^2dt.
\]
The equality can first be verified for finite Fourier sums and then obtained by $L^2$ approximation; it is the coefficient form of Parseval's identity. Summing the bounded nonnegative covariance coefficients proves the required $C\lVert\psi\rVert_{\infty,\Gamma_\delta}^2/n$ bound.

\[
 c_j(z)=m(\zeta(z))

 [\Sigma^{1/2}(I+m(\zeta(z))\Sigma)^{-1}\beta]_j.
\]
The two vectors in brackets have bounded Euclidean norm on the contour. The Cauchy--Schwarz inequality implies $\sum_j|c_j(z)|^2\le C$. Consequently, we have
\begin{align*}

 \frac1{2\pi\mathrm i}\oint\psi(z)c_j(z)\,dz\right|^2\\

 \le\frac Cn\lVert\psi\rVert_{\infty,\Gamma_\delta}^2.
\end{align*}
This uses Minkowski's integral inequality for the Euclidean norm and is valid for either sign of $\chi_4$.

After incorporating a multiplier and integrating, denote these feature integrals by $c_{r,j_1,\ldots,j_r}(\psi)$. The calculation above bounds the sum of their squared absolute values by $C\lVert\psi\rVert_\infty^2$. For two weights $\psi_1,\psi_2$, both

Real symmetry of the deterministic resolvent identifies the second expression with the covariance without conjugation. The fourth-cumulant term has the identical argument with its finite sequence of integrals. Complex endpoint vectors can be decomposed into real and imaginary parts before applying these bounds. Thus every pair coefficient used in the fixed-moment expansion has the asserted absolute integrated bound.

   +O(e^{-K(h+c_0)}).
\]
Since $0<\mathfrak q(0)<h$ uniformly, this proves the required separation. Multiplication of the endpoint factor by $\lambda/(1-\lambda)$ leaves $(\lambda-\kappa^{-1}-\upsilon)/\lambda\le1$. Multiplication by $\lambda/(\lambda-\kappa^{-1}-\upsilon)$ leaves $(1-\lambda)/\lambda\le C$. Therefore both positive parts have the stated mass and weighted bounds.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{The rational transform near the spectral edges.}\enspace
For $x=1+(\gamma^\circ)^{-1}+2(\gamma^\circ)^{-1/2}\cos t$, the denominator of \eqref{eq:lower-kernel} factors as
\[
 (\lambda-z_+)(\lambda-z_-),\qquad
 z_\pm=1+\sqrt{\gamma^\circ}e^{\pm\mathrm i t}.
\]
Multiplication by the endpoint factor produces a cubic numerator and a quadratic denominator. Its polynomial part is $\lambda-(1+\kappa^{-1}+\upsilon)$; its remainder is
\[
 (\kappa^{-1}+\upsilon-\gamma^\circ x)\lambda
 +(1+\kappa^{-1}+\upsilon)\gamma^\circ x
 -(\kappa^{-1}+\upsilon)(\gamma^\circ x-\gamma^\circ+1).
\]
Thus the integral is a bounded linear combination of $\int\eta$, $\int\lambda\,d\eta$, and
\[
 \frac{\mathcal J(z_+)-\mathcal J(z_-)}{z_+-z_-},\qquad
 \frac{z_+\mathcal J(z_+)-z_-\mathcal J(z_-)}{z_+-z_-}.
\]
At coincident roots these expressions are the derivatives of $\mathcal J$ and $z\mathcal J(z)$.

On the critical circle, $|f(z_\pm)|=e^{-Kh}$. If $|\sin t|\ge K^{-1}$, the first divided difference has absolute value at most $Ce^{-Kh}/|\sin t|$. If $|\sin t|<K^{-1}$, the segment between the two roots is at distance $O(K^{-2})$ from the circle. On that segment, $\operatorname{Re}\mathfrak q\ge h-CK^{-2}$ and $|\mathfrak q'|\le C$, so $|f'|\le CKe^{-Kh}$. The bounds on $\mathcal E$ give the same estimates for $\mathcal J$. The second divided difference is bounded by the first times a bounded root plus a bounded value of $\mathcal J$. Hence the transform is bounded by $Ce^{-Kh}\min(K,|\sin t|^{-1})$.

For $x$ at distance at most $\upsilon$ beyond an endpoint, the roots are within $C\sqrt\upsilon$ of their coincident value. The derivative bound is then $CKe^{-Kh+CK\sqrt\upsilon}\le C'Ke^{-Kh}$, because $K\sqrt\upsilon\to0$. On the support interval we have
$(x-x_-)(x_+-x)=4(\gamma^\circ)^{-1}\sin^2t$.
Also $x_+-x_-$ is bounded above and below, and $\upsilon=o(K^{-2})$. These facts imply \eqref{eq:lower-transform} for the continuous signed measure, with the weight \eqref{eq:edge-weight}.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Replacement by atomic measures.}\enspace
For each nonzero positive part $\eta^+$ or $\eta^-$, divide by its mass and integrate the continuous vector
\[
 (1,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda^M,\lambda^{-1}),\qquad
 M=\lceil C_1K\rceil.
\]
The integral lies in the convex hull of the image of the compact interval. Any convex combination with more than $M+3$ points has affinely dependent vectors. Subtract a suitable multiple of an affine dependence from its nonnegative weights until one weight is zero, and repeat. Limits of such combinations are also in the same compact convex hull. We obtain a positive atomic measure with at most $M+3$ points and exactly the displayed integrals. This is the finite-dimensional positive-cubature argument underlying \citet[Theorem~2]{BayerTeichmann2006}; the argument just given also allows the additional continuous function $\lambda^{-1}$.

Multiply the atomic weights by the endpoint factor. Endpoint atoms then have zero weight and are deleted. The target is unchanged, since its formula uses only the matched integrals of $1,\lambda,\lambda^{-1}$. The weighted bounds remain valid by the pointwise inequalities in Step~1.

The rational integrands in Step~2 are holomorphic and uniformly bounded in a fixed complex neighborhood of $[\kappa^{-1}+\upsilon,1]$: their poles lie near the critical circle, at positive distance from that interval. Lemma~\ref{lem:analytic-approximation} supplies polynomial approximations of degree $M$ with error $C\rho_0^{-M}$ for some fixed $\rho_0>1$. Choose $C_1$ so that this error is at most $e^{-K(h+c_0)}$. Moment matching then changes the transform by at most a constant times this quantity. Since $\mathcal W$ has a positive lower bound on the enlarged interval, it is absorbed into \eqref{eq:lower-transform}. This gives the asserted atomic $\nu_0,\nu_1$.

       \left(\log\frac4{\sqrt\varepsilon}-1\right).
      \]
      For $j\ge1$,  we have
      \[
       0\le H_{2j}-H_j\le\log2,
       \qquad
       0<
       \left(\frac{\binom{2j}{j}}{4^j}\right)^2
       \le1.
      \]
      Thus, for $0<\varepsilon\le1/4$, the absolute sum of the
      terms with $j\ge2$ is bounded by
      \[
       \left(\log\frac4{\sqrt\varepsilon}+2\log2\right)
       \frac{\varepsilon^2}{1-\varepsilon}
       \le
       C\varepsilon^2\log\frac4{\sqrt\varepsilon}.
      \]
      We have therefore established the uniform expansion
      \begin{equation}\label{eq:K-log-expansion}
       \mathbf K(1-\varepsilon)
       =
       \log\frac4{\sqrt\varepsilon}
       +
       \frac{\varepsilon}{4}
       \left(\log\frac4{\sqrt\varepsilon}-1\right)
       +
       O\left(
       \varepsilon^2\log\frac4{\sqrt\varepsilon}
       \right).
      \end{equation}
      
      Now take $\varepsilon=(1-a)^2b$ and write
      $\mathcal L=\log(4/\sqrt b)$. Uniformly for
      $0<a,b\le1/4$,
      \[
       \log\frac4{\sqrt\varepsilon}
       =
       \mathcal L-\log(1-a)
       =
       \mathcal L+a+O(a^2).
      \]
      Since $\varepsilon=b+O(ab)$, substituting into
      \eqref{eq:K-log-expansion} gives
      \[
       \mathbf K(1-(1-a)^2b)
       =
       \mathcal L+a+\frac b4(\mathcal L-1)
       +O\bigl(\mathcal L(a^2+ab+b^2)\bigr).
      \]
      In particular,  we have
      \begin{equation}\label{eq:alpha-denominator-expansion}
       \frac{\mathbf K(1-(1-a)^2b)}{\mathcal L}
       =
       1+\frac a{\mathcal L}
       +\frac b4\left(1-\frac1{\mathcal L}\right)
       +O(a^2+ab+b^2).
      \end{equation}
      Here $\mathcal L\ge\log8$, so the constants remain uniform
      even when $a$ and $b$ approach zero at different rates.
      
      \par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Combining the expansions.}\enspace
      Equations~\eqref{eq:alpha-unit-integral},
      \eqref{eq:alpha-numerator-expansion}, and
      \eqref{eq:alpha-denominator-expansion}, together with
      $(1+x)^{-1}=1-x+O(x^2)$ for sufficiently small $|x|$, imply
      \[
       \frac{\mathcal L}{2\sqrt a}\alpha(\kappa,\gamma)
       =
       1+a\left(\frac13-\frac1{\mathcal L}\right)
        +b\left(\frac14+\frac1{4\mathcal L}\right)
        +O(a^2+ab+b^2).
      \]
      All remainders are bounded by a single constant throughout
      a sufficiently small rectangle $0<a\le a_0$, $0<b\le b_0$.
      Using $a=\kappa^{-1}$, $b=\gamma^{-1}$, and
      $2\mathcal L=\log(16\gamma)$ proves
      \eqref{eq:alpha-joint-second} with the stated bound on
      $R(\kappa,\gamma)$.
      The coefficients of $\kappa^{-1}$ and $\gamma^{-1}$ are
      uniformly bounded, so \eqref{eq:alpha-joint-uniform} follows.
      Finally, $\log(16\gamma)/\log\gamma\to1$, which proves
      \eqref{eq:alpha-joint}.

\label{lem:elliptic-identities}
For $0<a<1$, let
\[
 s_u=\operatorname{sn}(u\mid a),\qquad
 c_u=\operatorname{cn}(u\mid a),\qquad
 d_u=\operatorname{dn}(u\mid a),
\]
and define $s_v,c_v,d_v$ analogously. Then we have $s_u^2+c_u^2=1, d_u^2+a s_u^2=1$, 
\begin{align}\label{eq:elliptic-identities}
 \operatorname{sn}(\mathbf F(\phi\mid a)\mid a)&=\sin\phi,
 \notag\\
 \operatorname{sn}(u+v\mid a)
 &=\frac{s_uc_vd_v+s_vc_ud_u}{1-a s_u^2s_v^2}, 
 \operatorname{cn}(u+v\mid a)
 =\frac{c_uc_v-s_us_vd_ud_v}{1-a s_u^2s_v^2},\notag\\
 \operatorname{dn}(u+v\mid a)
 &=\frac{d_ud_v-a s_us_vc_uc_v}{1-a s_u^2s_v^2}.
\end{align}
Jacobi's imaginary transformation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:imaginary-transform}
\begin{aligned}
 \operatorname{sn}(\mathrm i y\mid a)
 &=\mathrm i\frac{\operatorname{sn}(y\mid1-a)}{\operatorname{cn}(y\mid1-a)},&
 \operatorname{cn}(\mathrm i y\mid a)
 &=\frac1{\operatorname{cn}(y\mid1-a)},\\
 \operatorname{dn}(\mathrm i y\mid a)
 &=\frac{\operatorname{dn}(y\mid1-a)}{\operatorname{cn}(y\mid1-a)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The function $\operatorname{sn}(\,\cdot\mid a)$ has periods $4\mathbf K(a)$ and $2\mathrm i\mathbf K(1-a)$; hence $\operatorname{sn}^2(\,\cdot\mid a)$ has periods $2\mathbf K(a)$ and $2\mathrm i\mathbf K(1-a)$.

We first construct a conformal coordinate, then derive the real formula \eqref{eq:explicit-potential}, and finally verify the period, the level curve, the exponent, and the contour expression \eqref{eq:gK-contour}.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{The conformal coordinate and harmonic measure.}\enspace
The map
\[
 T_\delta(z)=\frac{R_\delta-L+z}{R_\delta+L-z}
\]
sends $\mathbb D_\delta$ to the right half-plane and sends the slit $[\ell,L]$ to $[k,1]$. Hence $T_\delta(z)^2$ sends
$\mathbb D_\delta\setminus[\ell,L]$ to
\[
 \mathbb C\setminus\bigl((-\infty,0]\cup[k^2,1]\bigr).
\]
The standard inversion formulas for the Jacobi function give a local holomorphic coordinate $U_\delta$ characterized by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U-coordinate}
 T_\delta(z)=k\operatorname{sn}(U_\delta(z)\mid k^2),
 \qquad
 0<\operatorname{Re}U_\delta(z)<\mathbf K(k^2),
\end{equation}
where different determinations differ by integer multiples of
$2\mathrm i\mathbf K(1-k^2)$. One way to fix the normalization is to start on the real interval $L-R_\delta<z<\ell$, where $0<T_\delta(z)<k$, and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U-real-app}
 U_\delta(z)=
 \mathbf F\left(
 \arcsin\frac{T_\delta(z)}k
 \,\middle|\,k^2
 \right).
\end{equation}
The inverse and period statements in \eqref{eq:U-coordinate} are the Jacobi inversion and period formulas cited in Lemma~\ref{lem:elliptic-identities}.

The outer circle corresponds to $\operatorname{Re}U_\delta=0$, while the two sides of the slit correspond to
$\operatorname{Re}U_\delta=\mathbf K(k^2)$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:u-via-U}
 1-\frac{\operatorname{Re}U_\delta(z)}{\mathbf K(k^2)}
\end{equation}
is single-valued and harmonic on the slit disk, extends continuously with value one on $\Gamma_\delta$ and zero on $[\ell,L]$, and is bounded between zero and one. By uniqueness for the Dirichlet problem, it equals $u_\delta$ in \eqref{eq:potential}. Equivalently, it is the harmonic measure of $\Gamma_\delta$ in $\mathbb D_\delta\setminus[\ell,L]$; see \citet[Sections~4.1 and~4.3]{ransford1995potential}.

At $z=0$, $0<T_\delta(0)<k$, so \eqref{eq:U-real-app} gives
\[
 U_\delta(0)=
 \mathbf F\left(
 \arcsin\frac{R_\delta-L}{k(R_\delta+L)}
 \,\middle|\,k^2
 \right),
\]
which is real.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{Derivation of the pointwise real formula.}\enspace
Fix $z\in\mathbb D_\delta\setminus[\ell,L]$ and choose a determination
$U_\delta(z)=x+\mathrm i y$ with
$
 0<x<\mathbf K(k^2),
 |y|<\mathbf K(1-k^2).
$
Put
$
 r=\operatorname{sn}^2(x\mid k^2),
 s=\operatorname{sn}^2(y\mid1-k^2).
$
The addition formulas \eqref{eq:elliptic-identities} and the imaginary transformation \eqref{eq:imaginary-transform}, applied to \eqref{eq:U-coordinate}, give
\begin{align*}
 &|T_\delta(z)|^2
 =\frac{k^2(r+(1-r)s)}{1-(1-k^2r)s}, \quad
 |k^2-T_\delta(z)^2|
 =\frac{k^2(1-r+(1-k^2)rs)}{1-(1-k^2r)s}, \\
 &|1-T_\delta(z)^2|
 =\frac{1-k^2r-(1-k^2)s}{1-(1-k^2r)s}.
\end{align*}
In particular, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r-from-T}
 \frac{1+|T_\delta(z)|^2-|1-T_\delta(z)^2|}
 {k^2+|T_\delta(z)|^2+|k^2-T_\delta(z)^2|}
 =r.
\end{equation}
Since $\operatorname{Re}T_\delta(z)>0$ and
\[
 \bigl(1+|T_\delta(z)|^2\bigr)^2
 -|1-T_\delta(z)^2|^2
 =4\bigl(\operatorname{Re}T_\delta(z)\bigr)^2,
\]
rationalizing the numerator in \eqref{eq:r-from-T} shows that the square of the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:potential-amplitude} equals $r$. Hence
$
 b_\delta(z)=\operatorname{sn}(x\mid k^2) 
 $
 and
 $
 0<b_\delta(z)<1.
$
Real inversion in the first identity of \eqref{eq:elliptic-identities} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Re-U}
 \operatorname{Re}U_\delta(z)
 =
 \mathbf F(\arcsin b_\delta(z)\mid k^2).
\end{equation}
Substitution of \eqref{eq:Re-U} into \eqref{eq:u-via-U} proves \eqref{eq:explicit-potential}. As $z$ approaches the slit, $x\to\mathbf K(k^2)$ and hence $b_\delta(z)\to1$; as $z$ approaches $\Gamma_\delta$, $x\to0$ and hence $b_\delta(z)\to0$. This proves the stated continuous boundary values, including the two endpoints by continuity of the inverse map.

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{The complex function $q_\delta$ and its period.}\enspace
Start with the real value of $U_\delta$ at zero in \eqref{eq:U-real-app} and continue it locally along paths in the slit disk. On every such local branch, \eqref{eq:u-via-U} gives the continued harmonic conjugate with $\widetilde u_\delta(0)=0$ as
$
 u_\delta(z)+\mathrm i\widetilde u_\delta(z)
 =
 1-\frac{U_\delta(z)}{\mathbf K(k^2)}.
$
Therefore \eqref{eq:complex-potential} is equivalently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-via-U}
 q_\delta(z)
 =
 \frac{\pi\{\mathbf K(k^2)-U_\delta(z)\}}
 {\mathbf K(1-k^2)}.
\end{equation}
Changing $U_\delta$ by $2j\mathrm i\mathbf K(1-k^2)$ changes $q_\delta$ by $-2j\pi\mathrm i$. Hence the local determinations of $q_\delta$ agree modulo $2\pi\mathrm i$, and its real part is the one stated in the lemma. Formula \eqref{eq:q0-explicit} follows from the preceding value of $U_\delta(0)$.

On the slit,
$\operatorname{Re}U_\delta=\mathbf K(k^2)$, so the two boundary values of $q_\delta$ are purely imaginary. Conjugation symmetry of \eqref{eq:U-coordinate} gives
\[
 q_{\delta,-}(x)
 =
 -q_{\delta,+}(x)
 \pmod{2\pi\mathrm i},
 \qquad \ell<x<L.
\]

\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{The level curve and the exponent.}\enspace
The equation $u_\delta(z)=\delta$ is equivalent to
\[
 \operatorname{Re}U_\delta(z)
 =
 (1-\delta)\mathbf K(k^2).
\]
Using \eqref{eq:U-coordinate} and solving the definition of $T_\delta$ for $z$, a full parametrization is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inner-curve}
 z_\delta(\theta)

 1-k\operatorname{sn}\left(
 (1-\delta)\mathbf K(k^2)
 -\mathrm i\theta\mathbf K(1-k^2)/\pi
 \,\middle|\,k^2
 \right)}
 {
 1+k\operatorname{sn}\left(
 (1-\delta)\mathbf K(k^2)
 -\mathrm i\theta\mathbf K(1-k^2)/\pi
 \,\middle|\,k^2
 \right)},
 \qquad 0\le\theta<2\pi.
\end{equation}
The map $z\mapsto U_\delta(z)$ is conformal after identifying values that differ by $2\mathrm i\mathbf K(1-k^2)$; hence \eqref{eq:inner-curve} is an analytic Jordan curve. The line
$\operatorname{Re}U_\delta=\mathbf K(k^2)$ maps to the slit. The inverse map in \eqref{eq:inner-curve} is analytic with bounded derivative when $\operatorname{Re}U$ lies in a sufficiently small fixed neighborhood of $\mathbf K(k^2)$ and $\operatorname{Im}U$ is taken modulo $2\mathbf K(1-k^2)$, uniformly under \eqref{eq:aspect-regime}. Comparing the two vertical lines therefore gives the Hausdorff distance
\[
 \operatorname{dist}_{\mathrm H}
 (\Lambda_\delta,[\ell,L])
 \le C\delta.
\]
Moreover, zero has real coordinate $U_\delta(0)$, and
$
 U_\delta(0)
 <
 (1-\delta)\mathbf K(k^2)
$,

Set $\tau=\gamma(1+\delta)^2$. Since
$R_\delta=L\sqrt\tau$ and $\ell=L/\kappa$,
\[
 k=
 \frac{\sqrt\tau-1+\kappa^{-1}}

 \qquad
 \frac{R_\delta-L}{k(R_\delta+L)}
 =
 \frac{(\sqrt\tau-1)/(\sqrt\tau+1)}k.
\]

\begin{equation}\label{eq:u0-alpha}
 u_\delta(0)

 \alpha(\kappa,\tau)

 \alpha\bigl(\kappa,\gamma(1+\delta)^2\bigr).
\end{equation}

Evaluating at zero and using \eqref{eq:u0-alpha} proves the exact identity in \eqref{eq:alpha-delta}.

Under \eqref{eq:aspect-regime}, $\gamma$ is eventually bounded above and bounded away from one. \[
 u_\delta(0)
 =
 \alpha(\kappa,\gamma)+O(\delta),
\]

$
 \delta<u_\delta(0)

$
 u_0(0)=\alpha(\kappa,\gamma).
$

For
$

$
 T_\delta(\mu(t))

 \mathrm i\cot(t/2).

\[
 y(t)=
 \mathbf F\left(
 \arctan\frac{\cot(t/2)}k

 \right),
\]

$
 k\operatorname{sn}(\mathrm i y(t)\mid k^2)
 =
 \mathrm i\cot(t/2).
$
Thus one determination on the outer circle is
$
 U_\delta(\mu(t))
 =
 \mathrm i y(t),
$
and \eqref{eq:q-via-U} gives
\[
 q_\delta(\mu(t))
 =
 \frac{
 \pi\{\mathbf K(k^2)-\mathrm i y(t)\}}
 {\mathbf K(1-k^2)}
 \pmod{2\pi\mathrm i}.
\]
As $t$ increases counterclockwise from $0$ to $2\pi$, $y(t)$ decreases from $\mathbf K(1-k^2)$ to $-\mathbf K(1-k^2)$. Hence $q_\delta$ increases by $2\pi\mathrm i$, fixing the sign of the period asserted above. Finally, we have
\[
 q_\delta(0)

 \frac{\pi\mathbf K(k^2)}

Therefore the coordinate, period, and formula \eqref{eq:q0-explicit} remain valid when $R_\delta,L,\ell$ are replaced by any positive numbers $R,L,\ell$ with $R>L>\ell$. This is the form used for the analogous potential in Appendix~\ref{app:lower}.

\label{lem:testing-tools}
      Let $\pi$ be a probability measure on a measurable parameter space
      $(\Theta,\mathcal T)$, and let $M$ be a Markov kernel from
      $(\Theta,\mathcal T)$ to a measurable observation space
      $(\mathsf X,\mathcal A)$. For $\mathcal C\in\mathcal T$ with

        A\in\mathcal A.

\label{lem:matrix-volume}
For $t>0$, integrate over real symmetric positive definite $s\times s$ matrices with Lebesgue measure $d\mathsf Q=\prod_{1\le i\le j\le s}d\mathsf Q_{ij}$. Then
\begin{align*}
 &\int_{\mathsf Q\succ0}\exp\bigl(-t\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf Q-I_s-\log\mathsf Q)\bigr)\,d\mathsf Q
 =e^{ts}t^{-s(t+(s+1)/2)}\pi^{s(s-1)/4}
       \prod_{j=1}^s\Gamma\left(t+\frac{s+2-j}{2}\right).
\end{align*}
For $t\asymp d$ and $s=O(\log d)$, the absolute value of the logarithm of this integral is at most $Cs^2\log d$.
<!-- end lemma-19 -->
<!-- proof-24: body_md -->
Write $\mathsf Q=TT^\top$ with $T$ lower triangular and positive diagonal. Differentiating its independent entries successively gives the Jacobian $2^s\prod_{j=1}^sT_{jj}^{s+1-j}$ for the stated measure. Each off-diagonal integral equals $\sqrt{\pi/t}$. Each diagonal integral, after the substitution $y=tT_{jj}^2$, equals
\[
 \frac12 t^{-(t+(s+2-j)/2)}
 \Gamma\left(t+\frac{s+2-j}{2}\right).
\]
Multiplying these factors, the Jacobian constant, and $e^{ts}$ proves the formula. In Stirling's formula the terms of orders $st\log t$ and $st$ cancel the displayed power and exponential. Each of the $s$ gamma arguments differs from $t$ by $O(s)$, so all remaining terms are bounded by $Cs^2\log d$. This proves the asserted estimate.
