<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:0}
\emph{Minkowski space} \(\mathbb{R}^{1,3}\) is the four-dimensional real vector space equipped with the indefinite non-degenerate bilinear form called the Minkowski metric. Formally, it is the pair \((\mathbb{R}^4, \eta)\), where \(\eta : \mathbb{R}^4 \times \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}\) is defined by
\begin{equation}
\eta(x, y) = x^0 y^0 - x^1 y^1 - x^2 y^2 - x^3 y^3,
\end{equation}
for vectors \(x = (x^0, x^1, x^2, x^3)\) and \(y = (y^0, y^1, y^2, y^3)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^4\). In matrix representation, the metric tensor is given by
\begin{equation}
\eta = \mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1).
\end{equation}
The coordinate \(x^0 = ct\) represents the time dimension (with \(c\) the speed of light), while \((x^1, x^2, x^3)\) correspond to spatial coordinates. The invariance of the interval
\begin{equation}
ds^2 = \eta_{\mu\nu} dx^\mu dx^\nu = (dx^0)^2 - (dx^1)^2 - (dx^2)^2 - (dx^3)^2
\end{equation}
under Lorentz transformations \(\mathrm{O}(1,3)\) defines the geometry of Minkowski space \cite{Lachieze2005}, \cite{Varlamov2004}.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:1}
The O(1,3) group is the Lorentz group, comprising all linear transformations that preserve the Minkowski space-time metric. Formally, it is the generalized orthogonal group associated with a metric of signature $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ or $\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$. It consists of all $4 \times 4$ matrices with real entries that satisfy the relation \cite{Lachieze2005}
\begin{equation}
\Lambda^{T} \eta \Lambda = \eta .
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:2}
The group $\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$ is defined as the proper Lorentz group, consisting of all matrices $\Lambda \in \mathrm{O}(1,3)$ with determinant $+1$, that is,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SO}(1,3) := \left\lbrace \Lambda \in \mathrm{O}(1,3) \mid \det(\Lambda) = +1 \right\rbrace.
\end{equation}
This group preserves the space-time orientation (excluding spatial and temporal reflections) but may include transformations that reverse the direction of time (non-orthochronous) \cite{Lachieze2005}.
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\label{def:3}
The group $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ is the proper, orthochronous Lorentz group, which is a subgroup of $\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$ that preserves the direction of time. Specifically,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3) := \left\lbrace \Lambda \in \mathrm{SO}(1,3) \mid \Lambda_{00} \geq 1 \right\rbrace,
\end{equation}
where the condition $\Lambda_{00} \geq 1$ ensures that the transformation does not reverse the time direction \cite{Lachieze2005}.
<!-- end definition-4 -->
<!-- definition-5: body_md -->
\label{def:4}
The Clifford algebra \(\text{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{1,3})\) is the associative algebra generated by the vector space \(\mathbb{R}^{1,3}\) equipped with the Minkowski metric \(\eta = \text{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)\), subject to the fundamental relation:
\begin{equation}
\gamma_i \gamma_j + \gamma_j \gamma_i = 2\eta_{ij}\mathbf{1}, \quad \text{for } i,j = 0,1,2,3,
\end{equation}
where \(\{\gamma_0, \gamma_1, \gamma_2, \gamma_3\}\) are the generators of the algebra, and \(\mathbf{1}\) denotes the identity element. Equivalently, for any vectors \(u, v \in \mathbb{R}^{1,3}\), the Clifford product satisfies:
\begin{equation}
uv + vu = 2\eta(u,v)\mathbf{1}.
\end{equation}
As a real vector space, \(\text{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{1,3})\) has dimension \(2^4 = 16\), and it is isomorphic to \(\text{Mat}(4, \mathbb{R})\) as an algebra \cite{Lachieze2005}.
<!-- end definition-5 -->
<!-- definition-6: body_md -->
\label{def:5}
The Clifford algebra \(\text{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{1,3})\) is the quotient of the tensor algebra \(T(\mathbb{R}^{1,3})\) by the ideal generated by elements of the form \(v \otimes v - \eta(v,v)\mathbf{1}\) for all \(v \in \mathbb{R}^{1,3}\) \cite{Lachieze2005}, \cite{Varlamov2004}.
<!-- end definition-6 -->
<!-- definition-7: body_md -->
\label{def:6}
The group $\mathrm{Mat}_2(\mathbb{C})$ consists of all complex $2 \times 2$ matrices \cite{Lachieze2005}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Mat}_2(\mathbb{C}) := \left\lbrace
\begin{bmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{bmatrix}
\ \middle|\ a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{C} \right\rbrace.
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-7 -->
<!-- definition-8: body_md -->
\label{def:7}
The group $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is the complex special linear group consisting of all $2 \times 2$ complex matrices with determinant $+1$, that is \cite{Lachieze2005},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C}) := \left\lbrace M \in \mathrm{Mat}_2(\mathbb{C}) \mid \det M = +1 \right\rbrace.
\end{equation}
<!-- end definition-8 -->
<!-- definition-9: body_md -->
\label{def:8}
The set $\mathrm{Herm}(2)$ consists of all $2 \times 2$ Hermitian matrices \cite{Lachieze2005}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Herm}(2) := \left\lbrace
H \in \mathrm{Mat}_2(\mathbb{C}) \ \middle|\ H^\dagger = H
\right\rbrace,
\end{equation}
where $H^\dagger$ denotes the conjugate transpose of $H$. Explicitly, any $H \in \mathrm{Herm}(2)$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
H = \begin{bmatrix}
t + z & x - iy \\
x + iy & t - z
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $t, x, y, z \in \mathbb{R}$.
<!-- end definition-9 -->
<!-- definition-10: body_md -->
\label{def:9}
The group $\mathrm{Spin}(1,3)$ is the double connected cover of the proper orthochronous Lorentz group $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ and is a subgroup of the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{1,3})$ associated with Minkowski space $\mathbb{R}^{1,3}$. This group consists of elements $S \in \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{1,3})$ satisfying \cite{Lachieze2005}, \cite{Varlamov2004}
\begin{equation}
S \gamma^{\mu} S^{-1} = \Lambda^{\mu}_{\nu} \gamma^{\nu},
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda \in \mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$. Furthermore, $\mathrm{Spin}(1,3)$ is isomorphic to the group $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$.
<!-- end definition-10 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:10}
The \emph{Minkowski Space-time:} is prevalent in physics, particularly in the context of special relativity. It emphasizes the \textit{affine} and \textit{vector space} structure. One can add points (events) with coordinates and there is a privileged origin. The primary focus is on the global $(t, x, y, z)$ coordinate system and the invariant interval $ds^2 = \eta_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu$ \cite{Misner-Thorne-Wheeler1973}.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- definition-11: body_md -->
\label{def:11}
The \emph{Minkowski manifold} is the quadruple $(\mathbb{R}^{1,3}, \eta, \mathrm{SO}^+(1,3), \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C}))$, where the metric $\eta$ defines the causal geometry, the group $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ describes its isometric symmetries, and the group $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$ acts as its double cover, providing the spin structure and a fundamental representation via the matrices of $\mathrm{Herm}(2)$ \cite{Lachieze2005}.
<!-- end definition-11 -->
<!-- definition-12: body_md -->
\label{def:12}
The \emph{Dirac matrices in Minkowski space} are defined in the Weyl (chiral) representation as \cite{Schmidt-Watcher2007}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:min-gamma}
\gamma^\mu_{ab} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & \sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} \\
\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}_{\dot{\alpha}\beta} & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^\mu = (\mathbf{1}_2, \sigma^i)$ and $\bar{\sigma}^\mu = (\mathbf{1}_2, -\sigma^i)$ denoting the Pauli matrices in the Minkowskian space. The \emph{spinor indices} $\alpha, \beta$ run over $\{1,2\}$ for left-handed Weyl spinors, while $\dot{\alpha}, \dot{\beta}$ run over $\{\dot{1}, \dot{2}\}$ for right-handed Weyl spinors.
<!-- end definition-12 -->
<!-- definition-13: body_md -->
\label{def:13}
Let $\mathcal{M} = \mathbb{R}^{1,3}$ be Minkowski space with coordinates $(x^0, x^1, x^2, x^3)$ and metric $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$ \cite{Lachieze2005}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{1-form} (or differential 1-form) is a section of the cotangent bundle $T^*M$, which can be expressed locally as \cite{Misner-Thorne-Wheeler1973}
    \begin{equation}
    \omega = \omega_\mu dx^\mu = \omega_0 dx^0 + \omega_1 dx^1 + \omega_2 dx^2 + \omega_3 dx^3,
    \end{equation}
    where $\omega_\mu$ are smooth functions and $\{dx^\mu\}$ form a basis for the cotangent space.

    \item A \textbf{2-form} is an antisymmetric $(0,2)$-tensor that can be written as \cite{Misner-Thorne-Wheeler1973}
    \begin{equation}
    F = \frac{1}{2} F_{\mu\nu} dx^\mu \wedge dx^\nu,
    \end{equation}
    where $F_{\mu\nu} = -F_{\nu\mu}$ are smooth functions, and the wedge product satisfies $dx^\mu \wedge dx^\nu = -dx^\nu \wedge dx^\mu$.
\end{enumerate}

The exterior derivative acts on these forms as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For a 0-form (function) $f$: $df = \partial_\mu f dx^\mu$
    \item For a 1-form $\omega$: $d\omega = \partial_\mu \omega_\nu dx^\mu \wedge dx^\nu$
\end{itemize}

The Hodge star operator $\star$, which depends on the metric $\eta$, maps $p$-forms to $(4-p)$-forms in four dimensions.

<!-- definition-14: body_md -->
Spinor coordinates establish a correspondence between Minkowski space-time and the set of Hermitian matrices $\mathrm{Herm}(2) \subset \mathrm{Mat}_2(\mathbb{C})$. For coordinates $x^{\mu} \in M$, this correspondence is given by \cite{Lachieze2005}, \cite{Torres2010}
\begin{equation}
X = x^{\mu} \sigma_{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix}
x^{1\dot{1}} & x^{1\dot{2}} \\
x^{2\dot{1}} & x^{2\dot{2}}
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
x^0 + x^3 & x^1 - i x^2 \\
x^1 + i x^2 & x^0 - x^3
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\mu = (\mathbf{1}, \vec{\sigma})$ with $\mu = 0,1,2,3$.
<!-- end definition-14 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
Regarding notation, the use of indices in Minkowski space-time (Minkowski manifold) for spinors can be substituted by a change of indices for a standard tensor. For example, a vector $x^{\mu}$ can be expressed in spinor notation as $x^{\alpha\dot{\alpha}}$, and similarly, the derivative operator $\partial_{\mu}$ can be written as $\partial_{\alpha\dot{\alpha}}$ \cite{Lachieze2005}, \cite{Torres2010}.

Spinor coordinates can be explicitly defined as follows \cite{Lachieze2005}, \cite{Torres2010}:
\begin{align}\label{eq:coordinates}
x_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}  & \equiv \ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sigma_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}^{\mu} x_{\mu}, \\
\label{eq:coordinates1}\partial^{\alpha\dot{\beta}} & \equiv \ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sigma^{\alpha\dot{\beta}}_{\nu} \partial^{\nu},
\end{align}
for $\mu = 0,1,2,3$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:coordinates} and Definition~\ref{def:13}, we can express the differential 1-form and 2-form in spinor notation as follows \cite{Torres2010}
\begin{align}\label{eq:relacion1}
dx_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} & \equiv \ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sigma_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}^{\mu} dx_{\mu}, \\ \label{eq:relacion2}
dx_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} & = \ i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda}dx_{\mu}\wedge dx_{\nu},
\end{align}
where $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda}$ is the Levi-Civita tensor.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For the second relation, we express the spinorial differential form as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dx}
dx_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} = \frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}^{\mu} \bar{\sigma}^{\nu\dot{\beta}\alpha} - \sigma_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}^{\nu} \bar{\sigma}^{\mu\dot{\beta}\alpha}) dx_{\mu} \wedge dx_{\nu}.
\end{equation}

Using the Pauli matrix identity:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spin}
\sigma_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}^{\mu} \bar{\sigma}^{\nu\dot{\beta}\alpha} - \sigma_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}^{\nu} \bar{\sigma}^{\mu\dot{\beta}\alpha} = 2i \epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\rho} \sigma_{\lambda\alpha\dot{\beta}},
\end{equation}
and substituting \eqref{eq:spin} into \eqref{eq:dx}, we obtain the relation \eqref{eq:relacion2}.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
For the case of Dirac matrices, we employ a relation analogous to \eqref{eq:spin} which corresponds to the spin tensor $\sigma^{\mu\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}$, that is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tensor-espinorial}
\gamma_{ab}^{\mu}\gamma_{ab}^{\nu}-\gamma_{ab}^{\nu}\gamma_{ab}^{\mu}=2i\sigma_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}^{\mu\nu},
\end{equation}
where  $\gamma_{ab}^{\mu}$ is given by (\ref{eq:min-gamma}).

x_{\mu} &\longmapsto \psi(x_{\mu}),

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:Dirac-operator}
The Dirac operator in spinor notation is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dirac}
D_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}=\sqrt{2}(\Pi^{+}\otimes\partial_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}+\Pi^{-}\otimes\partial_{\dot{\alpha}\beta}),
\end{equation}
where $\Pi^{+}$ and $\Pi^{-}$ are matrices $2\times 2$:
\begin{equation}
\Pi^{+}=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1\\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}, \quad \Pi^{-}=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0\\
1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
First, we can rewrite Eq. (\ref{eq:coordinates1}) in the following form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coordinates2}
\sqrt{2}\partial^{\alpha\dot{\beta}} = \sigma^{\alpha\dot{\beta}}_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}.
\end{equation}

Multiplying Eq. (\ref{eq:min-gamma}) by $\partial_{\mu}$ yields the Dirac operator:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coordinates3}
\partial_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}_{ab} =
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \partial_{\mu}\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} \\
\partial_{\mu}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}_{\dot{\alpha}\beta} & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

This can, in turn, be expressed as a linear combination of matrices:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear}
\partial_\mu\gamma^{\mu}_{ab} = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1\\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix} \otimes \left(\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\partial_\mu\right) + \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0\\
1 & 0
\end{bmatrix} \otimes \left(\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}_{\dot{\alpha}\beta}\partial_{\mu}\right).
\end{equation}

Finally, substituting Eq. (\ref{eq:coordinates2}) into Eq. (\ref{eq:linear}), we obtain:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:final}
\partial_\mu\gamma^{\mu}_{ab} = \sqrt{2} \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1\\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix} \otimes \partial_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} + \sqrt{2} \begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0\\
1 & 0
\end{bmatrix} \otimes \partial_{\dot{\alpha}\beta},
\end{equation}
and denoting $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ as $\Pi^{+}$ and $\Pi^{-}$ respectively, we finally obtain (\ref{eq:Dirac}); with this, we conclude the proof.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:Tensor-Dirac}
From Definition (\ref{def:function}) and Eq. (\ref{eq:tensor-espinorial}) and the above definition, we construct a differential 2-form for the spinor field $\psi$ expressed through the Dirac operator in spinor notation. This is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2-form-diff}

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:spinor-forms}
Using the preceding propositions and following an approach analogous to the Dirac operator studied in Proposition 3.8, we can define differential 1-forms and 2-forms in spinor notation as follows.

The differential 1-form in spinor notation is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1-form}
x^{\alpha\dot{\beta}} = x^{\mu} dx^{\mu} + x^{\nu} dx^{\nu} + x^{\mu} dx^{\nu} \gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} \gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} + x^{\nu} dx^{\mu} \gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} \gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}},
\end{equation}

and the corresponding differential 2-form is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2-form}
dx^{\alpha\dot{\beta}} = x^{\mu} dx^{\mu} \wedge dx^{\nu} + x^{\nu} dx^{\nu} \wedge dx^{\mu} + x^{\mu} dx^{\nu} \gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} \gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} \wedge dx^{\mu} + x^{\nu} dx^{\mu} \gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} \gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} \wedge dx^{\nu}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We begin by expanding the expression for the spinor-valued 1-form:
\begin{align*}
x^{\alpha\dot{\beta}} & = (x^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} + x^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}})(dx^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} + dx^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}),\\
& = x^{\mu}dx^{\mu}(\gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}})^{2} + x^{\mu}dx^{\nu}\gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} + x^{\nu}dx^{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}} + x^{\nu}dx^{\nu}(\gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}})^{2}.
\end{align*}

Taking into account the  algebra property $(\gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}})^{2} = (\gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha\dot{\beta}})^{2} = \mathbb{I}_{4}$, we obtain equation (\ref{eq:1-form}). The 2-form in equation (\ref{eq:2-form}) is defined by hypothesis, following the standard construction of differential forms from the exterior derivative of the 1-form.
