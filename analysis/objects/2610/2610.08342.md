\label{th}
Let $\psi:M\to\mathbb{E}(\kappa,\tau)$ be a stable immersion of a disk with constant mean curvature $H>0$ and circular boundary. Suppose
\begin{equation}\label{eqcondiciones}
4H^2+\kappa>0,\qquad \kappa\leq 4\tau^2.
\end{equation}
Then, $\psi$ is an embedding onto a closed spherical cap of $S_H$.
<!-- end teo-1 -->
<!-- lem-1: body_md -->
\label{lemderivadaconormal}
Let be $M$ a disk and $u\not\equiv0$ a solution of $Lu=0$ with $u_{|_{\partial M}}=0$ and $L$ satisfying \eqref{eqoperadorestable}. If $\mu$ is the unit exterior conormal of $M$ along $\partial M$, then $\partial_\mu u$ cannot have four alternating boundary signs.
<!-- end lem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let us uniformize $M$ by $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$. Arguing by contradiction, assume that $\partial_\mu u$ alternates of sign at $\xi_1,...,\xi_4\in\partial M$. Now, we find four disjoint sets $\Omega_1,...,\Omega_4$ around $\xi_1,...,\xi_4$ respectively such that $u$ has the opposite sign of $\partial_\mu u$. This can be done by taking interior normal coordinates around $\xi_k$ and using that $u=0$ at $\partial\mathbb{D}$. Indeed, given $e^{i\vartheta}\in\partial\mathbb{D}$ we consider normal coordinates
$$
\Psi(\vartheta,r)=\exp_{e^{i\vartheta}}(-r\mu(e^{i\vartheta})),
$$
where $r\geq0$ since $\mu$ is the exterior conormal. Thus, $\partial_r\Psi(\vartheta,0)=-\mu(e^{i\vartheta})$. Define the function $U(\vartheta,r)=u(\Psi(\vartheta,r))$. One has $U(\vartheta,0)=0$. For fixed $\vartheta$ we make Taylor in the $r$-variable, obtaining
$$
U(\vartheta,r)=U(\vartheta,0)+r\partial_r U(\vartheta,0)+\mathcal{O}(r^2).
$$
The first term vanishes and for the second one we obtain $-\partial_\mu u(e^{i\vartheta})$ by the chain rule. Thus,
$$
u(\Psi(\vartheta,r))=-r\partial_\mu u(e^{i\vartheta})+\mathcal{O}(r^2).
$$
By continuity, there exists a compact arc $\mathcal{J}$ containing $p$ such that the term $\mathcal{O}(r^2)$ can be uniformly bounded by $Cr^2$, where $C>0$ and thus the sign of $u$ is opposite to the one of $\partial_\mu u$ at $\mathcal{J}$.

We state that $u$ has at least three nodal domains. Indeed, if $u$ has only two nodal domains, the alternating sets $\Omega_1,\Omega_3$ where $u$ has the same sign should belong to the same connected component. But this configuration would make impossible to join the sets $\Omega_2$ and $\Omega_4$ where $u$ has the same sign and opposite to the one of $\Omega_1\cup\Omega_3$.  This contradiction proves that $u$ has at least three nodal domains and hence Courant's nodal domain theorem yields that the zero eigenvalue is at least the third distinct one of $-L$. This implies the existence of two negative eigenvalues of $-L$, contradicting the stability.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lem-2: body_md -->
\label{lemmaholomorfa}
Let be $M$ a disk and $\Phi$ a holomorphic quadratic differential on $M$, smooth up to $\partial M$. Then, either $\Phi\equiv0$ or $\mathrm{Im}\Phi(\textbf{t},\textbf{t})$ changes of sign at least four times on $\partial M$, where $\textbf{t}$ is a unit boundary tangent.
<!-- end lem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We uniformize $M$ by $\bar{\mathbb{D}}$, with coordinates $\zeta=s+it$, write $\langle,\rangle=\lambda^2|d\zeta|^2$ and express $\Phi=\phi(\zeta)d\zeta^2$. If we parametrize the boundary $|\zeta|=1$ by $\beta(\vartheta)=e^{i\vartheta}$, the tangent vector $\beta'$ has length $\lambda$ and thus $\textbf{t}=\pm\frac{1}{\lambda}\beta'(\vartheta)=\frac{\pm i}{\lambda}\beta(\vartheta)$. Consequently, $d\zeta(\textbf{t})=(ds+idt)(\frac{\pm i}{\lambda}e^{i\vartheta})=\frac{\pm i}{\lambda}\zeta$ and therefore 
$$
\Phi(\textbf{t},\textbf{t})=\phi(\zeta)d\zeta^2(\textbf{t},\textbf{t})=\phi(\zeta)\frac{-\zeta^2}{\lambda^2}.
$$
In particular, along $\partial\mathbb{D}$ we have
$$
\mathrm{Im}\Phi(\textbf{t},\textbf{t})=-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\mathrm{Im}(\zeta^2\phi(\zeta)).
$$
By defining $F(\zeta)=\zeta^2\phi(\zeta)$ we conclude $\mathrm{Im}F=-\lambda^2 \mathrm{Im}\Phi(\textbf{t},\textbf{t})$ on $\partial\mathbb{D}$.

Assume that $\Phi\not\equiv 0$ and let us consider $w(\vartheta)=\mathrm{Im} F(e^{i\vartheta})$. We prove the existence of $\vartheta_1,...,\vartheta_4\in[0,2\pi)$ such that $w(\vartheta_1),...,w(\vartheta_4)$ have alternating signs. For that, bear in mind that the holomorphic function has, at least, an order two zero at $\zeta=0$, i.e. $F(0)=F'(0)=0$ and thus its Taylor series is  $F(\zeta)=\sum_{n=2}^\infty c_n\zeta^n$, on a disk $D_r$ of radius $r<1$. Thus, at its boundary $\zeta=re^{i\vartheta}$ and denoting $c_n=a_n+ib_n$ we have
$$
\mathrm{Im} F(r e^{i\vartheta})=\sum_{n=2}r^n(a_n\sin(n\vartheta)+b_n\cos(n\vartheta)).
$$
Set $w_r(\vartheta)=\mathrm{Im} F(re^{i\vartheta})$. By the orthogonality of the functions $\{1,\cos(n\vartheta),\sin(n\vartheta)\}$ in $L^2(0,2\pi)$ we have
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}w_r(\vartheta)\{1,\cos\vartheta,\sin\vartheta\}\,d\vartheta=0.
$$
Now, since $F$ is continuous up to $\partial\mathbb{D}$ and $w_r\to w$ uniformly, letting $r\nearrow1$ we conclude
\begin{equation}\label{eqalternanciaceros}
\int_0^{2\pi}w(\vartheta)\,d\vartheta=\int_0^{2\pi}w(\vartheta)\cos\vartheta\,d\vartheta=\int_0^{2\pi}w(\vartheta)\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta=0.
\end{equation}
The first equation in \eqref{eqalternanciaceros} yields the existence of $\vartheta_1,\vartheta_2\in[0,2\pi)$ such that $w$ alternates of sign; without losing generality assume $w(\vartheta_1)>0$ and $w(\vartheta_2)<0$. In particular, there exists $n_1\in(\vartheta_1,\vartheta_2)$ such that $w(n_1)=0$. This cannot be the final configuration for the alternating signs of $w$ since $w(0)=w(2\pi)$ but $w_{|_{[0,\vartheta_1]}}>0$ and $w_{|_{[\vartheta_2,2\pi]}}<0$. Thus, there must exist $n_2\in(0,\vartheta_1)$ such that $w(n_2)=0$ and $w<0$ for $\vartheta<n_2$ close enough to $n_2$. This gives three alternating signs of $w$. If there were no four alternating points, a compact identification of $[0,2\pi]$ with $\partial\mathbb{D}$ would give two points $\xi_1=e^{i n_1}$ and $\xi_2=e^{i n_2}$ that separate $\partial\mathbb{D}$ into two complementary arcs where $\mathrm{Im} F$ take different signs. Let $\vartheta\in[n_2,n_2+2\pi]$ and define
$$
P(\vartheta)=\cos\left(\vartheta-\frac{n_1+n_2}{2}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{n_1-n_2}{2}\right).
$$
If $\vartheta\in(n_2,n_1)$ then $P(\vartheta)>0$, and if $\vartheta\in(n_1,n_2+2\pi)$ then $P(\vartheta)<0$, with $P(n_1)=P(n_2)=0$. After reversing the sign if necessary we have $wP\geq0$ everywhere and $wP>0$ on a non-empty open interval. Thus,
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}w(\vartheta)P(\vartheta)\,d\vartheta>0.
$$
But this contradicts \eqref{eqalternanciaceros} by just evaluating the expression of $P$.

<!-- lem-3: body_md -->
\label{fluxlema}
With the previous notation, the following identity holds:
\begin{equation}\label{eqflux}
\frac{1}{2\pi d}\int_0^{2\pi d}a(\theta)\,d\theta=-HR,
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the number of times that $\partial M$ is mapped onto $\Gamma_R$. Furthermore, $HR\leq 1$ and equality holds if and only if $a\equiv -1$ and thus $b=0$ in \eqref{eqNab}.
<!-- end lem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $\Omega\subset\{z=0\}$ be disk bounded by $\Gamma_R$ with unit normal $\eta$. In particular, the positive orientation of $\partial\Omega$ is $\textbf{t}$, since $\{e_r,\textbf{t},\eta\}$ is positive. On $\partial M$ we have the positive frame $\{\textbf{t},\textbf{n}\}$. We also ask $\{\mu,\pm\textbf{t}\}$ to be positively oriented. Because $\mu=\epsilon\textbf{n}$ and $\{\textbf{n},-\textbf{t}\}$ has the same orientation of $\{\textbf{t},\textbf{n}\}$, then $\{\epsilon\textbf{n},-\epsilon\textbf{t}\}$ is positive. Hence, the orientation of $\partial M$ is determined by $-\epsilon\textbf{t}$. We consider thus the 2-cycle
$$
Z=[\psi(M)]+\epsilon d [\Omega],
$$
where $d\geq1$ is the number of times that $\Gamma_R$ is covered by $\partial M$. This cycle bounds a 3-chain whose boundary is $Z$, and the divergence theorem to $\xi$ on such a 3-chain yields
$$
0=\int_M\langle N,\xi\rangle+\epsilon d\int_\Omega\langle\eta,\xi\rangle.
$$
For the second integral, recall that in the polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$, the area element in $\{z=0\}$ is $\frac{rW_r}{\delta^2}dr\,d\theta$; here, $W_r=\sqrt{1+\tau^2r^2}$. Hence,
$$
\int_\Omega\langle\eta,\xi\rangle=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R\frac{r}{\delta^2}\,dr\,d\theta=\frac{\pi R^2}{\delta_R}.
$$
We integrate again on $M$, but this time the tangent component $\xi^\top$. Since $\xi$ is Killing, $\mathrm{div}_M\xi^\top=2H\langle\xi,N\rangle$ and the line element of the circle is $d\ell=|\gamma'(\theta)|d\theta=\frac{RW}{\delta_R}d\theta$, the divergence theorem on $M$ yields
$$
2H\int_M\nu=\int_M2H\langle \xi,N\rangle=\int_{\partial M}\langle \xi,\mu\rangle\,d\ell=\frac{\epsilon}{W}\int_{\partial M}a\,d\ell=\frac{\epsilon R}{\delta_R}\int_0^{2\pi d}a(\theta)\,d\theta.
$$
We conclude
$$
\frac{1}{2\pi d}\int_0^{2\pi d}a(\theta)\,d\theta=-HR.
$$
As $|a|\leq1$ we obtain the bound $HR\leq1$ and equality holds if and only if $a=-1$ and then $b=0$.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{remarkrelacionHR}
This relation gives an a priori restriction between the coordinate radius $R$ and the possible mean curvature $H$ of a compact CMC surface with boundary. Although it will not be used throughout the paper, it would be better to express this restriction in terms of the distance of $\Gamma_R$ to the rotation axis, hence in terms of the distance that a parallel in $S_H$ can attain to the rotation axis. Let us fix $(R,\theta_0,0)\in\Gamma_R$ and consider the radial geodesic $r\mapsto(r,\theta_0,0)$. Thus,
$$
\rho(R)=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{E}(\kappa,\tau)}(\Gamma_R,(0,0,0))=\int_0^R\frac{dr}{\delta(r)}=\int_0^R\frac{4\,dr}{4+\kappa r^2}=
\left\lbrace
\begin{array}{lc}
\vspace{.1cm}\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\arctan\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{\kappa}R}{2}\right)&\kappa>0,\\
R,&\kappa=0,\\
\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{-\kappa}}\mathrm{arctanh}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{-\kappa}R}{2}\right)&\kappa<0.
\end{array}
\right.
$$
Since $HR\leq1$ and $R\mapsto\rho(R)$ is strictly increasing in any case, the maximum distance to the rotation axis that $S_H$ attains is $r_H=\rho(1/H)$, that is
$$
r_H=\left\lbrace
\begin{array}{lc}
\vspace{.1cm}\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\arctan\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{2H}\right)&\kappa>0,\\
\dfrac{1}{H},&\kappa=0,\\
\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{-\kappa}}\mathrm{arctanh}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{-\kappa}}{2H}\right)&\kappa<0.
\end{array}
\right.
$$
Moreover, if equality holds then $a=-1$ and $b=0$ in \eqref{eqNab} and hence the tangent plane of $S_H$ along $\Gamma_R$ is spanned by $\{\textbf{t},\xi\}$ and consequently it is vertical, therefore $\Gamma_R$ is the \emph{equator} of $S_H$, the parallel of $S_H$ with largest distance to the rotation axis, and $S_H$ is a symmetric bi-graph along $\Gamma_{1/H}$.

<!-- pro-1: body_md -->
\label{propderconormalu}
Let us denote by $k_R$ and $-\uptau_R$ the geodesic curvature and the torsion of the circle $\Gamma_R$ in $\{z=0\}$, respectively. On $\partial M$ we have
\begin{equation}\label{eqderconormalu}
\partial_\mu u=-\epsilon\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\left(\uptau_R+\sigma(\textbf{t},\textbf{n})\right)=\epsilon\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\alpha',
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is such that $a=\cos\alpha$ and $b=\sin\alpha$.
<!-- end pro-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Recall that
$$
\partial_\mu u=\mu\langle X,N\rangle=\langle\overline{\nabla}_\mu X,N\rangle+\langle X,\overline{\nabla}_\mu N\rangle=\langle\overline{\nabla}_\mu X,N\rangle-\langle X,S\mu\rangle.
$$
We compute first the second term. Since $\mu=\epsilon\textbf{n}$ and along the boundary $X=\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\textbf{t}$, we have
$$
\langle X,S\mu\rangle=\epsilon\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\langle\textbf{t},S\textbf{n}\rangle=\epsilon\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\sigma(\textbf{t},\textbf{n}).
$$
To compute the first term, it suffices to compute $\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{n} X$. Since $[X,e_r]=[X,\eta]=0$, the following commutation holds:
$$
\overline{\nabla}_{e_r}X=\overline{\nabla}_X e_r,\qquad \overline{\nabla}_\eta X=\overline{\nabla}_X\eta.
$$
Now, we use that $\textbf{n}=a\eta-b e_r$, hence
$$
\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{n} X=a\overline{\nabla}_\eta X-b\overline{\nabla}_{e_r}X=a\overline{\nabla}_X\eta-b\overline{\nabla}_Xe_r.
$$
Evaluating on $\partial M$,
$$
\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{n} X=\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\left(a\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{t}\eta-b\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{t} e_r\right).
$$
Now we use the Darboux equations
$$
\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{t} e_r=k_R\textbf{t}+\uptau_R\eta,\quad\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{t}\eta=-\uptau_Re_r,\qquad k_R=\frac{1}{R}+\frac{(4\tau^2-\kappa)R}{4W^2},\ \uptau_R=\frac{(4\tau^2-\kappa)\tau R^2}{4W^2}.
$$
Geometrically, $k_R$ is the curvature of the circle $\Gamma_R$ in $\{z=0\}$ and it must not be misunderstood with $\beta_R$ that was defined as the curvature of the projected circle in the base; they only agree if $\tau=0$. In fact, $k_R=\frac{\beta_R+2\tau^2R}{W^2}$.
Also, $-\uptau_R$ is the torsion of $\Gamma_R$. With these equations,
$$
\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{n} X=\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\left(a\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{t}\eta-b\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{t} e_r\right)=\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\left(-a\uptau_R e_r-bk_R\textbf{t}-b\uptau_R\eta\right).
$$
Taking product with $N=ae_r+b\eta$ yields
$$
\langle\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{n} X,N\rangle=-\frac{RW}{\delta_R}\uptau_R.
$$
Finally, since $N=a e_r+b\eta$ with $a^2+b^2=1$, there exists $\alpha$ such that
$$
a=\cos\alpha,\qquad b=\sin\alpha.
$$
The angle $\alpha$ is defined locally, but its derivative $\alpha'=t(\alpha)=\frac{d\alpha}{ds}$ is global; indeed $a'=-b\alpha'$, $b'=a\alpha'$ and thus $\alpha'=ab'-ba'$. A direct computation yields
$$
\overline{\nabla}_\textbf{t} N=a k_R\textbf{t}+(\alpha'+\uptau_R)\textbf{n}.
$$
Consequently, $\sigma(\textbf{n},\textbf{t})=-\alpha'-\uptau_R$ and we conclude with \eqref{eqderconormalu}.

If $\tau=0$ then $E=0$ and the aforementioned argument fails. Nonetheless, for this case we have $W=1$ and
