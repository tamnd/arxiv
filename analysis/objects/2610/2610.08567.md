<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $k>d\geq 2$ and $n\ge k(d+1)/d$.
If $\mathcal F\subseteq\binom{[n]}k$ contains no $d$-simplex-cluster,
then
\[
 |\mathcal F|\le\binom{n-1}{k-1}.
\]
Equality holds if and only if, for some $x\in[n]$,
$\mathcal F=\{A\in\binom{[n]}k:x\in A\}$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:markedbasic}
Let $k\ge1$ and $n\ge2k$.
Suppose that
$\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}$
and that $\mathcal G$ and $\mathcal H$
are cross-intersecting.
Put $\mathcal H^*=\mathcal H\setminus\mathcal G$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:markedbasic}
 |\mathcal H|
 \le \binom{n-1}{k-1}
   +\frac{n-k}{n}|\mathcal H^*|.
\end{equation}

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:markednew}
Let $k\ge2$ and $n\ge2k+2$.
Suppose that
$\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}$
and that $\mathcal G$ and $\mathcal H$
are cross-intersecting.
Put $\mathcal H^*=\mathcal H\setminus\mathcal G$,
and let $\mathcal T\subseteq\mathcal H^*$.
Suppose that for each $R\in\mathcal T$
there is a point $y\in[n]\setminus R$ such that,
for every $a\in R$, the only members of $\mathcal H$
containing $R\setminus\{a\}$ are
\[
 R
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 (R\setminus\{a\})\cup\{y\}.
\]
Writing $t=|\mathcal T|$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linkform}
 |\mathcal H|
 \le \binom{n-1}{k-1}
   +\frac{n-k}{n}|\mathcal H^*|
   -\frac{t}{n}.
\end{equation}
The inequality is strict if $n\ge2k+3$ and $t>0$.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Fix a cyclic ordering $(x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1})$ of $[n]$,
with subscripts taken modulo $n$.
Its $k$-element intervals are defined by
\[
 I_i=\{x_i,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{i+k-1}\},
 \qquad 0\le i\le n-1.
\]
Let $j$, $s$, and $r$ be the numbers of these intervals
belonging to $\mathcal G$, $\mathcal H^*$,
and $\mathcal T$, respectively.
Put
\[
 L=n-\frac nk j-s.
\]
We show that $L\ge r/k$ for every cyclic ordering.

Suppose first that $j\ge1$, and choose
$
 I_a=\{x_a,x_{a+1},\ldots,x_{a+k-1}\}\in\mathcal G.
$
Set $\widetilde x_i=x_{a+i}$ for $0\le i\le n-1$,
with subscripts taken modulo $n$.
Then $(\widetilde x_0,\ldots,\widetilde x_{n-1})$
describes the same cyclic ordering, starting at $x_a$,
and the chosen interval is
$\{\widetilde x_0,\ldots,\widetilde x_{k-1}\}$.
The counts $j,s,r$ are unchanged.
We now omit the tildes, so that
\[
 I_0=\{x_0,\ldots,x_{k-1}\}\in\mathcal G.
\]
Every interval from $\mathcal H$ must intersect $I_0$.
An interval starting at an index between $k$ and $n-k$
is disjoint from $I_0$.
Hence, the possible starting indices of intervals from
$\mathcal H$ are
\[
 -k+1,\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots,k-1 \pmod{n}.
\]
Apart from $I_0$, these possible intervals form
the following $k-1$ pairs:
\[
 (I_i,I_{i+k}),
 \qquad -k+1\le i\le-1.
\]
The two intervals in each pair are disjoint.
If one interval in a pair belongs to $\mathcal G$,
its partner cannot belong to $\mathcal H$.
Since $\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H$, every interval
from $\mathcal G$ other than $I_0$ occurs in one of the
$k-1$ pairs.
If a pair contains an interval from $\mathcal G$,
its partner cannot belong to $\mathcal H$.
Thus, the other $j-1$ intervals from $\mathcal G$
occupy distinct pairs, so $j-1\le k-1$ and hence $j\le k$.

These $j-1$ pairs contain no interval from $\mathcal H^*$,
and neither does $I_0$.
All intervals from $\mathcal H^*$ must occur
in the remaining
\[
 (k-1)-(j-1)=k-j
\]
pairs, with at most two in each pair.
Hence, $s\le2(k-j)$.
Since $\mathcal T\subseteq\mathcal H^*$, we also have
$r\le s\le2(k-j)$.
Using $n\ge2k+2$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyclepositive}
 \begin{aligned}
 L
 &=n-\frac nk j-s\\
 &\ge n-\frac nk j-2(k-j)
   =\frac{n-2k}{k}(k-j)\\
 &\ge\frac{n-2k}{2k}r
   \ge\frac rk.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Now suppose that $j=0$.
Every interval belonging to $\mathcal H$ then belongs
to $\mathcal H^*$, so $L=n-s$ is the number of intervals
absent from $\mathcal H$.
If $r=0$, then $L\ge0=r/k$, as required.
Assume that $r>0$.
Let $R=I_i\in\mathcal T$ be any such interval.
Its two adjacent intervals are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 I_{i-1}
 &= (R\setminus\{x_{i+k-1}\})\cup\{x_{i-1}\},\\
 I_{i+1}
 &= (R\setminus\{x_i\})\cup\{x_{i+k}\}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The added points $x_{i-1}$ and $x_{i+k}$ are distinct
and lie outside $R$.
If both adjacent intervals belonged to $\mathcal H$,
the hypothesis on $R$ would force both added points
to equal its associated point $y$.
Thus, at least one adjacent interval is absent
from $\mathcal H$.

Count pairs consisting of an interval from $\mathcal T$
and an adjacent interval absent from $\mathcal H$.
Each of the $r$ intervals from $\mathcal T$
occurs in at least one such pair.
Conversely, an absent interval $I_a$ can be paired
only with $I_{a-1}$ or $I_{a+1}$.
Thus, each of the $L$ absent intervals occurs
in at most two such pairs.
Hence, $r\le2L$.
Since $k\ge2$, we obtain
\[
 L\ge\frac r2\ge\frac rk.
\]

We now average over all $(n-1)!$ cyclic orderings of $[n]$.
Let $\Omega$ be the set of cyclic orderings of $[n]$.
For $\sigma\in\Omega$, let $j_\sigma$, $s_\sigma$,
and $r_\sigma$ denote the corresponding values
of $j$, $s$, and $r$.
Since each $k$-element set is an interval
in exactly $k!(n-k)!$ cyclic orderings,
double counting gives
\[
 \sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}j_\sigma
 =|\mathcal G|k!(n-k)!,\quad
 \sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}s_\sigma
 =|\mathcal H^*|k!(n-k)!,\quad
 \sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}r_\sigma
 =tk!(n-k)!.
\]
Since $L\ge r/k$ for every cyclic ordering, we have
\[\frac nk j_\sigma+s_\sigma+\frac1k r_\sigma\le n.\]
Hence,
\[
 \left(
 \frac nk|\mathcal G|+|\mathcal H^*|+\frac tk
 \right)k!(n-k)!
 =\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}
   \left(\frac nk j_\sigma+s_\sigma+\frac1k r_\sigma\right)\le n!.
\]
Dividing by $k!(n-k)!$ and multiplying by $k$ yields
\[
 n|\mathcal G|+k|\mathcal H^*|+t
 \le k\binom{n}{k}.
\]
Since $|\mathcal G|=|\mathcal H|-|\mathcal H^*|$,
it follows that
\[
 |\mathcal H|
 \le \binom{n-1}{k-1}
   +\frac{n-k}{n}|\mathcal H^*|
   -\frac tn,
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:linkform}.

Finally, suppose that $n\ge2k+3$ and $t>0$. Since $t=|\mathcal T|>0$, choose $R\in\mathcal T$.
Let $y$ be the point associated with $R$ in the hypothesis.
Take distinct points $p,q\in[n]\setminus(R\cup\{y\})$.
Choose a cyclic ordering in which the elements of $R$
are consecutive, with $p$ immediately before them
and $q$ immediately after them.
Write $R=\{x_1,\ldots,x_k\}$ in its cyclic order.
The two intervals adjacent to $R$ are
\[
 (R\setminus\{x_k\})\cup\{p\}
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 (R\setminus\{x_1\})\cup\{q\}.
\]
By the hypothesis on $R$ and $p,q\ne y$,
neither belongs to $\mathcal H$.
Moreover, $R\in\mathcal T$ is an interval in this ordering,
so $r\ge1$.



If $j=0$, then $L$ is the number of intervals outside $\mathcal H$.
Count pairs $(A,B)$, where $A\in\mathcal T$
and $B\notin\mathcal H$ is adjacent to $A$.
As shown above, every interval from $\mathcal T$
has at least one neighbour outside $\mathcal H$.
The chosen interval $R$ has two such neighbours.
Hence, there are at least $(r-1)+2=r+1$ pairs.
Each interval outside $\mathcal H$ has only two neighbours,
so it occurs in at most two pairs.
Hence, $r+1\le2L$.
Since $k\ge2$, we obtain
\[
 L\ge\frac{r+1}{2}>\frac r2\ge\frac rk.
\]
If $j\ge1$, then \eqref{eq:cyclepositive} gives
\[
 L\ge\frac{3r}{2k}>\frac rk.
\]
Thus, $L\ge r/k$ holds for every cyclic ordering
and is strict for at least one.
Averaging gives strict inequality in \eqref{eq:linkform}.

\label{lem:localrefined}
Let $k\ge4$ and $n>2k$.
Suppose that $\mathcal F\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}$
contains no $2$-simplex-cluster.
If $A,B\in\mathcal F$ satisfy $A\cap B=\{a,b\}$,
then either
\[
 \{a,b\}\cap P_1(A)\ne\emptyset,
\]
or
\[

 \qquad\text{and}\qquad

<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Suppose that $a,b\notin P_1(A)$.
Define
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 Y_a&=\{y\in[n]\setminus A:
       (A\setminus\{a\})\cup\{y\}\in\mathcal F\},\\
 Y_b&=\{y\in[n]\setminus A:
       (A\setminus\{b\})\cup\{y\}\in\mathcal F\}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Since $A\in\mathcal F$, both $A\setminus\{a\}$
and $A\setminus\{b\}$ have positive degree.
The assumption $a,b\notin P_1(A)$ excludes degree one.
Hence,
\[
 |Y_a|=\deg_{\mathcal F}(A\setminus\{a\})-1\ge1,\qquad
 |Y_b|=\deg_{\mathcal F}(A\setminus\{b\})-1\ge1.
\]

Assume that there are distinct points
$y_a\in Y_a$ and $y_b\in Y_b$.
Put
\[
 C_a=(A\setminus\{a\})\cup\{y_a\},
 \qquad
 C_b=(A\setminus\{b\})\cup\{y_b\}.
\]
These sets belong to $\mathcal F$, and
\[
 b\in B\cap C_a,\qquad
 a\in B\cap C_b,\qquad
 C_a\cap C_b=A\setminus\{a,b\}.
\]
Since $k\ge4$, the set $A\setminus\{a,b\}$ is nonempty.
Thus, $B,C_a,C_b$ are pairwise intersecting.
They are also distinct, since
$
 a\notin C_a, b\notin C_b, \{a,b\}\subseteq B.
$
On the other hand,
\[
 B\cap C_a\cap C_b
 =B\cap(A\setminus\{a,b\})
 =\emptyset.
\]
Moreover,
\[
 |B\cup C_a\cup C_b|
 \le |A\cup B|+2
 =2k.
\]
Hence, $B,C_a,C_b$ form a $2$-simplex-cluster,
contrary to the hypothesis.

It follows that $y_a=y_b$ for every choice
of $y_a\in Y_a$ and $y_b\in Y_b$.
Since $Y_a$ and $Y_b$ are nonempty, we obtain
$
 Y_a=Y_b=\{y\}
$
for some $y\in[n]\setminus A.$
Therefore, $a,b\in P_2(A)$ and $y_A(a)=y_A(b)=y$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:rigidity}
Let $2\le k<n$ and
$\mathcal F\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}$.
Suppose that
\[
 |\mathcal F|=\binom{n-1}{k-1},
\]
and that every member of $\mathcal F$
has exactly one $(k-1)$-subset of degree one.
Then $\mathcal F$ is a full star.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Put
\[
 q=n-k,\qquad M=\binom{n-1}{k-1}.
\]
By hypothesis, each member of $\mathcal F$ contains
exactly one $(k-1)$-subset of degree one.
Each such subset belongs to exactly one member of
$\mathcal F$, so there are exactly $M$ such subsets.

We count pairs $(T,C)$ satisfying
\[
 T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1},\qquad
 C\in\binom{[n]}{k}\setminus\mathcal F,\qquad
 T\subseteq C.
\]
For each $(k-1)$-set $T$, exactly
$q+1-\deg_{\mathcal F}(T)$ of the $k$-sets containing $T$
lie outside $\mathcal F$.
On the other hand, each $k$-set outside $\mathcal F$
contains exactly $k$ $(k-1)$-subsets.
Counting the pairs $(T,C)$ in these two ways gives
\[
 \sum_{T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}}
 \bigl(q+1-\deg_{\mathcal F}(T)\bigr)
 =k\left(\binom{n}{k}-M\right)=qM,
\]
where the last equality follows from
$\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n}{k}M$ and $q=n-k$.
The $M$ subsets of degree one each contribute $q$
to this sum.
Their total contribution is  $qM$, giving
\[
 \sum_{T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1},
                 \deg_{\mathcal F}(T)\ne1}
 \bigl(q+1-\deg_{\mathcal F}(T)\bigr)=0.
\]
Every summand is nonnegative.
Thus, every $(k-1)$-set whose degree is not one
has degree $q+1$.
Consequently,
\[
 \deg_{\mathcal F}(T)\in\{1,q+1\}
 \qquad\text{for every }T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}.
\]

Fix $A\in\mathcal F$, and let $x\in A$ be the unique
point such that
$
 \deg_{\mathcal F}(A\setminus\{x\})=1.
$
We show that every $k$-set containing $x$
belongs to $\mathcal F$.

Let $D\in\binom{[n]}{k}$ contain $x$.
Since $|A|=|D|$, we may write
\[
 A\setminus D=\{a_1,\ldots,a_h\},
 \qquad
 D\setminus A=\{y_1,\ldots,y_h\}.
\]
For $0\le i\le h$, put
\[
 A_i=
 \bigl(A\setminus\{a_1,\ldots,a_i\}\bigr)
 \cup\{y_1,\ldots,y_i\}.
\]
Thus, $A_0=A$ and $A_h=D$.
Since $x\in A\cap D$, none of the points $a_i$ equals $x$,
and every $A_i$ contains $x$.
We prove by induction on $i$ that
\[
 A_i\in\mathcal F
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \deg_{\mathcal F}(A_i\setminus\{x\})=1.
\]
The assertion holds for $i=0$ by the choice of $A$ and $x$.
Suppose it holds for $i-1$, where $1\le i\le h$.
The set $A_{i-1}$ has exactly one $(k-1)$-subset
of degree one, namely $A_{i-1}\setminus\{x\}$.
Since $a_i\ne x$, its subset
$A_{i-1}\setminus\{a_i\}$ does not have degree one.
Since every $(k-1)$-set has degree either $1$ or $q+1$,
we have
\[
 \deg_{\mathcal F}(A_{i-1}\setminus\{a_i\})=q+1.
\]
Thus, all $k$-sets containing
$A_{i-1}\setminus\{a_i\}$ belong to $\mathcal F$.
In particular,
\[
 A_i=(A_{i-1}\setminus\{a_i\})\cup\{y_i\}
 \in\mathcal F.
\]
To determine the degree of $A_i\setminus\{x\}$, put
\[
 C_i=(A_{i-1}\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y_i\}.
\]
The point $y_i$ lies outside $A_{i-1}$,
so $C_i$ is a $k$-set distinct from $A_{i-1}$.
By the induction hypothesis,
$A_{i-1}\setminus\{x\}$ has degree one.
Thus, $A_{i-1}$ is the only member of $\mathcal F$
containing $A_{i-1}\setminus\{x\}$.
Since $C_i$ also contains this subset and
$C_i\ne A_{i-1}$, we have $C_i\notin\mathcal F$.
Moreover,
\[
 A_i\setminus\{x\}
 =
 (A_{i-1}\setminus\{x,a_i\})\cup\{y_i\}
 \subseteq C_i.
\]
Hence, $A_i\setminus\{x\}$ has an extension in $\mathcal F$,
namely $A_i$, and an extension outside $\mathcal F$,
namely $C_i$.
Consequently,
\[
 1\le\deg_{\mathcal F}(A_i\setminus\{x\})\le q.
\]
Since its degree must be either $1$ or $q+1$, we obtain
$
 \deg_{\mathcal F}(A_i\setminus\{x\})=1.
$
This completes the induction.
In particular, $D=A_h\in\mathcal F$.

As $D$ was an arbitrary $k$-set containing $x$, we have
$
 \Big\{D\in\binom{[n]}{k}:x\in D\Big\}\subseteq\mathcal F.
$
The family on the left has size
$\binom{n-1}{k-1}=|\mathcal F|$.
Therefore, the two families are equal, and $\mathcal F$
is a full star.

Put
\[
 q=n-k,\qquad
 m=|\mathcal F|,\qquad
 M=\binom{n-1}{k-1},\qquad
 \Delta=M-m,
\]
and
\[
 K=\binom{k}{2},
 \qquad
 c=\frac{q-1}{2q}.
\]
Since $n>2k$, we have $q>k$.


\textbf{We first bound the weighted sum}
\[
 \mathcal W
 =\sum_{A\in\mathcal F}\bigl(p_1(A)+c\,p_2(A)\bigr).
\]
Let $a_j$ denote the number of sets
$T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1}$ with $\deg_{\mathcal F}(T)=j$,
for $0\le j\le q+1$.
We count pairs $(T,C)$ satisfying
\[
 T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1},\qquad
 C\in\binom{[n]}{k}\setminus\mathcal F,\qquad
 T\subseteq C.
\]
For each $T$ of degree $j$, exactly $q+1-j$ of the
$k$-sets containing $T$ lie outside $\mathcal F$.
Thus, counting by $T$ gives
$\sum_{j=0}^{q+1}(q+1-j)a_j$ pairs.
On the other hand, each $k$-set outside $\mathcal F$
contains exactly $k$ $(k-1)$-subsets, so counting by $C$
gives $k\left(\binom{n}{k}-m\right)$ pairs.
Since all terms in the first sum are nonnegative,
retaining only those with $j=1$ and $j=2$ gives
\[
 qa_1+(q-1)a_2
 \le \sum_{j=0}^{q+1}(q+1-j)a_j=k\left(\binom{n}{k}-m\right)=qM+k\Delta,
\]
where the last equality follows from $m=M-\Delta$,
$\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n}{k}M$, and $q=n-k$.
For $i\in\{1,2\}$, each $(k-1)$-set of degree $i$
contributes $i$ to $\sum_{A\in\mathcal F}p_i(A)$.
Hence,
\[
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal F}p_1(A)=a_1,
 \qquad
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal F}p_2(A)=2a_2.
\]
Dividing the preceding bound by $q$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted}
 \mathcal W\le M+\frac{k}{q}\Delta.
\end{equation}

\textbf{For each $D\in\binom{[n]}{2}$, define}
\[
 \mathcal H_D
 =\{A\setminus D:A\in\mathcal F,\ D\subseteq A\}
 \subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus D}{k-2}.
\]
Let $\mathcal H_D^*$ be the subfamily of $\mathcal H_D$
consisting of those sets $A\setminus D$ for which either
\[
 D\cap P_1(A)\ne\emptyset,
\]
or
\[
 D=\{a,b\}\subseteq P_2(A)
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 y_A(a)=y_A(b).
\]
Finally, set
\[
 \mathcal G_D=\mathcal H_D\setminus\mathcal H_D^*.
\]



Suppose that $A\setminus D$ and $B\setminus D$
are disjoint members of $\mathcal H_D$.
Then $A\cap B=D$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:localrefined}, applied to $(A,B)$ and $(B,A)$,
shows that both $A\setminus D$ and $B\setminus D$
belong to $\mathcal H_D^*$.
Since every disjoint pair in $\mathcal H_D$
has both members in $\mathcal H_D^*$,
each member of
$\mathcal G_D=\mathcal H_D\setminus\mathcal H_D^*$
intersects every member of $\mathcal H_D$,
so the two families are cross-intersecting.

For $A\in\mathcal F$ and $y\in[n]\setminus A$, define
\[
 c_y(A)=|\{a\in P_2(A):y_A(a)=y\}|.
\]
Since each $a\in P_2(A)$ has a unique associated point
$y_A(a)\notin A$, we have
\[
 \sum_{y\notin A}c_y(A)=p_2(A).
\]
Let
\[
 h_A=
 \left|\left\{
 D\in\binom{A}{2}:A\setminus D\in\mathcal H_D^*
 \right\}\right|.
\]
We count these $2$-element subsets $D$ according to
the two conditions in the definition of $\mathcal H_D^*$.

First, consider those satisfying $D\cap P_1(A)\ne\emptyset$.
For each $a\in P_1(A)$, there are exactly $k-1$ choices
of $b\in A\setminus\{a\}$, giving $k-1$ sets $D=\{a,b\}$.
Summing over $a\in P_1(A)$ counts each set meeting
$P_1(A)$ once, except that each set contained in
$P_1(A)$ is counted twice.
There are $\binom{p_1(A)}{2}$ sets of the latter type, so
\[
 \left|\left\{
 D\in\binom{A}{2}:D\cap P_1(A)\ne\emptyset
 \right\}\right|
 =(k-1)p_1(A)-\binom{p_1(A)}{2}.
\]

Next, we count the sets $D=\{a,b\}\in\binom{P_2(A)}{2}$
satisfying $y_A(a)=y_A(b)$.
For each fixed $y\notin A$, the set
$
 \{a\in P_2(A):y_A(a)=y\}
$
has exactly $c_y(A)$ elements.
Choosing two distinct elements from this set gives
$\binom{c_y(A)}{2}$ sets $D=\{a,b\}$ with
$y_A(a)=y_A(b)=y$.
For every $D=\{a,b\}\in\binom{P_2(A)}{2}$ satisfying
$y_A(a)=y_A(b)$, the common value
$y=y_A(a)=y_A(b)$ is uniquely determined.
Thus, summing over $y\notin A$ counts each qualifying
set $D$ exactly once, giving
\[
 \left|\left\{
 \{a,b\}\in\binom{P_2(A)}{2}:y_A(a)=y_A(b)
 \right\}\right|
 =\sum_{y\notin A}\binom{c_y(A)}{2}.
\]

Since $P_1(A)\cap P_2(A)=\emptyset$, no set
$D\in\binom{P_2(A)}{2}$ meets $P_1(A)$.
By the definition of $\mathcal H_D^*$, the sets counted
by $h_A$ are precisely those meeting $P_1(A)$ and those
contained in $P_2(A)$ whose two elements have the same
associated point.
These two collections are disjoint, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marks}
 h_A
 =(k-1)p_1(A)-\binom{p_1(A)}{2}
 +\sum_{y\notin A}\binom{c_y(A)}{2}.
\end{equation}


Let $\mathcal E$ consist of those $A\in\mathcal F$
such that $P_2(A)=A$ and all the points $y_A(a)$,
$a\in A$, are equal.
Put $e=|\mathcal E|$.

\textbf{For every $A\in\mathcal F\setminus\mathcal E$, we claim that}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pointgap}
 h_A\le(k-1)\bigl(p_1(A)+c\,p_2(A)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with strict inequality if $p_2(A)>0$ or $p_1(A)\ge2$.

By \eqref{eq:marks}, the difference between
the right-hand side and the left-hand side is
\begin{align}
 \binom{p_1(A)}{2}
 +\frac{p_2(A)}{2}
   \left(k-p_2(A)-\frac{k-1}{q}\right)
 +\binom{p_2(A)}{2}
 -\sum_{y\notin A}\binom{c_y(A)}{2}.\label{eq:gap}
\end{align}
Observe that $p_2(A)\leq k$ and
\[
 \binom{p_2(A)}{2}
 -\sum_{y\notin A}\binom{c_y(A)}{2}
 =
 \left|\left\{
 \{a,b\}\in\binom{P_2(A)}{2}:y_A(a)\ne y_A(b)
 \right\}\right|
 \ge0.
\]

If $p_2(A)\le k-1$, then
\[
 k-p_2(A)-\frac{k-1}{q}
 \ge1-\frac{k-1}{q}>0.
\]
Thus, the first two terms in \eqref{eq:gap}
and the sum of its last two terms are nonnegative.
The second term is positive when $p_2(A)>0$,
and the first is positive when $p_1(A)\ge2$.


Now suppose that $p_2(A)=k$ and $A\notin\mathcal E$. Then $P_2(A)=A$.
Partition $A$ according to the values of $y_A(a)$.
There are at least two nonempty parts, since
$P_2(A)=A$ and $A\notin\mathcal E$ imply that
the values $y_A(a)$, $a\in A$, are not all equal.
Choose one part of size $s$, where $1\le s\le k-1$.
Let $S$ be the chosen part, with $|S|=s$.
For every $a\in S$ and $b\in A\setminus S$, the points
$a$ and $b$ lie in different parts, so
$y_A(a)\ne y_A(b)$.
There are exactly $s(k-s)$ sets $D=\{a,b\}$ of this form.
Since
$\binom{k}{2}-\sum_{y\notin A}\binom{c_y(A)}{2}$
counts all $2$-element subsets of $A$ whose elements
have different values of $y_A$, we obtain
\[
 \binom{k}{2}
 -\sum_{y\notin A}\binom{c_y(A)}{2}
 \ge s(k-s)\ge k-1.
\]
Since $p_1(A)=0$, the expression in \eqref{eq:gap}
is at least
$
 k-1-\frac{K}{q}>0,
$
where $K=\binom{k}{2}$.
This proves the claim.

For $A\in\mathcal E$, we have $P_2(A)=A$, and the values
$y_A(a)$, $a\in A$, are all equal.
Every $D=\{a,b\}\in\binom{A}{2}$ satisfies
$D\subseteq P_2(A)$ and $y_A(a)=y_A(b)$,
so $A\setminus D\in\mathcal H_D^*$.
We obtain
\[
 p_1(A)=0,\qquad p_2(A)=k,\qquad
 h_A=\binom{k}{2}=K.
\]
Therefore,
\[
 h_A-(k-1)\bigl(p_1(A)+c\,p_2(A)\bigr)
 =K-(k-1)ck=\frac{K}{q}.
\]

We now sum separately over $\mathcal F\setminus\mathcal E$
and $\mathcal E$.
Using \eqref{eq:pointgap} for $A\in\mathcal F\setminus\mathcal E$
and the preceding identity for $A\in\mathcal E$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:totalmarks}
 \begin{aligned}
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal F}h_A
 &=\sum_{A\in\mathcal F\setminus\mathcal E}h_A
   +\sum_{A\in\mathcal E}h_A\\
 &\le (k-1)\sum_{A\in\mathcal F\setminus\mathcal E}
       \bigl(p_1(A)+c\,p_2(A)\bigr)
   +\sum_{A\in\mathcal E}
       \left((k-1)\bigl(p_1(A)+c\,p_2(A)\bigr)
       +\frac{K}{q}\right)\\
 &=(k-1)\sum_{A\in\mathcal F}
       \bigl(p_1(A)+c\,p_2(A)\bigr)
   +\frac{K}{q}|\mathcal E|\\
 &=(k-1)\mathcal W+\frac{K}{q}e.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\textbf{We next apply Lemma~\ref{lem:markednew} to
$\mathcal G_D$ and $\mathcal H_D$ for each
$D\in\binom{[n]}{2}$.}
Define
\[
 \mathcal T_D
 =\{A\setminus D: A\in\mathcal E,\ D\subseteq A\},
 \qquad
 e_D=|\mathcal T_D|.
\]
The definition of $\mathcal E$ gives
$\mathcal T_D\subseteq\mathcal H_D^*$.

Let $R\in\mathcal T_D$, and write $R=A\setminus D$,
where $A\in\mathcal E$ and $D\subseteq A$.
Let $y$ be the common value of $y_A(a)$ for $a\in A$.
Since $P_2(A)=A$, for each $a\in R$, the only members
of $\mathcal F$ containing $A\setminus\{a\}$ are
\[
 A
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 (A\setminus\{a\})\cup\{y\}.
\]
Both sets contain $D$, since $a\notin D$.
Fix $a\in R$. Since $a\notin D$ and $y\notin A$,
the sets
\[
 R
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 (R\setminus\{a\})\cup\{y\}
\]
both belong to $\mathcal H_D$ and contain
$R\setminus\{a\}$.
Indeed, their unions with $D$ are $A$ and
$(A\setminus\{a\})\cup\{y\}$, respectively,
both of which belong to $\mathcal F$.
To show that there are no other such members,
let $B\in\mathcal H_D$ contain $R\setminus\{a\}$.
By the definition of $\mathcal H_D$,
\[
 B\cup D\in\mathcal F,
 \qquad
 A\setminus\{a\}
 =(R\setminus\{a\})\cup D
 \subseteq B\cup D.
\]
Since $a\in P_2(A)$, we have
$\deg_{\mathcal F}(A\setminus\{a\})=2$.
Hence, $B\cup D$ must be either $A$ or
$(A\setminus\{a\})\cup\{y\}$.
Using $B=(B\cup D)\setminus D$, we obtain
\[
 B=R
 \quad\text{or}\quad
 B=(R\setminus\{a\})\cup\{y\}.
\]
This holds for every $a\in R$, with the same point
$y\notin A=R\cup D$, and verifies the additional
condition in Lemma~\ref{lem:markednew}.

The members of $\mathcal H_D$ are $(k-2)$-subsets
of $[n]\setminus D$.
After relabelling the elements of $[n]\setminus D$,
we may regard $\mathcal H_D$ as a family of
$(k-2)$-subsets of $[n-2]$.
Since $k\ge4$ and $n>2k$, we have
\[
 k-2\ge2,\qquad
 n-2\ge2(k-2)+3,\qquad
 (n-2)-(k-2)=q.
\]
We have already shown that $\mathcal G_D$ and
$\mathcal H_D$ are cross-intersecting and that
$\mathcal T_D$ satisfies the additional condition.
Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:markednew} with $n$ and $k$
replaced by $n-2$ and $k-2$, respectively, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linkcount}
 |\mathcal H_D|
 \le\binom{n-3}{k-3}
   +\frac{q}{n-2}|\mathcal H_D^*|
   -\frac{e_D}{n-2},
\end{equation}
with strict inequality whenever $e_D>0$.

Each $A\in\mathcal F$ contains exactly
$K=\binom{k}{2}$ subsets $D$ of size two.
For each of these choices, $A\setminus D$ contributes
one to $|\mathcal H_D|$.
By definition, exactly $h_A$ of these choices satisfy
$A\setminus D\in\mathcal H_D^*$.
Similarly, each $A\in\mathcal E$ contributes one
to $e_D$ for each of its $K$ subsets $D$ of size two.
Summing over $D\in\binom{[n]}{2}$ gives
\[
 \sum_{D\in\binom{[n]}{2}}|\mathcal H_D|=Km,\qquad
 \sum_{D\in\binom{[n]}{2}}|\mathcal H_D^*|=\sum_{A\in\mathcal F}h_A,\qquad
 \sum_{D\in\binom{[n]}{2}}e_D=Ke.
\]
Summing \eqref{eq:linkcount} over
$D\in\binom{[n]}{2}$, we obtain
\[
 Km
 \le\binom{n}{2}\binom{n-3}{k-3}
   +\frac{q}{n-2}\sum_{A\in\mathcal F}h_A
   -\frac{Ke}{n-2}.
\]
Substituting \eqref{eq:totalmarks} and cancelling
the terms involving $e$ gives
\begin{align}
 Km
 &\le\binom{n}{2}\binom{n-3}{k-3}
   +\frac{q}{n-2}
       \left((k-1)\mathcal W+\frac{K}{q}e\right)
   -\frac{Ke}{n-2}
 \nonumber\\
 &=\binom{n}{2}\binom{n-3}{k-3}
   +\frac{q(k-1)}{n-2}\mathcal W.
 \label{eq:cancellation}
\end{align}

We use the identity
\[
 \binom{n}{2}\binom{n-3}{k-3}
 +\frac{q(k-1)}{n-2}M=KM.
\]
Indeed, dividing by $M$ reduces this identity to
\[
 \frac{n(k-1)(k-2)}{2(n-2)}
 +\frac{q(k-1)}{n-2}
 =\frac{k(k-1)}{2},
\]
which follows from $q=n-k$.
Combining \eqref{eq:cancellation} with \eqref{eq:weighted}
and using the preceding identity, we obtain
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 Km
 &\le\binom{n}{2}\binom{n-3}{k-3}
   +\frac{q(k-1)}{n-2}
      \left(M+\frac{k}{q}\Delta\right)\\
 &=KM+\frac{k(k-1)}{n-2}\Delta=KM+\frac{2K}{n-2}\Delta.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Since $m=M-\Delta$, this is equivalent to
\[
 0\le K\left(1+\frac{2}{n-2}\right)\Delta
   =\frac{nK}{n-2}\Delta.
\]
The coefficient of $\Delta$ is positive, so
$\Delta\ge0$, proving $m\le M$.

\textbf{Finally, suppose that $m=M$.}
Then $\Delta=0$, and the chain of estimates leading
to the bound
\[
 Km\le KM+\frac{2K}{n-2}\Delta
\]
begins and ends with $KM$.
Equality must hold in every inequality used in this chain,
including \eqref{eq:weighted}, \eqref{eq:totalmarks},
and \eqref{eq:linkcount} for every $D\in\binom{[n]}{2}$.

If $e>0$, the identity
$\sum_{D\in\binom{[n]}{2}}e_D=Ke$ gives
$e_D>0$ for some $D\in\binom{[n]}{2}$.
For this $D$, inequality \eqref{eq:linkcount} is strict,
contradicting equality in the summed bound.
Hence, $e=0$ and $\mathcal E=\emptyset$.
With $\mathcal E=\emptyset$, equality in
\eqref{eq:totalmarks} forces equality in
\eqref{eq:pointgap} for every $A\in\mathcal F$.
The strictness assertion following \eqref{eq:pointgap}
gives
\[
 p_2(A)=0,\qquad p_1(A)\le1
 \qquad\text{for every }A\in\mathcal F.
\]
Equality in \eqref{eq:weighted}, together with
$p_2(A)=0$ for every $A\in\mathcal F$, gives
\[
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal F}p_1(A)=\mathcal W=M=m.
\]
The $m$ summands are at most one and sum to $m$,
so $p_1(A)=1$ for every $A\in\mathcal F$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:rigidity} now shows that
$\mathcal F$ is a full star.

Conversely, a full star has size $M$.
Every three of its members have a common point,
so it contains no $2$-simplex-cluster.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:boolean}
Let $n,r$ be integers with $2\le r\le n-2$, and let
$f:\binom{[n]}{r}\longrightarrow\{0,1\}$.
Suppose that there exist real numbers $a,b_1,\ldots,b_n$
such that
\[
 f(R)=a+\sum_{i\in R}b_i
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in\binom{[n]}{r}.
\]
Then $f$ is constant, or there exists $x\in[n]$ such that
one of the identities
\[
 f(R)=\mathbf1_{x\in R}
 =
 \begin{cases}
  1, & x\in R,\\
  0, & x\notin R,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad
 f(R)=\mathbf1_{x\notin R}=1-\mathbf1_{x\in R}
\]
holds for every $R\in\binom{[n]}{r}$.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
For distinct $i,j\in[n]$, choose
$T\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}{r-1}$.
The assumed representation of $f$ gives
\[
 b_i-b_j
 =f(T\cup\{i\})-f(T\cup\{j\})
 \in\{-1,0,1\}.
\]
If $b_1=\cdots=b_n=\beta$, then
$f(R)=a+r\beta$ for every $R$, so $f$ is constant.

Suppose that the coefficients are not all equal, and put
$\beta=\min_{i\in[n]}b_i$.
Choose $j\in[n]$ such that $b_j=\beta$.
For every $i\in[n]$, the preceding identity gives
\[
 b_i-\beta=b_i-b_j\in\{-1,0,1\}.
\]
Since $\beta$ is the smallest coefficient, we also have
$b_i-\beta\ge0$, so $b_i\in\{\beta,\beta+1\}$.
The value $\beta$ occurs at $j$.
As the coefficients are not all equal, some coefficient
is greater than $\beta$ and must equal $\beta+1$.
Thus, the coefficients take exactly these two values.
Define
\[
 P=\{i\in[n]:b_i=\beta+1\},
 \qquad
 a'=a+r\beta.
\]
Then $1\le|P|\le n-1$, and
\[
 f(R)=a'+|R\cap P|
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in\binom{[n]}{r}.
\]

We claim that $|P|=1$ or $n-1$.
Suppose instead that $2\le|P|\le n-2$.
Choose distinct $u,v\in P$ and distinct
$w,z\in[n]\setminus P$.
Since $0\le r-2\le n-4$, we can choose
$
 S\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{u,v,w,z\}}{r-2}.
$
The three $r$-sets obtained by adding
$\{w,z\}$, $\{u,w\}$, and $\{u,v\}$ to $S$ satisfy
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 f(S\cup\{w,z\})&=a'+|S\cap P|,\\
 f(S\cup\{u,w\})&=a'+|S\cap P|+1,\\
 f(S\cup\{u,v\})&=a'+|S\cap P|+2.
 \end{aligned}
\]
These are three distinct values, contradicting
$f(R)\in\{0,1\}$.
This proves the claim.

If $P=\{x\}$, then
\[
 f(R)=a'+\mathbf1_{x\in R}.
\]
There are $r$-sets containing $x$ and $r$-sets avoiding $x$,
so both $a'$ and $a'+1$ occur as values of $f$.
Since both belong to $\{0,1\}$, we have $a'=0$.
Thus, $f(R)=\mathbf1_{x\in R}$ for every $R$.

If $P=[n]\setminus\{x\}$, then
\[
 f(R)=a'+r-\mathbf1_{x\in R}.
\]
The two values $a'+r-1$ and $a'+r$ both occur
and belong to $\{0,1\}$, so $a'+r=1$.
It follows that
\[
 f(R)=1-\mathbf1_{x\in R}
     =\mathbf1_{x\notin R}
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in\binom{[n]}{r}.
\]
This completes the proof.

\label{lem:kneser}
Let $n,r$ be positive integers with $n>2r$, and put
$k=n-r$, $N=\binom{n}{r}$, and $D=\binom{k}{r}$.
For $\mathcal G\subseteq\binom{[n]}{r}$, put
$m=|\mathcal G|$ and $\alpha=m/N$, and define
$
 I=\left|\left\{
 (R,S)\in\mathcal G\times\mathcal G:
 R\cap S=\emptyset
 \right\}\right|.
$
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kneser}
 I\ge mD\left(\frac nk\alpha-\frac rk\right).
\end{equation}
If equality holds, then the indicator function
$f=\mathbf1_{\mathcal G}$, defined by $f(R)=1$ when
$R\in\mathcal G$ and $f(R)=0$ otherwise, can be written as
\[
 f(R)=a+\sum_{i\in R}b_i
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in\binom{[n]}{r},
\]
for some real numbers $a,b_1,\ldots,b_n$.

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $\mathsf A$ be the adjacency matrix of $KG(n,r)$.
For real-valued functions $u,v$ on $\binom{[n]}{r}$,
use the inner product and norm
\[
 \langle u,v\rangle
 =\sum_{R\in\binom{[n]}{r}}u(R)v(R),
 \qquad
 \|u\|^2=\langle u,u\rangle.
\]
The matrix $\mathsf A$ acts on these functions by
\[
 (\mathsf A u)(R)
 =\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{r},
                  S\cap R=\emptyset}u(S).
\]
Write $\mathbf1$ for the constant function with value one.
Each vertex has exactly $D$ neighbors, so
$\mathsf A\mathbf1=D\mathbf1$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:kneserspectrum}, the least eigenvalue
of $\mathsf A$ is
\[
 \lambda=-\binom{k-1}{r-1}=-\frac rkD,
\]
with multiplicity $n-1$.

For the indicator function $f=\mathbf1_{\mathcal G}$,
put $h=f-\alpha\mathbf1$.
Since $f$ takes the value one on exactly $m$ vertices
and zero on the remaining vertices, we have
\[
 \langle h,\mathbf1\rangle
 =m-N\alpha=0,\qquad
 \|h\|^2
 =m-2\alpha m+N\alpha^2
  =N\alpha(1-\alpha).
\]
For each $R\in\binom{[n]}{r}$, $(\mathsf A f)(R)$
is the number of members of $\mathcal G$ disjoint from $R$.
Since $f(R)=1$ when $R\in\mathcal G$ and zero otherwise,
\[
 \langle f,\mathsf A f\rangle
 =\sum_{R\in\mathcal G}(\mathsf A f)(R)=\sum_{R\in\mathcal G}
   \left|\{S\in\mathcal G:R\cap S=\emptyset\}\right|
 =I.
\]
Expanding $f=\alpha\mathbf1+h$ and using the symmetry
of $\mathsf A$, we obtain
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 I
 &=\langle\alpha\mathbf1+h,
          \mathsf A(\alpha\mathbf1+h)\rangle=\alpha^2\langle\mathbf1,\mathsf A\mathbf1\rangle
   +2\alpha\langle h,\mathsf A\mathbf1\rangle
   +\langle h,\mathsf A h\rangle\\
 &=ND\alpha^2+2D\alpha\langle h,\mathbf1\rangle
   +\langle h,\mathsf A h\rangle\\
&=ND\alpha^2+\langle h,\mathsf A h\rangle,
 \end{aligned}
\]
where the last two equalities use
$\mathsf A\mathbf1=D\mathbf1$,
$\langle\mathbf1,\mathbf1\rangle=N$, and
$\langle h,\mathbf1\rangle=0$.
Since $\lambda$ is the least eigenvalue of the symmetric
matrix $\mathsf A$, we have
$\langle h,\mathsf A h\rangle\ge\lambda\|h\|^2$.
Substituting $\lambda=-\frac rkD$ and
$\|h\|^2=N\alpha(1-\alpha)$ gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 I
 &\ge ND\alpha^2+\lambda\|h\|^2=ND\alpha^2-\frac rkD\,N\alpha(1-\alpha)\\
 &=ND\alpha
   \left(\left(1+\frac rk\right)\alpha-\frac rk\right)=mD\left(\frac nk\alpha-\frac rk\right).
 \end{aligned}
\]


Assume now that equality holds.
Let $E$ be the eigenspace of $\mathsf A$
corresponding to $\lambda$.
Equality in $\langle h,\mathsf A h\rangle\ge\lambda\|h\|^2$
implies $h\in E$.
Since $\mathsf A$ is symmetric and $\lambda$ has
multiplicity $n-1$, we have $\dim E=n-1$.
For each $i\in[n]$, define
\[
 u_i(R)=\mathbf1_{i\in R}-\frac rn,
\]
where $\mathbf1_{i\in R}$ equals one when $i\in R$
and zero otherwise.
If $i\notin R$, exactly $\binom{k-1}{r-1}$
$r$-sets disjoint from $R$ contain $i$;
if $i\in R$, no $r$-set disjoint from $R$ contains $i$.
Since exactly $D$ members of $\binom{[n]}{r}$ are
disjoint from $R$, we obtain
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 (\mathsf A u_i)(R)
 &=\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{r},
                   S\cap R=\emptyset}
      \left(\mathbf1_{i\in S}-\frac rn\right)=\binom{k-1}{r-1}(1-\mathbf1_{i\in R})-\frac rnD\\
 &=-\binom{k-1}{r-1}
      \left(\mathbf1_{i\in R}-\frac rn\right)=\lambda u_i(R).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Here the third equality uses
$\binom{k-1}{r-1}=\frac rkD$ and $k=n-r$.
Thus, $u_i\in E$ for every $i\in[n]$.

For every $R\in\binom{[n]}{r}$, we have
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^n u_i(R)
 =\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf1_{i\in R}-r
 =|R|-r=0.
\]
Hence, $\sum_{i=1}^n u_i$ is the zero function.
Suppose that real numbers $c_1,\ldots,c_{n-1}$ satisfy
$\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}c_\ell u_\ell=0$.
For each $i\in[n-1]$, choose
$T\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{i,n\}}{r-1}$.
Evaluating the relation at $T\cup\{i\}$ and
$T\cup\{n\}$ and subtracting gives
\[
 0=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}c_\ell
 \bigl(u_\ell(T\cup\{i\})-u_\ell(T\cup\{n\})\bigr)
 =c_i.
\]
Thus, $c_1=\cdots=c_{n-1}=0$, proving that
$u_1,\ldots,u_{n-1}$ are linearly independent.
Since these functions belong to $E$ and $\dim E=n-1$,
they form a basis of $E$.

As $h\in E$, there exist real numbers $b_1,\ldots,b_n$
such that $h=\sum_{i=1}^n b_i u_i$.
Put $a=\alpha-\frac rn\sum_{i=1}^n b_i$.
Using $f=\alpha\mathbf1+h$, $h=\sum_{i=1}^n b_i u_i$,
and $a=\alpha-\frac rn\sum_{i=1}^n b_i$, we obtain,
for every $R\in\binom{[n]}{r}$,
\[
 f(R)
 =\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^n b_i
      \left(\mathbf1_{i\in R}-\frac rn\right)=\left(\alpha-\frac rn\sum_{i=1}^n b_i\right)
   +\sum_{i\in R}b_i=a+\sum_{i\in R}b_i.
\]
This proves the equality assertion.

Let $d\ge3$, $k\ge d+1$, $n\ge2k-d$, and $r=n-k$.
Suppose that $d(r-1)>2r$.
If $\mathcal F\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}$ contains no

Put $m=|\mathcal F|$ and $M=\binom{n-1}{k-1}$.
If $m<M$, the desired bound holds strictly.
It remains to consider the case $m\ge M$.
Use the notation $\deg_{\mathcal F}$, $P_i(A)$, and
$p_i(A)$ from Section~\ref{sec:triangle}.

For $j\in\{0,\ldots,r+1\}$, let $a_j$ be the number
of $(k-1)$-sets of degree $j$.
We count pairs $(T,C)$ satisfying
\[
 T\in\binom{[n]}{k-1},\qquad
 C\in\binom{[n]}{k}\setminus\mathcal F,\qquad
 T\subseteq C.
\]
A set $T$ of degree $j$ is contained in exactly
$r+1-j$ choices of $C$, while each such $C$ contains
exactly $k$ choices of $T$.
Thus,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 r a_1+(r-1)a_2
 &\le\sum_{j=0}^{r+1}(r+1-j)a_j=k\left(\binom{n}{k}-m\right)\\
 &\le k\left(\binom{n}{k}-M\right)=rM.
 \end{aligned}
\]
For $i\in\{1,2\}$, each $(k-1)$-set of degree $i$
contributes $i$ to $\sum_{A\in\mathcal F}p_i(A)$.
Hence,
\[
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal F}p_1(A)=a_1,\qquad
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal F}p_2(A)=2a_2.
\]
It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transitionweight}
 \mathcal W
 :=\sum_{A\in\mathcal F}
   \left(p_1(A)+\frac{r-1}{2r}p_2(A)\right)=\frac{r a_1+(r-1)a_2}{r}\le M.
\end{equation}

For $D\in\binom{[n]}{d}$, define
\[
 \mathcal H_D
 =\{A\setminus D:A\in\mathcal F,\ D\subseteq A\}
 \subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus D}{k-d}.
\]
Let $\mathcal H_D^*$ consist of the members
$A\setminus D$ for which
\[
 D\cap P_1(A)\ne\emptyset
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad
 D\subseteq P_2(A).
\]
We show that both members of every disjoint pair
in $\mathcal H_D$ belong to $\mathcal H_D^*$.

Suppose that $A\setminus D$ and $B\setminus D$
are disjoint members of $\mathcal H_D$, so
$A\cap B=D$.
Write $D=\{x_1,\ldots,x_d\}$ and suppose that
$A\setminus D\notin\mathcal H_D^*$.
Then every $A\setminus\{x_i\}$ has degree at least two,
and at least one has degree at least three.
After relabeling, assume that
$A\setminus\{x_1\}$ has degree at least three.
We can choose
\[
 A_i=(A\setminus\{x_i\})\cup\{y_i\}\in\mathcal F,
 \qquad y_i\in[n]\setminus A,\quad i\in[d],
\]
with $y_1\ne y_2$.
Indeed, choose $y_2,\ldots,y_d$ first.
Since $\deg_{\mathcal F}(A\setminus\{x_1\})\ge3$,
there are at least two extensions other than $A$,
so $y_1$ can be chosen different from $y_2$.
The sets $B,A_1,\ldots,A_d$ are distinct and satisfy
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \bigcap_{i=1}^d A_i=A\setminus D\ne\emptyset,\qquad
 B\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^d A_i=\emptyset,\qquad
 x_i\in B\cap\bigcap_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}A_j
 \qquad(i\in[d]).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Moreover,
\[
 \left|B\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^d A_i\right|
 \le |A\cup B|+d=2k.
\]
They form a $d$-simplex-cluster, a contradiction.
Thus, $A\setminus D\in\mathcal H_D^*$.
Interchanging $A$ and $B$ gives
$B\setminus D\in\mathcal H_D^*$.

Every member of $\mathcal H_D\setminus\mathcal H_D^*$
now intersects every member of $\mathcal H_D$.
Since
\[
 k-d\ge1,\qquad
 n-d\ge2(k-d),\qquad
 (n-d)-(k-d)=r,
\]
we may apply Lemma~\ref{lem:markedbasic} to these
two families, after relabeling $[n]\setminus D$ as
$[n-d]$.
This gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transitionlink}
 |\mathcal H_D|
 \le\binom{n-d-1}{k-d-1}
   +\frac r{n-d}|\mathcal H_D^*|.
\end{equation}

Put $C=\binom{k-1}{d-1}$.
For $A\in\mathcal F$, let $h(A)$ be the number of
$d$-element subsets $D\subseteq A$ for which
$A\setminus D\in\mathcal H_D^*$.
Exactly
$
 \binom{k}{d}-\binom{k-p_1(A)}{d}
$
of these subsets meet $P_1(A)$, and exactly
$\binom{p_2(A)}{d}$ are contained in $P_2(A)$.
The two conditions are disjoint because
$P_1(A)\cap P_2(A)=\emptyset$.
By the definition of $\mathcal H_D^*$, we have
\[
 h(A)
 =\binom{k}{d}-\binom{k-p_1(A)}{d}
   +\binom{p_2(A)}{d}.
\]
For $0\le p\le k$, we have
\[
 \binom{k}{d}-\binom{k-p}{d}
 =\sum_{j=1}^{p}\binom{k-j}{d-1}\le pC.
\]
For $1\le p\le k$, we also have
\[
 \binom{p}{d}
 =\frac pd\binom{p-1}{d-1}\le\frac pd C,
\]
and this bound holds trivially when $p=0$.
Consequently,
\[
 h(A)\le C\left(p_1(A)+\frac{p_2(A)}d\right).
\]
The assumption $d(r-1)>2r$ is equivalent to
$1/d<(r-1)/(2r)$.
Summing over $A\in\mathcal F$ and using
\eqref{eq:transitionweight}, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transitionmarks}
 \sum_{D\in\binom{[n]}{d}}|\mathcal H_D^*|
 =\sum_{A\in\mathcal F}h(A)
 \le C\mathcal W\le CM.
\end{equation}

Each member of $\mathcal F$ contains exactly
$\binom{k}{d}$ subsets of size $d$, so
$
 \sum_{D\in\binom{[n]}{d}}|\mathcal H_D|
 =\binom{k}{d}m.
$
Summing \eqref{eq:transitionlink} and using
\eqref{eq:transitionmarks}, we obtain
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \binom{k}{d}m
 &\le\binom{n}{d}\binom{n-d-1}{k-d-1}
   +\frac r{n-d}\sum_{D\in\binom{[n]}{d}}
      |\mathcal H_D^*|\\
 &\le\binom{n}{d}\binom{n-d-1}{k-d-1}
   +\frac r{n-d}CM=\binom{k}{d}M.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus, $m\le M$.

If $m=M$, equality holds in all the preceding bounds.
Since $1/d<(r-1)/(2r)$, equality in
\eqref{eq:transitionmarks} forces $p_2(A)=0$
for every $A\in\mathcal F$.
The bound
\[
 \binom{k}{d}-\binom{k-p_1(A)}{d}\le Cp_1(A)
\]
is strict when $p_1(A)\ge2$, because
$\binom{k-2}{d-1}<C$.
Hence, $p_1(A)\le1$ for every $A\in\mathcal F$.
Equality in \eqref{eq:transitionmarks} also gives
$\mathcal W=M=m$.
It follows that
$
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal F}p_1(A)=m,
$
so $p_1(A)=1$ for every $A\in\mathcal F$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:rigidity} shows that $\mathcal F$
is a full star.

$
Hence, $I_0\cup I_h\cup I_4\cup I_{h+4}=[8]$.
Put $A_i=[8]\setminus I_i$ for
$i\in\{0,h,4,h+4\}$.
These four sets belong to $\mathcal F$ and satisfy
\[
 A_0\cap A_h\cap A_4\cap A_{h+4}
 =[8]\setminus(I_0\cup I_h\cup I_4\cup I_{h+4})
 =\emptyset.
\]
The definitions of the intervals also give
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 A_h\cap A_4\cap A_{h+4}&=\{x_{h-1}\},
 &\qquad A_0\cap A_4\cap A_{h+4}&=\{x_3\},\\
 A_0\cap A_h\cap A_{h+4}&=\{x_{h+3}\},
 &\qquad A_0\cap A_h\cap A_4&=\{x_7\}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus, $A_0,A_h,A_4,A_{h+4}$  form a $3$-simplex in $\mathcal F$,
a contradiction.

Therefore, every cyclic ordering has at most five intervals
in $\mathcal G$.
Let $\Omega$ be the set of cyclic orderings of $[8]$.
Then $|\Omega|=7!$.
Each $R\in\mathcal G$ occurs as an interval
in exactly $3!5!$ cyclic orderings.
Double counting gives
$
 3!5!\,|\mathcal G|
=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}
   \left|\{R\in\mathcal G:
   R\text{ is an interval in }\sigma\}\right|\le5|\Omega|=5\cdot7!.
$
Hence,
\[
 |\mathcal F|=|\mathcal G|
 \le\frac{5\cdot7!}{3!5!}=35.
\]

Suppose that equality holds.
Every cyclic ordering then has exactly five intervals
in $\mathcal G$.
For distinct $x,y\in[8]$, let $S=\{s_1,s_2\}$ and
$T=\{t_1,t_2\}$ be disjoint members of
$\binom{[8]\setminus\{x,y\}}{2}$.
Consider the cyclic ordering
\[
 (s_1,s_2,x,y,t_1,t_2,z_1,z_2),
\]
where $z_1,z_2$ are the remaining two points.
Exchanging $x$ and $y$ replaces $S\cup\{x\}$ and
$T\cup\{y\}$ by $S\cup\{y\}$ and $T\cup\{x\}$,
respectively. The other six three-element sets are
common to both orderings.
Let $t$ be the number of these six sets belonging
to $\mathcal G$, and let $g=\mathbf1_{\mathcal G}$.
Since each ordering has exactly five intervals
in $\mathcal G$, we obtain
\[
 g(S\cup\{x\})+g(T\cup\{y\})
 =5-t=g(S\cup\{y\})+g(T\cup\{x\}).
\]

Let $S,T\in\binom{[8]\setminus\{x,y\}}{2}$.
Since $|\{x,y\}\cup S\cup T|\le6$, we can choose
$
 U\in\binom{[8]\setminus(\{x,y\}\cup S\cup T)}{2}.
$
Applying the preceding identity to $(S,U)$ and $(T,U)$ gives
\[
 g(S\cup\{x\})-g(S\cup\{y\})
 =g(U\cup\{x\})-g(U\cup\{y\})=g(T\cup\{x\})-g(T\cup\{y\}).
\]
Thus, $g(S\cup\{x\})-g(S\cup\{y\})$ is independent
of $S$. Denote its common value by $c_{xy}$.
Put $b_8=0$ and $b_x=c_{x8}$ for $x\in[7]$.
For distinct $x,y\in[7]$, choose
$S\in\binom{[8]\setminus\{x,y,8\}}{2}$.
Then
\[
g(S\cup\{x\})-g(S\cup\{y\})
 =\bigl(g(S\cup\{x\})-g(S\cup\{8\})\bigr)
   -\bigl(g(S\cup\{y\})-g(S\cup\{8\})\bigr).
\]
It follows that $c_{xy}=b_x-b_y$ for distinct $x,y\in[7]$.
For $x\in[7]$, the definitions also give
$
 c_{x8}=b_x=b_x-b_8,
 c_{8x}=-c_{x8}=b_8-b_x.
$
Thus, $c_{xy}=b_x-b_y$ for all distinct $x,y\in[8]$.
Let $R\in\binom{[8]}{3}$, $x\in R$, and
$y\in[8]\setminus R$.
Put $R'=(R\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y\}$.
Taking $S=R\setminus\{x\}$ in the definition of $c_{xy}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{ad15}
 g(R)-g(R')=c_{xy}=b_x-b_y.
\end{equation}


Let $R,T\in\binom{[8]}{3}$.
For $R\ne T$, write
$
 R\setminus T=\{u_1,\ldots,u_s\}
$ and
$
 T\setminus R=\{v_1,\ldots,v_s\}, s\ge1.
$
Put $R_0=R$ and
$
 R_i=(R_{i-1}\setminus\{u_i\})\cup\{v_i\}, 1\le i\le s.
$
Then $R_s=T$.
At each step, $u_i\in R_{i-1}$ and $v_i\notin R_{i-1}$.
The  equality \eqref{ad15} gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 g(R)-g(T)
 &=\sum_{i=1}^s\bigl(g(R_{i-1})-g(R_i)\bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^s(b_{u_i}-b_{v_i})\\
 &=\sum_{z\in R}b_z-\sum_{z\in T}b_z.
 \end{aligned}
\]
This identity also holds when $R=T$.
Hence, $g(R)-\sum_{z\in R}b_z$ is constant.
Denote its value by $a$. Then
\[
 g(R)=a+\sum_{z\in R}b_z
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in\binom{[8]}{3}.
\]

Since $0<|\mathcal G|=35<56$, the function $g$ is not constant.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:boolean}, there is $x\in[8]$ such that
$
 \mathcal G\subseteq\{R\in\binom{[8]}{3}:x\in R\}
$
or
$
 \mathcal G\subseteq\{R\in\binom{[8]}{3}:x\notin R\}.
$
The first case gives $|\mathcal G|\le\binom{7}{2}=21$,
contrary to $|\mathcal G|=35$.
In the second case, the containing family has
$\binom{7}{3}=35$ members.
Hence,
$
 \mathcal G=\{R\in\binom{[8]}{3}:x\notin R\}.
$
Taking complements gives
$
 \mathcal F
 =\{A\in\binom{[8]}{5}:x\in A\}.
$

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
We proceed by induction on $d$.
For $d=2$, we have $n\ge3k/2$.
When $n\le2k$, every $2$-simplex is a $2$-simplex-cluster.
For $k=3$, a $2$-simplex $\{A,B,C\}$ has empty total
intersection and nonempty pairwise intersections.
Hence,
\[
 |A\cup B\cup C|
 =9-|A\cap B|-|A\cap C|-|B\cap C|\le6=2k.
\]
Thus, every $2$-simplex is a $2$-simplex-cluster
when $n\le2k$ or $k=3$.
Since $\mathcal F$ contains no $2$-simplex-cluster,
it contains no $2$-simplex in these cases.
Theorem~\ref{thm:MV} then gives the required bound
and equality characterization.
The remaining case $k\ge4$ and $n>2k$ follows
from Theorem~\ref{thm:triangle}.

Let $d\ge3$, and assume that the theorem holds for $d-1$.
For $n\ge2k-d+2$, the conclusion follows from
Theorem~\ref{thm:Currier}.

Suppose next that $n=2k-d+1$, and put $r=n-k$.
Then $k=r+d-1$.
The assumptions $n\ge k(d+1)/d$  and $k>d$ give
$r\ge k/d>1$, so $r\ge2$.
Here $n<2k$, so every $d$-simplex is a
$d$-simplex-cluster.
Since $\mathcal F$ contains no $d$-simplex-cluster,
it contains no $d$-simplex.
If $r=2$, then
$
 n=d+3\geq 6
$
and
$ k=d+1=n-2.$
Since $d=n-3$, Lemma~\ref{lem:ranktwo} applies.
For $r\ge3$ and $(d,r)\ne(3,3)$, we have
$d(r-1)>2r$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:transition} applies.
The remaining case $d=r=3$ gives
$(d,k,n)=(3,5,8)$.
Here $\mathcal F$ contains no $3$-simplex,
so Lemma~\ref{lem:eight} applies.

It remains to treat $n\le2k-d$.
Put $r=n-k$ and $s=k-r$.
Since $n\ge k(d+1)/d$ and $k>d$, we have
$
 r=n-k\ge k/d>1.
$
Thus, $r\ge2$.
Since $n\le2k-d$, we also have
$
 s=k-r=2k-n\ge d\geq 3.
$
Hence,
$
 k=r+s\geq r+3, n=k+r=2r+s\geq 2r+3.
$
Since $n<2k$, the family $\mathcal F$ contains
no $d$-simplex.
Define
\[
 \mathcal G=\{[n]\setminus A:A\in\mathcal F\}
 \subseteq\binom{[n]}{r}.
\]
For $E\in\mathcal G$, put $X=[n]\setminus E$ and
\[
 \mathcal G_E
 =\{B\in\mathcal G:B\cap E=\emptyset\}
 \subseteq\binom{X}{r}.
\]

We claim that the sets $X\setminus B$,
$B\in\mathcal G_E$, form an $s$-uniform family
containing no $(d-1)$-simplex.
Suppose that $B_1,\ldots,B_d\in\mathcal G_E$
are such that $X\setminus B_1,\ldots,X\setminus B_d$
form a $(d-1)$-simplex.
Put
\[
 A_0=X,\qquad
 A_i=[n]\setminus B_i\quad(i\in[d]).
\]
These are distinct members of $\mathcal F$.
Since $B_i\subseteq X$, we have
$A_i=E\cup(X\setminus B_i)$.
The simplex conditions give
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \bigcap_{i=0}^d A_i
 &=\bigcap_{i=1}^d(X\setminus B_i)=\emptyset,\quad
 \bigcap_{i=1}^d A_i
 =E\ne\emptyset,\\
 \bigcap_{0\le j\le d, j\ne i}A_j
 &=\bigcap_{j\in[d]\setminus\{i\}}(X\setminus B_j)
 \ne\emptyset
 \qquad(i\in[d]).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus, $A_0,\ldots,A_d$ form a $d$-simplex,
contradicting the assumption on $\mathcal F$.

In particular, the family
$\{X\setminus B:B\in\mathcal G_E\}$ contains no
$(d-1)$-simplex-cluster.
Its members have size $s\ge d$, and its ground set
$X$ has size $k$.
The original parameter condition gives $(d+1)r\ge n$,
and
\[
 (d-1)k-ds=dr-k=(d+1)r-n\ge0.
\]
Hence, $k\ge ds/(d-1)$, so the induction hypothesis
applies and gives
\[
 |\mathcal G_E|
 \le\binom{k-1}{s-1}
 =\binom{k-1}{r}
 =\frac{k-r}{k}\binom{k}{r}.
\]

Put
\[
 m=|\mathcal G|=|\mathcal F|,\qquad
 N=\binom{n}{r},\qquad
 \alpha=\frac mN,\qquad
 D=\binom{k}{r}.
\]
If $m=0$, the desired bound is immediate.
Assume $m>0$.
The number of ordered disjoint pairs in $\mathcal G$
is $\sum_{E\in\mathcal G}|\mathcal G_E|$.
Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:kneser} and summing the
preceding upper bound over $E\in\mathcal G$, we obtain
\[
 mD\left(\frac nk\alpha-\frac rk\right)
 \le\sum_{E\in\mathcal G}|\mathcal G_E|
 \le mD\frac{k-r}{k}.
\]
Dividing by $mD$ gives
\[
 \frac nk\alpha-\frac rk\le\frac{k-r}{k},
\]
which reduces to $\alpha\le k/n$.
Consequently,
\[
 |\mathcal F|=m
 \le\frac kn\binom{n}{r}
 =\binom{n-1}{k-1}.
\]

If $|\mathcal F|=\binom{n-1}{k-1}$, then
$\alpha=k/n$, and equality holds throughout the
bound on the number of ordered disjoint pairs.
The equality assertion in Lemma~\ref{lem:kneser}
shows that the indicator function of $\mathcal G$
has the form stated in Lemma~\ref{lem:boolean}.
Since $0<|\mathcal G|<N$, its indicator function is not constant.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:boolean}, there exists $x\in[n]$ such that
$
 \mathcal G\subseteq\{R\in\binom{[n]}{r}:x\in R\}
$
or
$
 \mathcal G\subseteq\{R\in\binom{[n]}{r}:x\notin R\}.
$
The first case gives
\[
 |\mathcal G|
 \le\binom{n-1}{r-1}
 =\frac rnN
 <\frac knN
 =|\mathcal G|,
\]
a contradiction.
In the second case, the containing family has
$
 \binom{n-1}{r}
 =\frac knN
 =|\mathcal G|
$
members, so the inclusion is an equality.
Hence,
$
 \mathcal G
 =\{R\in\binom{[n]}{r}:x\notin R\}.
$
It follows that
$
 \mathcal F
 =\{A\in\binom{[n]}{k}:x\in A\}.
$
Conversely, a full star has $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ members.
Every $d+1$ of its members have nonempty total intersection,
so it contains no $d$-simplex-cluster.
