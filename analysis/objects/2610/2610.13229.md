All polynomial invariants of a ternary cubic, viewed as polynomials in $\mu_{ijk}$, are generated by two algebraically independent
invariants of degree $4$ and degree $6.$
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{all_degree3}
Every invariant $I\in\mathcal{MI}_3$ has the form
$$
I=
\Phi_3\left(D_{123}^{\,3}Q(\mathsf X,\mathsf Y,\mathsf Z,U,V)\right),
$$
where $Q$ is antisymmetric under permutations of the labels
of the three points.
Conversely, every such expression is a projective
differential moment invariant.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{J-family}
For every $a\geq1$, the invariants
$$
J_{a,k}
=
\Phi_3\left[
D_{123}^{\,3}
\left(U^{a-k}V^k-U^kV^{a-k}\right)
\right],
\qquad
k=0,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{a-1}{2}\right\rfloor,
$$
form a basis of the space of polynomial moment invariants in
$\operatorname{Sym}^3(W_a)$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:general_main_result}
Let $n\geq1$, and let $Q(z_1,\ldots,z_n)$ be a symmetric relative
projective invariant of weight $-1$. Then
$
\Phi_n(Q)
$
is a projective differential moment invariant
of moment degree $n$.
Conversely, every projective differential moment
invariant $I$ of moment degree $n$ can be written as
$
I=\Phi_n(Q),
$
where $Q$ is a symmetric relative projective invariant
of weight $-1$ in $n$ sets of variables.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
Let $(g,f)$ be an admissible pair, and let
$$
\varepsilon_g=\operatorname{sgn}j_g(x,y),
\qquad (x,y)\in U,
$$
be the constant sign of the Jacobian on the domain $U$.
Then, for every $I\in\mathcal{MI}_n$, we have
$$
I(g\cdot f)=\varepsilon_g^{\,n}I(f).
$$
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $I=\Phi_n(Q)$, where $Q$ is a symmetric
relative invariant of weight $-1$.
A change of variables in the multiple integral and the
relative invariance law give
$$
\begin{aligned}
I(g\cdot f)
&=
\int_{\Omega^n}
Q(gz_1,\ldots,gz_n)
\prod_{i=1}^n
\bigl(
|j_g(x_i,y_i)|f(x_i,y_i)\,dx_i\,dy_i
\bigr)=
\\
&=
\int_{\Omega^n}
Q(z_1,\ldots,z_n)
\prod_{i=1}^n

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:polarization-identities}
For every $n\geq1$, the following identities hold:
\begin{align}
\Phi_n\circ\operatorname{Pol}_n
&=\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal{DP}_n},
\label{Phi-Pol-identity}\\
\operatorname{Pol}_n\circ\Phi_n
&=\operatorname{Sym}_n.
\label{Pol-Phi-sym}
\end{align}
Moreover, for every $k=1,\ldots,8$,
$F\in R_n$, and $I\in\mathcal{DP}_n$, we have
\begin{equation}
\label{polarization-equivariance}
\begin{aligned}
\mathscr D_k\Phi_n(F)
&=\Phi_n(\mathscr L_k^{[n]}F),\\
\operatorname{Pol}_n(\mathscr D_kI)
&=\mathscr L_k^{[n]}\operatorname{Pol}_n(I).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In particular, $\Phi_n$ and $\operatorname{Pol}_n$ are mutually
inverse maps between $R_n^{S_n}$ and
$\mathcal{DP}_n$ and intertwine the corresponding actions
of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_3$.

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
All maps in these identities are linear,
so it suffices to verify them on monomials.

Let
$b_i=b_{\alpha_i,\beta_i,r_i,s_i}$,
$i=1,\ldots,n$, be single-point moment
kernels of monomial form, and let
$$
M=\prod_{i=1}^{n}m_{b_i}
$$
be the corresponding moment monomial. By the definition of polarization,
$$
\operatorname{Pol}_n(M)
=
\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}
\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_i(z_{\sigma(i)}).
$$
For every permutation $\sigma\in S_n$, applying $\Phi_n$
replaces the factors $b_i(z_{\sigma(i)})$ with the corresponding
moments $m_{b_i}$, so
$$
\Phi_n\left(
\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_i(z_{\sigma(i)})
\right)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{n}m_{b_i}=M.
$$
Hence,
$$
\Phi_n(\operatorname{Pol}_n(M))
=
\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}M=M,
$$
which proves~\eqref{Phi-Pol-identity}.
Now let
$$
F=\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_i(z_i)\in R_n.
$$
Then $\Phi_n(F)=M$, and therefore
$$
\operatorname{Pol}_n(\Phi_n(F))
=
\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}
\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_i(z_{\sigma(i)})
=
\operatorname{Sym}_nF.
$$
By linearity, we obtain~\eqref{Pol-Phi-sym} for
arbitrary $F\in R_n$. In particular, if $F$ is symmetric,
then $\operatorname{Sym}_nF=F$, which implies that
$\Phi_n$ and $\operatorname{Pol}_n$ are mutually inverse on
$R_n^{S_n}$ and $\mathcal{DP}_n$.

We now verify the first identity
in~\eqref{polarization-equivariance}.
We have
$$
\mathscr L_k^{[n]}F
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
(\mathscr L_kb_i)(z_i)
\prod_{j\neq i}b_j(z_j).
$$
Applying $\Phi_n$ and using the definition of $\Phi_1$,
we obtain
$$
\Phi_n(\mathscr L_k^{[n]}F)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\Phi_1(\mathscr L_kb_i)
\prod_{j\neq i}m_{b_j}.
$$
On the other hand, by definition,
$
\mathscr D_km_{b_i}
=
\Phi_1(\mathscr L_kb_i),
$
and $\mathscr D_k$ acts on $\mathcal{DP}$ as a derivation.
Therefore,
$$
\mathscr D_k\Phi_n(F)
=
\mathscr D_k\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}m_{b_i}\right)
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\Phi_1(\mathscr L_kb_i)
\prod_{j\neq i}m_{b_j}
=
\Phi_n(\mathscr L_k^{[n]}F).
$$
Linearity gives the first equality
in~\eqref{polarization-equivariance} for arbitrary
$F\in R_n$.

For the second equality, observe that the operator
$\mathscr L_k^{[n]}$ commutes with permutations of the sets
of variables and hence with the operator $\operatorname{Sym}_n$.
Let $I\in\mathcal{DP}_n$ and
$
F=\operatorname{Pol}_n(I).
$
Then $F$ is symmetric and, by~\eqref{Phi-Pol-identity},
$\Phi_n(F)=I$. Thus,
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{Pol}_n(\mathscr D_kI)
&=
\operatorname{Pol}_n\bigl(\mathscr D_k\Phi_n(F)\bigr)
=
\operatorname{Pol}_n\bigl(\Phi_n(\mathscr L_k^{[n]}F)\bigr)
=
\operatorname{Sym}_n(\mathscr L_k^{[n]}F)
=
\mathscr L_k^{[n]}\operatorname{Sym}_nF=
\\
&=
\mathscr L_k^{[n]}F
=
\mathscr L_k^{[n]}\operatorname{Pol}_n(I).
\end{align*}
This proves the second equality and completes the proof of the lemma.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By the characterization of relative invariants of weight $-1$
in terms of the Lie algebra action, every polynomial
$F\in\mathcal P_{n,-1}$ satisfies the system
$$
\mathscr L_k^{[n]}F=0,
\qquad k=1,\ldots,8.
$$

First, consider the case $n=1$. Let
$
F=F(x,y,p,q)\in\mathcal P_{1,-1}.
$
For the translation generators
$
X_1=\partial_x,
X_2=\partial_y,
$
we have
$
\operatorname{div}X_1=\operatorname{div}X_2=0,
$
and their first prolongations coincide with the generators
themselves. Therefore, the corresponding operators on the space
of a single first jet take the form
$$
\mathscr L_1^{[1]}=\partial_x,
\qquad
\mathscr L_2^{[1]}=\partial_y.
$$
The conditions
$$
\mathscr L_1^{[1]}F=0,
\qquad
\mathscr L_2^{[1]}F=0
$$
give the system
$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}=0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=0.
$$
Thus, $F$ does not depend on $x$ and $y$, that is,
$
F=F(p,q).
$

For the generators
$
X_3=x\partial_x,
X_4=y\partial_y,
$
we have
$
\operatorname{div}X_3=1,
\operatorname{div}X_4=1,
$
and their first prolongations take the form
$$
X_3^{(1)}
=
x\partial_x-p\partial_p,
\qquad
X_4^{(1)}
=
y\partial_y-q\partial_q.
$$
Therefore, the corresponding operators on the space of a single first
jet are
$$
\mathscr L_3^{[1]}
=
x\partial_x-p\partial_p+1,
\qquad
\mathscr L_4^{[1]}
=
y\partial_y-q\partial_q+1.
$$
Since we have already established that $F=F(p,q)$, the conditions
$$
\mathscr L_3^{[1]}F=0,
\qquad
\mathscr L_4^{[1]}F=0
$$
take the form
$$
-p\frac{\partial F}{\partial p}+F=0,
\qquad
-q\frac{\partial F}{\partial q}+F=0,
$$
or, equivalently,
$$
p\frac{\partial F}{\partial p}=F,
\qquad
q\frac{\partial F}{\partial q}=F.
$$
The first equality means that $F$ is homogeneous of degree $1$
in the variable $p$, while the second means that it is homogeneous of degree $1$
in the variable $q$. Since $F$ is a polynomial, it follows that
$$
F=c\,pq
$$
for some constant $c$.

Now, for the generator
$
X_5=y\partial_x,
$
we have
$
\mathscr L_5^{[1]}
=
y\partial_x-p\partial_q.
$
Hence,
$$
0=\mathscr L_5^{[1]} F
=-p\frac{\partial}{\partial q}(cpq)
=-cp^2,
$$
so $c=0$. Thus,
$
\mathcal P_{1,-1}=0.
$

We now turn to the case $n=2$. Let
$
F=
F(x_1,y_1,p_1,q_1,x_2,y_2,p_2,q_2)
\in\mathcal P_{2,-1}.
$
As in the case of a single jet, we first use
the translation generators. For these,
$$
\mathscr L_1^{[2]}
=
\partial_{x_1}+\partial_{x_2},
\qquad
\mathscr L_2^{[2]}
=
\partial_{y_1}+\partial_{y_2}.
$$
Therefore, the equations
$$
\mathscr L_1^{[2]}F=0,
\qquad
\mathscr L_2^{[2]}F=0
$$
mean that $F$ depends on the coordinates of the two points
only through their differences
$$
u=x_2-x_1,
\qquad
v=y_2-y_1.
$$
Thus,
$
F=F(u,v,p_1,q_1,p_2,q_2).
$

We now show that $F$ vanishes on the hyperplane
$v=0$.
For the generator
$
X_8=xy\partial_x+y^2\partial_y,
$
we have the following first prolongation:
$$
\mathscr L_8
=
xy\partial_x+y^2\partial_y
-yp\partial_p
-(xp+2yq)\partial_q
+3y.
$$
Thus, on two jets,
$$
\mathscr L_8^{[2]}
=
\sum_{i=1}^2
\left(
x_i y_i\partial_{x_i}
+y_i^2\partial_{y_i}
-y_i p_i\partial_{p_i}
-(x_i p_i+2y_iq_i)\partial_{q_i}
+3y_i
\right).
$$
Since $F$ depends only on coordinate differences,
at $v=0$ we may set
$$
(x_1,y_1)=(0,0),
\qquad
(x_2,y_2)=(u,0).
$$
Therefore, only one term remains in $\mathscr L_8^{[2]}$:
$
-(x_2p_2)\partial_{q_2}
=-up_2\partial_{q_2}.
$
Thus,
$$
\left.\mathscr L_8^{[2]}\right|_{v=0}
=
-up_2\partial_{q_2}.
$$
Hence, the equation $\mathscr L_8^{[2]}F=0$ implies
$$

\left.
\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_2}
\right|_{v=0}
=0,
$$
and therefore, as a polynomial identity,
$$
\left.
\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_2}
\right|_{v=0}=0.
$$

$
\mathscr L_5^{[2]}
=
v\partial_u
-p_1\partial_{q_1}
-p_2\partial_{q_2}.
$

$$
-p_1
\left.
\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_1}
\right|_{v=0}
-p_2
\left.
\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_2}
\right|_{v=0}

$$
\left.
\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_1}
\right|_{v=0}=0.

-q_1\partial_{q_1}
-q_2\partial_{q_2}

-q_2

\frac{\partial F}{\partial q_2}
\right|_{v=0}
+2F|_{v=0}

the proof of the theorem.

<!-- theorem-11: body_md -->
\label{moment-functional-independence}
Let $P_1,\ldots,P_N\in\mathbb R[x,y,p,q]$ be linearly
independent, and suppose that each of their monomials has degree in $p,q$
at least two. Then the functionals
$$
M_i(f)
=
\iint_{\mathbb R^2}
f(x,y)P_i(x,y,p,q)\,dx\,dy,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,N,
$$
are algebraically independent on the class of twice-differentiable
functions $f$ with compact support.
In particular, the integral realization of the algebra
$\mathcal{DP}_{\geq3}$ is injective.
<!-- end theorem-11 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
For the proof, we use standard methods of the calculus of variations for functionals depending on the function $f$ and its first derivatives.
Let $E_i$ denote
$$
E_i
=
P_i
-
D_x\!\left(f\frac{\partial P_i}{\partial p}\right)
-
D_y\!\left(f\frac{\partial P_i}{\partial q}\right).
$$
the Euler--Lagrange expression for the integrand $fP_i$.
Then, after simplification and integration by parts, the first variation of the functional $M_i$ takes the form
$$
DM_i(f)[h]
=
\int_{\mathbb R^2}
E_i(j^2f)\,h\,dx\,dy,
$$
where $j^2f=(x,y,f,p,q,f_{xx},f_{xy},f_{yy})$
is the second jet of the function $f$.

We first prove that $E_1,\ldots,E_N$ are linearly independent
as functions on the second-jet space.
Suppose that $\sum_i c_iE_i=0$, and let $P=\sum_i c_iP_i$.
Then the Euler--Lagrange expression for $fP$ vanishes identically.
Its coefficients of $f_{xx},f_{xy},f_{yy}$ are
$-fP_{pp}$, $-2fP_{pq}$, and $-fP_{qq}$, respectively.
Since the equality holds for all jet values,
we have $P_{pp}=P_{pq}=P_{qq}=0$. Thus, $P$ has degree
at most one in the variables $p,q$.
However, all monomials of $P$ have degree in $p,q$
at least two, so $P=0$.
The linear independence of the $P_i$ now gives
$c_1=\cdots=c_N=0$.

Thus, the vectors of values
$(E_1(\theta),\ldots,E_N(\theta))$ span
$\mathbb R^N$. Choose jets $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_N$
such that the matrix
$$
A=\bigl(E_i(\theta_j)\bigr)_{i,j=1}^{N}
$$
is nonsingular.
Since the determinant of $A$
depends continuously on the jet coordinates and is nonzero,
it remains nonzero under sufficiently small perturbations.
Therefore, the base points $(x_j,y_j)$ can be chosen to be pairwise distinct
without affecting the nonsingularity of the matrix.



These jets can be realized by a single twice-differentiable function
$f_*$.
Indeed, near each base point $(x_j,y_j)$, choose
a quadratic polynomial whose second jet at that point
coincides with the prescribed jet $\theta_j$.
Next, multiply this polynomial by a smooth function $\chi_j$
that equals $1$ in a neighborhood of $(x_j,y_j)$
and equals $0$ outside a slightly larger neighborhood.
Multiplication by $\chi_j$ therefore does not change the value of the polynomial
or its derivatives up to second order at $(x_j,y_j)$.
Since the base points are pairwise distinct, these neighborhoods can
be chosen to be pairwise disjoint. The sum of the resulting localized
polynomials gives a compactly supported function $f_*$ such that
$$
j^2f_*(x_j,y_j)=\theta_j,
\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.
$$


Now choose nonnegative twice-differentiable functions
$h_j$ with unit integral
and supports in small neighborhoods of the corresponding points.
By the continuity of $E_i(j^2f_*)$, as these
supports shrink, we have
$$
DM_i(f_*)[h_j]
=
\int_{\mathbb R^2}E_i(j^2f_*)h_j\,dx\,dy
\longrightarrow E_i(\theta_j).
$$
Thus, the supports can be chosen so that the matrix
$B=(DM_i(f_*)[h_j])_{i,j=1}^{N}$ is nonsingular.

Consider the map
$$
\Psi:\mathbb R^N\longrightarrow\mathbb R^N,
\qquad
\Psi(t)
=
\left(
M_i\left(f_*+\sum_{j=1}^{N}t_jh_j\right)
\right)_{i=1}^{N}.
$$
It is polynomial in $t$, and its Jacobian matrix
at zero equals $B$. By the inverse function theorem,
the image of $\Psi$ contains an open subset of $\mathbb R^N$.

If there were a polynomial $R$ such that
$R(M_1(f),\ldots,M_N(f))=0$ for all admissible $f$,
it would vanish on this open subset.
Therefore, $R$ would be the zero polynomial, proving
the algebraic independence of the functionals.

depends on only finitely many moment

$x^\alpha y^\beta p^rq^s$, $r+s\geq3$, satisfy
the hypotheses of the assertion just proved. Thus, no nonzero
element of $\mathcal{DP}_{\geq3}$ is realized
as the zero functional, that is, the integral realization
of this algebra is injective.

To count degrees, we use the formal expression
$$
C_{ij}=p_ix_j+q_iy_j-w_i,
\qquad
\ell_i=(p_i,q_i,-w_i),
$$
in which $w_i$ is temporarily an independent symbol.
Each factor $C_{ij}$ is homogeneous of degree $1$
in the components of $\ell_i$ and has degree
at most $1$ in the coordinates $(x_j,y_j)$.
The coordinate degree bounds below
refer to this formal expression.
After substituting $w_i=x_ip_i+y_iq_i$,
the degree in the components of $\ell_i$ becomes
the same total degree in the derivatives $p_i,q_i$,

$$
D_{123}^{\,3}\prod_{i\ne j}C_{ij}^{b_{ij}}.
$$
For each $i=1,2,3$, set
$$
r_i=\sum_{j\ne i}b_{ij},
\qquad
s_i=\sum_{j\ne i}b_{ji}.
$$

$
r_i=s_i,
\qquad i=1,2,3.

The factor $D_{123}^{\,3}$ has degree $3$ in

3+r_i,
$

in the derivatives at the three points. The map $\Phi_3$

to components of $\mathcal{DP}_3$ determined by
the multisets of derivative degrees
of the three moment factors. These components
form a direct sum.

r_i=s_i=a,\qquad i=1,2,3.

<!-- theorem-13: body_md -->
\label{thm:nonfinite-generation}
The space $\mathcal{MI}_3$ is infinite-dimensional.
The algebra $\mathcal{MI}$ is not finitely generated
as an $\mathbb R$-algebra.

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Assign to the moment generator
$m_{\alpha,\beta,r,s}$ the auxiliary degree $r+s$,
and extend it to moment monomials by adding
the degrees of their factors.

By Theorem~\ref{J-family}, for every $a\geq1$,
the invariant $J_{a,0}$ is nonzero and belongs to
$\mathcal{MI}_3$.
Each of its moment factors has derivative degree
$a+3$, so $J_{a,0}$ is homogeneous
of auxiliary degree $3(a+3)$.
These degrees are pairwise distinct, so the invariants
$J_{a,0}$, $a\geq1$, are linearly independent.
Thus,
$$
\dim_{\mathbb R}\mathcal{MI}_3=\infty.
$$

Suppose that $\mathcal{MI}$ is finitely generated.
Since the operators $\mathscr D_k$ preserve
moment degree, each homogeneous component
of a moment invariant is also an invariant.
Replacing a finite generating system
with the homogeneous components of its generators gives
a finite system of homogeneous generators.

However, $\mathcal{MI}_0=\mathbb R$ and
$\mathcal{MI}_1=\mathcal{MI}_2=0$.
Therefore, all nonzero generators of positive degree
have moment degree at least $3$.
Products of two or more such factors
have degree at least $6$.
Thus, $\mathcal{MI}_3$ would have to be the linear
span of finitely many generators
of degree $3$, contradicting its
infinite dimensionality.

The following equality holds:
$$
\mathcal K^{\mathfrak{sl}_3}
=
\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal{MI}).
$$
Thus, every rational projective differential
moment invariant can be expressed as a ratio of two
polynomial moment invariants.
<!-- end theorem-14 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Let
$$
R=\frac{P}{Q}\in\mathcal K^{\mathfrak{sl}_3},
\qquad
P,Q\in\mathcal{DP},\qquad
\gcd(P,Q)=1.
$$
For every $X\in\mathfrak{sl}_3$, we have
$
\mathscr D_XR=0,
$
and hence
$
Q\,\mathscr D_XP-P\,\mathscr D_XQ=0.
$
Since $P$ and $Q$ are relatively prime, this equality
implies
$$
P\mid\mathscr D_XP,
\qquad
Q\mid\mathscr D_XQ.
$$
Thus, for some $h_X\in\mathcal{DP}$, we have
$$
\mathscr D_XP=h_XP,
\qquad
\mathscr D_XQ=h_XQ.
$$
Here, we may assume that $P,Q\neq0$, since the case
$R=0$ is immediate.
Let $\deg_m$ denote the maximum total degree
of a polynomial in the moment variables.
The operator $\mathscr D_X$ preserves each homogeneous
component, so
$\deg_m(\mathscr D_XP)\leq\deg_mP$.
If $h_X\neq0$, then
$$
\deg_m(h_XP)=\deg_mh_X+\deg_mP,
$$
and hence $\deg_mh_X=0$.
Thus, $h_X=\lambda_X\in\mathbb R$;
homogeneity of $P$ and $Q$ is not required.

The map $X\mapsto\lambda_X$ is linear.
Under the standard convention for the commutator of vector
fields, we have
$$
[X^{(1)},Y^{(1)}]=[X,Y]^{(1)},
\qquad
\operatorname{div}[X,Y]
=
X(\operatorname{div}Y)-Y(\operatorname{div}X).
$$
Therefore, the weighted operators
$\mathscr L_X=X^{(1)}+\operatorname{div}X$
satisfy
$[\mathscr L_X,\mathscr L_Y]=\mathscr L_{[X,Y]}$.
By the definition of the action on moment generators, this gives
$
[\mathscr D_X,\mathscr D_Y]
=
\mathscr D_{[X,Y]}.
$
Applying this identity to $P$, we obtain
$$
\lambda_{[X,Y]}P
=
[\mathscr D_X,\mathscr D_Y]P
=
(\lambda_Y\lambda_X-\lambda_X\lambda_Y)P
=
0.
$$
Since $P\neq0$, we have $\lambda_{[X,Y]}=0$.
Since
$
\mathfrak{sl}_3=[\mathfrak{sl}_3,\mathfrak{sl}_3],
$
and the character $\lambda$ vanishes on all commutators,
$\lambda([X,Y])=0$, it vanishes identically on the entire
algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_3$. Hence,
$
\lambda_X=0$
{for all } $X\in\mathfrak{sl}_3.$
Thus,
$
\mathscr D_XP=\mathscr D_XQ=0,
$
that is, $P,Q\in\mathcal{MI}$. Therefore,
$$
R=\frac{P}{Q}
\in\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal{MI}).
$$
The reverse inclusion is immediate.
