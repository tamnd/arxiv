<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:even-no-real}
For every integer $m\geq1$, the polynomial $\mathrm{Le}_{2m}$ has no real zero.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
By (2.1), for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$,
\[
\operatorname{Le}_{2m}(x)
=1+2\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}
\binom{2m-j}{j}x^{2m-2j}
\geq 1>0,
\]
since all coefficients in the sum are positive and all
exponents are even. Hence $\operatorname{Le}_{2m}$
has no real zero.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:odd-real}
For every integer $m\geq0$, the polynomial $\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}$ has exactly one real zero. This zero is positive and simple.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let \(n=2m+1\) with \(m\geq0\). From~\eqref{eq:leonardo-explicit},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:classification-odd-expansion}
\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}(x)
=
2\sum_{j=0}^{m}
\binom{2m+1-j}{j}x^{2m+1-2j}-1.
\end{equation}

All coefficients of the nonconstant terms are positive, whereas the
constant term is \(-1\). Hence, after the zero coefficients are omitted,
the coefficient sequence of \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}(x)\) contains exactly one sign
change. By Descartes' rule of signs, \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}\) has exactly one
positive real zero, counted with multiplicity. Therefore, this zero is
simple.

We next examine the negative real zeros. Replacing \(x\) by \(-x\) in
\eqref{eq:classification-odd-expansion}, and using the fact that all
powers \(2m+1-2j\) are odd, we obtain
\[
\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}(-x)
=
-2\sum_{j=0}^{m}
\binom{2m+1-j}{j}x^{2m+1-2j}-1.
\]
All nonzero coefficients of \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}(-x)\) are negative, so its
coefficient sequence contains no sign change. Hence, by Descartes'
rule of signs, \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}\) has no negative real zero.

Finally,
\[
\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}(0)=-1\neq0,
\]
so \(x=0\) is not a zero. Consequently, \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}\) has exactly one
real zero, which is positive and simple.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:positive-root-bounds}
Let $n$ be a positive odd integer, and let $\xi_n$ denote the unique
positive zero of $\mathrm{Le}_n$. If
\[
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},
\]
then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:classification-positive-bounds}
2\sinh\!\left(\frac{\log\varphi}{n+1}\right)
<
\xi_n
\leq
\frac{1}{n+1}.
\end{equation}
Equality in the upper bound holds if and only if $n=1$. Moreover,
\[
\lim_{m\to\infty}\xi_{2m+1}=0.
\]
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We first prove the upper bound. For odd $n\geq3$, the polynomial
$\mathrm{Le}_n$ can be written as
\[
\mathrm{Le}_n(x)
=
-1+(n+1)x
+
\sum_{\substack{3\leq k\leq n\\ k\ \mathrm{odd}}}
a_kx^k,
\qquad a_k>0.
\]
Since $\mathrm{Le}_n(\xi_n)=0$ and $\xi_n>0$, we have
\[
1
=
(n+1)\xi_n
+
\sum_{\substack{3\leq k\leq n\\ k\ \mathrm{odd}}}
a_k\xi_n^k
>
(n+1)\xi_n.
\]
Thus
\[
\xi_n<\frac{1}{n+1}.
\]
For $n=1$, $\mathrm{Le}_1(x)=2x-1$, so
\[
\xi_1=\frac12=\frac{1}{1+1}.
\]
Hence
\[
\xi_n\leq\frac{1}{n+1},
\]
with equality if and only if $n=1$.

For the lower bound, using
\[
\mathrm{Le}_n(x)=2F_{n+1}(x)-1,
\]
the equation $\mathrm{Le}_n(\xi_n)=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:positive-root-fibonacci-value}
F_{n+1}(\xi_n)=\frac12.
\end{equation}
Since $\xi_n>0$, write
\[
\xi_n=2\sinh t
\]
for some $t>0$. Then
\[
\sqrt{\xi_n^2+4}=2\cosh t,
\]
and the characteristic roots associated with the Fibonacci polynomial
recurrence are
\[
\alpha=e^t,
\qquad
\beta=-e^{-t}.
\]
Since \(n+1\) is even, the Binet representation given above yields
\[
F_{n+1}(\xi_n)
=
\frac{e^{(n+1)t}-e^{-(n+1)t}}
     {e^t+e^{-t}}
=
\frac{\sinh((n+1)t)}{\cosh t}.
\]
Together with \eqref{eq:positive-root-fibonacci-value}, this gives
\[
\sinh((n+1)t)=\frac12\cosh t>\frac12.
\]
Since
\[
\sinh(\log\varphi)
=
\frac{\varphi-\varphi^{-1}}{2}
=
\frac12,
\]
and $\sinh$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$, we obtain
\[
(n+1)t>\log\varphi.
\]
Therefore,
\[
t>\frac{\log\varphi}{n+1},
\]
and hence
\[
\xi_n
=
2\sinh t
>
2\sinh\!\left(\frac{\log\varphi}{n+1}\right).
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:classification-positive-bounds}.

Finally, as $n\to\infty$ through odd integers,
\[
2\sinh\!\left(\frac{\log\varphi}{n+1}\right)\to0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{1}{n+1}\to0.
\]
Thus, by the squeeze theorem,
\[
\lim_{\substack{n\to\infty\\ n\ \mathrm{odd}}}\xi_n=0,
\]
or equivalently,
\[
\lim_{m\to\infty}\xi_{2m+1}=0.
\]
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:positive-root-asymptotics}
For each positive odd integer \(n\), let \(\xi_n\) denote
the unique positive zero of \(\mathrm{Le}_n\), and let \(\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\).
Then
\[
\xi_{n+2}<\xi_n
\]
for every positive odd integer \(n\). Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:positive-root-asymptotic}
\lim_{\substack{n\to\infty\\ n\ \mathrm{odd}}}
(n+1)\xi_n
=
2\log\varphi.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\[
\xi_n\sim\frac{2\log\varphi}{n+1}
\qquad
(n\to\infty,\ n\ \mathrm{odd}),
\]
and the lower bound in
\eqref{eq:classification-positive-bounds}
is asymptotically sharp.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let \(n\) be a positive odd integer.
The Fibonacci recurrence and
\eqref{eq:leonardo-fibonacci} give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Le}_{n+2}(x)-\mathrm{Le}_n(x)
&=
2\bigl(F_{n+3}(x)-F_{n+1}(x)\bigr)\\
&=
2xF_{n+2}(x).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since \(F_{n+2}(x)>0\) for \(x>0\), evaluating this identity
at \(x=\xi_n\) yields
\[
\mathrm{Le}_{n+2}(\xi_n)
=
2\xi_nF_{n+2}(\xi_n)
>
0.
\]
On the other hand, \(\mathrm{Le}_{n+2}(0)=-1\).
The Intermediate Value Theorem therefore gives a zero of
\(\mathrm{Le}_{n+2}\) in \((0,\xi_n)\).
By the uniqueness of the positive zero established in
Theorem~\ref{thm:odd-real}, this zero is \(\xi_{n+2}\).
Hence
\[
0<\xi_{n+2}<\xi_n.
\]

To obtain the asymptotic behavior, write
\[
\xi_n=2\sinh t_n,
\qquad t_n>0.
\]
By Theorem~\ref{thm:positive-root-bounds},
\(\xi_n\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\) through odd integers,
and therefore \(t_n\to0\).
As shown in the proof of that theorem,
\[
\sinh\bigl((n+1)t_n\bigr)
=
\frac12\cosh t_n.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\sinh\bigl((n+1)t_n\bigr)
\longrightarrow
\frac12
=
\sinh(\log\varphi).
\]
The continuity of the inverse hyperbolic sine gives
\[
(n+1)t_n\longrightarrow\log\varphi.
\]
Using \(\sinh t_n/t_n\to1\), we obtain
\[
(n+1)\xi_n
=
2(n+1)t_n\,\frac{\sinh t_n}{t_n}
\longrightarrow
2\log\varphi.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:positive-root-asymptotic}.

Finally,
\[
2(n+1)\sinh\!\left(
\frac{\log\varphi}{n+1}
\right)
\longrightarrow
2\log\varphi.
\]
Combining this limit with
\eqref{eq:positive-root-asymptotic}, we conclude that
\[
\lim_{\substack{n\to\infty\\ n\ \mathrm{odd}}}
\frac{\xi_n}{
2\sinh\!\left(\dfrac{\log\varphi}{n+1}\right)
}
=
1.
\]
Thus the lower bound is asymptotically sharp.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:odd-no-imaginary}
For every integer $m\geq0$, the polynomial $\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}$ has no purely imaginary zero.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $y\in\mathbb{R}$. Substituting $x=iy$ into~\eqref{eq:classification-odd-expansion} gives
\[
\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}(iy)
=
-1+2i\sum_{j=0}^{m}
(-1)^{m-j}
\binom{2m+1-j}{j}
y^{2m+1-2j}.
\]
The sum is real, and therefore
\[
\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}(iy)\bigr)=-1.
\]
Thus $\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}(iy)\neq0$ for every real $y$.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:even-all-imaginary}
For every integer \(m\geq1\), all \(2m\) zeros of\hspace{0.2em}\(\mathrm{Le}_{2m}\) are
distinct and purely imaginary, with imaginary parts lying in
\((-2,2)\).
More precisely, there exist real numbers
\[
0<\mu_1<\mu_2<\cdots<\mu_m<2
\]
such that the zero set of $\mathrm{Le}_{2m}$ is
\[
\{\pm i\mu_1,\ldots,\pm i\mu_m\}.
\]
In particular, every zero of $\mathrm{Le}_{2m}$ is simple.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
By~\eqref{eq:classification-chebyshev} and
\(i^{2m}=(-1)^m\), we have
\[
\mathrm{Le}_{2m}(x)
=
2(-1)^mU_{2m}\left(\frac{x}{2i}\right)-1.
\]
Thus, setting \(t=x/(2i)\), the equation \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m}(x)=0\)
is equivalent to
\[
U_{2m}(t)=\frac{(-1)^m}{2}.
\]
Define
\[
H_m(t)=U_{2m}(t)-\frac{(-1)^m}{2}.
\]
The zeros of \(H_m\) correspond to those of \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m}\)
under the transformation \(x=2it\).

For \(j=0,1,\ldots,2m\), let
\[
\theta_j=\frac{(j+\frac12)\pi}{2m+1},
\qquad
t_j=\cos\theta_j.
\]
Since \(0<\theta_0<\theta_1<\cdots<\theta_{2m}<\pi\)
and cosine is strictly decreasing on \((0,\pi)\), we have
\[
1>t_0>t_1>\cdots>t_{2m}>-1.
\]
Using the trigonometric representation of \(U_{2m}\) and
the equality \((2m+1)\theta_j=(j+\frac12)\pi\), we obtain
\[
U_{2m}(t_j)
=
\frac{\sin((2m+1)\theta_j)}{\sin\theta_j}
=
\frac{(-1)^j}{\sin\theta_j}.
\]
Since \(0<\theta_j<\pi\), we have \(0<\sin\theta_j\leq1\).
Consequently,
\[
|U_{2m}(t_j)|\geq1,
\qquad
\operatorname{sgn}(U_{2m}(t_j))=(-1)^j.
\]

The constant subtracted in the definition of \(H_m\) has
absolute value \(1/2\), which is smaller than
\(|U_{2m}(t_j)|\). Therefore, this subtraction does not
change the sign, and
\[
\operatorname{sgn}(H_m(t_j))=(-1)^j,
\qquad j=0,\ldots,2m.
\]
In particular,
\[
H_m(t_j)H_m(t_{j+1})<0,
\qquad j=0,\ldots,2m-1.
\]
By the Intermediate Value
Theorem~\cite[Theorem~5.3.7]{Bartle and Sherbert2011},
\(H_m\) has at least one real zero in each interval
\((t_{j+1},t_j)\), \(j=0,\ldots,2m-1\).
These \(2m\) intervals are pairwise disjoint and contained
in \((-1,1)\), so \(H_m\) has at least \(2m\) distinct
real zeros in \((-1,1)\).

Since \(H_m\) has degree \(2m\), there is exactly one zero
in each of these intervals, and these zeros exhaust its
zero set. Moreover, all of them are simple, since a
degree-\(2m\) polynomial with \(2m\) distinct zeros cannot
have a multiple zero.

The polynomial \(U_{2m}\) is even, so
\(H_m(-t)=H_m(t)\). Hence the zeros of \(H_m\) occur in
symmetric pairs. Furthermore, \(U_{2m}(0)=(-1)^m\) gives
\[
H_m(0)
=
(-1)^m-\frac{(-1)^m}{2}
=
\frac{(-1)^m}{2}
\neq0.
\]
Thus zero is not a root, and the zeros of \(H_m\) can be
written as
\[
\pm s_1,\ldots,\pm s_m,
\qquad
0<s_1<\cdots<s_m<1.
\]

Returning to \(x=2it\), the zeros of \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m}\) are
\(\pm2is_1,\ldots,\pm2is_m\).
Setting \(\mu_j=2s_j\), we obtain
\[
\operatorname{Zeros}(\mathrm{Le}_{2m})
=
\{\pm i\mu_1,\ldots,\pm i\mu_m\},
\qquad
0<\mu_1<\cdots<\mu_m<2.
\]
Therefore, all \(2m\) zeros of \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m}\) are distinct,
purely imaginary, with imaginary parts lying in \((-2,2)\).
Since \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m}\) has degree \(2m\), every zero is simple.

\label{cor:odd-remaining-zeros}
For every integer \(m\geq1\), the zeros of \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}\), other than its
unique positive real zero, consist of \(2m\) nonreal zeros, counted with
multiplicity. These zeros occur in complex conjugate pairs, and each of
them has both nonzero real and nonzero imaginary parts.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
By Theorem~\ref{thm:odd-real}, the polynomial \(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}\)
has exactly one real zero, which is positive and simple.
Since its degree is \(2m+1\), the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra
implies that the remaining \(2m\) zeros are nonreal, counted
with multiplicity.

These zeros occur in complex conjugate pairs because
\(\mathrm{Le}_{2m+1}\) has real coefficients. Their imaginary parts
are nonzero since they are nonreal, and their real parts
are nonzero by Theorem~\ref{thm:odd-no-imaginary}.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:fp-equivalence}
The points \(x=\pm2i\) are not zeros of \(\mathrm{Le}_n\).
For every \(\theta\in\mathbb C\) with \(\sin\theta\neq0\),
the equation \(\mathrm{Le}_n(2i\cos\theta)=0\) is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-trig}
\sin((n+1)\theta)=\lambda_n\sin\theta.
\end{equation}
If, in addition, \(|\lambda_n\sin\theta|<1\), these
conditions are equivalent to
\[
\theta=T_{n,k}(\theta)
\]
for some \(k\in\mathbb Z\).
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
We first exclude \(x=\pm2i\), which correspond to
\(\sin\theta=0\) under \(x=2i\cos\theta\).
Using \(U_n(1)=n+1\) and \(U_n(-1)=(-1)^n(n+1)\)
in~\eqref{eq:classification-chebyshev}, we obtain
\[
\mathrm{Le}_n(\pm2i)
=
2(\pm i)^n(n+1)-1
\neq0,
\]
since \(\left|2(\pm i)^n(n+1)\right|=2(n+1)>1\).

Now let \(\theta\in\mathbb C\) satisfy \(\sin\theta\neq0\).
Combining~\eqref{eq:classification-chebyshev}
and~\eqref{eq:chebyshev-trigonometric} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-trigonometric-representation}
\mathrm{Le}_n(2i\cos\theta)
=
2i^n\frac{\sin((n+1)\theta)}{\sin\theta}-1.
\end{equation}
Since \(\sin\theta\neq0\), setting this expression equal
to zero gives~\eqref{eq:fp-trig}, and the converse follows
by substitution.

Assume further that \(|\lambda_n\sin\theta|<1\), and set
\(w=\lambda_n\sin\theta\) and \(\alpha=\arcsin w\).
Then \(\sin\alpha=w\), so the equation \(\sin z=w\)
is equivalent to
\[
0=\sin z-\sin\alpha
=
2\cos\left(\frac{z+\alpha}{2}\right)
\sin\left(\frac{z-\alpha}{2}\right).
\]
Thus either \(\sin((z-\alpha)/2)=0\) or
\(\cos((z+\alpha)/2)=0\). The first case gives
\((z-\alpha)/2=j\pi\), while the second gives
\((z+\alpha)/2=\pi/2+j\pi\). Hence all solutions are
\[
z=2j\pi+\alpha
\quad\text{or}\quad
z=(2j+1)\pi-\alpha,
\qquad j\in\mathbb Z.
\]
These two families can be written in the single form
\[
z=k\pi+(-1)^k\arcsin w,
\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
\]

If~\eqref{eq:fp-trig} holds, applying this representation
with \(z=(n+1)\theta\) and \(w=\lambda_n\sin\theta\) gives
\[
(n+1)\theta
=
k\pi+(-1)^k\arcsin(\lambda_n\sin\theta)
\]
for some \(k\in\mathbb Z\). Dividing by \(n+1\) yields
\(\theta=T_{n,k}(\theta)\).

Conversely, if \(\theta=T_{n,k}(\theta)\), taking the sine
of both sides of
\[
(n+1)\theta
=
k\pi+(-1)^k\arcsin(\lambda_n\sin\theta)
\]
and using \(\sin(k\pi+(-1)^k\alpha)=\sin\alpha\)
recovers~\eqref{eq:fp-trig}. This completes the proof.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:fp-contraction}
For $k=1,\ldots,n$, let
\[
D_{n,k}
=
\left\{
\theta\in\mathbb{C}:
\left|\theta-\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\right|
\leq\frac{1}{n+1}
\right\}.
\]
Then $T_{n,k}$ is well defined on $D_{n,k}$ and maps this disk into itself. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-lipschitz}
|T_{n,k}(\theta)-T_{n,k}(\eta)|
\leq
\kappa_n|\theta-\eta|,
\qquad \theta,\eta\in D_{n,k},
\end{equation}
where
\[
\kappa_n
=
\frac{\sqrt{61}}{8(n+1)}
<1.
\]
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Fix $k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and write $\theta=u+iv\in D_{n,k}$, where $u,v\in\mathbb{R}$. Since the center of the disk is real,
\[
|v|\leq\frac{1}{n+1}\leq\frac12.
\]
The complex sine satisfies
\[
|\sin(u+iv)|^2
=
\sin^2u+\sinh^2v
\leq
\cosh^2v.
\]
Hence
\[
|\lambda_n\sin\theta|
\leq
\frac12\cosh\!\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\right)
\leq
\frac12\cosh\!\left(\frac12\right).
\]
Also,
\[
\cosh\!\left(\frac12\right)
=
1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^j(2j)!}
\leq
1+\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^j}
=
\frac76.
\]
It follows that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-argument-bound}
|\lambda_n\sin\theta|
\leq
\frac{7}{12}
<
\frac35
<
1.
\end{equation}
Thus, the principal inverse sine in~\eqref{eq:fp-map} is well defined throughout $D_{n,k}$. We use the common bound $\rho=3/5$ below.

For $|w|<1$, integration along the segment from $0$ to $w$ gives
\[
\arcsin w
=
w\int_0^1
\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-t^2w^2}}.
\]
Since
\[
\left|\sqrt{1-t^2w^2}\right|
\geq
\sqrt{1-t^2|w|^2},
\]
we obtain
\[
|\arcsin w|
\leq
|w|\int_0^1
\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-t^2|w|^2}}
\leq
\frac{|w|}{\sqrt{1-|w|^2}}.
\]
In particular, $|w|\leq3/5$ implies $|\arcsin w|\leq3/4$. Therefore,
\[
\left|
T_{n,k}(\theta)-\frac{k\pi}{n+1}
\right|
\leq
\frac{3}{4(n+1)}
<
\frac{1}{n+1},
\qquad \theta\in D_{n,k},
\]
which proves $T_{n,k}(D_{n,k})\subseteq D_{n,k}$.

The strict bound in~\eqref{eq:fp-argument-bound} also shows that $T_{n,k}$ is analytic on an open set containing $D_{n,k}$. Differentiating gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-derivative}
T_{n,k}'(\theta)
=
\frac{(-1)^k}{n+1}
\frac{\lambda_n\cos\theta}
{\sqrt{1-\lambda_n^2\sin^2\theta}}.
\end{equation}
From $|\lambda_n\sin\theta|\leq\rho$ and $|\lambda_n|=1/2$, we have $|\sin\theta|\leq2\rho$. Consequently,
\[
|\cos\theta|^2
=
|1-\sin^2\theta|
\leq
1+|\sin\theta|^2
\leq
1+4\rho^2.
\]
Furthermore,
\[
\left|
\sqrt{1-\lambda_n^2\sin^2\theta}
\right|
\geq
\sqrt{1-|\lambda_n\sin\theta|^2}
\geq
\sqrt{1-\rho^2}.
\]

\[

\leq
\frac{\sqrt{1+4\rho^2}}
{2(n+1)\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}
=
\frac{\sqrt{61}}{8(n+1)}
=
\kappa_n.
\]
Since $n\geq1$, we have $\kappa_n\leq\sqrt{61}/16<1$.

\[
T_{n,k}(\theta)-T_{n,k}(\eta)
=

\]
Taking absolute values and applying the derivative bound proves~\eqref{eq:fp-lipschitz}.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:fp-convergence}
For every \(k=1,\ldots,n\), the map \(T_{n,k}\) has a unique
fixed point \(\theta_*^{(k)}\) in \(D_{n,k}\).
For every initial value \(\theta_0^{(k)}\in D_{n,k}\), the iteration
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-iteration}
\theta_{r+1}^{(k)}
=
T_{n,k}\bigl(\theta_r^{(k)}\bigr),
\qquad r=0,1,2,\ldots,
\end{equation}
converges geometrically to \(\theta_*^{(k)}\) and satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-error}
\left|
\theta_r^{(k)}-\theta_*^{(k)}
\right|
\leq
\frac{\kappa_n^r}{1-\kappa_n}
\left|
\theta_1^{(k)}-\theta_0^{(k)}
\right|,
\qquad r\geq0.
\end{equation}
In particular, the initial values
\[
\theta_0^{(k)}=\frac{k\pi}{n+1},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,n,
\]
are admissible for every \(n\geq1\).

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Each $D_{n,k}$ is a nonempty closed subset of $\mathbb{C}$ and is therefore complete in the usual metric. Theorem~\ref{thm:fp-contraction} shows that $T_{n,k}$ maps $D_{n,k}$ into itself and is a contraction with constant $\kappa_n<1$. Banach's fixed-point theorem~\cite{Berinde2007} gives the unique fixed point and convergence from every initial value in the disk.

Repeated application of~\eqref{eq:fp-lipschitz} yields
\[
\left|
\theta_{j+1}^{(k)}-\theta_j^{(k)}
\right|
\leq
\kappa_n^j
\left|
\theta_1^{(k)}-\theta_0^{(k)}
\right|,
\qquad j\geq0.
\]
Thus,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|
\theta_r^{(k)}-\theta_*^{(k)}
\right|
&\leq
\sum_{j=r}^{\infty}
\left|
\theta_{j+1}^{(k)}-\theta_j^{(k)}
\right|\\
&\leq
\sum_{j=r}^{\infty}
\kappa_n^j
\left|
\theta_1^{(k)}-\theta_0^{(k)}
\right|\\
&=
\frac{\kappa_n^r}{1-\kappa_n}
\left|
\theta_1^{(k)}-\theta_0^{(k)}
\right|.
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves~\eqref{eq:fp-error} and geometric convergence. The prescribed initial values are the centers of the disks and hence belong to them.

\label{thm:fp-all-zeros}
Let \(n\geq1\). For each \(k=1,\ldots,n\), let
\(\theta_*^{(k)}\) denote the unique fixed point of \(T_{n,k}\)
in \(D_{n,k}\), given by Theorem~\ref{thm:fp-convergence}.
Then the numbers
\[
x_k=2i\cos\theta_*^{(k)},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,n,
\]
are pairwise distinct and constitute all zeros of \(\mathrm{Le}_n\).
In particular, every zero of \(\mathrm{Le}_n\) is simple.

Moreover, for any initial values
\(\theta_0^{(k)}\in D_{n,k}\), the iterations defined
by~\eqref{eq:fp-iteration} yield the exact limit representations

=
\lim_{r\to\infty}2i\cos\theta_r^{(k)},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,n.
\]

<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
For each \(k=1,\ldots,n\), the inclusion
\(\theta_*^{(k)}\in D_{n,k}\) gives
\[
0<\frac{k\pi-1}{n+1}
\leq \operatorname{Re}\theta_*^{(k)}
\leq \frac{k\pi+1}{n+1}<\pi.
\]
Thus all fixed points belong to
\[
S=\{\theta\in\mathbb C:0<\operatorname{Re}\theta<\pi\},
\]
and \(\sin\theta_*^{(k)}\neq0\).
By Theorem~\ref{thm:fp-contraction},
\(\lvert\lambda_n\sin\theta_*^{(k)}\rvert<1\).
Hence Lemma~\ref{lem:fp-equivalence} implies
\[
\mathrm{Le}_n\bigl(2i\cos\theta_*^{(k)}\bigr)=0.
\]

The disks \(D_{n,k}\) are pairwise disjoint, since the
distance between any two distinct centers is at least
\(\pi/(n+1)>2/(n+1)\), the sum of their radii.
Therefore, their fixed points are pairwise distinct.

Moreover, cosine is injective on \(S\).
Indeed, if \(\theta,\eta\in S\) and
\(\cos\theta=\cos\eta\), then
\[
\theta-\eta=2j\pi
\quad\text{or}\quad
\theta+\eta=2j\pi
\qquad (j\in\mathbb Z).
\]
The second possibility is excluded by
\(0<\operatorname{Re}(\theta+\eta)<2\pi\),
while the first forces \(j=0\), since
\(-\pi<\operatorname{Re}(\theta-\eta)<\pi\).
Thus \(\theta=\eta\).

Consequently, the numbers
\(x_k=2i\cos\theta_*^{(k)}\) are \(n\) distinct zeros
of the degree-\(n\) polynomial \(\mathrm{Le}_n\).
They therefore constitute its complete zero set,
and every zero is simple.

Finally, the limit representations follow from
Theorem~\ref{thm:fp-convergence} and the continuity
of the cosine function.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:fp-symmetry}
Let \(n\geq1\), and let
\[
\theta_*^{(k)}\in D_{n,k},
\qquad
x_k=2i\cos\theta_*^{(k)},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,n,
\]
be the fixed points and zeros given by
Theorem~\ref{thm:fp-all-zeros}. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-branch-symmetry}
\theta_*^{(n+1-k)}
=
\pi-\overline{\theta_*^{(k)}},
\qquad
x_{n+1-k}
=
\overline{x_k},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,n.
\end{equation}
In particular, if \(n\) is odd, the branch corresponding to
\[
k=\frac{n+1}{2}
\]
yields the unique positive real zero:
\[
x_{(n+1)/2}=\xi_n.
\]
If \(n\) is even, then
\[
x_{n+1-k}=-x_k,
\qquad k=1,\ldots,n.
\]
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Fix \(k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\), set \(j=n+1-k\), and define
\[
\mathcal{R}(\theta)=\pi-\overline{\theta}.
\]
Since
\[
\left|
\mathcal{R}(\theta)-\frac{j\pi}{n+1}
\right|
=
\left|
\theta-\frac{k\pi}{n+1}
\right|,
\]
we have
\[
\mathcal{R}(D_{n,k})=D_{n,j}.
\]

On the unit disk, the principal inverse sine is odd and
commutes with complex conjugation:
\[
\arcsin(-w)=-\arcsin w,
\qquad
\arcsin(\overline{w})
=
\overline{\arcsin w},
\qquad |w|<1.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\sin(\pi-\overline{\theta})
=
\overline{\sin\theta},
\qquad
\overline{\lambda_n}=(-1)^n\lambda_n.
\]
The bound established in
Theorem~\ref{thm:fp-contraction} therefore gives, for every
\(\theta\in D_{n,k}\),
\[
\arcsin\!\left(
\lambda_n\sin(\pi-\overline{\theta})
\right)
=
(-1)^n
\overline{
\arcsin(\lambda_n\sin\theta)
}.
\]
Since \(j=n+1-k\) and
\((-1)^{j+n}=-(-1)^k\), it follows that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fp-map-symmetry}
T_{n,j}(\pi-\overline{\theta})
=
\pi-\overline{T_{n,k}(\theta)}.
\end{equation}

Applying~\eqref{eq:fp-map-symmetry} to the fixed point
\(\theta_*^{(k)}\), we obtain
\[
T_{n,j}\!\left(
\pi-\overline{\theta_*^{(k)}}
\right)
=
\pi-\overline{\theta_*^{(k)}}.
\]
Thus \(\pi-\overline{\theta_*^{(k)}}\) is a fixed point of
\(T_{n,j}\) in \(D_{n,j}\). By the uniqueness established in
Theorem~\ref{thm:fp-convergence},
\[
\theta_*^{(j)}
=
\pi-\overline{\theta_*^{(k)}}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_j
&=
2i\cos\!\left(
\pi-\overline{\theta_*^{(k)}}
\right)\\
&=
-2i\,\overline{\cos\theta_*^{(k)}}\\
&=
\overline{x_k}.
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves~\eqref{eq:fp-branch-symmetry}.

If \(n\) is odd, taking \(k=(n+1)/2\) gives
\[
x_{(n+1)/2}
=
\overline{x_{(n+1)/2}},
\]
so this zero is real. By Theorem~\ref{thm:odd-real}, it is
the unique positive real zero \(\xi_n\).

If \(n\) is even, all zeros are purely imaginary by
Theorem~\ref{thm:even-all-imaginary}. Hence
\(\overline{x_k}=-x_k\), which yields
\(x_{n+1-k}=-x_k\).

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:fp-symmetry-computation}
Proposition~\ref{prop:fp-symmetry} explicitly identifies the paired
branches \(k\) and \(n+1-k\). For the central initial values, this
pairing is also preserved throughout the iteration. Indeed, for
\[
\theta_0^{(k)}=\frac{k\pi}{n+1},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,n,
\]
we have
\[
\theta_0^{(n+1-k)}
=
\frac{(n+1-k)\pi}{n+1}
=
\pi-\frac{k\pi}{n+1}
=
\pi-\overline{\theta_0^{(k)}},
\]
since the initial values are real.

Now suppose that, for some \(r\geq0\),
\[
\theta_r^{(n+1-k)}
=
\pi-\overline{\theta_r^{(k)}}.
\]
Using the iteration formula and
\eqref{eq:fp-map-symmetry}, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\theta_{r+1}^{(n+1-k)}
&=
T_{n,n+1-k}\!\left(\theta_r^{(n+1-k)}\right)\\
&=
T_{n,n+1-k}\!\left(
\pi-\overline{\theta_r^{(k)}}
\right)\\
&=
\pi-\overline{
T_{n,k}\!\left(\theta_r^{(k)}\right)
}\\
&=
\pi-\overline{\theta_{r+1}^{(k)}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence, by induction,
\[
\theta_r^{(n+1-k)}
=
\pi-\overline{\theta_r^{(k)}},
\qquad r\geq0.
\]

Therefore, the symmetry between the paired branches is preserved at
every step of the fixed-point iteration. Consequently, it is sufficient
to compute only the branches
\[
k=1,\ldots,
\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil,
\]
since the remaining zeros can be recovered by complex conjugation.

\left|
\widehat{\theta}_k-\theta_*^{(k)}
\right|
\leq

\left|
\theta_{r+1}^{(k)}-\theta_r^{(k)}
\right|,

\left|\widehat{x}_k-x_k\right|

\left|
\theta_{r+1}^{(k)}-\theta_r^{(k)}
\right|.
\end{equation}

\label{eq:fp-stopping-root-error}

By the contraction property and the triangle inequality,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|
\widehat{\theta}_k-\theta_*^{(k)}
\right|
&\leq
\kappa_n
\left|
\theta_r^{(k)}-\theta_*^{(k)}
\right|\\

\right|
\right).
\end{aligned}

For every \(\theta\in D_{n,k}\),

=

\leq\cosh^2v,
\]

=
2|\sin\theta|
\leq
2\cosh\!\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\right).

\right|.

\eqref{eq:fp-aposteriori-root}.
