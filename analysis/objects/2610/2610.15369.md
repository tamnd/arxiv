<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let
\[
 f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^3 w_i\varphi_\Sigma(x-\mu_i),
 \qquad w_i>0,\quad \sum_{i=1}^3 w_i=1,
\]
be a density on $\mathbb R^2$.  Then $f$ has at most four modes, and this
bound is sharp.  If the whitened means $\Sigma^{-1/2}\mu_i$ form an obtuse
or right triangle, or are collinear, then $f$ has at most three modes.
The bound three is sharp in each of these three classes.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:dimension}
For every $d\geq2$, a mixture of three Gaussian densities on
$\mathbb R^d$ with a common positive definite covariance has at most four
modes, and four is attained. The sharp bound is three when the whitened
means are collinear or form a right or obtuse triangle.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- head-1: body_md -->
After whitening, Section~\ref{sec:reduction} profiles out the location
variable and identifies modes with local minima of a separable free energy
$F$ on the probability simplex. Appendix~\ref{app:reduction} proves this
correspondence, including at degenerate stationary points and for affinely
dependent means. Its stationary equations split into scalar branches, so
mode counting becomes the problem of counting where a small number of branch
sums cross one. Sections~\ref{sec:curvature}
and~\ref{sec:folds} bound each branch type. Those separate bounds still permit
one configuration with four minima among the first three types;
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} rules out that configuration by comparing the
heights of the required extrema. A final acute-only branch contributes at
most one more minimum.
Appendix~\ref{app:genericity} removes genericity, and
Section~\ref{sec:sharpness} proves sharpness and the corollary above.
Appendices~\ref{app:reduction}--\ref{app:algebra} contain the detailed
calculations and perturbation arguments. We use only the finiteness result
of \citet[Theorem~1]{okuno2026}.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:curvature}
For every upper branch $S_i$ in the noncollinear generic setup,
$\{\lambda:K(\lambda)>\alpha_i\}$ is an interval.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The key point is that $K$ has no local minimum at a level relevant to the
superlevel set.  We give the calculation because it is the main shape input
to the branch count.

For either lower coordinate, write $a$ for its coefficient and $p$ for its
solution, and set
\[
 z=\frac1{1-ap},\qquad \rho=p'.
\]
Differentiating the coordinate equation in \eqref{eq:stationary} gives the
common differential system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho-z-system}
 \rho=pz,\qquad z'=z^2(z-1),\qquad \rho'=\rho z^2.
\end{equation}
Relabel the two values of $z$ as $u>v>0$, with corresponding positive
slopes $\rho_+,\rho_-$.  Put
\[
 J=\rho_++\rho_-=G',\qquad
 r=\sqrt{J'/J}=\sqrt{G''/G'},
\]
so that $K=J/(1+r)$.  At a fixed $\lambda$, let $X$ take the values $u,v$
with probabilities $\rho_+/J,\rho_-/J$.  Then
$\mathbb E[X^2]=r^2$ and $v<r<u$.

Set $s=u+v$, $t=uv$, $N=(r+u)(r+v)$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta-Z}
 \delta=u+v-r,\qquad
 Z=\frac1u+\frac1v-\frac1r-\frac3{3\delta-2}.
\end{equation}
The two-point identities
\[
 \operatorname{Var}(X^2)=N(r\delta-t),
 \qquad
 \mathbb E[X^3]+r^3=\frac{N(sr-t)}s
\]
follow without a fourth-moment expansion.  Indeed,
$(X^2-u^2)(X^2-v^2)=0$ gives
\[
 \mathbb E[X^4]=(u^2+v^2)r^2-u^2v^2,
\]
and subtracting $r^4$ yields the variance identity.  Likewise,
$X^2=sX-t$ gives
\[
 \mathbb E[X]=\frac{r^2+t}{s},\qquad
 \mathbb E[X^3]=sr^2-\frac{t(r^2+t)}s,
\]
which factors into the displayed third-moment identity.  The probabilities
defining $X$ also depend on $\lambda$, so we differentiate $J'$ explicitly:
\begin{align*}
 J'&=\rho_+u^2+\rho_-v^2,\\
 J''&=\rho_+(3u^4-2u^3)\\
    &\quad+\rho_-(3v^4-2v^3).
\end{align*}
Hence
\[
 2rr'=\frac{J''}{J}-\left(\frac{J'}J\right)^2
 =3\mathbb E[X^4]-2\mathbb E[X^3]-r^4,
\]
and therefore
\begin{align}
 2r\{r'-r^2(1+r)\}
 &=3\operatorname{Var}(X^2)-2\{\mathbb E[X^3]+r^3\}\notag\\
 &=\frac{rtN(3\delta-2)}s Z.
 \label{eq:rprime-Z-main}
\end{align}
Consequently, on the region $\delta>1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kprime-Z-main}
 \frac{K'}K
 =-\frac{uv(r+u)(r+v)(3\delta-2)}
 {2(u+v)(1+r)}Z.
\end{equation}
The prefactor is strictly positive.  Thus at a stationary point of $K$
with $\delta>1$, $Z=0$ and direct differentiation of $K=J/(1+r)$ gives
$r'=r^2(1+r)$.  At that same point, \eqref{eq:rho-z-system} gives
\begin{align*}
 \left(\frac1u+\frac1v-\frac1r\right)'
 &=-(u-1)-(v-1)+(r+1)\\
 &=3-\delta.
\end{align*}
To obtain the remaining derivative, recall $s=u+v$, $t=uv$, and
$\delta=s-r$.  The scalar differential equation gives
\[
 u'+v'=s^2(s-1)-t(3s-2).
\]
Using $s^3-r^3-\delta^3=3sr\delta$ and
$s^2+r^2-\delta^2=2sr$, we find
\begin{align*}
 \delta'-\delta^2(\delta-1)
 &=s^2(s-1)-r^2(r+1)\\
 &\quad-t(3s-2)-\delta^2(\delta-1)\\
 &=3sr\delta-2sr-t(3s-2)\\
 &=rt(3\delta-2)Z=0.
\end{align*}
Hence $\delta'=\delta^2(\delta-1)$ at the stationary point.
Differentiating \eqref{eq:delta-Z} before imposing these pointwise
relations therefore gives
\begin{align}
 Z'&=3-\delta+\frac{9\delta^2(\delta-1)}{(3\delta-2)^2}\notag\\
 &=2+(\delta-1)
 \left[\left(\frac{3\delta}{3\delta-2}\right)^2-1\right]\notag\\
 &>2.
 \label{eq:Zprime-main}
\end{align}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:Kprime-Z-main} now shows that $K''<0$ at every
stationary point for which $\delta>1$.

It remains to check that this region contains every stationary point that
could disconnect the required superlevel set.  If both lower coefficients
are positive, then $u>v>1$ and $r<u$, hence $\delta>v>1$ everywhere.  If
$a_j>0>a_k$, put $\alpha_j=a_j^{-1}$ and
$\gamma=(-a_k)^{-1}$.  In this case $u>1>v>0$ and
$\rho_+=\alpha_j(u-1)$, $\rho_-=\gamma(1-v)$.  Eliminating their ratio from
$r^2=(\rho_+u^2+\rho_-v^2)/J$ first gives
\[
 \frac{\gamma}{\alpha_j}
 =\frac{(u-r)(u+r)(u-1)}
 {(r-v)(r+v)(1-v)}.
\]
On the other hand,
\[
 \gamma-\alpha_j-K
 =\frac{\gamma(r+v)-\alpha_j(r+u)}{1+r}.
\]
Substituting the ratio and factoring gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-cap-main}
 \gamma-\alpha_j-K
 =\frac{\alpha_j(r+u)(u-v)}
 {(1+r)(r-v)(1-v)}(\delta-1).
\end{equation}
Thus $K<\gamma-\alpha_j$ is equivalent to $\delta>1$.  By
\eqref{eq:gram},
\[
 0<\alpha_i<\gamma-\alpha_j.
\]

Finally, if a strict superlevel set of an analytic $K$ were disconnected,
the gap between two components would contain a stationary local minimum.
With two positive lower coefficients, every stationary point instead has
$K''<0$.  In the mixed case, an intervening minimum for
$\{K>\alpha_i\}$ has value at most $\alpha_i<\gamma-\alpha_j$; by
\eqref{eq:mixed-cap-main} it again lies in $\delta>1$ and has $K''<0$, a
contradiction.  Hence $\{K>\alpha_i\}$ is an interval.  Appendix~\ref{app:algebra}
records the factorization details used above.

\label{lem:threefolds}
Every upper-branch derivative has at most three distinct zeros on its
physical domain. If its fold curve ends at a lower-coordinate barrier zero
and that coordinate has inverse coefficient larger than $\alpha_i$, then
every nonnegative horizontal level meets the fold curve exactly once.

In the acute case, the branch $S_3$ with the largest barrier contributes

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:separation}
In the ordered setup \eqref{eq:S1}--\eqref{eq:S2}, suppose
$\alpha_2>\alpha_1$. Every stationary point of $S_1$ has
height below $V_0$. If $S_2$ has two decreasing pieces separated by an increasing
piece, its last local minimum has height above $V_0$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For $S_1^{\mathrm{eq}}$, the first lower barrier and the upper barrier both
equal zero, and their inverse coefficients satisfy $\alpha_2>\alpha_1$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:threefolds}, applied to horizontal level zero, gives
exactly one stationary point. Since $(S_1^{\mathrm{eq}})'$ is initially
negative and finally positive, $S_1^{\mathrm{eq}}$ has one minimum and then
increases to $V_0$.
Consequently
 $(S_1^{\mathrm{eq}})'(\lambda)>0$ implies
$S_1^{\mathrm{eq}}(\lambda)<V_0$.
Both $L(d)$ and $L(d)/(1-L(d))$ decrease with $d$, so $\eta_2>0$ gives
\[
 S_1(\lambda)<S_1^{\mathrm{eq}}(\lambda),\qquad
 S_1'(\lambda)<(S_1^{\mathrm{eq}})'(\lambda).
\]
At any stationary point of $S_1$, the second inequality gives
$(S_1^{\mathrm{eq}})'>0$; the first inequality then gives
$S_1(\lambda)<V_0$.

For the second claim, two separated decreasing pieces force the derivative
signs $-,+,-,+$. Thus the last local minimum lies on the final decreasing
piece of the associated fold curve. Along that curve the stationary height
satisfies \eqref{eq:criticalheight}, so it decreases toward its
endpoint value $V_0$. Its value at the last minimum is therefore strictly
larger than $V_0$. Appendix~\ref{app:branches} gives the fold intersections
and endpoint limits in detail.

For the original parameters, set

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:appendix-extrema}
The stationary points of $F$ on $\mathcal P$ correspond bijectively to
the stationary points of $f$. The correspondence is
$p\mapsto x=\bar\mu(p)$, with inverse given by the posterior probabilities.
Moreover, $p$ is a local minimum of $F$ if and only if $x$ is a local
maximum of $f$. Neither nondegeneracy nor affine independence of the
means is required.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Put $\widetilde w_i=w_i\exp(-\|\mu_i\|^2/2)$ and define
\begin{align*}
 \mathcal E(x,p)
 &=\sum_i p_i\log\frac{p_i}{\widetilde w_i}
   +\frac12\|x\|^2-x\cdot\bar\mu(p)\\
 &=F(p)+\frac12\|x-\bar\mu(p)\|^2.
\end{align*}
Hence $F(p)=\min_{x\in\mathbb R^d}\mathcal E(x,p)$: the location variable has
been profiled out.
For fixed $x$, its unique minimizer over $\mathcal P$ is
\[
 p_i(x)=
 \frac{\widetilde w_i\exp(x\cdot\mu_i)}
 {\sum_j\widetilde w_j\exp(x\cdot\mu_j)}.
\]
Indeed, subtracting the minimum value leaves the relative entropy
$\sum_i p_i\log(p_i/p_i(x))$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \phi(x):=-\log((2\pi)^{d/2}f(x))
 =\min_{p\in\mathcal P}\mathcal E(x,p).
 \label{eq:appendix-variational}
\end{equation}
This is the finite-state Gibbs variational identity. Its maximization form
is the free-energy formulation of EM \citep{nealhinton1998}; for Gaussian
mean shift it equals $-\mathcal E$, up to the Gaussian normalization constant
\citep{carreira2006}. The posterior and mean updates give the associated
Gaussian mean-shift EM iteration \citep{carreira2007}.
Differentiating in $x$ gives
$\nabla\phi(x)=x-\bar\mu(p(x))$.
On the other hand, constrained stationarity of $F$ gives
\[
 \log(p_i/\widetilde w_i)-\mu_i\cdot\bar\mu(p)=\text{constant}.
\]
Thus a stationary $p$ satisfies $p=p(\bar\mu(p))$, and a stationary $x$
satisfies $x=\bar\mu(p(x))$. These two relations establish the stated bijection.

Suppose first that $p_*$ is a local minimum of $F$, and let
$x_*=\bar\mu(p_*)$. The posterior is continuous and $p(x_*)=p_*$, so for $x$
sufficiently close to $x_*$,
\begin{align*}
 \phi(x)
 &=F(p(x))+\frac12\|x-\bar\mu(p(x))\|^2\\
 &\ge F(p_*)=\phi(x_*).
\end{align*}
Hence $x_*$ is a local maximum of $f$. Conversely, if $x_*$ is a local
minimum of $\phi$ and $p_*=p(x_*)$, then $\bar\mu(p_*)=x_*$. For all $p$
sufficiently close to $p_*$, continuity of $\bar\mu$ and
\eqref{eq:appendix-variational} yield
\[
 F(p)=\mathcal E(\bar\mu(p),p)
 \ge\phi(\bar\mu(p))\ge\phi(x_*)=F(p_*).
\]
This proves the converse.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:appendix-minimum}
Consider a stationary point for which all
$h_i=1/p_i-a_i$ are nonzero. If all $h_i>0$, it is a strict local
minimum of $F$. If at least two $h_i<0$, it is not a local minimum.
If exactly one $h_i<0$, then its constrained Hessian is positive
definite if and only if $S_i'(\lambda)<0$, where $S_i$ is the branch
sum with coordinate $i$ upper and the other coordinates lower.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
The constrained Hessian is
\[
 Q(v)=\sum_i h_iv_i^2,\qquad \sum_i v_i=0.
\]
Positivity when every $h_i>0$ is immediate. If $h_i,h_j<0$, the tangent
vector with $v_i=1$, $v_j=-1$, and the remaining entry zero gives
$Q(v)<0$.

Suppose now that only $h_i$ is negative, and call the other indices
$j,k$. Eliminate $v_i=-v_j-v_k$. In the independent coordinates
$(v_j,v_k)$, the quadratic form is
\begin{align*}
 Q(v)&=h_i(v_j+v_k)^2+h_jv_j^2+h_kv_k^2\\
 &=(h_i+h_j)v_j^2+2h_iv_jv_k+(h_i+h_k)v_k^2.
\end{align*}
Its symmetric matrix and determinant are
\begin{align}
 M&=\begin{pmatrix}h_i+h_j&h_i\\h_i&h_i+h_k\end{pmatrix},\notag\\
 \det M&=(h_i+h_j)(h_i+h_k)-h_i^2\notag\\
 &=h_ih_j+h_ih_k+h_jh_k\notag\\
 &=h_ih_jh_k\left(\frac1{h_i}+\frac1{h_j}+\frac1{h_k}\right)
 =h_ih_jh_k S_i'.
 \label{eq:appendix-hessian-determinant}
\end{align}
In direction $(1,-1)$ the value is $h_j+h_k>0$, so $M$ has at least
one positive eigenvalue. Its two eigenvalues are both positive exactly
when their product, $\det M$, is positive. Because $h_ih_jh_k<0$,
this is equivalent to $S_i'<0$.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:appendix-finite}
For any three means and positive weights, the stationary points of $F$
and of $f$ are finite, including tied barriers, right or collinear
configurations, and repeated means.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
After whitening, \citet[Theorem~1]{okuno2026} gives at most fifteen
critical points of $f$, including degenerate points and repeated or
collinear centers. Lemma~\ref{lem:appendix-extrema} transfers this
finiteness to $F$. Only finiteness, not the numerical bound fifteen,
is used below.
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:appendix-persistence}
Every mode is isolated and strict. Given a mixture with $k$ modes,
every sufficiently small perturbation of its means and positive weights
has at least $k$ modes.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
If a local maximum were not strict, there would be equal-valued points
arbitrarily close to it inside a neighborhood on which its value is
maximal. Each such point would itself be a local maximum and hence a
stationary point. This contradicts Lemma~\ref{lem:appendix-finite}.

Choose disjoint small closed balls about the finitely many modes. On
each boundary, the density is strictly smaller than at the corresponding
center. Compactness makes each gap positive. The mixture density depends
continuously, uniformly on the union of these balls, on the means and
weights. All boundary gaps therefore persist under sufficiently small
perturbations. On each ball the perturbed density attains its maximum
in the interior, giving at least one mode in each ball.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{counter:prop:counterexample}
There is an acute-triangle Gaussian mixture whose reduced free energy has
two distinct nondegenerate strict minima with coordinate 1 upper and
coordinates 2 and 3 lower.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
\textbf{Preserve two known minima.}
Put $\theta=\log2$ and take the equilateral potential from
Appendix~\ref{app:example}, up to an additive constant:
\begin{equation}\label{counter:eq:base}
 F_0(p)=\sum_i p_i\log p_i-2\theta\sum_i p_i^2,\qquad
 p^{(0)}=\left(\frac13,\frac13,\frac13\right),\quad
 p^{(1)}=\left(\frac23,\frac16,\frac16\right).
\end{equation}
Both displayed points are stationary. For a tangent vector with
$v_1+v_2+v_3=0$, their Hessian forms are
\begin{align*}
 D^2F_0(p^{(0)})[v,v]&=(3-4\theta)\sum_i v_i^2,\\
 D^2F_0(p^{(1)})[v,v]&=\left(\frac92-6\theta\right)v_1^2
                         +(3-2\theta)(v_2-v_3)^2.
\end{align*}
The bounds $2/3<\theta<3/4$, proved in Appendix~\ref{app:example}, make
both forms positive definite. Now set
\begin{equation}\label{counter:eq:penalty}
 \widetilde F(p)=F_0(p)+\frac{\theta}{2}(p_2-p_3)^2.
\end{equation}
At both points $p_2=p_3$, so the added term and its gradient vanish.
Its Hessian form is $\theta(v_2-v_3)^2\geq0$. Thus both points remain
nondegenerate strict minima of $\widetilde F$.

\textbf{Check the branch labels.}
On the simplex, $p_2+p_3=1-p_1$, and therefore
\begin{align*}
 (p_2-p_3)^2
 &=2p_2^2+2p_3^2-(1-p_1)^2\\
 &=-p_1^2+2p_2^2+2p_3^2+2p_1-1.
\end{align*}
Hence $\widetilde F$ has the generic separable form
\[
 \widetilde F(p)=\sum_i\left(
 p_i\log p_i-\frac{a_i}{2}p_i^2-b_ip_i\right)-\frac\theta2,
\]
with
\begin{equation}\label{counter:eq:coefficients}
 (a_1,a_2,a_3)=(5\theta,2\theta,2\theta),\qquad
 (b_1,b_2,b_3)=(-\theta,0,0).
\end{equation}
Write $\mathbf h(p):=(h_1(p),h_2(p),h_3(p))$. The two branch-sign
vectors are
\[
 \mathbf h(p^{(0)})=(3-5\theta,\,3-2\theta,\,3-2\theta),\qquad
 \mathbf h(p^{(1)})=(\tfrac32-5\theta,\,6-2\theta,\,6-2\theta).
\]
Again $2/3<\theta<3/4$ shows that both have signs $(-,+,+)$.
Thus the two minima lie on the same upper branch.

\textbf{Realize the potential by a Gaussian mixture.}
Take covariance $I_2$ and
\begin{equation}\label{counter:eq:mixture}
 \mu_1=(\sqrt{6\theta},0),\quad
 \mu_2=(0,\sqrt\theta),\quad
 \mu_3=(0,-\sqrt\theta),\qquad
 (w_1,w_2,w_3)=\frac{(\sqrt2,1,1)}{2+\sqrt2}.
\end{equation}
The squared distances are $d_{12}^2=d_{13}^2=7\theta$ and $d_{23}^2=4\theta$,
which give the coefficients $a_i$ in \eqref{counter:eq:coefficients}.
All are positive, so the triangle is acute. Moreover,
\[
 \log w_i-\frac{a_i}{2}
 =b_i-\theta-\log(2+\sqrt2),\qquad i=1,2,3.
\]
The mixture potential therefore differs from $\widetilde F$ only by a
constant on the simplex. By Lemma~\ref{lem:appendix-extrema}, its two minima
correspond to distinct modes at
$(\sqrt{6\theta}/3,0)$ and $(2\sqrt{6\theta}/3,0)$.
