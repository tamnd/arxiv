\label{prop:BR-free}
For every $\pi\in\mathsf{Av}(1324)$, neither $w_{\mathrm p}(\pi)$ nor
$w_{\mathrm v}(\pi)$ contains the factor ${\color{blue!50!black}\mathsf{B}}{\color{red!50!black}\mathsf{R}}$.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Suppose first that $w_{\mathrm v}(\pi)$ contains a factor ${\color{blue!50!black}\mathsf{B}}{\color{red!50!black}\mathsf{R}}$,
corresponding to consecutive points $b<r$ in value order. Since $r$ is
red and lies above the blue point $b$, the staircase property forces $r$
to lie to the left of $b$. As $r$ is internal, there is a red point $a$
below and to the left of $r$; as $b$ is internal, there is a blue point
$d$ above and to the right of $b$. Since $b$ and $r$ are consecutive in
value order, we have $a<b<r<d$. Thus the four points occur from left to
right as
\[
 a,\ r,\ b,\ d,
\]
and form an occurrence of $1324$.

Now suppose that $w_{\mathrm p}(\pi)$ contains a factor ${\color{blue!50!black}\mathsf{B}}{\color{red!50!black}\mathsf{R}}$,
corresponding to consecutive positions occupied by a blue point $b$
followed by a red point $r$. Since a red point cannot lie northeast of a
blue point, we have $r<b$. Again choose a red point $a$ below and to the
left of $r$, and a blue point $d$ above and to the right of $b$. The
consecutiveness of $b$ and $r$ in position order gives the left-to-right
order
\[
 a,\ b,\ r,\ d,
\]
while $a<r<b<d$. These four points again form a $1324$, a contradiction.

$2^n$ dominoes of size $n$ in which both cells are formed of decreasing sequences of points (since there are no restrictions on the interleavings available).  Every point of such a
domino is a left-to-right minimum or right-to-left maximum, so each has weight~$1$.  Moreover, since
$0\leq i(\delta),r(\delta)\leq n$ for any domino $\delta$, we have
\[
 2^n\leq d_n(x,y)\leq M^n d_n(1,1),
 \qquad \text{where }M=\max\{1,x\}\max\{1,y\}.
\]
The ordinary domino sequence has finite exponential growth, and hence
\[
 0<\rho\leq\frac12.
\]
Since the coefficients are nonnegative, Pringsheim's theorem
\cite[Theorem~IV.6]{flajolet:analytic-combin:} implies that $z=\rho$ is a singularity.

The finite singularities of an algebraic function satisfying a
polynomial equation are contained among the zeros of the leading
coefficient and the discriminant of that polynomial; see
\cite[Section~VII.7]{flajolet:analytic-combin:}.  The leading coefficient of
$R_2(D,z;x,y)$ in~\eqref{eq:domino-cubic}, viewed as a polynomial in $D$,
is
\[
 x^4z^4\bigl(1+2(y-1)z\bigr).
\]
For $0<z\leq\tfrac12$ this is nonzero, since
\[
 1+2(y-1)z=(1-2z)+2yz>0.
\]
Thus $\rho$ is a zero of the discriminant.  By equation~\eqref{eq:domino-discriminant}, we conclude that $\Phi(\rho;x,y)=0$.

It remains to show that $\Phi(z;x,y)$ has exactly one zero in
$(0,\tfrac12]$.  The substitution $z=u/(1+2u)$ maps $(0,\infty)$
bijectively onto $(0,\tfrac12)$.  Put
\[
\begin{aligned}
 Q(u)&=(1+2u)^3\Phi\!\left(\frac{u}{1+2u};x,y\right)\\
 &=4x(x^2-9xy+27y^2)u^3
   +12x(6y-x)u^2+3(4x-3y)u-4.
\end{aligned}
\]
With a view to applying Descartes' rule of signs, we consider the signs of the coefficients.
The leading coefficient of $Q$ is positive, since
\[
x^2-9xy+27y^2
=
\left(x-\frac92y\right)^2+\frac{27}{4}y^2>0.
\]

Now if $x\leq6y$, the coefficient of $u^2$ is non-negative, and thus (regardless of the sign of the coefficient of $u$) the coefficients of $Q$ have exactly one sign change.  Thus, when $x\leq 6y$ we conclude that $Q$ has exactly one positive root.

If $x>6y$, the coefficient of $u^2$ is negative, and that of $u$ is positive. Thus there are one or three positive roots in this case. We have
\[
 \mathrm{disc}_u Q
 =-314928\,xy^2(x-y)^3<0,
\]
so $Q$ has exactly one real root.  Since $Q(0)=-4$ and its leading
coefficient is positive, this root is positive.

Hence in either case $Q$ has exactly one positive root, and thus $\Phi(z;x,y)$ has exactly one zero in $(0,\tfrac12)$.  Furthermore,
\[
 \Phi\!\left(\frac12;x,y\right)
 =\frac{x}{2}(x^2-9xy+27y^2)>0,
\]
so $\tfrac12$ is not a zero.

It follows that $\rho$ is the unique zero of $\Phi(z;x,y)$ in
$(0,\tfrac12)$, and therefore its smallest positive zero.  Finally,
the Exponential Growth Formula \cite[Theorem~IV.7]{flajolet:analytic-combin:} gives
\[
 \mu(x,y)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}d_n(x,y)^{1/n}=\rho^{-1},
\]
as required.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:unweighted-certificate-bound}
Suppose that $\kappa$ is $\lambda$-good for a shuffle
automaton $\mathcal A$ with finite state set $Q$.  Then, for every reachable ID $(q;u',\ell')$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:unweighted-word-bound}
 s_q(u',\ell')
 \leq
 \frac{\kappa_+}{\kappa_-}
 \lambda^{|u'|+|\ell'|},
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_-=\min_{q\in Q}\kappa(q)$ and $\kappa_+=\max_{q\in Q}\kappa(q)$.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
We claim, by induction on $|u'|+|\ell'|$, that the following inequality holds at any reachable ID $(q;u',\ell')$.
\[
 s_q(u',\ell')
 \leq
 \frac{\kappa(q)}{\kappa_-}
 \lambda^{|u'|+|\ell'|}.
\]

If $u'=\ell'=\epsilon$, then $s_q(\epsilon,\epsilon)=1$, and $1\leq\frac{\kappa(q)}{\kappa_-}$.

Otherwise, by~\eqref{eq:unweighted-shuffle-recurrence},~\eqref{eq:unweighted-certificate} and the induction
hypothesis,
\begin{align*}
 s_q(u',\ell') &=  \sum_{e\in E(q;u',\ell')}
 s_{q_e}(u_e',\ell_e')\\
 &\leq
 \sum_{e\in E(q;u',\ell')} \frac{\lambda^{|u'|+|\ell'|-1}}{\kappa_-}
\kappa(q_e)\\
 &\leq
 \frac{\kappa(q)}{\kappa_-}
 \lambda^{|u'|+|\ell'|}.
\end{align*}
The statement in the proposition now follows since $\kappa(q)\leq\kappa_+$.

<!-- proposition-7: body_md -->
\label{prop:weighted-certificate-bound}
Suppose that $\kappa$ is $\lambda$-good for a shuffle automaton $\mathcal{A}$ with finite state set $Q$ and transition weighting $\tau$.  Then, for every reachable ID $(q;u',\ell')$,
\[
 F_q(u',\ell')
 \leq
 \frac{\kappa_+}{\kappa_-}
 \lambda^{|u'|+|\ell'|},
\]
where $\kappa_-=\min_{q\in Q}\kappa(q)$ and $\kappa_+=\max_{q\in Q}\kappa(q)$.
<!-- end proposition-7 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
The proof is the same induction as in
Proposition~\ref{prop:unweighted-certificate-bound}.  If both input
tapes are exhausted, then $F_q(\epsilon,\epsilon)=1$, which is at most
$\kappa(q)/\kappa_-$.  Otherwise,
\eqref{eq:weighted-shuffle-recurrence}, \eqref{eq:weighted-good} and the induction hypothesis give

 &\leq

 &\leq
 \frac{\kappa(q)}{\kappa_-}
 \lambda^{|u'|+|\ell'|}.
\end{align*}
The result follows since $\kappa(q)\leq\kappa_+$.

<!-- proposition-10: body_md -->
\label{prop:three-step-certificate}
For the parameters in~\eqref{eq:three-step-parameters}, the state
weighting $\kappa$ given in
Appendix~\ref{app:three-step-state-weighting} is $(1+t)$-good for
the three-step shuffle automaton.
<!-- end proposition-10 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
For each state $q$, let $K_q(T)\in\mathbb Q[T]$ be the
polynomial of degree at most four specified in
Appendix~\ref{app:three-step-state-weighting}, and define
\[
 \kappa(q)=K_q(t).
\]
For a state $q$ and a pair $(u_4,\ell_4)$ of newly-revealed
letters, form the polynomial
\[
 S_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(T) = (1+T)K_q(T) - \sum_e T^{d(e)}K_{q_e}(T),
\]
where $d(e)\in\{0,1,2\}$ is such that the corresponding transition weight is $1,t,t^2$, respectively. 
The evaluation of $S_{q,u_4,\ell_4}$ at $t$ is the \emph{slack} obtained by rearranging the inequality~\eqref{eq:weighted-good} for any reachable ID at state $q$ whose newly-revealed letters are $u_4$ and $\ell_4$. We must show that $S_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(t)\geq 0$.

Let $R_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(T)$ be the remainder in $\mathbb{Q}[T]$ when $S_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(T)$ is divided by $m(T)$. Thus $R_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(T)$ has degree at most 4. Since $m(t)=0$,
\[
 S_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(t)
 =
 R_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(t),
\]
and so we must verify that all 1536 numbers $R_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(t)$ are non-negative. To avoid issues using the irrational number $t$, we consider the values of the remainders across a small interval containing $t$ whose endpoints are rational. Put
$a=1158423/10^6$ and $b=1158424/10^6$. For each nonzero remainder
$R_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(T)=\sum_{j=0}^4r_jT^j$, we have
\[
 R_{q,u_4,\ell_4}(t)\geq
 \sum_{r_j\geq0}r_j a^j+
 \sum_{r_j<0}r_j b^j,
\]
and so it suffices to show that
$ \sum_{r_j\geq0}r_j a^j+
 \sum_{r_j<0}r_j b^j \geq 0$.

All of the 1536 remainders are nonnegative. (In fact, 514 are the zero polynomial, and 1022 are strictly positive, but we do not require this information for the proof.) 
When one of the input tapes no longer has any symbols, the corresponding transition from the ID is omitted, and the inequalities remain valid. Hence the weighting $\kappa$ is $\lambda$-good, when $\lambda=1+t$.

To show that the weighting $\kappa$ is itself strictly positive on each state, we use the same approach as taken for evaluating the slacks. Note that since the state weightings use only 139 polynomials, it suffices to verify that each of these polynomial is positive at $T=t$. This approach gives a strictly positive lower bound in every case. Hence \(\kappa(q)>0\) for all \(q\).
