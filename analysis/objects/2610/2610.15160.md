<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
Let $H_{1}$ and $H_{2}$ be infinite-dimensional closed subspaces of
a Hilbert space $H$. Then there exist infinite-dimensional closed
subspaces
\[
L\subseteq H_{1},
\qquad
F\subseteq H_{2},
\]
such that
\[
L\perp F.
\]
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We construct orthonormal sequences $(e_n)$ in $H_{1}$ and $(f_n)$
in $H_{2}$ inductively.

Choose a unit vector $e_{1}\in H_{1}$. Since $H_{2}$ is
infinite-dimensional, we can choose a unit vector
\[
f_{1}\in H_{2}\cap e_{1}^{\perp}.
\]

Suppose that orthonormal vectors
\[
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
\quad\text{and}\quad
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
\]
have been chosen so that
\[
\langle e_i,f_j\rangle=0
\qquad (1\leq i,j\leq n).
\]
Since the span of $e_{1},\ldots,e_{n},f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}$ is finite-dimensional and
$H_{1}$ is infinite-dimensional, we may choose a unit vector
\[
e_{n+1}\in H_{1}\cap
\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n},f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}^{\perp}.
\]
Similarly, since the span of $e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1},f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}$ is
finite-dimensional and $H_{2}$ is infinite-dimensional, we may
choose a unit vector
\[

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
Let $H_{0}$ be an infinite-dimensional closed subspace of a Hilbert
space $H$ such that $H_{0}^{\perp}$ is also infinite-dimensional.
Let $p_{H_{0}}$ denote the orthogonal projection onto $H_{0}$. Then
the image of $p_{H_{0}}$ in the Calkin algebra

restriction to the unit ball of $H_{0}$ would have relatively
compact image, whereas that restriction is the identity operator on
the infinite-dimensional space $H_{0}$. Hence the image
\[
\pi(p_{H_{0}})

of $p_{H_{0}}$ in $B(H)/K(H)$ is nonzero and positive.

\]

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:lift}
Let $A$ be a $C^*$-algebra, let $J$ be a closed two-sided ideal of $A$,
let $\pi:A\to A/J$ be the quotient map, and let $e,f\in(A/J)_+$ satisfy
$ef=0$. Let $x,y\in A_+$ with $\pi(x)=e$ and $\pi(y)=f$, and put
\[
z=(x-y)_+,\qquad w=(x-y)_-.
\]
Then $z,w\in A_+$, $zw=0$, $\pi(z)=e$, $\pi(w)=f$, $\|z\|\leq\|x\|$ and
$\|w\|\leq\|y\|$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
That $z,w\geq0$ and $zw=0$ is standard. By the compatibility of the
continuous functional calculus with $*$-homomorphisms,
$\pi(z)=(\pi(x-y))_+=(e-f)_+$ and $\pi(w)=(e-f)_-$. Since $e,f\geq0$
commute and $ef=0$, every character $\chi$ of the commutative
$C^*$-algebra $C^*(e,f)$ satisfies $\chi(e)\chi(f)=0$, so
$(\chi(e)-\chi(f))_+=\chi(e)$ and $(\chi(e)-\chi(f))_-=\chi(f)$. Hence
$(e-f)_+=e$ and $(e-f)_-=f$. Finally, $x-y\leq x$ gives
$\sigma(x-y)\subseteq(-\infty,\|x\|]$, hence $\|z\|\leq\|x\|$, and
$y-x\leq y$ gives $\|w\|=\|(y-x)_+\|\leq\|y\|$.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:diam}
Let $H$ be an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. Then
\[
\Gamma_{+}(B(H))
\quad\text{and}\quad
\Gamma_{+}\bigl(B(H)/K(H)\bigr)
\]
are connected graphs and
\[
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(B(H))=4,
\qquad
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}\bigl(B(H)/K(H)\bigr)=3.
\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Put
\[
A=B(H)/K(H)
\]
and let
\[
\pi:B(H)\longrightarrow A
\]
be the quotient map.

Let $S,T\in Z_{+}(A)$ be arbitrary. Since $S$ and $T$ are positive
zero divisors, there exist nonzero positive elements
\[
S_{1},T_{1}\in A
\]
such that
\[
SS_{1}=0,
\qquad
TT_{1}=0.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:lift} (applied to positive lifts of $S,S_1$ and of $T,T_1$), there exist nonzero positive operators
\[
\widetilde S,\widetilde S_{1},
\widetilde T,\widetilde T_{1}\in B(H)
\]
lifting respectively $S,S_{1},T,T_{1}$ and satisfying
\[
\widetilde S\widetilde S_{1}=0,
\qquad
\widetilde T\widetilde T_{1}=0.
\]
None of these four operators is compact, since their images in the
Calkin algebra are nonzero.

It follows that
\[
\operatorname{ran}\widetilde S_{1}
\subseteq
\ker\widetilde S,
\qquad
\operatorname{ran}\widetilde T_{1}
\subseteq
\ker\widetilde T.
\]
A noncompact operator cannot have finite-dimensional range. Hence
\[
\operatorname{ran}\widetilde S_{1}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\operatorname{ran}\widetilde T_{1}
\]
are infinite-dimensional, and therefore
\[
\ker\widetilde S
\quad\text{and}\quad
\ker\widetilde T
\]
are infinite-dimensional.

By the first lemma, there exist infinite-dimensional closed subspaces
\[
L\subseteq\ker\widetilde S,
\qquad
F\subseteq\ker\widetilde T
\]
such that
\[
L\perp F.
\]
Let $p_L$ and $p_F$ denote the orthogonal projections onto $L$ and
$F$, respectively. Since $L$ and $F$ are infinite-dimensional, both
projections are noncompact. Moreover,
\[
\widetilde S p_L=0,
\qquad
p_Lp_F=0,
\qquad
p_F\widetilde T=0.
\]

Let
\[
\pi_L=\pi(p_L),
\qquad
\pi_F=\pi(p_F)
\]
denote their images in the Calkin algebra.

Because $F\subseteq L^\perp$ and $L\subseteq F^\perp$, both
$L^\perp$ and $F^\perp$ are infinite-dimensional. Hence, by the
second lemma, $\pi_L$ and $\pi_F$ are nonzero positive zero divisors
in $A$. Furthermore,
\[
S\pi_L=0,
\qquad
\pi_L\pi_F=0,
\qquad
\pi_FT=0.
\]
Thus
\[
S,\ \pi_L,\ \pi_F,\ T
\]
gives a walk of length $3$ from $S$ to $T$ (consecutive entries are distinct, because a positive element whose square vanishes is zero). Hence
\[
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(A)\leq3.
\]

We now show that the diameter is strictly greater than $2$.

Choose an orthogonal decomposition
\[
H=E\oplus F\oplus G,
\]
where $E,F,G$ are infinite-dimensional closed subspaces. Define
\[
H_{0}=E\oplus F,
\qquad
H_{1}=E\oplus G,
\qquad
H_{2}=G.
\]
Each of these subspaces is infinite-dimensional and has
infinite-dimensional orthogonal complement.

Let $p_0,p_1,p_2$ denote the corresponding orthogonal projections.
By the second lemma, the images
\[
S=\pi(p_0),
\qquad
T=\pi(p_1)
\]
are nonzero positive zero divisors in the Calkin algebra.

We have
\[
H_{0}\cap H_{1}=E,
\qquad
H_{0}+H_{1}=H,
\qquad
H_{2}\subseteq H_{1}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
1=p_0+p_2
\]
and
\[
p_1p_2=p_2p_1=p_2.
\]

Suppose that $G_0\in B(H)$ satisfies
\[
G_0p_0\in K(H)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
G_0p_1\in K(H).
\]
Then
\[
G_0p_2
=
G_0p_1p_2
\]
is compact, since $K(H)$ is a two-sided ideal in $B(H)$. Hence
\[
G_0
=
G_0(p_0+p_2)
=
G_0p_0+G_0p_2
\]
is compact.

On the other hand,
\[
p_0p_1
\]
is the orthogonal projection onto the infinite-dimensional space
\[
H_{0}\cap H_{1}=E,
\]
and is therefore not compact. Hence
\[
ST=\pi(p_0p_1)\neq0.
\]

Now suppose that there were a nonzero positive element $G_0\in A$
satisfying
\[
G_0S=G_0T=0.
\]
Choose a positive lift $\widetilde G_0\in B(H)$ of $G_0$. Then
\[
\widetilde G_0p_0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\widetilde G_0p_1
\]
are compact, since their images in the Calkin algebra are
respectively $G_0S=0$ and $G_0T=0$. By the preceding argument,
$\widetilde G_0$ is compact. Thus $G_0=0$, a contradiction.

Therefore $S$ and $T$ have no common nonzero positive neighbor.
Since $ST\neq0$, they are not adjacent, and since they have no
common neighbor, they cannot be joined by a path of length $2$.
Hence
\[
d(S,T)>2.
\]
Together with the upper bound, this gives
\[
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(A)=3.
\]

It remains to prove the assertion for $B(H)$. In this part $H$ may be
any Hilbert space with $\dim H\geq3$, finite or infinite; we shall use
this in the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:factors}. Note that a positive
operator $S\in B(H)$ is a zero divisor if and only if
$\ker S\neq\{0\}$. Indeed, if $0\neq\xi\in\ker S$, the rank-one
projection $p_\xi$ onto $\mathbb C\xi$ satisfies $Sp_\xi=0$;
conversely, if $0\neq R\in B(H)$ satisfies $SR=0$, then
$\operatorname{ran}R\subseteq\ker S$. Moreover, $Sp=0$ for every
projection $p$ onto a subspace of $\ker S$.

Let $S,T\in Z_{+}(B(H))$ and put
\[
M=\ker S,\qquad N=\ker T,
\]
both nonzero closed subspaces.

\emph{Step 1.} If $M\cap N\neq\{0\}$, let $p$ be the projection onto
$M\cap N$. Then $Sp=pT=0$, so $S,p,T$ is a path of length at most $2$.

\emph{Step 2.} Suppose there are unit vectors $e\in M$, $f\in N$ with
$e\perp f$, and let $p_e$, $p_f$ be the rank-one projections onto
$\mathbb Ce$, $\mathbb Cf$. Then
\[
Sp_e=0,\qquad p_ep_f=0,\qquad p_fT=0,
\]
so $S,p_e,p_f,T$ is a path of length at most $3$.

\emph{Step 3.} Suppose that neither Step~1 nor Step~2 applies. If
$\dim M\geq2$, pick a unit vector $f\in N$; then $M\cap f^\perp$ has
codimension at most $1$ in $M$, hence contains a unit vector $e$, and
Step~2 applies. The same holds if $\dim N\geq2$. Hence
$M=\mathbb Ce$ and $N=\mathbb Cf$ are one-dimensional, with $e,f$ unit
vectors which are neither orthogonal nor proportional. Let $q$ be the
projection onto $\{e,f\}^\perp$, which is nonzero because
$\dim H\geq3$. Then
\[
Sp_e=0,\qquad p_eq=0,\qquad qp_f=0,\qquad p_fT=0,
\]
so $S,p_e,q,p_f,T$ is a path of length $4$.

Thus $\Gamma_{+}(B(H))$ is connected and
$\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(B(H))\leq4$.

To see that the diameter is at least $4$, choose two linearly
independent, non-orthogonal unit vectors $e,f\in H$, let $p_e,p_f$ be the
rank-one projections onto $\mathbb Ce,\mathbb Cf$, and put
\[
S=1-p_e,\qquad T=1-p_f.
\]
Then $S,T\in Z_{+}(B(H))$, since $\ker S=\mathbb Ce$ and
$\ker T=\mathbb Cf$. If $G\geq0$ and $GS=0$, then $G$ vanishes on
$\operatorname{ran}S=e^\perp$, so $G=Gp_e=p_eG$ and hence $G=\lambda p_e$
with $\lambda>0$ (for $G\neq0$). Thus the neighbours of $S$ are exactly the operators
$\lambda p_e$ ($\lambda>0$), and likewise the neighbours of $T$ are exactly
the operators $\mu p_f$ ($\mu>0$). Now:
\begin{itemize}
\item $S$ and $T$ are not adjacent: $ST=0$ would give
$f^\perp=\operatorname{ran}T\subseteq\ker S=\mathbb Ce$, which is
impossible because $\dim f^\perp\geq2$;
\item they have no common neighbour, since $\lambda p_e\neq\mu p_f$;
\item there is no path $S,x,y,T$ of length $3$: it would need
$x=\lambda p_e$, $y=\mu p_f$ and $xy=0$, i.e.\ $p_ep_f=0$, i.e.\
$e\perp f$, which was excluded.
\end{itemize}
Hence $d(S,T)\geq4$. Together with the upper bound, this gives
$\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(B(H))=4$.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:factors}
Let $A$ be a von Neumann factor. If $A\not\simeq M_{2}(\mathbb C)$
and $A\neq\mathbb C$, then $\Gamma_{+}(A)$ is connected and
\[
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(A)\leq4.
\]
If, in addition, $A$ is not of type~I (equivalently, $A$ has no minimal
projection), then $\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(A)\leq3$.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
A type~I factor is $*$-isomorphic to $B(K)$ for some Hilbert space $K$,
and $\dim K\geq3$ because $A\neq\mathbb C$ and $A\not\simeq M_2(\mathbb C)$.
Since $\Gamma_{+}$ is invariant under $*$-isomorphisms, the part of the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:diam} concerning $B(H)$, which is valid for every
Hilbert space of dimension at least $3$, shows that
$\Gamma_{+}(A)$ is connected with
$\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(A)=4$.

It remains to consider a factor $A$ which is not of type~I. Then $A$ has no
minimal projections, and we regard $A$ as a von Neumann algebra acting on a
Hilbert space $H$. We use the standard fact that, for every $R\in A$, the
orthogonal projections onto $\ker R$ and onto
$\overline{\operatorname{ran}R}$ belong to $A$; see \cite[p.~19]{OLSEN}.
Recall also that the projections of $A$ form a complete lattice, so that
meets and joins of projections of $A$ are again in $A$.

Let $S,T\in Z_{+}(A)$ and let $p,q\in A$ be the projections onto $\ker S$
and $\ker T$. Then $p,q\neq0$ (since $S,T$ are zero divisors) and
$p,q\neq1$ (since $S,T\neq0$). Every projection $e\leq p$ satisfies $Se=0$,
and every projection $e\leq q$ satisfies $Te=0$. We show that
$d(S,T)\leq3$.

\emph{Case A: $p\wedge(1-q)\neq0$.} Put $e=p\wedge(1-q)$. Then
$Se=0$, $eq=0$ and $qT=0$, so $S,e,q,T$ is a path of length at most $3$.

\emph{Case B: $p\wedge(1-q)=0$.} Put $a=pqp$, a positive element of the von
Neumann algebra $pAp$ (with unit $p$). We claim that there are nonzero
orthogonal projections $e,e'\in pAp$ which commute with $a$. If
$\sigma(a)$, computed in $pAp$, contains at least two points, choose
disjoint open sets $U_1,U_2\subset\mathbb R$ each meeting $\sigma(a)$ and
let $e=\chi_{U_1}(a)$, $e'=\chi_{U_2}(a)$; these are nonzero, orthogonal, and
commute with $a$. If $\sigma(a)=\{\lambda\}$, then $a=\lambda p$ commutes
with everything in $pAp$, and, since $A$ has no minimal projection, we can
write $p=e+e'$ with $e,e'$ nonzero orthogonal projections.

Now $e,e'\leq p$, so $e=ep=pe$ and $e'=pe'=e'p$, whence
\[
eqe'=e\,pqp\,e'=eae'=a\,ee'=0.
\]
Let $f\in A$ be the projection onto $\overline{\operatorname{ran}(qe')}$.
Then $f\leq q$, so $Tf=0$, and $eqe'=0$ means that
$\operatorname{ran}(qe')\subseteq\ker e$, i.e.\ $fe=0$. Moreover
$f\neq0$: if $qe'=0$, then $\operatorname{ran}e'\subseteq\ker q$, so
$e'\leq p\wedge(1-q)=0$, a contradiction. Since $e\leq p$ gives $Se=0$,
we obtain the path $S,e,f,T$ of length at most $3$.

Thus every two vertices of $\Gamma_{+}(A)$ can be joined by a path of
length at most $3$, so $\Gamma_{+}(A)$ is connected and
$\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{+}(A)\leq3$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
The graph $\Gamma_{+}(M_{2}(\mathbb C))$ is disconnected.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Every positive matrix is unitarily equivalent to a diagonal matrix
with nonnegative diagonal entries. A positive zero divisor in
$M_{2}(\mathbb C)$ is therefore of the form
\[
a=\lambda p,
\]
where $\lambda>0$ and $p$ is a rank-one projection.

It is consequently enough to consider the graph of rank-one
projections. A rank-one projection $p$ is the orthogonal projection
onto a one-dimensional subspace $L_p$ of $\mathbb C^2$. For rank-one
projections $p$ and $q$,
\[
pq=0
\]
if and only if
\[
L_p\perp L_q.
\]

In $\mathbb C^2$, if $L_p$ and $L_q$ are distinct and orthogonal,
then $L_q=L_p^\perp$. Hence the connected component containing
$\lambda p$, for $\lambda>0$, consists precisely of the vertices
\[
\{\lambda p:\lambda>0\}
\cup
\{\mu(1-p):\mu>0\}.
\]
Since there are infinitely many one-dimensional subspaces of
$\mathbb C^2$, there are infinitely many such components. Thus
\[
\Gamma_{+}(M_{2}(\mathbb C))
\]
is disconnected.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The preceding results illustrate a distinction between the positive
zero-divisor graph of a $C^*$-algebra and the usual zero-divisor graph
of a commutative ring. Recall that, for a commutative ring $R$, the
zero-divisor graph $\Gamma(R)$ is connected whenever it is nonempty,
and

$$
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma(R)\leq 3;
$$

moreover, this bound is sharp \cite{AndersonLivingston1999}. In contrast, $\Gamma_+(B(H))$ has diameter $4$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:diam}), so the bound $3$ of the commutative ring case fails for the positive zero-divisor graph.

In the present setting, the vertices are restricted to positive
elements. Thus, although the adjacency relation is again given by
orthogonality,

$$
a\sim b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad ab=0,
$$

the positivity requirement may exclude intermediate vertices that are
available in the classical zero-divisor graph. On the other hand, in
the Calkin algebra and in $B(H)$, the existence of sufficiently large
orthogonal subspaces allows one to connect arbitrary positive
zero-divisors by short paths. For factors, the presence of projections
in the von Neumann algebra provides a corresponding mechanism.

This comparison naturally raises the following questions: For which
$C^*$-algebras $A$ is $\Gamma_+(A)$ connected? More generally, is
there a universal constant $N$ such that

$$
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_+(A)\leq N
$$

for every $C^*$-algebra $A$ for which $\Gamma_+(A)$ is connected?
These questions indicate how the order structure of a $C^*$-algebra
can affect the geometry of its positive zero-divisor graph.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:sep}
If $X$ is a separable compact Hausdorff space, then $ZDRR(C(X))=0$.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let $f\in C(X)$ be real valued and let $\varepsilon>0$. For $y\in\mathbb R$
let $U_y$ be the interior of $f^{-1}(y)$. The sets $U_y$ are pairwise
disjoint and open. If $\{d_n\}$ is a countable dense subset of $X$, every
nonempty $U_y$ contains some $d_n$, so $U_y\neq\varnothing$ for at most
countably many $y$. Choose $t\in(0,\varepsilon)$ with
$U_t=\varnothing$ and put $b=f-t\cdot1$. Then $\|f-b\|=t<\varepsilon$ and the
zero set of $b^2$ is $f^{-1}(t)$, which has empty interior. Hence $b^2$
is a non-zero-divisor, and $ZDRR(C(X))\le0$. (This is the argument of
\cite{Taghavi2013}.)

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:almostP}
Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff almost-$P$-space. Then
\[
ZDRR(C(X))=RR(C(X))=\dim X.
\]
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
For $f\in C(X)_+$, multiplication by $f$ is injective on $C(X)$ if
and only if $Z(f)=f^{-1}(\{0\})$ has empty interior. Indeed, if
$Z(f)$ has nonempty interior, a nonzero continuous function supported
in that interior is annihilated by $f$; conversely, if $fg=0$ for a
nonzero $g$, then the open set $\{x:|g(x)|>0\}$ is contained in
$Z(f)$, so $Z(f)$ has nonempty interior.

Now let $f\in C(X)_+$ be a non-zero-divisor. Its zero set $Z(f)$ is a
closed $G_\delta$ subset of $X$. If $Z(f)$ were nonempty, the
almost-$P$ property would imply that $Z(f)$ has nonempty interior,
contrary to the fact that $f$ is a non-zero-divisor. Hence $Z(f)=\varnothing$,
so $f$ is invertible. The converse is immediate. Thus, on an
almost-$P$-space, a positive element of $C(X)$ is a non-zero-divisor
if and only if it is invertible.

Consequently, in the definition of $ZDRR(C(X))$ the admissible
approximating tuples are exactly the admissible tuples in the
definition of $RR(C(X))$. Therefore
\[
ZDRR(C(X))=RR(C(X)).
\]
Finally, the classical formula for compact Hausdorff spaces gives
$RR(C(X))=\dim X$, and the result follows.

\label{lem:prod}

Let $G=\bigcap_{k\ge1}U_k$ be a nonempty $G_\delta$ subset of

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:interval}
Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space. Then
\[
ZDRR\bigl(C(X\times[0,1])\bigr)=0 .
\]
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Put $Y=X\times[0,1]$. Let $f\in C(Y)$ be real valued and let
$\varepsilon>0$. For $x\in X$ write $f_x=f(x,\cdot)\in C[0,1]$. Since $Y$
is compact, $x\mapsto f_x$ is continuous from $X$ to $C[0,1]$ with the
supremum norm. For a closed interval $I=[p,q]\subseteq[0,1]$ with
rational endpoints $p<q$, the set
\[
K_I=\{f_x|_I:\ x\in X\}\subseteq C(I)
\]
is therefore compact. Since $C(I)$ is infinite dimensional, $K_I$ has
empty interior. The restriction map $R_I:C[0,1]\to C(I)$ is a bounded
linear surjection, hence open by the open mapping theorem, so the closed
set
\[
B_I=\{v\in C[0,1]:\ R_I v\in -K_I\}
\]
has empty interior (a nonempty open subset of $B_I$ would be mapped by
$R_I$ onto a nonempty open subset of $-K_I$). There are countably many
rational intervals $I$, so by the Baire category theorem there is
$v\in C[0,1]$ with $\|v\|<\varepsilon$ and $v\notin\bigcup_I B_I$. Define
$b(x,t)=f(x,t)+v(t)$. Then $b$ is real valued and
$\|f-b\|=\|v\|<\varepsilon$.

We claim that the zero set of $b$ has empty interior. Otherwise it
contains a set $V\times J$ with $V\subseteq X$ open and nonempty and
$J\subseteq[0,1]$ a nonempty open interval. Choose a rational interval
$I\subseteq J$ and a point $x\in V$. Then $f_x(t)+v(t)=0$ for all
$t\in I$, that is $R_Iv=-f_x|_I\in -K_I$, so $v\in B_I$, a
contradiction. Hence $b^2$ has a zero set with empty interior, and $b^2$
is a non-zero-divisor in $C(Y)$. Therefore $ZDRR(C(Y))\le 0$.
<!-- end proof-10 -->
<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:gap}
For every positive integer $n$ there is a compact Hausdorff space $Y_n$
with
\[
ZDRR\bigl(C(Y_n)\bigr)=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
RR\bigl(C(Y_n)\bigr)\ge n .
\]
In particular one may take $Y_n=X^n\times[0,1]$, where $X$ is the space
of Section~3.1.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
By Theorem~\ref{thm:interval}, $ZDRR(C(Y_n))=0$. The set
$X^n\times\{0\}$ is closed in $Y_n$ and homeomorphic to $X^n$, and the
covering dimension does not increase on passing to closed subspaces of a
normal space. Hence $RR(C(Y_n))=\dim Y_n\ge\dim X^n=n$.
<!-- end proof-11 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
The unital subalgebra $C(X^n)\subseteq C(X^n\times[0,1])$ has
$ZDRR=n$, while the larger algebra has $ZDRR=0$. Thus $ZDRR$ can drop
when one tensors with $C[0,1]$, whereas $RR(C(Y\times[0,1]))=\dim(Y\times[0,1])$
is never smaller than $RR(C(Y))=\dim Y$.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- question-2: body_md -->
Is $ZDRR(A\otimes C[0,1])=0$ for every unital $C^*$-algebra $A$? Is there
a simple unital noncommutative $C^*$-algebra $A$ with $ZDRR(A)>0$?
<!-- end question-2 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
Since $ZDRR(A)\le RR(A)$, we have $ZDRR(A)=0$ whenever $RR(A)=0$; in
particular $ZDRR$ vanishes for AF algebras, UHF algebras, Cuntz algebras
and von Neumann algebras. A noncommutative example with $ZDRR(A)>0$
therefore requires $RR(A)\ge1$.
