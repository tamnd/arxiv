<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:independent}
An ordered tuple $(F_1,\ldots,F_k)$ of finite subsets of $\mathbb Z^d$ is \emph{independent} if $0\in F_i$ for every $i$, and every choice
\[
 v_i\in F_i\setminus\{0\}\qquad(1\leq i\leq k)
\]
is linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $d\geq2$, and let $(F_1,\ldots,F_{d-1})$ be an independent tuple of nonempty finite subsets of $\mathbb Z^d$. If the tiles have a joint co-tile, then they have a fully periodic joint co-tile.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:periodic}
For $0\leq j\leq d$, a function on $\mathbb Z^d$ is \emph{$j$-periodic} if it has a period subgroup of rank $j$. This does not require its full period group to have rank exactly $j$. In particular, \emph{codimension-one periodic} means invariant under some subgroup of rank $d-1$; fully periodic functions, including constants, also satisfy this condition. A set is \emph{$j$-periodic} if its indicator is $j$-periodic. It is \emph{piecewise $(d-1)$-periodic} if it is a finite disjoint union of $(d-1)$-periodic sets, whose period subgroups may differ from one piece to another.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:periodize}
Let $d\geq2$, and let $F_1,\ldots,F_k\subset\mathbb Z^d$ be nonempty finite sets with a joint co-tile $A$. Suppose that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomposition}
 \mathbf1_A=c-\sum_{i=1}^r\varphi_i,
\end{equation}
where $c$ is a fully periodic real function and each $\varphi_i$ is a bounded real function invariant under a subgroup of rank $d-1$. Then the family has a piecewise $(d-1)$-periodic joint co-tile, and consequently a fully periodic joint co-tile.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:subfamily}
Let $d\geq2$, and let $F_1,\ldots,F_k\subset\mathbb Z^d$ be nonempty finite sets. Suppose some $d-1$ of these sets, normalized to contain $0$, form an independent tuple. If the entire family has a joint co-tile, then it has a fully periodic joint co-tile.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:companions}
Let $d\geq2$ and $\{0\}\subsetneqq F\subset\mathbb Z^d$ be finite. Then $F$ admits a fully periodic co-tile if and only if there are finite sets $G_1,\ldots,G_{d-2}$ containing $0$ and a set $A\subset\mathbb Z^d$ such that $(G_1,\ldots,G_{d-2},F)$ is independent and
\[
 F\oplus A=G_1\oplus A=\cdots=G_{d-2}\oplus A=\mathbb Z^d.
\]
For $d=2$ the list of companions is empty.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- corollary-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:decision}
There is an algorithm that, given $d\geq2$ and a finite family of nonempty finite subsets of $\mathbb Z^d$ containing an independent $(d-1)$-subfamily, decides whether the family has a joint co-tile. On a positive instance it produces a scalar period and a fundamental pattern for a periodic joint co-tile. On a negative instance it produces a finite unsatisfiable collection of the tiling equations. The dimension may be part of the input.
<!-- end corollary-3 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:MSSdecomp}
Let $1\leq k\leq d$, and let $(F_1,\ldots,F_k)$ be an independent tuple of nontrivial finite subsets of $\mathbb Z^d$. Every joint co-tile indicator is an integer constant plus a finite sum of bounded real functions, each invariant under a subgroup of rank $k$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:MSSreplacement}
Let $F_1,\ldots,F_k\subset\mathbb Z^d$ be nonempty finite sets. If they have a piecewise $(d-1)$-periodic joint co-tile, then they have a fully periodic joint co-tile.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:range}
Suppose a Boolean configuration $a$ has a decomposition~\eqref{eq:decomposition} with the periods~\eqref{eq:hyperplanes}. Without changing $a$ or the hyperplanes, one can arrange $0\leq\varphi_i\leq1$ for every $i$, after changing $c$ by a fully periodic real function and increasing $q_0$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Averaging the decomposition along $q_0H_i$ gives
\[
 \varphi_i=-\pi_{q_0H_i}a+c-
 \sum_{j\ne i}\pi_{q_0H_i}\varphi_j.
\]
Every cross term is fully periodic, since it is invariant under $q_0H_i+q_0H_j$. Hence $\varphi_i$ differs from
\[
 \psi_i=1-\pi_{q_0H_i}a\in[0,1]
\]
by a fully periodic function. Set $\rho_i=\varphi_i-\psi_i$. Then
$a=(c-\sum_i\rho_i)-\sum_i\psi_i$.
All corrections are computed from the original representation, so these replacements can be made simultaneously. A common increase of $q_0$ accommodates their periods.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:normal}
Let $d\geq2$, and suppose $a:\mathbb Z^d\to\{0,1\}$ has a decomposition
\[
 a=c-\sum_{i=1}^r\varphi_i
\]
with $c$ fully periodic and real-valued, and with each $\varphi_i$ bounded, real-valued, and codimension-one periodic. For every prescribed integer $p\geq1$, there are
\[
 b\in\overline{\operatorname{Orb}_{p\mathbb Z^d}(a)}
 \quad\text{and}\quad q\geq1,\qquad p\mid q,
\]
such that, for each coset $C=x+q\mathbb Z^d$, the function $b_C:\mathbb Z^d\to\{0,1\}$ defined by $b_C(y)=b(x+qy)$ satisfies one of the following alternatives:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item \emph{Codimension-one periodic restriction.} There is a subgroup $L_C\leq\mathbb Z^d$ of rank $d-1$ such that
\[
 b_C(y+h)=b_C(y)\qquad(y\in\mathbb Z^d,\ h\in L_C).
\]
The subgroup may depend on $C$, and the constant functions $0$ and $1$ are included in this alternative.
\item \emph{Exceptional affine restriction.} There are three pairwise nonparallel surjective integer linear forms $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3:\mathbb Z^d\to\mathbb Z$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalE}
 b_C(y)=2-\sum_{j=1}^3\{\alpha_j\ell_j(y)+\beta_j\},
\end{equation}
where $1,\alpha_j,\beta_j$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$ for each $j$, and the rational span of $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ has dimension two.
\end{enumerate}
The forms $\ell_j$ in \textup{(ii)} may be chosen from the primitive integer linear forms defining the distinct saturated period hyperplanes in the original decomposition. The choice of these three forms and of the parameters $\alpha_j,\beta_j$ may depend on $C$. The conclusions persist under replacement of $q$ by any multiple.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:polynomial}
Suppose
\[
 a=c-\sum_{i=1}^r\varphi_i,\qquad
 a\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb Z^d},\quad 0\leq\varphi_i\leq1,
\]
where $c$ is $q_0\mathbb Z^d$-periodic and $\varphi_i$ is $q_0H_i$-periodic for the distinct hyperplanes~\eqref{eq:hyperplanes}. There is a multiple $q$ of $q_0$ such that, on each coset $C=x+q\mathbb Z^d$, each component either is constant modulo one or satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polynomialphase}
 \varphi_i(x+qy)\equiv P_{i,C}(\ell_i(y))\pmod1,
\end{equation}
where $P_{i,C}$ is a real polynomial of positive degree with irrational leading coefficient. This dichotomy persists on further scalar lattice cosets. A component is \emph{constant modulo one} on $C$ when its fractional part is independent of $y$; the component itself need not be constant. In that alternative, the component is either constant as a real-valued function or takes only the values $0$ and $1$ on $C$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The case $r=0$ is empty. For $r=1$, integrality of $a$ makes $\varphi_1$ constant modulo one on every $q_0\mathbb Z^d$ coset. Suppose $r\geq2$, and fix $i$. For every $j\ne i$, choose $v_j\in H_j$ such that $r_j=\ell_i(v_j)\ne0$. Reduce the decomposition modulo one and apply the commuting differences
\[
 \prod_{j\ne i}\Delta_{q_0v_j}.
\]
They annihilate the constant term and every component other than $\varphi_i$. On a fixed coset $x+q_0\mathbb Z^d$, invariance under $q_0H_i$ gives a well-defined sequence
\[
 \varphi_i(x+q_0y)\bmod1=u_i(\ell_i(y)),
 \qquad u_i:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb R/\mathbb Z.
\]
Surjectivity of $\ell_i$ then gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed1ddiff}
 \prod_{j\ne i}\Delta_{r_j}u_i=0.
\end{equation}
Let $R$ be a positive common multiple of all $|r_j|$. In the Laurent polynomial ring of the one-dimensional shift, each $\Delta_{r_j}$ divides $\Delta_R$, including when $r_j<0$. Multiplying these divisibility identities in~\eqref{eq:mixed1ddiff} yields $\Delta_R^{r-1}u_i=0$; no division in $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ is involved. Newton's finite-difference formula now gives, for each $a\in\{0,\ldots,R-1\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Newtoncircle}
 u_i(a+Rn)=\sum_{h=0}^{r-2}\binom{n}{h}(\Delta_R^h u_i)(a)
 \quad\text{in }\mathbb R/\mathbb Z,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
\end{equation}
The formula holds also for negative $n$, since the binomial polynomials satisfy the same difference recurrence in both directions. Arbitrary real lifts of the finitely many coefficients in~\eqref{eq:Newtoncircle} give a real polynomial representative of degree at most $r-2$ on each progression.

To pass from these progressions to scalar lattice cosets, fix $z\in\mathbb Z^d$ and write $\ell_i(z)=a+Rt$ with $0\leq a<R$. If $P_a$ represents $u_i(a+Rn)$, then
\[
 \varphi_i(x+q_0z+q_0Ry)
 \equiv P_a\bigl(t+\ell_i(y)\bigr)\pmod1.
\]
Thus each progression representation gives a polynomial in the same primitive form $\ell_i$ on the corresponding scalar lattice coset.

There are finitely many indices and initial cosets, so a common scalar refinement gives polynomial representatives for every component on every coset. A real polynomial with rational nonconstant coefficients is constant modulo one on sufficiently fine arithmetic progressions: a common denominator of those coefficients provides such a modulus. For a polynomial having an irrational nonconstant coefficient, remove its rational terms above the largest irrational degree in this way. On each resulting progression the remaining representative has positive degree and irrational leading coefficient. Taking a common refinement for the finitely many polynomials proves~\eqref{eq:polynomialphase}.

An integer affine restriction with nonzero step preserves irrationality of the leading coefficient. This proves persistence under further refinement. Finally, a $[0,1]$-valued function with constant fractional part is constant unless that fractional part is zero, in which case its values lie in $\{0,1\}$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:stationary}
Under the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:polynomial}, there is a tuple
\[
 (b,\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_r)
 \in\overline{\operatorname{Orb}_{q_0\mathbb Z^d}(a,\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_r)}
\]
with the following properties. There is a multiple $Q$ of $q_0$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item For every $i$ and every $x,y\in\mathbb Z^d$ with $y-x\in Q\mathbb Z^d$, the conditions $\psi_i(x)=0$ and $\psi_i(y)=1$ imply $b(x)=1$ and $b(y)=0$.
\item For every $i$, every multiple $q$ of $q_0$, every $x\in\mathbb Z^d$, and every pair $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R$, if $\alpha$ is irrational and
\[
 \psi_i(x+qy)\equiv\alpha\ell_i(y)+\beta\pmod1
 \qquad\text{for all }y\in\mathbb Z^d,
\]
then $1,\alpha,\beta$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$.
\end{enumerate}
The selected tuple satisfies $b=c-\sum_i\psi_i$, with each $\psi_i$ taking values in $[0,1]$ and invariant under $q_0H_i$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $X$ be the indicated orbit closure in the compact metrizable space
\[
 \{0,1\}^{\mathbb Z^d}\times\prod_{i=1}^r[0,1]^{\mathbb Z^d}.
\]
The decomposition $b=c-\sum_i\psi_i$ and the periods of the components are closed conditions preserved by $q_0\mathbb Z^d$ translations; $c$ is fixed by these translations. Hence these conditions hold throughout $X$. Averaging point masses over F\o lner boxes in $q_0\mathbb Z^d$ and taking a weak limit gives an invariant Borel probability measure $\mu$ on $X$.

Choose $Q$ divisible by $q_0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qchoice}
 Q\mathbb Z^d\subset q_0H_i+q_0H_j\qquad(i\ne j).
\end{equation}
If there is at most one component, take $Q=q_0$. Fix $i,x,y$ with $y-x\in Q\mathbb Z^d$, and let
\[
 \mathcal D_{i,x,y}
 =\{(b,\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_r)\in X:
       \psi_i(x)=0,\ \psi_i(y)=1\}.
\]
The event $\mathcal D_{i,x,y}$ is invariant under $q_0H_i$. On this event the decomposition gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpointdifference}
 b(x)-b(y)=1+
 \sum_{j\ne i}\bigl(\psi_j(y)-\psi_j(x)\bigr).
\end{equation}
For each $j\ne i$, write $y-x=u_j+w_j$ with $u_j\in q_0H_i$ and $w_j\in q_0H_j$, using~\eqref{eq:Qchoice}. The $j$th summand in~\eqref{eq:endpointdifference} equals $\psi_j(x+u_j)-\psi_j(x)$. Its integral over $\mathcal D_{i,x,y}$ vanishes by invariance of both $\mu$ and $\mathcal D_{i,x,y}$ under $u_j$. Consequently
\[
 \int_{\mathcal D_{i,x,y}}\bigl(b(x)-b(y)\bigr)\,d\mu=\mu(\mathcal D_{i,x,y}).
\]
Since $b(x)-b(y)\leq1$, equality forces $b(x)=1$, $b(y)=0$ almost surely on $\mathcal D_{i,x,y}$. Intersecting the resulting full-measure sets over the countably many $i,x,y$ proves (a) almost surely.

For (b), choose $e_i\in\mathbb Z^d$ with $\ell_i(e_i)=1$. Fix $i$, a multiple $q$ of $q_0$, and $x\in\mathbb Z^d$. Representatives of the affine parameters in $[0,1)$, whenever the affine identity holds, are recovered by the Borel formulas
\begin{equation}\label{eq:measurablephases}
 \beta=\{\psi_i(x)\},\qquad
 \alpha=\{\psi_i(x+qe_i)-\psi_i(x)\}.
\end{equation}
The affine identity is a countable collection of coordinate equalities modulo one, so the event that it holds with irrational $\alpha$ is Borel. For fixed integers $A,B,D$ with $B\ne0$, consider the subevent
\[
 A\alpha+B\beta+D=0.
\]
Its translates under $nqe_i$, $n\in\mathbb Z$, are pairwise disjoint. Indeed, along such a translation orbit the slope $\alpha$ is fixed, whereas the intercept becomes $\{\beta+n\alpha\}$. If the same relation holds at distinct times $n,m$, write $\{\beta+j\alpha\}=\beta+j\alpha-k_j$ with $k_j\in\mathbb Z$ for $j=n,m$. Subtracting the two relations gives
\[
 B(n-m)\alpha=B(k_n-k_m),
\]
contrary to irrationality. Invariance and finiteness of $\mu$ imply that this event has measure zero. Nontrivial relations with $B=0$ are excluded by irrationality of $\alpha$.

There are only countably many indices, scalar moduli, lattice points representing cosets, and integer relations. We can therefore select one tuple for which (a) and (b) hold simultaneously for all of them. Changing representatives of $\alpha$ or $\beta$ by integers does not affect the claimed rational independence. Replacing $x$ by another representative $x+qz$ changes the intercept by $\alpha\ell_i(z)$ modulo one, which also preserves this independence.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:moments}
Suppose $U_i:\mathbb Z^d\to[0,1]$ satisfies
\[
 U_i(y)\equiv P_i(\ell_i(y))\pmod1,
\]
where the $\ell_i$ have pairwise distinct kernels and every $P_i$ has positive degree and irrational leading coefficient. On $\mathbb Z^d$ and on every finite-index affine coset,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moments}
 \langle U_i\rangle=\frac12,\qquad
 \langle U_i^2\rangle=\frac13,\qquad
 \langle U_iU_j\rangle=\frac14\quad(i\ne j).
\end{equation}
In particular, the variance of the sum of $R$ such functions is $R/12$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Each polynomial phase is equidistributed modulo one. To check joint equidistribution of a pair, take integers $A,B$, not both zero. If $A\ne0$, restrict
\[
 A P_i(\ell_i(y))+B P_j(\ell_j(y))
\]
to an integer line in $\ker\ell_j$ on which $\ell_i$ is nonzero. The restricted polynomial has positive degree and irrational leading coefficient. The original multivariate polynomial must therefore have an irrational nonconstant coefficient. The case $A=0$ follows directly from the second phase. The same argument works on any finite-index affine coset, using a direction in its period lattice intersected with $\ker\ell_j$.

Weyl's theorem now gives joint uniform distribution on $(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z)^2$. The fractional-part functions and their products are Riemann integrable on the torus. Integral phase values form a set of density zero, so choosing the value $1$ instead of $0$ at such points does not affect any of the moments. This proves~\eqref{eq:moments}. Expanding the second moment of the sum gives the stated variance; only pairwise joint equidistribution is used.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
If there are no components, choose a scalar period $q$ of $a=c$ divisible by $p$ and take $b=a$. Otherwise combine period hyperplanes and apply Lemma~\ref{lem:range}. Choose the initial modulus $q_0$ divisible by $p$ and by all required scalar periods. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary} to obtain a tuple $(b,\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_r)$. Its Boolean coordinate belongs to $\overline{\operatorname{Orb}_{p\mathbb Z^d}(a)}$, and
\[
 b=c-\sum_i\psi_i.
\]

Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:polynomial} to the selected tuple, and take its resulting modulus $q$ divisible by $Q$. This order is deliberate: the polynomial refinement may depend on the selected tuple, while the conclusions of Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary} were arranged simultaneously for every possible scalar modulus.

Fix a coset $C=x+q\mathbb Z^d$. A component with constant fractional part is either constant as a real-valued function or Boolean there. Suppose $\psi_i$ is Boolean and nonconstant on $C$, and choose $z_0,z_1\in C$ with $\psi_i(z_0)=0$ and $\psi_i(z_1)=1$. If $z\in C$ satisfies $\psi_i(z)=0$, apply Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary}(a) to $z,z_1$ to obtain $b(z)=1$. If $\psi_i(z)=1$, apply the same lemma to $z_0,z$ to obtain $b(z)=0$. These applications are valid because $q$ is a multiple of $Q$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Booleanidentification}
 b=1-\psi_i\qquad\text{on }C.
\end{equation}
Since $\psi_i$ is invariant under $qH_i$, equation~\eqref{eq:Booleanidentification} makes $b_C$ invariant under $H_i$. This is alternative (i), so no further classification of the other components on this coset is needed.

We may therefore assume that every component with constant fractional part is constant as a real-valued function on $C$. Subtract these constant component values from the constant value of $c$ on $C$, and denote the result by $K$. If there are no remaining components, $b_C$ is constant and again satisfies (i). Otherwise, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ksum}
 b_C=K-\sum_{j=1}^{R}U_j,\qquad R\geq1,
\end{equation}
where $K$ is constant and every $U_j$ has an irrational polynomial phase, with the defining linear forms having pairwise distinct kernels. Let $\theta=\langle b_C\rangle=K-R/2$ and $S=\sum_{j=1}^R U_j$. Lemma~\ref{lem:moments} gives
\[
 \langle S\rangle=\frac R2,
 \qquad
 \langle S^2\rangle=\frac R3+\frac{R(R-1)}4.
\]
Consequently $\langle S^2\rangle-\langle S\rangle^2=R/12$. Since $b_C=K-S$ and $b_C^2=b_C$, it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variancebound}
 \theta(1-\theta)=\frac{R}{12}.
\end{equation}
As $0\leq\theta\leq1$, this forces $R\leq3$. Only the individual second moments and the pairwise mixed moments have been used; joint equidistribution of all $R$ phases is not required.

We rule out $R=1$ and $R=2$. With one component, reducing~\eqref{eq:Ksum} modulo one forces its phase to be constant modulo one. With two components, choose a vector $v\in\ker\ell_2$ with $s=\ell_1(v)\ne0$. Taking a difference of~\eqref{eq:Ksum} modulo one gives
\[
 \Delta_sP_1(n)\in\mathbb Z\qquad(n\in\mathbb Z).
\]
The polynomial on the left has irrational leading coefficient, including when it is a constant difference of an affine polynomial. It cannot be integer-valued on all of $\mathbb Z$. Thus $R=3$.

For each of these three phases, choose directions in the other two kernels on which its own defining linear form is nonzero. Taking the two corresponding differences modulo one gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:secondphasedifference}
 \Delta_s\Delta_tP_i(n)\in\mathbb Z\qquad(n\in\mathbb Z),\qquad st\ne0.
\end{equation}
If $\deg P_i\geq2$, the left side again has irrational leading coefficient and cannot be integer-valued. Hence every phase is affine with irrational slope. Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary}(b) gives rational independence of $1,\alpha_i,\beta_i$. No affine phase can take an integer value at any lattice point, so the $[0,1]$-valued components agree pointwise with
\[
 U_i(y)=\{\alpha_i\ell_i(y)+\beta_i\}.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:variancebound} gives $\theta=1/2$, and then $K=2$ in~\eqref{eq:Ksum}.

It remains to check the rational rank of the three forms. Reducing~\eqref{eq:normalE} modulo one and subtracting its value at zero shows that
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^3\alpha_i\ell_i(y)\in\mathbb Z
 \qquad(y\in\mathbb Z^d).
\]
If the three integer linear forms were rationally independent, three independent columns of their coefficient matrix would give an invertible rational system for the $\alpha_i$. Since the displayed linear form has integer coefficients, all three $\alpha_i$ would be rational. This is impossible. The forms are pairwise nonparallel, so their rational rank is exactly two.

The argument applies to each of the finitely many cosets for the modulus already chosen, so the alternatives hold simultaneously. To check persistence, refine $C=x+q\mathbb Z^d$ to a coset $x+qz+mq\mathbb Z^d$. The new restriction is $y\mapsto b_C(z+my)$. For an exceptional restriction, its $i$th affine phase becomes
\[
 m\alpha_i\ell_i(y)+\beta_i+\alpha_i\ell_i(z).
\]
Rational independence of $1,\alpha_i,\beta_i$ implies rational independence of $1,m\alpha_i,\beta_i+\alpha_i\ell_i(z)$. For a codimension-one-periodic restriction with period subgroup $L$, the new period subgroup is
\[
 \{h\in\mathbb Z^d:mh\in L\}=m^{-1}(L\cap m\mathbb Z^d),
\]
which still has rank $d-1$. This proves persistence and completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:Elevels}
Let $u$ be exceptional. Neither $\{u=0\}$ nor $\{u=1\}$ contains a nonempty codimension-one-periodic subset.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $L\leq\mathbb Z^d$ have rank at least $d-1$, and fix $z\in\mathbb Z^d$. At most one of the three forms $\ell_j$ vanishes on $L$: two distinct kernels intersect in rank $d-2$. If $\ell_j$ does not vanish on $L$, its image is a nonzero subgroup of $\mathbb Z$, and the irrational affine phase defining $U_j$ is uniformly distributed modulo one along boxes in $L$. Thus the average of $U_j$ on $z+L$ is $1/2$. If $\ell_j$ vanishes on $L$, the function is constant there with value $U_j(z)$. It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hyperplanemean}
 \langle u\rangle_{z+L}=
 \begin{cases}
 \frac12,&\text{if no $\ell_j$ vanishes on $L$},\\[2pt]
 1-U_j(z),&\text{if $\ell_j$ vanishes on $L$}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Both alternatives lie strictly between zero and one. Hence $u$ is not constant on $z+L$. A nonempty set invariant under $L$ contains a whole coset of $L$, which proves the assertion for both level sets.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:noantiperiod}
An exceptional function has no translate equal to its complement: there is no $t\in\mathbb Z^d$ such that $u(y+t)=1-u(y)$ for all $y$.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Suppose such a $t$ exists. Average this identity over translates of $H_1=\ker\ell_1$. The other two fractional-part functions have mean $1/2$ on every such coset, so~\eqref{eq:hyperplanemean} gives
\[
 U_1(y+t)+U_1(y)=1\qquad(y\in\mathbb Z^d).
\]
Reduce modulo one and subtract the identity at $y$ from the identity at $y+e$, where $\ell_1(e)=1$. This gives $2\alpha_1\in\mathbb Z$, contradicting irrationality.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:graph}
Let $F_1,\ldots,F_k\subset\mathbb Z^d$ be nonempty finite sets, and let $b:\mathbb Z^d\to\{0,1\}$ satisfy $\mathbf1_{F_i}*b=1$ for every $i$. Suppose that, for some $q\geq1$ and every $r\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}^d$, the residue restriction
\[
 b_r(y)=b(r+qy),\qquad y\in\mathbb Z^d,
\]
is either invariant under a subgroup of rank $d-1$ or exceptional in the sense of~\eqref{eq:E} and its accompanying conditions. Then the family has a piecewise $(d-1)$-periodic joint co-tile.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Let $\mathcal R=\{0,\ldots,q-1\}^d$ be the chosen set of residue representatives, and define
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal R_{\mathrm{per}}
   &=\{r\in\mathcal R:b_r\text{ is codimension-one periodic}\},\\
 \mathcal R_{\mathrm{exc}}
   &=\{r\in\mathcal R:b_r\text{ is exceptional}\}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Residues with $b_r\equiv0$ or $b_r\equiv1$ belong to $\mathcal R_{\mathrm{per}}$. The two sets are disjoint: an exceptional function has nonempty support, whereas Lemma~\ref{lem:Elevels} prevents that support from being codimension-one periodic. By hypothesis they exhaust $\mathcal R$. We will leave $b_r$ unchanged for $r\in\mathcal R_{\mathrm{per}}$ and replace it by a constant for $r\in\mathcal R_{\mathrm{exc}}$.

Fix $i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}$ and $r\in\mathcal R$. For each tile point $f\in F_i$, there are unique vectors $s(r,f)\in\mathcal R$ and $h(r,f)\in\mathbb Z^d$ such that
\[
 r-f=s(r,f)+qh(r,f).
\]
Here $r$ represents the output residue class where the tiling equation is evaluated, whereas $s(r,f)$ represents the residue class sampled by its summand indexed by $f$. Substituting $x=r+qy$ into $\sum_{f\in F_i}b(x-f)=1$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:occurrences}
 \sum_{f\in F_i}b_{s(r,f)}(y+h(r,f))=1
 \qquad(y\in\mathbb Z^d).
\end{equation}
Thus a summand in this equation is a translate of a residue restriction. The sum is indexed by the original set $F_i$, not by its image in $(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^d$: distinct tile points contribute separate summands even when they give the same residue restriction or the same translated function.

Let
\[
 J_{i,r}=\{f\in F_i:s(r,f)\in\mathcal R_{\mathrm{exc}}\}
\]
be the set of tile points indexing exceptional summands in~\eqref{eq:occurrences}. Suppose $J_{i,r}$ is nonempty, choose $f_0\in J_{i,r}$, and put
\[
 u(y)=b_{s(r,f_0)}(y+h(r,f_0)).
\]
This function is exceptional because it is a translate of an exceptional restriction. All summands in~\eqref{eq:occurrences} are Boolean and nonnegative. Hence the support of every summand other than the one indexed by $f_0$ is contained in $\{u=0\}$. If $f\notin J_{i,r}$, its summand has codimension-one-periodic support. Lemma~\ref{lem:Elevels} therefore forces that summand to vanish identically. The equation reduces to
\[
 \sum_{f\in J_{i,r}}b_{s(r,f)}(y+h(r,f))=1.
\]
Every summand in this reduced equation has box average $1/2$. Averaging gives $|J_{i,r}|/2=1$, and thus
\[
 |J_{i,r}|=2\qquad\text{whenever }J_{i,r}\ne\varnothing.
\]
This equality counts tile points, so it retains any multiplicities arising from repeated residues.

Construct a finite undirected multigraph $\mathcal G$ with vertex set $\mathcal R_{\mathrm{exc}}$. For each pair $(i,r)$ with $J_{i,r}\ne\varnothing$, write $J_{i,r}=\{f_1,f_2\}$ and insert one edge joining
\[
 s=s(r,f_1)\quad\text{and}\quad t=s(r,f_2).
\]
Different pairs $(i,r)$ may give parallel edges; at this stage a loop is also allowed. There are at most $kq^d$ edges. Write $h_1=h(r,f_1)$ and $h_2=h(r,f_2)$. The reduced tiling equation is
\[
 b_s(y+h_1)+b_t(y+h_2)=1.
\]
After replacing $y$ by $y-h_1$, it becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edgeidentity}
 b_t(y+\delta)=1-b_s(y),\qquad
 \delta=h_2-h_1.
\end{equation}
Attach the shift $\delta$ to this edge when traversed from $s$ to $t$, and attach $-\delta$ when it is traversed in the opposite direction.

For a walk with vertices $s_0,s_1,\ldots,s_m$ and successive traversal shifts $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_m$, repeated application of~\eqref{eq:edgeidentity} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:walkcomposition}
 b_{s_m}\left(y+\sum_{j=1}^m\delta_j\right)
 =\frac{1-(-1)^m}{2}+(-1)^m b_{s_0}(y).
\end{equation}
If the walk is closed and $m$ is odd, this identity says that a translate of the exceptional function $b_{s_0}$ equals $1-b_{s_0}$. Lemma~\ref{lem:noantiperiod} rules this out. Thus $\mathcal G$ has no odd closed walk, including no loop, and is bipartite.

Choose a proper two-coloring
\[
 \varepsilon:\mathcal R_{\mathrm{exc}}\longrightarrow\{0,1\},
 \qquad \varepsilon(s)+\varepsilon(t)=1
 \quad\text{for every edge joining }s\text{ and }t.
\]
Define the new residue restrictions by
\[
 \widetilde b_r(y)=
 \begin{cases}
  b_r(y),&r\in\mathcal R_{\mathrm{per}},\\
  \varepsilon(r),&r\in\mathcal R_{\mathrm{exc}},
 \end{cases}
 \qquad y\in\mathbb Z^d,
\]
and define $\widetilde b:\mathbb Z^d\to\{0,1\}$ by
\[
 \widetilde b(r+qy)=\widetilde b_r(y)
 \qquad(r\in\mathcal R,\ y\in\mathbb Z^d).
\]
We verify every tiling equation using its index pair $(i,r)$. If $J_{i,r}=\varnothing$, all its summands are unchanged. If $J_{i,r}=\{f_1,f_2\}$, the summands indexed by $F_i\setminus J_{i,r}$ were identically zero and remain so. Its two remaining summands are now the constants $\varepsilon(s(r,f_1))$ and $\varepsilon(s(r,f_2))$, whose sum is one by the edge associated with $(i,r)$. Hence $\mathbf1_{F_i}*\widetilde b=1$ for every $i$. The same coloring therefore preserves the entire family of tiling equations simultaneously.

Finally, for each $r\in\mathcal R$ define
\[
 A_r=\{r+qy:y\in\mathbb Z^d,\ \widetilde b_r(y)=1\}.
\]
If $r\in\mathcal R_{\mathrm{per}}$, choose a rank-$(d-1)$ period subgroup $L_r$ of $b_r$; then $A_r$ is invariant under $qL_r$. If $r\in\mathcal R_{\mathrm{exc}}$, the set $A_r$ is either empty or the whole coset $r+q\mathbb Z^d$, and is invariant under $q\mathbb Z^d$. The sets $A_r$ are pairwise disjoint, and their union is the support of $\widetilde b$. Thus they give a finite disjoint decomposition into codimension-one-periodic sets of a joint co-tile, as required.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:completion}
Let $\{0\}\subsetneqq F\subset\mathbb Z^d$ be finite, and suppose that $F\oplus A=\mathbb Z^d$ with $A$ fully periodic. There are $d-1$ further finite sets containing $0$ which, together with $F$, form an independent $d$-tuple and all have $A$ as a co-tile.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Fix a finite-index period subgroup $L$ of $A$. Distinct points of $F$ lie in distinct cosets of $L$: otherwise their translates of the nonempty set $A$ would coincide, contrary to uniqueness in the tiling.

Suppose an independent tuple of $t<d$ tiles with joint co-tile $A$ has been constructed, beginning with $F$ when $t=1$. Let $\mathcal V$ be the finite collection of rational subspaces spanned by all choices of one nonzero vector from each of these $t$ tiles. Every $V\in\mathcal V$ has dimension $t<d$.

For each $f\in F\setminus\{0\}$, we can choose
\[
 g_f\in(f+L)\setminus\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal V}V.
\]
To justify this avoidance, write $L=B\mathbb Z^d$ with an invertible integer matrix $B$. For each $V\in\mathcal V$, choose a nonzero rational linear form $\lambda_V$ vanishing on $V$. The polynomial
\[
 \prod_{V\in\mathcal V}\lambda_V(f+Bz)
\]
is nonzero, since each factor has a nonzero linear part. A nonzero polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ cannot vanish on all of $\mathbb Z^d$: induction on the number of variables reduces this assertion to the fact that a nonzero univariate polynomial has only finitely many roots. Some integer $z$ therefore gives the required $g_f$.

Set $g_0=0$ and $G=\{g_f:f\in F\}$. Distinctness of the cosets $f+L$ shows that $G$ has $|F|$ elements. Since $g_f+A=f+A$ for each $f\in F$, we have $G\oplus A=\mathbb Z^d$. The avoidance condition says exactly that adjoining $G$ preserves independence. Repeating the construction gives an independent tuple of length $d$.
<!-- end proof-11 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
If $F$ has a fully periodic co-tile $A$, apply Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} and retain $d-2$ of the added tiles. The resulting $(d-1)$-tuple is independent and shares the same $A$. Conversely, a joint co-tile for the independent tuple $(G_1,\ldots,G_{d-2},F)$ can be replaced by a fully periodic joint co-tile by Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. This is in particular a fully periodic co-tile for $F$.
<!-- end proof-12 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
The input condition is a finite one. For each candidate $(d-1)$-subfamily, check the rational rank of every choice of one nonzero vector from each tile. If normalizing points have not been specified, there are only finitely many choices of such points to check. All these rank computations use exact rational arithmetic. If any tile is a singleton, its equation forces the joint co-tile to be $\mathbb Z^d$; the family has a joint co-tile exactly when every tile is a singleton. We may therefore assume that all tiles are nontrivial.

Run the following two searches in parallel. For the positive search, enumerate $q=1,2,\ldots$ and all Boolean functions
\[
 b:(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^d\longrightarrow\{0,1\}.
\]
For each function, check the finite collection of equations
\begin{equation}\label{eq:periodicsearch}
 \sum_{f\in F_i}b(r-f)=1
 \qquad\bigl(1\leq i\leq k,\ r\in(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^d\bigr).
\end{equation}
The sum is over the original tile points: if two points of $F_i$ have the same residue, both occurrences are retained. A successful check produces a joint co-tile by taking the inverse image of $\{r:b(r)=1\}$ in $\mathbb Z^d$. Conversely, every fully periodic set is invariant under $q\mathbb Z^d$ for some $q\geq1$, since every finite-index subgroup contains such a scalar subgroup. By Corollary~\ref{cor:subfamily}, the positive search therefore succeeds whenever a joint co-tile exists.

For the negative search, let
\[
 B_n=[-n,n]^d\cap\mathbb Z^d,
 \qquad D_n=\bigcup_{i=1}^k(B_n-F_i).
\]
For each $n$, test all assignments $b:D_n\to\{0,1\}$ against
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obstructionsearch}
 \sum_{f\in F_i}b(x-f)=1
 \qquad(1\leq i\leq k,\ x\in B_n).
\end{equation}
If none satisfies these equations, they form the required finite unsatisfiable collection.

If every such finite system were satisfiable, let $X_n\subset\{0,1\}^{\mathbb Z^d}$ be the set of configurations satisfying~\eqref{eq:obstructionsearch}. Each $X_n$ would be nonempty, because an assignment on $D_n$ extends arbitrarily to the rest of the lattice. The sets $X_n$ are closed and nested. Compactness would give a point in $\bigcap_nX_n$, and this point would satisfy every joint tiling equation. Thus the negative search succeeds whenever no joint co-tile exists.

Every individual stage of either search is finite. Alternating their computational steps gives an algorithm which terminates on every input in the asserted class, with exactly one of the two certificates. Both searches and the independence checks are specified uniformly in $d$, so the dimension may be part of the input.

\]
Every choice of nonzero vectors from the tiles consists of nonzero multiples of distinct coordinate vectors, so the tuple is independent. Under the homomorphism $x\mapsto\sum_jx_j\pmod4$, each tile maps bijectively to $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$. Hence $A$ is a fully periodic joint co-tile.

Every nonzero choice has the same rational span, namely $\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}(e_1,\ldots,e_{d-1})$. Changing the vector selected from the first tile, for example from $e_1$ to $2e_1$, preserves this span and violates~\eqref{eq:star}. The same argument applies after any permutation of the tiles, so property $(\star)$ fails in every order.

\label{ex:fullrank}
Let
\[
 F_1=\{0,e_1,e_2,e_3\},\qquad
 F_2=\{0,e_1+e_2,e_1+2e_2,e_1+e_2+e_3\}.
\]
The determinant of the three displayed nonzero vectors is $1$ for each tile, so both tiles span $\mathbb Q^3$. No nonzero point of $F_2$ is parallel to a coordinate axis. Thus the pair is independent.

The pairs $(e_1,e_1+e_2)$ and $(e_2,e_1+2e_2)$ span the same $xy$-plane. Their first entries differ, so $(F_1,F_2)$ fails $(\star)$; their second entries also differ, so $(F_2,F_1)$ fails $(\star)$. The homomorphism
\[
 \chi(x,y,z)=x+2y+3z\pmod4
\]
takes the values $0,1,2,3$ on the displayed points of $F_1$, and $0,3,1,2$ on those of $F_2$. Consequently $\ker\chi$ is a fully periodic joint co-tile.

\label{ex:nonperiodic}
For $d\geq2$, let
\[
 F_i=\{0,e_i\}\quad(1\leq i\leq d-1),
 \qquad
 A=\left\{x\in\mathbb Z^d:
 \sum_{i=1}^{d-1}x_i\equiv\mathbf1_{\{0\}}(x_d)\pmod2\right\}.
\]
The tiles are independent, and translation by $e_i$ reverses the displayed parity condition. Therefore $F_i\oplus A=\mathbb Z^d$ for every $i$.

If $t\in\mathbb Z^d$ is a period of $A$, then
\[
 \mathbf1_{\{0\}}(n+t_d)-\mathbf1_{\{0\}}(n)
 \equiv\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}t_i\pmod2
 \qquad(n\in\mathbb Z).
\]
For $t_d\ne0$, the left-hand side equals $1$ modulo $2$ at exactly two values of $n$ and is zero elsewhere. It cannot be constant. Thus every period of $A$ has $t_d=0$, and the displayed congruence then requires $\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}t_i$ to be even. Conversely, every vector satisfying these two conditions is a period. The full period group is therefore
\[
 \left\{t\in\mathbb Z^d:t_d=0,\quad
     \sum_{i=1}^{d-1}t_i\equiv0\pmod2\right\},
\]
which has rank $d-1$. Hence $A$ is codimension-one periodic but not fully periodic. The same family has the fully periodic joint co-tile
\[
 A'=\left\{x\in\mathbb Z^d:\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}x_i\equiv0\pmod2\right\}.
\]

\label{ex:carry}
Let $d\geq2$, and choose real numbers $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ such that $1,\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$. Define
\begin{align*}
 u(x)={}&2-\{\alpha x_1+\beta\}
          -\{\alpha x_2+\gamma\}\\
        &\hspace{18mm}-\{-\alpha(x_1+x_2)-\beta-\gamma\}.
\end{align*}
The three quantities inside the fractional parts sum to zero, and none is an integer. Their fractional parts therefore sum to either $1$ or $2$, so $u$ is Boolean. Each fractional-part summand is invariant under a rank-$(d-1)$ subgroup, with respective defining linear forms $x_1$, $x_2$, and $x_1+x_2$.

These forms are pairwise nonparallel and span a two-dimensional rational space. The slope-intercept pairs are $(\alpha,\beta)$, $(\alpha,\gamma)$, and $(-\alpha,-\beta-\gamma)$; each pair is rationally independent together with $1$. Thus $u$ has the exceptional form to which Lemma~\ref{lem:Elevels} applies. Both level sets are nonempty: the first two phases are jointly equidistributed modulo one, and their fractional parts have sum both less than and greater than $1$. Neither level set contains a nonempty codimension-one-periodic subset, by that lemma. In particular, neither level set is piecewise $(d-1)$-periodic. Hence a bounded real codimension-one decomposition of a Boolean configuration need not be a disjoint periodic partition.
