<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{NP}
Let $G'$ be the graph obtained from a graph $G$ with
$m=|E(G)|$ by replacing each edge $v_iv_j$ with the gadget
$Q^{ij}$ as described above. Then $G$ has a vertex cover of cardinality at most $k$ if
and only if $G'$ has a $1$-limited dominating set of cardinality at
most $k+8m$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $C$ be a vertex cover of $G$ with $|C|\le k$. We regard $C$ as a
subset of $V(G')$. For each edge $v_iv_j\in E(G)$, fix one of its endvertices that
belongs to $C$. If the
chosen endvertex is $v_j$, let
\[
D^{ij}=
\{a^{ij},c^{ij},d^{ij},e^{ij},b^{ij},
  j^{ij},k^{ij},\ell^{ij}\},
\]
and if the chosen endvertex is $v_i$, let, symmetrically,
\[
D^{ij}=
\{b^{ij},e^{ij},f^{ij},d^{ij},a^{ij},
  i^{ij},k^{ij},\ell^{ij}\}.
\]
Set
\[
D=C\cup\bigcup_{v_iv_j\in E(G)}D^{ij}.
\]
Each set $D^{ij}$ contains exactly eight gadget vertices, and hence
$|D|=|C|+8m\le k+8m$.

A direct inspection shows that $D^{ij}$ dominates every vertex of
$Q^{ij}$ and that each vertex of $D^{ij}$ has at most one neighbor
outside $D$. Moreover, both $a^{ij}$ and $b^{ij}$ belong to
$D^{ij}$. Thus, every original vertex in $C$ has all its neighbors in
$D$. If $v_i\notin C$, then for each edge $v_iv_j$ incident with
$v_i$ we have $v_j\in C$, so $v_j$ is the chosen endvertex and
$a^{ij}\in D^{ij}$. Hence $v_i$ is dominated as well. Therefore $D$ is a
$1$-limited dominating set of $G'$ with $|D|\le k+8m$.

\medskip

Conversely, let $D$ be a $1$-limited dominating set of $G'$ with
$|D|\le k+8m$.
We first record two simple observations. The $1$-limited constraint implies that if a vertex of a triangle
belongs to $D$, then at least one other vertex of that triangle also
belongs to $D$.
Moreover, every diamond contains at least $3$ vertices of $D$.
Indeed, if $k^{ij}$ or $\ell^{ij}$ belongs to $D$, then at least two
of its three neighbors in the diamond must also belong to $D$.
If neither belongs to $D$, then domination of $k^{ij}$ and
$\ell^{ij}$ requires one of $i^{ij},j^{ij}$ to belong to $D$, but
such a vertex would have both $k^{ij}$ and $\ell^{ij}$ outside $D$,
a contradiction. Thus every diamond contains at least $3$ vertices of $D$.


We claim that every gadget contains at least $8$ vertices of
$D$. Suppose, to the contrary, that
$|D\cap V(Q^{ij})|\le7$. For convenience, set
$L^{ij}=\{a^{ij},c^{ij},d^{ij},g^{ij}\}$ and 
$R^{ij}=\{b^{ij},e^{ij},f^{ij},h^{ij}\}$. Both $L^{ij}$ and $R^{ij}$ contain a vertex
of $D$, since otherwise $c^{ij}$ or $f^{ij}$, respectively, would be
undominated.

Neither set can contain exactly one vertex of $D$. Indeed, if
$|D\cap L^{ij}|=1$, then the triangle observation implies that this
vertex is $g^{ij}$. Thus $a^{ij},c^{ij},d^{ij}\notin D$. Since
$d^{ij}$ must be dominated, $e^{ij}\in D$, and the $1$-limited
constraint at $e^{ij}$ forces $b^{ij},f^{ij}\in D$. As the diamond
already contributes at least three vertices, the assumption
$|D\cap V(Q^{ij})|\le7$ implies $h^{ij}\notin D$. But then
$g^{ij}$ has two neighbors outside $D$, a
contradiction. The argument for $R^{ij}$ is symmetric.

Consequently,
$|D\cap L^{ij}|\ge2$
and
$|D\cap R^{ij}|\ge2$.
Since the diamond contains at least three vertices of $D$ and
$|D\cap V(Q^{ij})|\le7$ we  derive
that 
$|D\cap L^{ij}|=|D\cap R^{ij}|=2$,
and the diamond contains exactly three vertices of $D$. The triangle
observation now implies that $g^{ij},h^{ij}\notin D$. If the only vertex of the diamond outside $D$ were $k^{ij}$ or
$\ell^{ij}$, then $i^{ij}\in D$ would have two neighbors outside $D$.
Hence the unique vertex of the diamond outside $D$ is one of
$i^{ij},j^{ij}$; by symmetry, assume that $i^{ij}\notin D$. Since
$g^{ij},h^{ij},i^{ij}\notin D$, domination of $g^{ij}$ forces
$c^{ij}\in D$, and the $1$-limited constraint at $c^{ij}$ then forces
$a^{ij},d^{ij}\in D$, contradicting
$|D\cap L^{ij}|=2$. Thus
$|D\cap V(Q^{ij})|\ge8$.


We next show that a gadget contributes at least $9$ vertices whenever
$v_i,v_j\notin D$. We claim that in this case
$|D\cap L^{ij}|\ge3$. If $a^{ij}\in D$, then, since
$v_i\notin D$, the $1$-limited constraint at $a^{ij}$ forces
$c^{ij},d^{ij}\in D$. If $a^{ij}\notin D$, then its domination
requires one of $c^{ij},d^{ij}$ to belong to $D$. The triangle
observation forces the other one to belong to $D$ as well, and the
$1$-limited constraint at $c^{ij}$ then forces $g^{ij}\in D$.
Thus $|D\cap L^{ij}|\ge3$. Symmetrically,
$|D\cap R^{ij}|\ge3$. Together with the three vertices forced in the
diamond, this gives
$|D\cap V(Q^{ij})|\ge9$.

Let
$C=D\cap V(G)$
and let
$F=\{v_iv_j\in E(G):v_i,v_j\notin C\}$
be the set of edges not covered by $C$. Since every gadget contributes
at least $8$ vertices to $D$, and every edge of $F$ forces one
additional vertex in its gadget, we have
$|D|\ge |C|+8m+|F|$.
As $|D|\le k+8m$, it follows that
$|C|+|F|\le k$.

For each edge of $F$, choose one of its endvertices, and let $X$ be
the set of chosen vertices. Every edge outside $F$ is covered by $C$,
while every edge in $F$ is covered by $X$. Hence $C\cup X$ is a
vertex cover of $G$. Moreover, $C$ and $X$ are disjoint, and
$|X|\le|F|$, so
\[
|C\cup X|=|C|+|X|\le |C|+|F|\le k.
\]
Thus $G$ has a vertex cover of cardinality at most $k$.

configuration $P\in\{X,Y,Z\}$ every vertex outside $D_P$ has a
neighbor in $D_P$, and every vertex of $D_P$ has at most one neighbor
outside $D_P$, except for the two square boundary vertices.

When two configurations are placed consecutively, their boundary
blocks are joined by the three edges prescribed in the construction
of $G_k$. We refer to this operation as concatenation. A concatenation
is cyclic if the last configuration is joined to the first in the
same way. In such a concatenation, the corresponding square vertices
are adjacent and complete each other's $1$-limited constraint.
Therefore every cyclic concatenation of $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ determines
a $1$-limited dominating set.

$4+3(q-1)=3q+1=k$

$$|D_Y|+(q-1)|D_X|
   =18+13(q-1)
   =13q+5
   =\tfrac{13}{3}k+\tfrac23.$$
Finally, let $k=3q+2$, where $q\ge1$. The cyclic concatenation
$ZX^{q-1}$ consists of
$5+3(q-1)=3q+2=k$
Goldberg blocks and determines a $1$-limited dominating set of size
   $$|D_Z|+(q-1)|D_X|
   =23+13(q-1)
   =13q+10
   =\tfrac{13}{3}k+\tfrac43.$$
This proves the general upper bound.

\label{glavnithm}
For the Goldberg snark $G_k$, $k\ge3$, we have
$$\gamma_1^{\mathrm L}(G_k)=
\begin{cases}
\frac{13}{3}k, & k\equiv0\pmod{3},\\

Let $D$ be a minimum $1$-limited dominating set of $G_k$.

Suppose first that $k=3$. If $w_i=3$ for some $i$, then
Lemma~\ref{weight3} gives
$|D|=w_{i-1}+w_i+w_{i+1}\ge6+3+6>13$.
Otherwise $w_i\ge4$ for every $i$. If $|D|=12$, then the weight
pattern is $444$, contradicting Lemma~\ref{weight4}(iv). Hence
$|D|\ge13$.
For $k=4$, Lemma~\ref{4blocks} gives
$|D|=W_j(4)\ge18$, while for $k=5$, Lemma~\ref{5blocks} gives
$|D|=W_j(5)\ge22$. 
                                                                      

Assume now that $k\ge6$. If $W_i(2)\le9$ for every $i\in[k]$, the
required lower bound follows from Lemma~\ref{total2}. Hence we may
assume that $W_j(2)\ge10$ for some $j\in[k]$. If $W_i(5)\le22$ for
every $i\in[k]$, the result follows from Lemma~\ref{total=5}. Thus we
may further assume that $W_t(5)\ge23$ for some $t\in[k]$.

For $k=6$, Lemma~\ref{6blocks} gives an index $\ell$ such that
$W_{\ell}(6)\ge26$. Since a $6$-block contains all blocks of $G_6$,
$|D|=W_{\ell}(6)\ge26$. 
For $7\le k\le11$, the required lower bounds follow from
the corresponding parts of Lemma~\ref{obstaja23}, while for $k=12$,
Lemma~\ref{vsota6} gives $|D|=W_i(12)\ge52$. 

                                                                       

It remains to consider $k\ge13$. Write
$k=6n+a$, where $a\in[6]$. Then $n\ge2$.
To apply Lemma~\ref{vsota6} to the remaining $6n$ blocks, it suffices
to find $a$ consecutive blocks with sufficiently large total weight.
We claim that, for each $a\in[6]$, there exists $s\in[k]$ such that
$W_s(a)\ge c_a$, where
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
a&1&2&3&4&5&6\\ \hline
c_a&5&10&13&18&23&26.
\end{array}
\]
Indeed, $W_j(2)\ge10$ implies that at least one of the blocks $B_j$ and $B_{j+1}$ has weight at
least $5$, giving the case $a=1$, while the case $a=2$ is immediate.
By Lemmas~\ref{wi3} and~\ref{8in12},
$W_j(3)=W_j(2)+w_{j+2}\ge10+3=13$
and
$W_j(4)=W_j(2)+W_{j+2}(2)\ge10+8=18$.
The case $a=5$ follows from $W_t(5)\ge23$, and finally
$W_t(6)=W_t(5)+w_{t+5}\ge23+3=26$.


The chosen $a$ consecutive blocks and the remaining $6n$ consecutive
blocks partition the cyclic sequence of all blocks. Hence, by
Lemma~\ref{vsota6},
$|D|=W_s(a)+W_{s+a}(6n)\ge c_a+26n$.
Since
\[
c_a=
\begin{cases}
\frac{13a}{3}, & a\equiv0\pmod3,\\
\frac{13a+2}{3}, & a\equiv1\pmod3,\\
\frac{13a+4}{3}, & a\equiv2\pmod3,
\end{cases}
\]
and $k=6n+a$, this is precisely the claimed lower bound for
$\gamma_1^{\mathrm L}(G_k)$. The corresponding upper bound follows from
Lemma~\ref{upper}, and hence equality holds in every case.
