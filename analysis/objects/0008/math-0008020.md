<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{prop_sub}
\ensuremath{\ensuremath{L_B(n)}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}\ is a sublattice of \ensuremath{L_B(n+1)}.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We must show that for any two elements $a$ and $b$ of \ensuremath{L_B(n)}, $inf(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}},\ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}})$ and $sup(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}},\ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}})$ are in $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{L_B(n)}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$.
 Let us first consider the element $c=inf(a,b)$. It is clear that $\ensuremath{c^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ is in $\ensuremath{\ensuremath{L_B(n)}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$, and we will show that $\ensuremath{c^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ is equal to 
$inf(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}},\ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}})$. This statement comes directly from Brylawski's result on dominance ordering \cite{Bry73}:
$$
inf(a,b) = c\mbox{ if and only if, for all $j \geq 1$, one has }\sum_{i=1}^j c_i =
min(\sum_{i=1}^j a_i,\sum_{i=1}^j b_i).
$$

Let us consider now $e = sup(a,b)$. We will show that $\ensuremath{e^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ is equal to
$d = sup(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}},\ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}})$.
We have $e \ge a$ and $e \ge b$, therefore
$\ensuremath{e^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \ge \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ and $\ensuremath{e^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \ge
\ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$. This implies that $\ensuremath{e^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \ge d$.
To show that $d \ge \ensuremath{e^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$,
let us begin by showing that $d_1 = c_1 + 1$. We can suppose that $a_1 \ge b_1$.
The partition $(a_1, a_1, a_1-1, a_1-2, \dots )$ is greater than $a$ and $b$,
and so it is greater than or equal to $e$. Moreover, $e \ge a$ implies $e_1 \ge a_1$
and so $e_1 = a_1$. Since $\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \le d \le \ensuremath{e^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$,
we then have $d_1 = a_1+1 = e_1+1$.
Let $f=(d_1-1, d_2, d_3,\dots)$. Since $d \le \ensuremath{e^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ and $e_1 = a_1$,
$f$ is a partition: $d_1-1 \ge d_2$. Moreover, $d \ge \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$
and $d \ge \ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$, and so $f \ge a$ and $f \ge b$.
This implies that $f \ge sup(a,b) = c$ and that
$d \ge \ensuremath{e^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$, which ends the proof.

\label{prop_prelim}
Let $a$ be a partition. Then, we have:
\begin{enumerate}
\item if $a \in C(n)$ or $a \in NP(n)$ then\\
 \centerline{$dirreach(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}) = \ensuremath{dirreach(a)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$;}
\item if $a \in  P_{\ell}(n)$ then 
 $\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}} \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell +2}}}}$ and\\
 \centerline{$dirreach(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}) = \ensuremath{dirreach(a)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}
                       \cup \lbrace \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell +2}}}} \rbrace$;}
\item if $a \in S(n)$ 
and  $b$ is such that $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}} b$, then we have
 $\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}} \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} \ensuremath{\stackrel{2}{\longrightarrow}} \ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ and\\
 \centerline{$dirreach(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}) = \ensuremath{(dirreach(a)
                                      \setminus \lbrace b \rbrace)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}
                   \cup \lbrace \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} \rbrace$;}
\item if $a \in  NS(n)$ then 
 $\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}} \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$ and\\
 \centerline{$dirreach(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}) = \ensuremath{dirreach(a)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}
                       \cup \lbrace \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} \rbrace$.}                  
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end prop-2 -->

It is obvious that the right hand side of each of these equations is a 
subset of its correspond left hand side, thus it is sufficient to prove the converse. 
So, let us consider an element $c = \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}}$ in  $dirreach(\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}})$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
If $i \neq 1$ then $c = \ensuremath{(a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}})^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \in \ensuremath{dirreach(a)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$. 
If $a \in NP(n)$ then there is no transition at the first column of $\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$.
If $a \in C(n)$, and if $c= \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}}$ then $c$ is also equal to $\ensuremath{(a \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}})^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$.
\item
If $i \neq 1$ then $c = \ensuremath{(a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}})^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \in \ensuremath{dirreach(a)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$. 
Otherwise if $i=1$, it is clear that $c = \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell +2}}}}$.
\item
If $i \neq 1$ then $c = \ensuremath{(a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}})^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \in \ensuremath{dirreach(a)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$. 
However, the element $b=a \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}}$ is obtained directly from $a$, but $\ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ is not obtained directly from $\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$. So we have:
$c \in \ensuremath{(dirreach(a) \setminus \lbrace b \rbrace)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$.
Otherwise if $i=1$, it is clear that $c = \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$.
\item 
If $i \neq 1$ then $c = \ensuremath{(a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}})^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \in \ensuremath{dirreach(a)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$. 
Otherwise if $i=1$, it is clear that $c = \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{th_complet}
For all $n \geq 1$, we have:
$$L_B(n+1) = \ensuremath{L_B(n)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} \bigsqcup \ensuremath{S(n)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} \bigsqcup \ensuremath{NS(n)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} 
\bigsqcup_{n > \ell \ge 1} \ensuremath{P_{\ell}(n)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell +2}}}}.$$
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
First of all, it is easy to check that this union is a disjoint union.

Let us recall the strategy of the construction of \ensuremath{L_B(n+1)}. First, \ensuremath{\ensuremath{L_B(n)}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} is a sublattice of \ensuremath{L_B(n+1)}; we then add to \ensuremath{\ensuremath{L_B(n)}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} all directly reachable elements (and transitions) from this set to obtain a new set $L^1$; finally, we add to $L^1$ all  directly reachable elements (and transitions) from $L^1$ to obtain a new set $L^2$, and so on. The key idea of this theorem is to show that the set $L^1$ is already equal to \ensuremath{L_B(n+1)}, or, equivalently, that all directly reachable elements from $L^1$ are elements of $L^1$. 

In Proposition 2, we have shown that $L^1$ is represented as the disjoint union in the right hand side of the claim. 
So let $g \in L^1$, and let $h = g \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}}$ be a directly reachable element of $g$; we shall prove that $h \in L^1$.
Several cases are possible.
\begin{trivlist}
\item[$\ \bullet$] $g= \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ with $a \in \ensuremath{L_B(n)}$. From Proposition 2, all directly reachable elements from $g$ are in $L^1$.

\item[$\ \bullet$] $g = \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$ with $a \in S(n)$. The transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}} c$ is possible in \ensuremath{L_B(n)}. All transitions $g = \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} h = \ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$ are the same as transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} b$, except the transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{1}{\longrightarrow}} c$. 
Moreover, it is clear that, if $b$ belongs to $S(n)$, then $h$ belongs to $\ensuremath{S(n)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$. Regarding $c$, we have the transition $\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}  \ensuremath{\stackrel{2}{\longrightarrow}} \ensuremath{c^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$, and $h = \ensuremath{c^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ belongs to $\ensuremath{L_B(n)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$.

\item[$\ \bullet$] $g= \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$ with $a \in NS(n)$. All transitions $g \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} h$ are the same as transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} b$, and $h = \ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$. If $i >2$ then $b$ belongs to $NS(n)$, and then $h$ belongs to $\ensuremath{NS(n)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}}$. Otherwise, $i$ can be $2$ in the case where $a$ has a non-slippery step of length 1 at 1. In this case, $b$ has a cliff at 1, and $\ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} = \ensuremath{c^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$ with $c \in L_B(n)$. Hence $h = \ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} \in \ensuremath{L_B(n)^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}$.

\item[$\ \bullet$] $g= \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+2}}}}$ with $a \in P_{\ell}(n)$. This case requies more attention. We distinguish three subcases:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $a$ has a cliff at $\ell+1$. Then all transitions $g \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} h$ are the same as transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} b$, and $h = \ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+2}}}}$. Moreover, $b$ is an element of $P_{\ell}(n)$ so $h \in \ensuremath{P_{\ell}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+2}}}}$.
\item $a$ has a non-slippery step at $\ell+1$. Then all transitions $g \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} h$ are the same as transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} b$, and $h = \ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+2}}}}$. Moreover, $b$ is an element of $P_{\ell}(n)$ so $h \in \ensuremath{P_{\ell}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+2}}}}$.
\item $a$ has a slippery step at $\ell+1$. The transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{\ell+1}{\longrightarrow}} c$ is possible. 
All transitions $g \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} h$ are the same as transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} b$, except the transition $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{\ell+1}{\longrightarrow}} c$. It is easy to check that if $b$ is in $P_{\ell}$, then $h = \ensuremath{b^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+2}}}} \in \ensuremath{P_{\ell}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+2}}}}$.  Regarding $c$,  
we have the transition $\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+2}}}} \ensuremath{\stackrel{\ell+2}{\longrightarrow}} \ensuremath{c^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+1}}}}$. Moreover $c$ is an element of $P_{\ell-1}$, so  $h = \ensuremath{c^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+1}}}} \in \ensuremath{P_{\ell -1}^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{\ell+1}}}} \in L^1$. This completes the proof.
\end{enumerate}                           
\end{trivlist}

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{th_lbi}
The set \ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}\ is a lattice. Moreover, if
\mbox{$a=(\infty,a_2,\dots,a_k)$} and \mbox{$b=(\infty,b_2,\dots, b_l)$}
are two elements of \ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}, then
$\inf_{\ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}}(a,b)=c$ in \ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}, where $c$ is defined by:
$$
c_i = max(\sum_{j\geq i} a_j, \sum_{j \geq i} b_j) - \sum_{j >i} c_j\hskip
0.3cm \mbox{ for all } i\mbox{ such that } 2 \leq i\leq max(k,l).
$$
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We shall prove that $c$ is an element
of $L_B(\infty)$ and that $c$ is equal to $inf_{\ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}}(a,b)$.
Let $n = 2(\sum_{i\geq 2}a_i + \sum_{i \geq 2}b_i)$.
Let $a'=(n-\sum_{i\geq 2}a_i,a_2,\ldots,a_k)$,
$b'=(n-\sum_{i\geq 2}b_i,b_2,\ldots,b_l)$ and
$c'=(n-\sum_{i\geq 2}c_i,c_2,\ldots,c_{max(k,l)})$.
It is then obvious that $a'$ and $b'$ are two partitions of $n$ and that $c'$
is the infimum
of $a'$ and $b'$ by the dominance ordering in \ensuremath{L_B(n)}. Therefore, $c'$ is a
decreasing sequence, and so $c$ is an element of $L_B(\infty)$.
Moreover, according to the definition of $\geq_{\ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}}$, $c$ is
the maximal element of $L_B(\infty)$ which is smaller than $a$ and $b$,
and so $c=inf_{\ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}}(a,b)$.

By definition, $L_B(\infty)$ has a maximal element. Since it is
closed for the
infimum, \ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}\ is a lattice.

<!-- lemme-1: body_md -->
\label{th_chi}
The map $\chi$ defined by:
$$
\chi : \ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}} \longrightarrow \ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}
$$
$$
a=(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k) \mapsto \chi(a) = (\infty,a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k)
$$
is a lattice isomorphism.\\
 Moreover,
$a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} b$ in \ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}\ if and only if $\chi(a) \ensuremath{\stackrel{i+1}{\longrightarrow}}
\chi(b)$ in \ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}.
<!-- end lemme-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
$\chi$ is clearly bijective. Moreover, it is clear from the
definitions that for all $a$ and $b$ in
\ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}, $a \ensuremath{\stackrel{i}{\longrightarrow}} b$ if and only if $\chi(a) \ensuremath{\stackrel{i+1}{\longrightarrow}} \chi(b)$.
Therefore, $\chi$ is an order isomorphism. Since \ensuremath{L_B(\infty)}\ is a lattice,
this implies that $\chi$ is a lattice isomorphism.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
For all integer $n \geq 1$, \ensuremath{L_B(n)}\ is a sublattice of \ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}.
\label{th_retrouve2}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Let $a$ and $b$ be two elements of \ensuremath{L_B(n)}, we shall prove that $inf_{\ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}}(a,b)$
and $sup_{\ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}}(a,b)$ belong to \ensuremath{L_B(n)}. Let $c$ be $inf_{\ensuremath{L_B(n)}}(a,b)$ and
$c'$ be $inf_{\ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}}(a,b)$. We have, $a \geq c' \geq_{\ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}} c$,
which means that
$\sum_{i \geq 1}a_i \leq \sum_{i \geq 1}c'_i \leq \sum_{i \geq 1}c_i$,
and so $\sum_{i \geq 1}c'_i=n$. This implies that $c'$ belongs to \ensuremath{L_B(n)},
and we obtain $c'=c$. The proof for the supremum is similar.

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\cite{Lat02}
The map $\pi$ from \ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}\ into the Young lattice
such that $\pi(a)_i$ is equal to $\sum_{j\ge i}a_j$
is an order embedding which preserves the infimum.
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let $a$ and $b$ be two elements of \ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}. We must show that $\pi(a)$
and $\pi(b)$ belong to the Young lattice, that $a \ge b$ in \ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}\ is
equivalent to $\pi(a) \ge \pi(b)$ in the Young lattice and that
$inf(\pi(a),\pi(b))$ in the Young lattice is equal to $\pi(inf(a,b))$
in \ensuremath{\widetilde{L_B(\infty)}}.
The first two points are easy:
$\pi(x)$ is obviously a decreasing sequence of integers for any $x$, and
the order is preserved.
Now, let $c = inf(a,b)$. Then,
$$
\begin{array}{lclr}
\pi(c)_i & = & \sum_{j\ge i}c_j &\\
         & = & max(\sum_{j\ge i}a_j,\sum_{j\ge i}b_j)& \mbox{by~Theorem~\ref{th_lbi}}\\
         & = & max(\pi(a)_i,\pi(b)_i) &\\
         & = & inf(\pi(a),\pi(b))_i &\mbox{in the Young lattice}
\end{array}
$$
which proves the claim.

<!-- prop-5: body_md -->
Let $\chi^{-1}$ be the inverse of the lattice isomorphism defined in
Lemma~\ref{th_chi}. Then, the set $\chi^{-1}(\overline{\ensuremath{L_B(n)}})$ is
a subtree of \ensuremath{T_B(\infty)}\ having the same root.
<!-- end prop-5 -->
<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
We will call \emph{$X_k$ subtree} any subtree $T$ of\ \ensuremath{T_B(\infty)}\ which is rooted
at an element \mbox{$a =(\raisebox{-2.5ex}{ 
$\stackrel{\underleftrightarrow{\mbox{\normalsize $i,\dots,i$}}}{\mbox{\tiny $^{k}$}}$},a_{k+1},\dots)$} with
$a_{k+1} \le i-1$ and which is either the whole subtree of \ensuremath{T_B(\infty)}\ rooted at $a$ in the case $a$
has only one son, or $a$ and its left subtree otherwise.
Moreover, we define $X_0$ as a simple node.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- prop-6: body_md -->
A $X_k$ subtree, with $k\ge 1$, is composed by a chain of $k+1$
nodes (the rightmost chain) whose edges are labeled by $1$, $2$,
$\dots$, $k$ and whose $i$-th node is the root of a $X_{i-1}$
subtree for all i between $1$ and $k+1$. (See
Figure~\ref{fig_def_Xk}.)
\label{prop_rec_xk}
<!-- end prop-6 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
The claim is obvious for $k=1$. Indeed, in this case
the root $a$ has the form
$(i,a_2,\dots)$ with $a_2 \le i-1$, therefore its left son has the form
$(i+1,i-1,\dots)$, \emph{i.e.}\ it starts with a cliff, and has only one son.
This son also starts with a cliff; we can then deduce that $X_1$ is
simply a chain, which is the claim for $k=1$.

Suppose now that the claim is proved for any $i<k$ and consider the root $a$
of a $X_k$ subtree: \mbox{$a=(\raisebox{-2.5ex}{ 
$\stackrel{\underleftrightarrow{\mbox{\normalsize $i,\dots,i$}}}{\mbox{\tiny $^{k}$}}$},a_{k+1},\dots)$}
with $a_{k+1}\le i-1$. Its left son is \mbox{$\ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}} =
(i+1,i,\dots,i,a_{k+1},\dots)$} with $a_{k+1}\le i-1$, therefore it
is the root of a $X_1$ subtree. Moreover, \ensuremath{a^\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}}\ has one right
son: \mbox{$a^{\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}} = (i+1,i+1,i,\dots,i,a_{k+1},\dots)$},
which by definition is the root of a $X_2$ subtree. After $k-1$ such
stages, we obtain \mbox{$a^{\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}\dots\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{k-1}}}}$}, which is
equal to \mbox{$(i+1,\dots,i+1,i,a_{k+1})$}. This node is the root
of a $X_{k-1}$ subtree and has a right son:
$$a^{\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{1}}}\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{2}}}\dots\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{k-1}}}\ensuremath{{\downarrow_{k}}}}, \emph{i.e.}\
(\raisebox{-2.5ex}{ 
$\stackrel{\underleftrightarrow{\mbox{\normalsize $i+1,\dots,i+1$}}}{\mbox{\tiny $^{k}$}}$},a_{k+1},\dots)$$
and we still have
$a_{k+1}\le i-1$. Therefore, this node is the root of a $X_k$
subtree, and from the definition of $T_B(\infty)$ we know that it
has no other son. This completes the proof.
<!-- end proof-8 -->

The tree \ensuremath{T_B(\infty)}\ can be represented by the infinite chain defined as

$\stackrel{\underleftrightarrow{\mbox{\normalsize $1,\dots,1$}}}{\mbox{\tiny $^{i-1}$}}$})$, is

<!-- corol-1: body_md -->
The $X_k$ subtree of \ensuremath{T_B(\infty)}\ with root $(1,\dots,1)$
contains exactly the partitions of length $k$.
\label{th_part_born}
<!-- end corol-1 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Because of their recursive structure shown in
Proposition~\ref{prop_rec_xk},
$X_k$ subtrees contain no edge with label greater
than $k$. Therefore, if the root of a $X_k$ subtree is of length $k$ then
all its nodes have length $k$.
Moreover, no $X_l$ subtrees with $l \not= k$ and with a root of length $l$
can contain any node of length $k$.
This remark, together with Theorem~\ref{th_arbrechaine}, implies the result.
<!-- end proof-9 -->
<!-- corol-2: body_md -->
Let $c(\ell,k)$ denote the number of paths in a $X_k$ tree originating from the
root and having length $\ell$. We have:
$$ c(\ell,k) = \left
\{\begin{array}{llll}
1 & \mbox{ if }  \ell = 0 \mbox{ or } k = 1\\
\sum_{i=1}^{inf(\ell,k)} c(\ell-i,i) & \mbox{ otherwise }
\end{array}
\right.$$
Moreover, $|L_B(n)| = c(n,n)$ and the number of partitions of $n$ with length
exactly $k$ is \mbox{$c(n-k,k)$}.
<!-- end corol-2 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
The formula for $c(\ell,k)$ is derived directly from the structure of $X_k$ trees
(Proposition~\ref{prop_rec_xk} and Figure~\ref{fig_def_Xk}).
To obtain the formula for $|\ensuremath{L_B(n)}|$, we recall 
Proposition~\ref{prop_rec_xk} and Theorem~\ref{th_arbrechaine}. Applying
them, we observe that if we keep the nodes
of depth $n$ at most, then the subtrees obtained from \ensuremath{T_B(\infty)}\ and $X_n$ turn
out to be isomorphic.
The last formula is directly
derived from Theorems~\ref{th_arbrechaine} and \ref{th_part_born}.
