<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{main-theorem}
Let $f$ be either a holomorphic Hecke eigencuspform or a Hecke--Maass cusp form of full level whose Hecke eigenvalues are denoted $(\lambda_f(n))_{n\geqslant 1}$ and let $\varpi_f\geqslant 0$ be such that the following bound holds
\begin{equation}
    \label{RPbound}
    |\lambda_f(n)|\leqslant n^{\varpi_f+o(1)},\ n\rightarrow\infty
\end{equation}
and let
\begin{equation}
    \label{thetafdef}
    \theta_{f}=\frac{21}{52(1+4\varpi_f)}.
\end{equation}
Let $q$ be a prime and $a\geqslant 1$ be an integer such that $(a,q)=1$. 
For any $X\geqslant 1$  and $\eta>0$ satisfying 
\begin{equation}
    \label{qbound}
    q\leqslant X^{\theta_f-\eta},
\end{equation}
we have
	$$\mathop{\sum_{n\geqslant 1}}_{n\equiv a\bmod q}\lambda_f(n)^2V\left(\frac{n}X\right)-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\geqslant 1}}
{{\scriptstyle (n,q)=1}}}\lambda_f(n)^2V\left(\frac{n}X\right)\ll_{f,V,\eta} (X/q)^{1-\delta}$$
	for some $\delta=\delta(\eta)>0$.
	In particular if $f$ is holomorphic $\varpi_f=0$ and 
	$$\theta_f=21/52=2/5+1/260.$$
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rmksquare} The Ramanujan--Petersson conjecture for cusp forms predicts that
for any $f$, $$\varpi_f=0.$$
The best known bound, due to Kim and Sarnak \cite{KimSar}*{Proposition 2} is
$$\varpi_f\leqslant 7/64.$$
This is unfortunately not sufficient to insure that
$\theta_f>\theta_4=2/5$. For this we would need $7/64$ to be replaced by any exponent smaller than $1/416$. 

We also observe that the closely related Theorem \ref{main-theorem2} below is independent of any approximation toward the Ramanujan--Petersson conjecture.

In the work \cite{huang}, Huang improved the error term for the sharp-cut sum $$\sum_{n\leqslant X}\lambda_f(n)^2$$ from $O\big(X^{3/5}\big)$ to $O\big(X^{3/5-1/560+o(1)}\big)$, resolving a long standing problem going back to Rankin and Selberg. In that Archimedean case Huang was able to avoid the use of the pointwise bound \eqref{RPbound}.
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
This work \cite{FrIwAnn} of Friedlander--Iwaniec was recently extended in \cites{KMS,KMS2} to Dirichlet convolutions of the form
$$1\star \lambda_f(n)=\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{lm=n}\lambda_f(m)$$
for $f$ a Hecke cuspform; again the critical range was 
$$l=q^{1/2-\eta+o(1)},\ m=q^{1/2-\eta+o(1)}.$$
Notice that $(d_3(n))_{n\geqslant 1}$ and $(1\star \lambda_f(n))_{n\geqslant 1}$ are the coefficients attached to $\operatorname{GL}_{3,\mathbf{Q}}$ Eisenstein series representations (the isobaric sums $1\boxplus 1\boxplus 1$ and $1\boxplus \pi_f$, respectively) and by the same methods, one can improve the standard level of distribution $\theta_3=1/2$ of $(\lambda_\pi(n))_{n\geqslant 1}$ for $\pi$ any fixed $\operatorname{GL}_{3,\mathbf{Q}}$ Eisenstein series representation. Doing the same for $\pi$ a cuspidal representation seems to be a serious challenge.
<!-- end remark-4 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{main-theorem2}Let $\pi$ be a $\operatorname{GL}_{3,\mathbf{Q}}$ cuspidal automorphic representation of conductor $1$ and $(\lambda_\pi(n))_{n\geqslant 1}$ be the coefficient of its standard $L$-function. Set  $$\lambda_{1\boxplus\pi}(n):=1\star\lambda_{\pi}(n):=\sum_{lm =n}\lambda_{\pi}(m).$$
Let $q$ be a prime and $a\geqslant 1$ be an integer such that $(a,q)=1$. For any $\eta>0$ and $X$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
    \label{qbound2}
    q\leqslant X^{21/52-\eta}=X^{2/5+1/260-\eta},
\end{equation}
we have 
$$\mathop{\sum_{n\geqslant 1}}_{n\equiv a\bmod q}\lambda_{1\boxplus\pi}(n)V\left(\frac{n}{X}\right)-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\geqslant 1}}
{{\scriptstyle (n,q)=1}}}\lambda_{1\boxplus\pi}(n)V\left(\frac{n}{X}\right)\ll_{\pi,V,\eta} (X/q)^{1-\delta}$$
for some $\delta=\delta(\eta)>0$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
Unlike \eqref{qbound} the exponent in  \eqref{qbound2} is independent of any approximation to the Ramanujan--Petersson conjecture for $\operatorname{GL}_{3}$ automorphic representations.
<!-- end remark-5 -->

In particular, since $L\geqslant 1$, this implies that $$q\leqslant X^{15/37}$$ (which is the limit of our method, fortunately $15/37>2/5$).

In view of this and \eqref{LMupperbound} we may assume that

<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
The above proof can be carried out for more general convolutions
$1\star\lambda_\pi$
for $\lambda_\pi$ of degree $d\geqslant 2$ such that \eqref{nontrivialgeneric} can be obtained near and below the convexity range $q^{d/2}$: let $X=q^{\frac{d+2}2}$ be the a.p. range for $1\star\lambda_\pi$. By duality and a dyadic decomposition, one has to bound non-trivially  bilinear sums of the shape
$$\sum_{l\sim L}\sum_{m\sim M}\lambda_{\overline{\pi}}(m)\operatorname{\mathrm{Kl}}_{d+1}(lm;q)$$
for $L,M\geqslant 1$ such that $LM\leqslant q^{d/2}$. Let us assume for simplicity that $LM=q^{d/2}$.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item If $L\leqslant q^{\eta}$ for $\eta>0$ small enough then the $m$-sum 
$$\sum_{m\sim M}\lambda_{\overline{\pi}}(m)\operatorname{\mathrm{Kl}}_{d+1}(lm;q)$$
can be bounded non-trivially since $M\geqslant q^{d/2-\eta}$ is near the convexity range for $\lambda_{\overline{\pi}}$. 

\item If $L\geqslant q^{d/2-\eta}$ the linear sum
$$\sum_{l\sim L}\operatorname{\mathrm{Kl}}_{d+1}(lm;q)$$
can be bounded non-trivially after applying the Poisson summation formula (as $d/2-\eta>1/2$ because $d\geqslant 2$). 

\item In the remaining range
we have
$$\min(L;M)\geqslant q^{\eta}, \max(L,M)\geqslant q^{\frac{d}4}.$$
\begin{itemize}
    \item  If $d\geqslant 3$ we can then apply Cauchy--Schwarz to this bilinear sum with the shorter variable inside the square and the longer variable being smoothed;
 since the longer variable is well above the P\'olya--Vinogradov range  $q^{1/2}$ (since $q^{d/4}\geqslant q^{3/4}$) there will be some saving from sums of products of Kloosterman sums and we are done.
 \item In the case $d=2$, then in the last situation we obtain only 
 $\max(L,M)\geqslant q^{1/2}$ which is the P\'olya--Vinogradov range and the above approach does not provide any saving if $L=M=q^{1/2}$. However using a technique going back to Vinogradov--Korobov  developed in this context by Friedlander--Iwaniec in \cite{FrIwAnn} one can still bound non-trivially the bilinear form of Kloosterman sums and eventually get some saving (see \cites{KMS,KMS2}).
\end{itemize}	
\end{enumerate} 
For instance the above method can be applied to the convolution of degree $1+6=7$   $$1\star\lambda_{\pi}$$ 
to pass above the standard distribution exponent $\theta_7=2/(7+1)=1/4$
for $1\star\lambda_{\pi}$ with
$\lambda_{\pi}(n)$
given by \eqref{RS23def}. The main point is that  the Kloosterman sheaf $\mathcal{K}\ell_7$ is a ``good" sheaf
in the sense of \cite{LMS}*{\S 1} so that \eqref{nontrivialgeneric} holds for $K(\bullet)=\operatorname{\mathrm{Kl}}_7(l\bullet;q)$ at or slightly below the convexity range $q^{6/2}=q^3$ (see \cite{LMS}*{Theorem 1.1}).

\label{functional-equ}
Proof of the functional equation \eqref{Functional eq}.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- head-1: body_md -->
The proof is exactly the same as that of \cite{LMS}*{Corollary 9.2}.
We denote  $\kappa=0$ if $\chi(-1)=1$ and $\kappa=1$ if $\chi(-1)=-1$ and denote
 $$\varepsilon_\chi=q^{-1/2}\sum_{x\in{\mathbf{F}^\times_q}}\chi(x)e(\frac{x}q)$$
the normalized Gauss sum. 

Recall that  $$\lambda_{1\boxplus\pi}(n):=1\star\lambda_{\pi}(n):=\sum_{lm =n}\lambda_{\pi}(m).$$

The $L$-function $$L((1\boxplus\pi)\times \chi,s)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{\lambda_{1\boxplus\pi}(n)\chi(n)}{n^s}=L(\chi,s)L(\pi\times \chi,s)$$ has analytic continuation to $\mathbf{C}$ and satisfies a functional equation of the form:
\begin{equation}\label{FE-gl4}
\Lambda((1\boxplus\pi)\times \chi,s)=\varepsilon_\chi^4\Lambda((1\boxplus\overline{\pi})\times \overline{\chi},1-s)\end{equation}
where
$$\Lambda((1\boxplus\pi)\times \chi,s)=q^{2s}L_\infty(1\boxplus\pi,s+\kappa)L((1\boxplus\pi)\times \chi,s)$$
is the completed $L$-function and 
 $$L_\infty(1\boxplus\pi,s)=\prod_{i=1}^4\Gamma_\mathbf{R}(s-\mu_{i}),\ \Gamma_\mathbf{R}(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)$$
with
$$\{\mu_{i},\ i=1,2,3,4\}=\{0,\nu_2-\nu_1,2\nu_1+\nu_2-1,1-\nu_1-2\nu_2\}$$
denoting the local Archimedean factor of $1\boxplus\pi$.

We have 
$$V(x)=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\mathop{\int}\limits \widetilde V(s)x^{-s}ds$$
(the integration is along the vertical line $\mathfrak{Re}\, s=1+1/14$) so that
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
&\mathop{\sum_{n\geqslant 1}}_{n\equiv a\bmod q}\lambda_{1\boxplus\pi}(n)V(\frac{n}{X})=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}\overline{\chi}(a)\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\lambda_{1\boxplus\pi}(n)\chi(n)V(\frac{n}{X})\\
=&\frac{1}{\varphi(q)} \mathop{\sum_{n\geqslant 1}}_{(n,q)=1}\lambda_{1\boxplus\pi}(n)V(\frac{n}{X})+ \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle \chi\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}}
{{\scriptstyle \chi\not=\chi_0}}}\overline{\chi}(a)\frac{1}{2i\pi}\mathop{\int}\limits\widetilde{V}(s)\frac{\Lambda((1\boxplus\pi)\times \chi,s)}{L_\infty(1\boxplus\pi,s+\kappa)}(\frac{X}{q^2})^sds.
\end{split}\end{equation*}
By applying the functional equation \eqref{FE-gl4}, the second term above equals
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\frac{q^2}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle \chi\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}}
{{\scriptstyle \chi\not=\chi_0}}}\overline{\chi}(a)\varepsilon_\chi^4\,\frac{1}{2i\pi}\mathop{\int}\limits\widetilde{V}(s)L((1\boxplus\overline{\pi})\times \overline{\chi},1-s)\frac{L_\infty(1\boxplus\overline{\pi},1-s+\kappa)}{L_\infty(1\boxplus\pi,s+\kappa)}(\frac{X}{q^4})^sds.
\end{split}\end{equation*}

In the integral we make the change of variable $s\leftrightarrow 1-s$ getting
$$\frac{X}{q^2\varphi(q)}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle \chi\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}}
{{\scriptstyle \chi\not=\chi_0}}}\overline{\chi}(a)\varepsilon_\chi^4\frac{1}{2i\pi}\mathop{\int}\limits_{(-1/14)}  L((1\boxplus\overline{\pi})\times \overline{\chi},s)\frac{L_\infty(1\boxplus\overline{\pi},s+\kappa)}{L_\infty(1\boxplus\pi,1-s+\kappa)}\widetilde V(1-s)(\frac{X}{q^4})^{-s}ds$$
and shifting the contour back to $\mathfrak{Re}\, s=3/2$ without hitting any poles we obtain the sum
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
&\frac{X}{q^2\varphi(q)}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\lambda_{1\boxplus\overline{\pi}}(n)\bigl(\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle \chi\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}}
{{\scriptstyle \chi\not=\chi_0}}}\overline{\chi}(an)\varepsilon_\chi^4\bigr)\frac{1}{2i\pi}\mathop{\int}\limits_{(3/2)}  \frac{L_\infty(1\boxplus\overline{\pi},s+\kappa)}{L_\infty(1\boxplus\pi,1-s+\kappa)}\widetilde V(1-s)(\frac{nX}{q^4})^{-s}ds\\
=&\frac{X}{q^2\varphi(q)}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\lambda_{1\boxplus\overline{\pi}}(n)\bigl(\varphi(q)q^{-1/2}\mathrm{Kl}_4(an;q)-q^{-2}\bigr) 
\savestack{\tmpbox}{\stretchto{ 
  \scaleto{ 
    \scalerel*[\widthof{\ensuremath{V}}]{\kern-.4pt\bigwedge\kern-.4pt} 
    {\rule[-\textheight/2]{1ex}{\textheight}}                        
  }{\textheight}  
}{0.5ex}} 
\stackon[2pt]{V}{\scalebox{-1}{\tmpbox}} 
^4(\frac{n}{q^4/X}).
\end{split}\end{equation*}
This completes the proof of \eqref{Functional eq}.
