<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:colength}
	The number \(c_{n}=1+(n-2)\cdot 2^{\,n-1}\) is the codimension of the zero-dimensional square-free binomial ideal \(I_n \triangleright R_n \) of size $n$.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:polynomial_in_In}
	The following polynomials are elements in \(I_n\)
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item[(1)] \(x_n^{2n-3}-x_n\).
		\item[(2)] For each \(k\) from \(2\) to \(n-1\):
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item[(i)] \(x_kx_n^{2(n-2)}-x_k\).
			\item[(ii)] \(x_k^2-x_n^2\).
		\end{enumerate}
		\item[(3)] \(x_1-x_2x_3\cdots x_n\).
	\end{enumerate}
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Write \(f_i=x_1\cdots x_{i-1}x_{i+1}\cdots x_n-x_i\), for all \(1\leq i\leq n\), so that \(I_n=\langle f_1,\ldots,f_n\rangle\). Since \(x_1\equiv x_2x_3\cdots x_n\pmod{I_n}\) and \(x_i^2\equiv x_n^2\pmod{I_n}\), for every \(1\leq i\leq n-1\), we obtain from $f_n$ that
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
	x_n
	&\equiv x_1x_2\cdots x_{n-1}\\
	&\equiv
	(x_2x_3\cdots x_n)x_2\cdots x_{n-1}\\
	&=
	x_n\prod_{i=2}^{n-1}x_i^2\\
	&\equiv x_n^{2n-3}\pmod{I_n}.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Therefore \(x_n^{2n-3}-x_n\in I_n\), which proves (1).
	
	Similarly, for $2\leq k\leq n-1$, using $f_k$ and
	$x_1\equiv x_2\cdots x_n$ gives
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
	x_k
	&\equiv x_1\prod_{\substack{2\leq i\leq n\\i\neq k}}x_i\\
	&\equiv
	(x_2\cdots x_n)
	\prod_{\substack{2\leq i\leq n\\i\neq k}}x_i\\
	&=
	x_kx_n^2
	\prod_{\substack{2\leq i\leq n-1\\i\neq k}}x_i^2\\
	&\equiv x_kx_n^{2(n-2)}
	\pmod{I_n}.
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Thus \(x_kx_n^{2(n-2)}-x_k\in I_n\), proving (2)(i). For \(2\leq k\leq n-1\), multiplying \(f_k\) by \(x_k\) and \(f_n\) by \(x_n\) gives \(x_kf_k-x_nf_n=x_n^2-x_k^2\), and hence \(x_k^2-x_n^2\in I_n\). This proves (2)(ii). Finally, \(x_1-x_2x_3\cdots x_n=-f_1\in I_n\), which proves (3). Thus all the polynomials belong to \(I_n\).
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:generating-set}
	For \(n \geq 3\), the set\\ \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{$\mathcal G_n =\left\{ x_n^{2n-3}-x_n, x_{n-1}x_n^{2(n-2)} - x_{n-1}, x_{n-1}^2- x_n^2,\dots, x_2x_n^{2(n-2)}-x_2, x_2^2-x_n^2, x_1-x_2x_3\cdots x_n \right\}$}\\ is a Gr\"{o}bner basis for \(I_n\) with respect to the lexicographic order \( x_1 > x_2 > \cdots > x_n \).
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The ideal \( I_n = \langle f_1, f_2, \dots, f_n \rangle \), where \( f_i = x_1x_2 \cdots x_{i-1} x_{i+1} \cdots x_n - x_i \) for \( i = 1, \dots, n \). For each \(f_i\), the leading term \( \operatorname{Lt}(f_i) \) is divisible by the leading term of an appropriate element in \( \mathcal G_n \). 
	
	Consider the S-pair \(S(f_i, f_j)\) of any two elements \(f_i\) and \(f_j\) in \(I_n\), defined by
	\[
	S(f_i, f_j) = \operatorname{LCM}(\operatorname{Lt}(f_i), \operatorname{Lt}(f_j))\left\{\frac{1}{\operatorname{Lt}(f_i)} \cdot f_i - \frac{1}{\operatorname{Lt}(f_j)} \cdot f_j\right\},
	\]
	where \(\operatorname{Lt}(f_i)\) is the leading term of \( f_i \) and \(\operatorname{LCM}\) denotes the least common multiple. Now given any two distinct generators \(f_i\) and \(f_j\) where \(i \neq j\), we have
	
	\noindent \textit{Case 1: \(i, j > 1\).} If \(j \neq i\) and \(i, j > 1\), then	\(\operatorname{LCM}\bigl(\operatorname{Lt}(f_i),\operatorname{Lt}(f_j)\bigr) = x_1\cdots x_n\) and
	\[
	S(f_i,f_j)=(x_1\cdots x_n) \left\{ \frac{1}{x_1x_2 \cdots x_{i-1}x_{i+1}\cdots x_n}f_i - \frac{1}{x_1x_2 \cdots x_{j-1}x_{j+1}\cdots x_n}f_j \right\} 
	\]
	\[
	=x_if_i-x_jf_j= -x_i^2+x_j^2.
	\]
	This is a combination of \(q_i=x_i^2-x_n^2\) and \(q_j=x_j^2-x_n^2\), so it reduces to zero modulo \(\mathcal G_n\).
	
	\noindent \textit{Case 2: \(i =1\) or \(j = 1\).} Assume without loss of generality that \(i = 1\). Then \(\operatorname{LCM}\bigl(\operatorname{Lt}(f_1),\operatorname{Lt}(f_j)\bigr)=- \operatorname{Lt}(f_j)\) and a direct computation gives
	\[
	S(f_1,f_j)
	=(x_2x_3\cdots x_{j-1}x_{j+1}\cdots x_n) f_1 + f_j
	= x_jx_n^{2(n-2)}-x_j,
	\]
	which is exactly \(p_j\in\mathcal G_n\). Thus the S-polynomial reduces to zero.
	
	It remains to verify the $S$-pairs of the elements of $\mathcal G_n$ themselves. For convenience, write
	\begin{align*}
	p_n & =x_n^{2n-3}-x_n,\\
	p_k & =x_kx_n^{2(n-2)}-x_k,\quad 2\leq k\leq n-1,\\
	q_k & =x_k^2-x_n^2,\quad 2\leq k\leq n-1,
	\end{align*}
	and
	\[
	p_1=x_1-x_2x_3\cdots x_n.
	\]	
	For $2\leq k<\ell\leq n-1$,
	\[
	S(p_k,p_\ell)
	=x_\ell p_k-x_kp_\ell=0.
	\]
	Also,
	\[
	S(p_k,p_n)
	=x_np_k-x_kp_n=0.
	\]	
	For $2\leq k<\ell\leq n-1$,
	\[
	S(q_k,q_\ell)
	=x_\ell^2q_k-x_k^2q_\ell
	=x_n^2(x_k^2-x_\ell^2)
	=x_n^2(q_k-q_\ell),
	\]
	which is a linear combination of \(q_k\) and \(q_\ell\), and hence reduces to zero.
	
	For $k\neq\ell$, the leading terms of $q_k$ and $p_\ell$ are relatively prime, so \(S(q_k,p_\ell)\) reduces to zero. Similarly, \(S(q_k,p_n)\) reduces to zero. For the remaining pair $q_k$, $p_k$,
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
	S(q_k,p_k)
	&=x_n^{2(n-2)}q_k-x_kp_k\\
	&=x_k^2-x_n^{2n-2}\\
	&=(x_k^2-x_n^2)
	-x_n(x_n^{2n-3}-x_n)\\
	&=q_k-x_np_n,
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	which is a combination of \(q_k\) and \(p_n\), and therefore reduces to zero with respect to $\mathcal G_n$.
	
	Finally, the leading term of $p_1$ is $x_1$, which is relatively prime to the leading terms of all the other elements of $\mathcal G_n$. Hence \(S(p_1,p)\longrightarrow 0\) for every $p\in \mathcal G_n\setminus\{p_1\}$.
	
	Moreover, each defining generator $f_i$ reduces to zero modulo $\mathcal G_n$. Indeed, \(f_1=-p_1\). For \(2\leq i\leq n-1\), reducing the leading term of \(f_i\) by \(p_1\), and then reducing the resulting monomial using the polynomials \(q_j=x_j^2-x_n^2\), \(2\leq j\leq n-1\), \(j\neq i\), the remainder is exactly \(p_i\), which is in \(\mathcal G_n\). For \(f_n\), reducing its leading term by \(p_1\) and subsequently using \(q_j=x_j^2-x_n^2\), \(2\leq j\leq n-1\), gives \(x_n^{2n-3}-x_n=p_n\in\mathcal G_n\), which then reduces to zero.
	
	Thus \(I_n=\langle \mathcal G_n\rangle\), and every $S$-polynomial of every pair of elements of $\mathcal G_n$ reduces to zero. By Buchberger's criterion, $\mathcal G_n$ is a Gröbner basis for $I_n$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:monomial-basis} 
	For \(0 \leq b < 2n-4 \), the monomial \(m(b, T)\) defined as \[m(b,T)=x_n^{b}\prod_{i\in T}x_i \] where \(T\subseteq \{2,3,\dots,n-1\} \) is any subset of size \(k\) with \(0\leq k \leq n-2\), and \[ m(b,T)=x_n^{2n-4} \] when \(b=2n-4\) and \(|T|=0\), spans the finite-dimensional quotient ring \(R_n/J_n\).
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
This set of monomials represents all those that cannot be reduced using the generators of the ideal \(J_n\). The number of elements in this basis is \(2^{n-1}\times (n-2)+1 \), corresponding to the choices of \(T\) and \(b\).
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:reduced-companion}
	For the fixed lexicographic monomial order \(x_1>x_2>\cdots>x_n\) and arbitrary \(n\), consider the companion matrices $T_{j= 1,\dots, n}$ associated with the square-free binomial ideal $I_n \triangleright  R_n$. For each \(T_j\), after reducing the matrix by deleting the first row and column, the resulting matrices are permutation matrices \(P_j \in M_{c_n-1}(F) \) for \(j=1, \dots, n\).
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
From Corollary~\ref{cor:monomial-basis}, the generating set for \( I_n \) with respect to the lexicographic order yields a basis for \( R_n / I_n \) consisting of monomials
	\[
	\mathcal{B} = \left\{
	m(b,T)=x_n^b\prod_{i\in T}x_i:
	T\subseteq\{2,\ldots,n-1\},\
	0\leq b\leq 2n-4
	\right\},
	\]
	with the convention that \(x_n^{2n-4} \) is the only monomial when \(T = \varnothing\) and \( b = 2n-4 \). The element \(m(0,\varnothing)=1\) is the first basis element. Let \(\mathcal{B}'=\mathcal{B}\setminus\{1\}\). By Proposition~\ref{prop:colength}, $\dim R_n/I_n = c_n < \infty $.
	
	For each \( j \;=\; 1,\dots, n \), consider the \( F\)-linear multiplication map \( m_{x_j}: R_n/I_n \rightarrow R_n/I_n \) defined by	
	\[
	m_{x_j}(f) = x_j f\; \bmod{ I_n}.
	\]  In the basis set \(\mathcal{B}\), the action of \( m_{x_j} \) behaves differently depending on whether \(j\) is in \(T\), \(j=1\), \(j=n\), or \(j \notin T \).	The structure of \(I_n\) imposes relations that simplify products \( x_j \cdot m(b, T) \) in a uniform way:	
	
	\noindent \textit{(R1) \;\; Case \(2\le j\le n-1\) and \(j \notin T\).} If \(j \notin T\) and \(j\in\{2,\ldots,n-1\}\), then	
	\[
	x_j \cdot m(b, T) = x_j \cdot x_n^b \prod_{i \in T} x_i = x_n^b \cdot x_j \prod_{i \in T} x_i \equiv x_n^b \prod_{i \in T \cup \{j\}} x_i = m(b, T \cup \{j\}) \bmod{ I_n},
	\] provided \(b\leq2n-5\). 
	
	\noindent \textit{(R2) \;\; Case \(2\le j\le n-1\) and \(j \in T\).} If \(j \in T\) and \(j\in\{2,\ldots,n-1\}\), then	
	\[
	x_jm(b,T)
	=x_n^b x_j^2
	\prod_{i\in T\setminus\{j\}}x_i
	\equiv
	x_n^{b+2}
	\prod_{i\in T\setminus\{j\}}x_i
	\pmod{I_n}
	=m(b+2,T\setminus\{j\}),
	\]
	provided \(b\leq2n-7\). When \(b=2n-6\), this gives \(x_jm(2n-6,T)
	=m(2n-4,T\setminus\{j\})\), which is reduced using the relation \(x_jx_n^{2n-4}-x_j\in I_n\). When \(b=2n-5\), we obtain
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
	x_jm(2n-5,T)
	&=x_n^{2n-5}x_j^2
	\prod_{i\in T\setminus\{j\}}x_i\\
	&\equiv
	x_n^{2n-3}
	\prod_{i\in T\setminus\{j\}}x_i\\
	&\equiv
	x_n
	\prod_{i\in T\setminus\{j\}}x_i\\
	&=m(1,T\setminus\{j\}).
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	
	\noindent \textit{(R3)\;\; Case \( j = n \).} If $j=n$, then
	\[
	x_n\cdot m(b,T)
	=x_n^{b+1}\prod_{i\in T}x_i
	=m(b+1,T)\pmod{I_n},
	\]
	whenever $b+1<2n-4$. If $b=2n-5$ and $T\neq\varnothing$, then
	\[
	x_n m(2n-5,T)
	=x_n^{2n-4}\prod_{i\in T}x_i
	\equiv
	\prod_{i\in T}x_i
	=m(0,T)\pmod{I_n},
	\]
	using \(x_i x_n^{2n-4}-x_i\in I_n\), for all \(2\leq i\leq n-1\). If $T=\varnothing$, then \(x_nx_n^{2n-5}=x_n^{2n-4}\), which is the exceptional basis element. Finally,
	\[
	x_nx_n^{2n-4}
	=x_n^{2n-3}
	\equiv x_n\pmod{I_n}.
	\]
	Thus multiplication by $x_n$ cyclically permutes the basis elements in $\mathcal{B}'$ corresponding to each fixed $T$.
	
	\noindent \textit{(R4)\;\; Case \( j = 1 \).} Since $1\notin T$, this case is not covered by (R1). Using \(x_1\equiv x_2x_3\cdots x_n\pmod{I_n}\), we obtain
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
	x_1m(b,T)
	&\equiv
	x_n^{b+1}
	\left(\prod_{i=2}^{n-1}x_i\right)
	\left(\prod_{i\in T}x_i\right)\\
	&\equiv
	x_n^{b+1+2|T|}
	\prod_{i\in S\setminus T}x_i
	\pmod{I_n},
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	where \(S=\{2,\ldots,n-1\}\) and the second congruence follows from \(x_i^2\equiv x_n^2\pmod{I_n}\), for all \(2\leq i\leq n-1\). After reducing the power of $x_n$ by \(x_n^{2n-3}\equiv x_n\pmod{I_n}\), the result is again a basis element of $\mathcal{B}'$. Again, observe that \(T\) and \(S\setminus T\) are distinct, so this action cannot fix any basis element. It remains to prove that these actions are bijections on \(\mathcal B'\).
	
	From  \(x_n^{2n-3}-x_n =x_n(x_n^{2n-4}-1)\in I_n\), we see that multiplication by $x_n$ is not injective on the full basis $\mathcal B$, since
	\[
	x_n\cdot x_n^{2n-4}\equiv x_n
	=x_n\cdot1\pmod{I_n}.
	\]
	Thus it is necessary to remove the constant basis element and work on \(\mathcal{B}'=\mathcal{B}\setminus\{1\}\). Thus, $m_{x_j}$ maps $\mathcal{B}'$ into $\mathcal B'$ for every $j=1,\ldots,n$.
	
	For $j=n$, the relations in (R3) show directly that multiplication by $x_n$ permutes the elements of $\mathcal{B}'$. For $2\leq j\leq n-1$, Proposition~\ref{prop:generating-set} gives \(x_j^2\equiv x_n^2\pmod{I_n}\). Hence, as multiplication operators on $R_n/I_n$, \(m_{x_j}^2=m_{x_n}^2\). Since $m_{x_n}$ is a bijection on $\mathcal B'$, $m_{x_n}^2$ is bijective. Consequently, $m_{x_j}^2$ is bijective, and therefore $m_{x_j}$ is injective on the finite set $\mathcal B'$. Hence $m_{x_j}$ is bijective.
	
	The same argument applies to $j=1$, since \(x_1^2\equiv x_n^2\pmod{I_n}\). Therefore multiplication by every $x_j$, $1\leq j\leq n$, permutes the elements of $\mathcal{B}'$.
	
	The matrix representation of the $F$-linear map $m_{x_j}$ with respect to $\mathcal B'$ is therefore a permutation matrix \(P_j\in M_{c_n-1}(F)\). This matrix corresponds to the companion matrix $T_j$ with the first row and column, corresponding to the constant basis element $1$, deleted. Hence, the reduced companion matrices $P_j$ are permutation matrices.

<!-- ex-1: body_md -->
\label{eq:permutation-matrices}
	For \(n = 3\), consider the lexicographic order with \(x_1 > x_2 > x_3\). The ideal \(I_3\) is generated by
	\[
	f_1 = x_2x_3 - x_1, \quad f_2 = x_1x_3 - x_2, \quad f_3 = x_1x_2 - x_3.
	\]
	The basis \(\mathcal{B}\) for \(R_3 / I_3\) consists of monomials \(m(b, T) = \displaystyle x_3^b \prod_{i \in T} x_i\), where \(T \subseteq \{2, 3, \dots, n-1\} = \{2\}\) and \(0 \leq b \leq 2\). So \(T\) can be either \(\varnothing\) or \(\{2\} \). If \(T = \varnothing\), then \(m(b,\varnothing) = x_3^b\) for \(0 \leq b \leq 2n-4 = 2\), and so \(m(0,\varnothing) = x_3^0 = 1 \), \(m(1,\varnothing) = x_3^1 = x_3\) and \(m(2,\varnothing) = x_3^2\). <!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The permutation matrix \(P_j \in M_{c_n-1}(F)\) represents the action of \(x_j\) on \(\mathcal{B}'\), where each basis element \(m_r \in \mathcal{B}'\) is indexed by a pair \((b, T)\) with \(T \subseteq \{2, \dots, n-1\}\) for every \(0\leq b<2n-4\), except that $(0,\varnothing)$ is omitted, together with the exceptional

	\begin{align*}
	(P_j)_{k,r} & = \text{coefficient of}\; m_r\, \text{in}\; (m_k:=x_j\cdot m_{r^{\prime}} \bmod{ I_n})\\ & = \begin{cases}
	\text{coefficient of}\; m_{\sigma_j(k)},\, & \text{if}\; r=\sigma_j(k)\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}\\ (P_j)_{k,r} & = \delta_{r,\sigma_j(k)},
	\end{align*} where \(\displaystyle m_{r} := m(b,T) = x_n^{b} \prod_{i \in T} x_i\), \(1 \leq r, k \leq c_n-1\), \(r = b \times 2^{n-2} + t\) and \(\displaystyle t = \sum_{i=1}^{n-2} t_i2^{i-1}\) is a unique number in the range \(0 \leq t \leq 2^{n-2}-1\) such that for each \(a_i \in \{2 < 3 < \cdots < n-1\}\), \(t_i = \begin{cases}
	1, \; & \text{if}\; a_i\in T\\ 0, \; & \text{if}\; a_i \notin T
	\end{cases}\). There is a bijection between the index \(r\) and the parameter \((b,T)\) of the basis elements. Put \(M=2^{n-2}\). For $b=0$ and $1\leq t\leq M-1$, assign the index \(r=t\). For \(1\leq b\leq2n-5\) and \(0\leq t\leq M-1\), assign \(r=bM+t\). Finally, assign \(r=(2n-4)M=c_n-1\) to the exceptional basis element $x_n^{2n-4}$. Thus the indices run precisely through \(1,\ldots,c_n-1\). The trace of \(P_j\) is the sum of the diagonal entries \((P_j)_{k,k}\), which correspond to fixed points of \(\sigma_j\).
	
	We show that \((P_j)_{k,k} = 0\) for all \(k\). Suppose the source element is \(m_{r'} = m(b',T')\). Consider the action of \(x_j\) on a basis element \(m_{r'} = m(b',T') \in \mathcal{B}'\).
	
	\noindent {\textit{Case I}: \(2\le j\le n-1\) and \(j \notin T'\), \(j \neq n\).} Then
	\[ 
	m_k := m_{x_j}(m(b',T'))= x_n^{b'} \prod_{i \in T} x_i = m(b', T) \bmod{ I_n}, 
	\] where \(T := T'\cup \{j\}\). Clearly, \(t> t'\), since \(T'\subsetneq T \). Therefore, the index \(k\) of the image is strictly greater than \(r'\), so \(k\neq r'\), and thus \((P_j)_{k,k} = 0\) for all \(k\).
	
	\noindent {\textit{Case II}: \(2\le j\le n-1\) and \(j \in T'\).}  Then
	\[
	m_k := x_n^{b'} \cdot x_j^2 \prod_{i \in T''} x_i = m(b' + 2, T'\setminus \{j\}) \bmod{ I_n}, 
	\] whenever $b'+2<2n-4$. At \(b'=2n-6\) and \(b'=2n-5\), the images reduce to their equivalent forms as in (R2). In every case the subset changes from $T'$ to $T'':= T'\setminus\{j\}$, and so the pair \((b',T')\) differs from \((b'',T'')\), where \(b''\)  varies according to the case of \(b'\). Hence, the index \( k \) of the image differs from the source \(r'\). This implies that \((P_j)_{k,k} = 0\) for all \(k\).
	
	\noindent {\textit{Case III}: \(j = n\).} If \(b'<2n-5\), then
	\[
	m_k := x_n^{b'+1} \prod_{i \in T'} x_i = m(b' + 1, T') \bmod{ I_n},
	\] so the value \(b'\) increases, implying that \(k \neq r'\). Similarly, if $b'=2n-5$ and $T'\neq\varnothing$, then
	\[
	m_k = m_{x_n}(m(2n-5,T'))
	=
	m(0,T')\pmod{I_n},
	\] using \(x_i x_n^{2n-4}-x_i\in I_n\). If $b'=2n-5$ and $T'=\varnothing$, the image is the exceptional basis
	element $x_n^{2n-4}$. Finally,
	\[
	m_k = m_{x_n}(x_n^{2n-4})
	=
	x_n^{2n-3}
	\equiv x_n = m(1,\varnothing)
	\pmod{I_n}.
	\]
	Thus in every case \(k\neq r'\), and this ensures that \( (P_j)_{k,k} = 0 \) for all \(k\).
	
	\noindent {\textit{Case IV}: \( j = 1 \).} Then
	\[
	m_k := m_{x_1}(m(b',T'))
	\equiv
	x_n^{b'+1+2|T'|}
	\prod_{i\in S\setminus T'}x_i
	\pmod{I_n},
	\]
	where \(S=\{2,\ldots,n-1\}\). Thus the subset $T'$ is replaced by its complement \(S\setminus T'\). Since \(S\) is nonempty (as \(n\ge3\)), a finite set cannot equal its own complement, so the resulting basis element is different from \(m(b',T')\). Therefore \((P_1)_{k,k}=0\).
	
	In all cases, \(m_{x_j}(m_{r'}) \neq m_{r'}\), meaning that \(\sigma_j(r') \neq r'\) for all \( r' \). Hence, \(\sigma_j\) has no fixed points, and is therefore a derangement.

\label{thm:commuting-reduced}
	 For all $1\le i,j\le n$, the reduced companion matrices $P_i$ and $P_j$ in \( M_{c_n-1}(F)\) commute. That is,

	[P_i,P_j]=0 .

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let $A_n=R_n/I_n$ and, for each $k$, let $m_{x_k}:A_n\to A_n$ denote multiplication by $x_k$.
	Because $A_n$ is a commutative $F$-algebra, the operators commute on the whole space. That is, for any \(i, j\) and for any element \(a \in A_n\),
	\[
	m_{x_i}(m_{x_j}(a)) = x_i(x_j a) = (x_i x_j)a = (x_j x_i)a = m_{x_j}(m_{x_i}(a)).
	\] 
    So \(m_{x_i}\,m_{x_j} = m_{x_j}\,m_{x_i}\) on \(A_n\). By Lemma~\ref{lem:nonconstant-invariant}, the subspace
	\[
	W := \mathrm{span}_F(\mathcal{B}^\prime)\subset A_n
	\]
	is \emph{common invariant} for all $m_{x_k}$. Choose the ordered basis \(\mathcal{E}=(\,1,\,\mathcal{B}^\prime\,)\) of $A_n$, so that every $m_{x_k}$ has a block upper–triangular matrix
	\[
	   T_k \;=\;
	\begin{bmatrix}
	\alpha_k & \rho_k \\
	0 & B_k
	\end{bmatrix}
	\]
	with respect to \(\mathcal{E}\), where \( \alpha_k \) is the scalar coefficient of 1 in the normal form of \(m_{x_k}(1)=x_k\) in \(A_n\) (since \(x_k\) is a nonconstant basis monomial, \(\alpha_k =0\)), \(\rho_k\) is the row vector of coordinates of \(x_k\) in the basis \(\mathcal{B}^\prime\), and the lower–left block is $0$ because $W$ is $m_{x_k}$–invariant.
	By definition, the \emph{reduced companion matrix} $P_k$ is the matrix of the restriction $m_{x_k}\!_{\restriction_W}$ in the basis $\mathcal{B}^\prime$, so $P_k=B_k$.
	
	Since $T_iT_j=T_jT_i$ (the $m_{x_k}$ commutes on $A_n$), the $(2,2)$-block of the product gives
	\[
	B_iB_j = B_jB_i .
	\]
	Therefore $P_iP_j=P_jP_i$, i.e.\ $[P_i,P_j]=0$.

\label{thm:structure_aut_Gn}
	For integers \(n \geq 3\). Let

G_n \cong C_2^{\,r}\oplus C_{2n-4},\qquad r:=n-2,

\operatorname{Aut}(G_n)\cong \mathrm{GL}(r\!+\!1,2)\times (\mathbb{Z}/(2n-4)\mathbb{Z})^\times,
\qquad
|\operatorname{Aut}(G_n)|=|\mathrm{GL}(r\!+\!1,2)|\;\phi(2n-4).
\]

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(b) Even $n$ ($s\ge2$).}
Write $C_{2n-4}\cong C_{2^{s}}\oplus C_m$ and let $e$ be the unique element of order $2$ in $C_{2^{s}}$.
Then
\[
\operatorname{Aut}(G_n)\cong
\underbrace{\operatorname{Hom}(C_{2^{s}},\,C_2^{\,r})}_{\cong\,C_2^{\,r}}
\rtimes
\Big(
\underbrace{\operatorname{Hom}(C_2^{\,r},\langle e\rangle)\rtimes \mathrm{GL}(r,2)}_{\cong\,C_2^{\,r}\rtimes \mathrm{GL}(r,2)}
\times (\mathbb{Z}/(2n-4)\mathbb{Z})^\times
\Big),
\]
and
\[
|\operatorname{Aut}(G_n)|=2^{\,2r}\,|\mathrm{GL}(r,2)|\;\phi(2n-4).
\]

<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Split $C_{2n-4}\cong C_{2^{s}}\oplus C_m$ with $\gcd(2^{s},m)=1$.
No nontrivial homomorphisms exist between the odd part $C_m$ and the $2$-primary part of $G_n$, hence by \citep[Lemma~2.1]{Hillar-Rhea} we have
\[
\operatorname{Aut}(G_n)\cong \operatorname{Aut}\big(G_n^{(2)}\big)\times \operatorname{Aut}(C_m)\cong \operatorname{Aut}\big(G_n^{(2)}\big)\times (\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times.
\]
Thus it suffices to compute $\operatorname{Aut}\big(G_n^{(2)}\big)$.

\smallskip
\emph{Case $s=1$.}
Then $G_n^{(2)}=C_2^{\,r}\oplus C_2\cong C_2^{\,r+1}$, an $(r\!+\!1)$-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_2$-vector space.
Group automorphisms are precisely $\mathbb{F}_2$-linear automorphisms:
$\operatorname{Aut}\big(G_n^{(2)}\big)\cong \mathrm{GL}(r\!+\!1,2)$.
Multiplying by $(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times\cong (\mathbb{Z}/(2n-4)\mathbb{Z})^\times$ gives the desired structure and order.

\smallskip
\emph{Case $s\ge2$.}
Now $G_n^{(2)}=C_2^{\,r}\oplus C_{2^{s}}$.
Let $e\in C_{2^{s}}$ be the unique element of order $2$ (the $2^{s-1}$-st power of a generator).
It is characteristic, hence fixed by any automorphism.
On the $2$-torsion layer
\[
\Omega_1\big(G_n^{(2)}\big)=C_2^{\,r}\oplus \langle e\rangle\cong \mathbb{F}_2^{\,r}\oplus \mathbb{F}_2,
\]
the induced linear action must fix the line $\langle e\rangle$, so the image of the restriction map
$\operatorname{Aut}\big(G_n^{(2)}\big)\twoheadrightarrow \operatorname{Aut}\big(\Omega_1(G_n^{(2)})\big)$
is the stabilizer of $\langle e\rangle$ in $\mathrm{GL}(r\!+\!1,2)$, which is
\[
\operatorname{Stab}(\langle e\rangle)\cong \operatorname{Hom}(C_2^{\,r},\langle e\rangle)\rtimes \mathrm{GL}(r,2)\cong C_2^{\,r}\rtimes \mathrm{GL}(r,2).
\]

<!-- prop-7: body_md -->
\label{prop:isomorphism-btw-Gn-quotient-Ln}
	The group \( G_n \) is isomorphic to \( \mathbb{Z}^n / L_n \).
<!-- end prop-7 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
The lattice \( L_n \subset \mathbb{Z}^n \) is generated by the vectors
	\begin{equation}
	    \begin{cases}
	v_i = 2e_i - \sum_{j=1}^n e_j & (1 \leq i \leq n), \\
	v_{n+1} = (2n - 4)e_n, \\
	v_{i+n+1} = 2e_i - 2H(n-3)e_n & (1 \leq i \leq n-1),
	\end{cases} 
    \label{eqn:s5n1}
	\end{equation}
	where \( H(n-3) \) is the Heaviside step function, \( e_1, \dots, e_n\) are canonical basis for \( \mathbb{Z}^n \). Since \( L_n \) has rank \( n \), we may select \( n \) linearly independent generators, such as \( \{v_1, \dots, v_n\} \). These form the columns of the relation matrix
	\[
	R := 2\boldsymbol{I}_n - Q_n \in M_n(\mathbb{Z}),
	\]
	where \( Q_n \) is the all-ones matrix, and \( \boldsymbol{I}_n\) the \( n \times n \) identity. Explicitly,
	\[
	R = 
	\begin{pmatrix}
	1 & -1 & -1 & \cdots & -1 \\
	-1 & 1 & -1 & \cdots & -1 \\
	-1 & -1 & 1 & \cdots & -1 \\
	\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
	-1 & -1 & -1 & \cdots & 1
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\]
	
	The group is isomorphic to the cokernel of the linear map \( R: \mathbb{Z}^n \to \mathbb{Z}^n \), i.e., \( G_n \cong \mathbb{Z}^n / \operatorname{im}(R) \). To determine the structure of \( \mathbb{Z}^n / L_n \), we compute the Smith normal form (SNF) of \( R \). The invariant factors of \( R \) will reveal the group structure and the index \( [\mathbb{Z}^n : L_n] = |\det(R)| \).
	
	Define the all-ones vector \( \mathbf{1} \) and the vector \( f \) as
	\[
	\mathbf{1} = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i = (1,1,\dots,1)^T, \quad f = \sum_{i=2}^n e_i = (0,1,1,\dots,1)^T \in \mathbb{Z}^n,
	\]
	and consider the unimodular matrices
	\[
	U = \boldsymbol{I}_n + f e_1^T, \quad V = \boldsymbol{I}_n + e_1 f^T.
	\]
	These matrices correspond to sequences of elementary row and column operations: subtracting the first row/column from the others, then adding twice the first row/column to the others. One may verify that \( \det(U) = \det(V) = 1 \), since \( e_1^T f = 0 \).
	
	Now compute the product
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
	URV &= (I + f e_1^T)(2I - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T)(I + e_1 f^T) \\
	&= (I + f e_1^T)\left[2(I + e_1 f^T) - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T(I + e_1 f^T)\right] \\
	&= (I + f e_1^T)\left[2I + 2e_1 f^T - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T - \mathbf{1}(e_1^T \mathbf{1}) f^T\right] \\
	&= (I + f e_1^T)\left[2I + (2e_1 - \mathbf{1})f^T - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T\right].
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Multiplying out and simplifying using the identities \( e_1^T f = 0 \), \( e_1^T \mathbf{1} = 1 \), and \( f^T \mathbf{1} = n-1 \), we obtain
	\[
	URV = 2I - e_1 e_1^T - 2f f^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mathbf{0}^T \\ \mathbf{0} & M' \end{pmatrix},
	\]
	where \( M' = -2(Q_{n-1} - \boldsymbol{I}_{n-1}) \).
	
	Now, the matrix \( K = Q_{n-1} - \boldsymbol{I}_{n-1} \) has eigenvalues \( n-2 \) (with eigenvector \( \mathbf{1} \)) and \( -1 \) (with multiplicity \( n-2 \)). Hence,
	\[
	\det(K) = (n-2)(-1)^{n-2}.
	\]
	Since all entries of \( K \) are 0 or 1, the greatest common divisor of its entries is 1, so the first invariant factor of \( K \) is 1. The invariant factors \( d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_{n-1} \) must satisfy \( d_1 \cdots d_{n-1} = |\det(K)| = n-2 \), and since \( d_1 = 1 \) and $K$ is an integer matrix of full rank $n-1$, it follows that
	\[
	\operatorname{SNF}(K) = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1, \dots, 1, n-2),
	\]
	with the entry \(1\) occurring \( n-2 \) times. Therefore,
	\[
	\operatorname{SNF}(M') = \operatorname{diag}(2, 2, \dots, 2, 2(n-2)),
	\]
	with \( 2 \) repeated \(n - 2\) times, and combining with the leading 1 gives
	\[
	\operatorname{SNF}(R) = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2, 2, \dots, 2, 2(n-2)).
	\]
	
	Thus,
	\[
	\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n \cong \mathbb Z^n /  \operatorname{im}(R) \cong (\mathbb Z/2 \mathbb Z)^{\,n-2}  \oplus \mathbb{Z}/(2(n-2))\mathbb Z,
	\]
	with \( n-2 \) copies of \( \mathbb{Z}/2 \), and
	\[
	|\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n| = |\det(R)| = 1 \cdot 2^{n-2} \cdot 2(n-2) = (n-2)2^{n-1}.
	\]
	This completes the proof.

<!-- prop-8: body_md -->
\label{prop:radical_ILn}
	The lattice ideal \(I_{L_n}\) is radical and zero-dimensional.
<!-- end prop-8 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
The lattice ideal \(I_{L_n} \subset R_n\) is generated by binomials of the form 
	\begin{alignat}{2}
	\label{eq:main}
	f_i = x_i - \prod_{j \neq i} x_j \quad (1 \leq i \leq n), & & \quad f_{n+1} = x_n^{2n-4} - 1, &\quad f_{i+n+1} = x_i^2 - x_n^{2H(n-3)} \quad (1 \leq i \leq n-1).  \end{alignat} Since \(F\) is algebraically closed of characteristic zero, and \(I_{L_n}\) is generated by binomials without monomial factors, it is radical.
	
	We now show that \(I_{L_n} \subset R_n\) is zero-dimensional, i.e., \(\dim_k R_n / I_{L_n} < \infty\). The variety \(V(I_{L_n})\) consists of points \((x_1, \dots, x_n) \in F^n\) satisfying the system
	\[
	x_i = \prod_{j \neq i} x_j \quad (1 \leq i \leq n), \qquad x_n^{2n-4} = 1, \qquad x_i^2 = x_n^{2H(n-3)} \quad (1 \leq i \leq n-1).
	\]
	
	We consider two cases.
	
	\noindent {\itshape Case 1: \( n = 3 \).}
	Here, \(H(0) = 0\), so \( x_i^2 = 1 \) for \( i = 1, 2, 3 \). Hence, \(x_i = \pm 1\). The relations \(x_1 = x_2 x_3\), \(x_2 = x_1 x_3\), and \(x_3 = x_1 x_2\) imply
	\[
	x_1 x_2 x_3 = x_1^2 = x_2^2 = x_3^2 = 1.
	\]
	Thus, the product \(x_1 x_2 x_3 = 1\), and each \(x_i^2 = 1\). The only solutions are
	\[
	(1,1,1), \quad (1,-1,-1), \quad (-1,1,-1), \quad (-1,-1,1),
	\]
	yielding \( |V(I_{L_3})| = 4 \).
	
	\noindent {\itshape Case 2: \( n > 3 \).} 
	Now \( H(n-3) = 1 \), so \( x_i^2 = x_n^2 \) for \( 1 \leq i \leq n-1 \). Thus, \( x_i = \varepsilon_i x_n \) with \( \varepsilon_i = \pm 1 \). Also, \( x_n^{2n-4} = 1 \), so \( x_n \) is a \((2n-4)\)th root of unity.
	
	From \(x_n = \prod_{j=1}^{n-1} x_j\), we substitute \(x_j = \varepsilon_j x_n\) to obtain
	\[
	x_n = \left( \prod_{j=1}^{n-1} \varepsilon_j \right) x_n^{n-1}.
	\]
	Since \(x_n \neq 0\) (as \(x_n^{2n-4}=1\)), divide both sides by \(x_n\)
	\[
	1 = \left( \prod_{j=1}^{n-1} \varepsilon_j \right) x_n^{n-2}.
	\]
	Let \(\varepsilon = \prod_{j=1}^{n-1} \varepsilon_j \in \{\pm 1 \}\). Then \(\varepsilon = x_n^{-(n-2)}\). Note that \(x_n^{-(n-2)}\) is also a \((2n-4)\)th root of unity, and in fact \((x_n^{-(n-2)})^2 = x_n^{-2(n-2)} = 1\) since \(x_n^{2n-4} = 1\). Hence, \(x_n^{-(n-2)} = \pm 1\).
	
	For each \( x_n \) satisfying \( x_n^{2n-4} = 1 \), the condition \( \varepsilon = x_n^{-(n-2)} \) determines the product of the signs \( \varepsilon_1, \ldots, \varepsilon_{n-1} \). There are \( 2^{n-2} \) choices of signs \( (\varepsilon_1, \ldots, \varepsilon_{n-1}) \in \{ \pm 1 \}^{n-1} \) with fixed product \( \varepsilon \). Since there are \( 2n-4 \) choices for \( x_n \), the total number of solutions is
	\[
	|V(I_{L_n})| = (2n-4) \cdot 2^{n-2} = 2^{n-1}(n-2) = c_n - 1.
	\]	
	
	In both cases, \(|V(I_{L_n})| = c_n -1\), as shown in Proposition~\ref{prop:colength}. Since \(V(I_{L_n})\) has finitely many points and \( I_{L_n} \) is radical, we conclude that \(\dim_F R_n / I_{L_n} = |V(I_{L_n})| = c_n -1 < \infty\). Hence, \(I_{L_n}\) is zero-dimensional.

\label{prop:relation_In_ILn}
	Let \(R_n = F[x_1, \dots, x_n]\) and let \(\mathfrak{m} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)\) denote the maximal ideal at the origin.

	I_n \;=\; I_{L_n} \;\cap\;  \mathfrak{m}.

	V(I_n) = V(I_{L_n}) \cup \{0\}.
	\]
	Both ideals are generated by binomials without monomial factors and are shown to be radical and zero-dimensional. Therefore, the ideals of these varieties are intersections of their reduced primary components.

	\[
	I_n = I_{L_n} \cap  \mathfrak{m}.
	\]

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:lattice-algebras}
	Let \(L_n \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^n\) be a lattice generated by the set of vectors in \eqref{eqn:s5n1}.  Let \(I_{L_n} \subset R_n\) be the associated lattice ideal. Then there is a canonical \(F\)-algebra isomorphism
	\[
	R_n / I_{L_n} \cong F[\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n].
	\] In other words, if the associated lattice ideal \(I_{L_n}\) in \(R_n\)  is of finite codimension, then its length is the same as the order of the group \(\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n\). In this case, \[ \text{len} (R_n/I_{L_n}) = | G_n |. \]
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
By Proposition~\ref{prop:isomorphism-btw-Gn-quotient-Ln}, \(G_n \cong \mathbb{Z}^n / L_n\) with independent generators \(h_1, \dots, h_{n-2}\) of order 2 and a generator \( t\) of order \(2n-4\) defined by 
	
	$$
	g_i=h_i\,t\quad(1\le i\le n-2),\qquad g_{n-1}=(h_1\cdots h_{n-2})\,t^{\,n-1},\qquad g_n=t.
	$$ The group algebra $F[\mathbb{Z}^n/L_n]$ is thus the commutative $F$-algebra of dimension \(|\mathbb{Z}^n /L_n| = c_n -1\) generated by $u_{h_1},\dots, u_{h_{n-2}}, u_t$ with the obvious group relations
	
	$$
	u_{h_i}^2=1,\qquad u_t^{\,2n-4}=1,\qquad u_{h_i}u_t=u_t u_{h_i},
	$$
	and all generators commute.
	
	Define a map \(\Phi: R_n \to F[\mathbb{Z}^n/L_n]\) by  
	\[
	\Phi(x_i) = u_{g_i}, \quad \text{for } i = 1, \dots, n,
	\]  
	where \(g_i = \bar{e}_i \in \mathbb{Z}^n / L_n\) is the image of \( e_i \) in \( \mathbb{Z}^n / L_n  \).  Expressed in terms of the chosen generators \(h_j\) and \(t\) the explicit image of each \(x_i\) is 	
	$$
	\Phi(x_i)=u_{h_i}\,u_t \quad(1\le i\le n-2),\qquad \Phi(x_{n-1}) \;=\; u_{h_1}\,u_{h_2}\cdots u_{h_{n-2}}\,u_t^{\,n-1}, \qquad \Phi(x_n)=u_t.
	$$
	
	Extend \(\Phi\) linearly and multiplicatively to all of \(R_n\). This map is a canonical \( F \)-algebra homomorphism by construction: it preserves addition due to linearity, multiplication due to the identity \(u_g u_{g'} = u_{gg'}\) induced by the usual group-algebra convolution, and the unit since \(\Phi(1) = u_1\).
	
	We now verify that \(\Phi\) vanishes on the generators of \(I_{L_n}\), so it descends to a well-defined map \(\overline{\Phi}: R_n / I_{L_n} \to F[\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n]\). 
	
	The defining binomials of $I_{L_n}$ are precisely the relations in the quotient group $\mathbb{Z}^n/L_n$: the relation \(\bar{e}_i = \sum_{j \neq i} \bar{e}_j\) in \(\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n\) implies \(u_{g_i} = \prod_{j \neq i} u_{g_j}\), so \(\Phi(f_i) = 0\); the relation \((2n-4)\bar{e}_n = 0\) implies \(u_t^{2n-4} = 1\), so \(\Phi(f_{n+1}) = 0\); and the relations \(2\bar{e}_i = 2H(n-3)\bar{e}_n\) imply \(u_{g_i}^2 = u_t^{2H(n-3)}\), so \(\Phi(f_{i+n+1}) = 0\). Thus every generator of $I_{L_n}$ maps to zero, so $\Phi(I_{L_n})=0$. Consequently $\Phi$ induces a well-defined homomorphism
	
	$$
	\overline{\Phi}:R_n/I_{L_n}\;\longrightarrow\;F[\mathbb{Z}^n/L_n].
	$$
	
	The induced map is surjective because the \(u_{g_i}\) generate the group algebra. Since \(\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n\) is a finite abelian group, the group algebra \(F[\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n]\) is a finite-dimensional vector space over \(F\). Its dimension is exactly \(|\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n|\). Moreover, because \(I_{L_n}\) is radical and zero dimensional (Proposition~\ref{prop:radical_ILn}), the ring \(R_n/I_{L_n}\) is an Artinian reduced ring, and its length equals its dimension as a \(F\)-vector space. Therefore, \(\overline{\Phi }\) is injective. Hence, \(\overline{\Phi}\) is an isomorphism of \(F\)-algebras, and 
	\[
	\operatorname{len}(R_n / I_{L_n}) = \dim_F (R_n/I_{L_n}) = | \mathbb{Z}^n / L_n | = \dim_F F[\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n] = |G_n|.
	\]

By Theorem~\ref{thm:lattice-algebras}, we have that the lattice ideal \( I_{L_n} \subset R_n = F[x_1,\dots,x_n] \) admits the canonical identification  
	\[
	R_n / I_{L_n} \;\cong\; F[\mathbb{Z}^n / L_n] \;\cong\; F[G_n],
	\]
	where \( G_n \cong \mathbb{Z}^n / L_n \) is the associated finite abelian group. Under this isomorphism, each monomial class in \( R_n / I_{L_n} \) corresponds naturally to a basis element of the group algebra \( F[G_n] \).  
	In particular, the action of multiplication by the variable \( x_j \) is precisely the action of left multiplication by the group element 
	\[
	g_j := \overline{e_j} \in G_n,
	\]
	the image of the \( j \)-th standard basis vector of \( \mathbb{Z}^n \). Hence, the permutation matrix \( P_j \) representing \( m_{x_j} \) is exactly the matrix of the regular representation of \( g_j \) on \( F[G_n] \).
	
	This identification allows the study of \( P_j \) to be reduced to understanding the left regular action of \( g_j \) on the finite group \( G_n \); the orbit structure of this action corresponds precisely to the coset decomposition of \( G_n \) modulo the cyclic subgroup \( \langle g_j \rangle \).
	
	By Proposition~\ref{prop:structure_Gn}, the group \( G_n \) decomposes as \( G_n \;\cong\; C_2^{\,n-2} \times C_{2n-4}\), with commuting generators that may be chosen so that $g_j=h_j\,t$ for $1\le j\le n-1$, where $h_j$ has order $2$ and $t$ has order $2n-4$. Since these components commute, the order of $g_j$ equals the order of the product $h_j t$, i.e., $\operatorname{ord}(g_j)=\operatorname{lcm}(2,2n-4)=2n-4=2(n-2)$. 
	Left multiplication by \( g_j \) thus partitions \( G_n \) into cosets of the cyclic subgroup  \( \langle g_j \rangle \), each forming an orbit of length $\operatorname{ord}(g_j)=2(n-2)$.  
	As \(|G_n| = (n-2)2^{\,n-1}\) (Proposition~\ref{prop:structure_Gn}), the number of distinct cosets, and hence the number of disjoint cycles in the permutation induced by \( g_j \), is
	\[
	\frac{|G_n|}{\operatorname{ord}(g_j)} = \frac{(n-2)2^{\,n-1}}{2(n-2)} = 2^{\,n-2}.
	\]
	Therefore,  \( \sigma_j \) decomposes into exactly \( 2^{n-2} \) cycles each of length \( 2(n-2) \), giving
	\[
	\operatorname{Ord}(P_j) = 2(n-2)
	\quad\text{and}\quad
	\chi_{P_j}(t) = \big(t^{2(n-2)} - 1\big)^{2^{\,n-2}},
	\]
	as claimed.
