Let $\rho=\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}$ be a sequence of density matrices acting on (\ref{la:Hilbert}), such that $\rho_n=\operatorname{tr}_{n'-n} \rho_{n'}$ for all $n'\geq n$. We say that $\rho$ is $k$-exchangeable if $\rho_n$ is invariant under the permutations $\mathrm{S}_{n_1}\times \mathrm{S}_{n_2}\times \dots\times \mathrm{S}_{n_k}$, for all $n$.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:definetti} Let $\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}$ be a $k$-exchangeable sequence of density matrices on the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_1^{\otimes n_1}\otimes \mathcal{H}_2^{\otimes n_2}\otimes \dots \otimes \mathcal{H}_k^{\otimes n_k}$. Then there exists a unique probability measure $\mu$ on vectors of density operators $(\tau_1,\tau_2,\dots,\tau_k)$, where $\tau_i$ is an operator on $\mathcal{H}_i$, such that for all $n$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deFinetti}
    \rho_{n}=\int \tau_1^{\otimes n_1}\otimes \tau_2^{\otimes n_2}\otimes\dots\otimes  \tau_k^{\otimes n_k}\mathrm{d} \mu(\tau_1,\tau_2,\dots,\tau_k)
\end{equation}

Consider the model given by equation (\ref{eq:H1}) with $k\geq 2$ and relative sizes $a_1> a_2\geq \dots \geq a_k$. Let $\beta_\mathrm{1}\in (\frac{2}{a_1},\frac{2}{a_{2}})$ be the solution to: 
            \begin{equation}\label{eq:kpol}
    \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{2-a_i\beta_1}=\frac{k-1}{2}
\end{equation}
Furthermore if $a_1<\sum_{i=2}^k a_i $, define $\beta_2\in (\frac{2}{a_3},\infty)$ by $\Delta y(\beta_2)=0$. Otherwise set $\beta_2=\infty$. 

The magnetisation magnitudes 
$y_i=y_i(\beta)$ as functions of $\beta$ satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}[i]
    \item $y_i(\beta)=0$ for $\beta\leq \beta_1$.
    \item For all $\beta \in (\beta_1,\beta_2) $, $\Delta y >0$ and
    \begin{align}\label{eq:diff0}
    \begin{cases}
        \operatorname{artanh} \frac{y_1}{a_1} &=\frac{\beta}{2}(y_1-\Delta y)\\
     \operatorname{artanh} \frac{y_i}{a_i} &=\frac{\beta}{2} (y_i+\Delta y) ,\quad i\neq 1.
    \end{cases}
\end{align}
    
    In particular, there exist constants                                                                                                                                                  
    $c_i>0$ such that:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:critexp}
y_i(\beta)=c_i\sqrt{\beta-\beta_1}+\mathcal{O}(\beta-\beta_1)^{\frac{3}{2}}
    \end{equation}
    \item For all $\beta\in (\beta_2,\infty)$, $\Delta y <0$ and 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:low}
        \operatorname{artanh} \frac{y_i}{a_i} =\frac{\beta}{2}y_i,\quad \forall i.
    \end{equation}
    In particular if $\beta_2<\frac{2}{a_i}$, define $\beta_i=\frac{2}{a_i}$ and we then have:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crit2}
        y_i(\beta)=a_i\sqrt{\frac{3a_i}{2}}\sqrt{\beta-\beta_i}+\mathcal{O}(\beta-\beta_i)^{\frac{3}{2}},\quad \beta\geq \beta_i
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lemma:minfunc} 
The function $g_{\theta}(x)$ has a unique local minimum given by:
    \begin{equation}
       x_\theta^*:=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{x\in [0,1]} g_\theta(x)=\begin{cases} 0,\quad \theta  \leq 2.\\[0.3em]
        x \, : \, \operatorname{artanh}{x}=\frac{\theta x}{2}>0,\quad \theta  > 2. 
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    Moreover $x_\theta^*$ is increasing in $\theta$ and the critical exponent at $\theta=2$ is $\frac{1}{2}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crit}
x_{\theta}^*=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{\theta-2}+\mathcal{O}(\theta-2)^{\frac{3}{2}},\quad \theta\geq 2
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Differentiate:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tay}
     g_{\theta}'(x)=\operatorname{artanh}(x)-\frac{\theta x}{2}=x\Big(1-\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)+\frac{x^3}{3}+\frac{x^5}{5}+\mathcal{O}(x^7)
\end{equation}
For $\theta  \leq 2$, the derivative is positive for all $x>0$ thus $x_\theta^*=0$. While for $\theta>2$ let $x_{\theta}^*>0$ be the unique positive zero to $g'_\theta(x)=0$. This is indeed the unique minimum since the derivative is negative for $(0,x_\theta^*)$. Notice also that $x_\theta^*$ is strictly increasing for $\theta\geq 2$ since 
$ \frac{\operatorname{artanh} x}{x} $ is strictly increasing in $x$. We obtain (\ref{eq:crit}) by keeping the qubic term in the Taylor expansion (\ref{eq:tay}) and solve for $x$:
$$ 0 = x\Big(1-\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)+\frac{x^3}{3} $$

<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For $\beta\leq \frac{2}{a_1}$ the free energy is minimised term-wise by $x_i=0$, see Lemma \ref{lemma:minfunc}. For the rest of this proof assume that $\beta>\frac{2}{a_1}$. \newline\newline
We will consider the three different cases of $\Delta y= y_1- y_2-\dots-y_k$ being zero, positive and negative. We will see that these are the three phases we are looking for.\newline\newline
First we prove that $\Delta y <0$ implies $y_i=y_{\beta a_i}^*$ for all $i$. 

Notice that $y_1\geq y_i$ because the unique minimisers of $g_{\beta a_i}$ satisfy $y_{\beta a_1}^*> y_{\beta a_i}^*$, see Lemma \ref{lemma:minfunc}. Then $\Delta y <0$ implies that the vectors $\vec{y}_i$ form a polygon. And the free energy takes the form:
$$ f_{\beta}(y)=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^k a_i g_{\beta a_i}(x_i) $$
By definition, $y_i=y_{\beta a_1}^*$ for all $i$.

Secondly we show that the phase $\Delta y<0$ occurs if and only if $a_1<a_2+\dots +a_k$. Furthermore if true, $\Delta y<0$ occurs in an interval $\beta\in (\beta_2,\infty)$ for some $\beta_2\in (\frac{2}{a_3},\infty)$. 

If $a_1\geq a_2+\dots +a_k $, the fact $x_{\beta a_1}^*\geq x^*_{\beta a_i} $ implies that $\Delta y \geq 0 $. If $a_1<a_2+\dots +a_k$, let $\beta$ be sufficiently large. Then $\Delta y <0$ because $x_{\beta a_i}^*\rightarrow 1$ as $\beta\rightarrow\infty$. 

By Lemma \ref{lemma:minfunc}, we know that $\Delta y<0$ is equivalent to: 
$$ \operatorname{artanh} x_1 < \operatorname{artanh}{x_2}+\dots +\operatorname{artanh}{x_k} $$
And $x_2=x_{\beta a_2}^*$ increase faster then $x_1=x_{\beta a_2}^*$ because $x_{\theta}^*$ is concave for $\theta\in [2,\infty)$. This shows that the phase $\Delta y<0$ occurs in an interval. And $\beta_2$ is the unique solution to:
\begin{equation}\label{be2}
    \operatorname{artanh} x_{\beta a_1}^* = \operatorname{artanh}{x_{\beta a_2}^*}+\dots +\operatorname{artanh}{x_{\beta a_k}^*}
\end{equation}
The fact that $\beta_2>\frac{2}{a_3}$ follows from $y_{\beta a_1}^*>y_{\beta a_2}^*$ for $\beta \in (\frac{2}{a_2},\infty)$. Equation (\ref{eq:crit2}) follows directly from equation (\ref{eq:crit}) in Lemma \ref{lemma:minfunc}.
\newline\newline
Now we focus on the positive phase $\Delta y>0$. We prove that $\Delta y >0$ occurs in an interval $\beta\in (\beta_1,\beta_2) $, where $\beta_1$ is given by (\ref{eq:kpol}).

With $\Delta y>0$ the free energy takes the form:
$$  f_\beta(y)= \frac{1}{4}(\Delta y)^2 +\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^k  a_ig_{\beta a_i}(x_i) $$
Differentiate:
\begin{align}\label{eq:diff}
    \begin{cases}
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_1}&=\frac{1}{\beta}\operatorname{artanh} x_1 -\frac{1}{2}(y_1-\Delta y)\\ 
     \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i}&=\frac{1}{\beta}\operatorname{artanh} x_i -\frac{1}{2} (y_i+\Delta y) ,\quad i\neq 1.
     \end{cases}
\end{align}
From (\ref{eq:diff}) we see that $x_i>0$. If not we would have the contradiction $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i}<0$ and $y_i=0$. With $x_i>0$ the derivatives must be zero.
\begin{align}\label{eq:diff3}
\begin{cases}
    \operatorname{artanh} x_1 &=\frac{\beta}{2}(y_1-\Delta y)\\
     \operatorname{artanh} x_i &=\frac{\beta}{2} (y_i+\Delta y) ,\quad i\neq 1.
     \end{cases}
\end{align}
The solution of this system is analytic with respect to $\beta$. To see this, linearise the equation and solve the linear system of equations with Gaussian elimination. The only issue that can occur is division by zero but we know the solution is bounded. 

Let us prove that the phase ends with $\beta_2$. Notice that $x_1$ is increasing and thus non-zero in this phase. If there ever is a $\beta$ such that $\Delta y = 0 $, then from (\ref{eq:diff3}) we see that $y_i=y_{\beta a_i }^*$. From equation (\ref{be2}) we realise that this $\beta$ is precisely $\beta_2$.

Next let us find the beginning of the phase $\beta_1$. If $\Delta y \rightarrow 0$ with $y_1\not\rightarrow 0$ we arrive at $\beta_2$. So try with $y_1\rightarrow 0$. We also have $y_i\rightarrow 0$ since $\Delta y >0$. We can now linearise system (\ref{eq:diff3}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:system}
 \begin{cases}
     y_1=\frac{\beta a_1}{2} (y_1-\Delta y) \vspace{0.3cm} \\ 
      y_i=\frac{\beta a_i}{2}(y_i+\Delta y), \quad i\neq 1.
 \end{cases}
 \end{equation}  
If $\beta$ satisfies (\ref{eq:system}) it is easy to check that (\ref{eq:eq}) holds: 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eq}
    \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{2-a_i\beta}=\frac{k-1}{2}
\end{equation} 
This equation has a unique solution in $\beta\in (\frac{2}{a_1},\frac{2}{a_2})$. But we still need to show that $\beta_1<\frac{2}{a_2}$. We do this by showing that $\Delta y >0$ for $\beta\in (\frac{2}{a_2}-\epsilon,\frac{2}{a_2})$ with $\epsilon$ small enough. 

Firstly show that $x_1>0$ for $\beta \in (\frac{2}{a_2}-\epsilon,\frac{2}{a_2})$. Compare all $y_i=0$ with $y_1=\epsilon$ and $\vec{y}_2=-\vec{y}_1$. Since the vectors cancel each other, only $g_{\beta a_1}(\epsilon)$ and $g_{\beta a_2}(\epsilon)$ needs to be analysed. Use the Taylor expansion of given by (\ref{eq:tay0}).
The difference in free energy is then:
$$ \frac{a_1}{4\beta}\epsilon^2(2-a_1\beta)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^3),\quad \forall \beta\in  (\frac{2}{a_2}-\epsilon,\frac{2}{a_2}) $$
Which is negative for small $\epsilon$. Notice that the contribution from $g_{\beta a_2}(\epsilon)$ is absorbed in $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^3)$.

Secondly show that if $y_1>0$ then $\Delta y>0$ for $\beta\in (\frac{2}{a_1},\frac{2}{a_2}) $. To see this start with $\Delta y=0$ and $y_1,y_i>0$. Decrease $y_i$ with $\epsilon$ to $y_i'=y_i-\epsilon$. The change in free energy is:
$$ f_\beta(y_i-\epsilon)-f_\beta(y_i) = \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)-\epsilon \Big(x_i(1-\frac{\beta a_i}{2})+\frac{x_i^3}{3}\Big) $$
Which is negative for small enough $\epsilon$.

To find equation (\ref{eq:critexp}), let us keep the cubic term in the Taylor expansion in (\ref{eq:diff3}).
\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
        \frac{2}{3}x^3_1=(\beta -\beta_1)(y_1-\Delta y)\\ 
         \frac{2}{3}x^3_i=(\beta -\beta_1)(y_i+\Delta y)
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
We can expand $y_i$ in powers of $(\beta-\beta_1)$. By consistency we must have (\ref{eq:critexp}). \newline\newline
Finally consider the last phase $\Delta y =0$. We know that $\Delta y >0$ in $(\beta_1,\beta_2)$ and $\Delta y<0$ in $(\beta_2,\infty)$. Thus we must have $\Delta y =0$ in $(\frac{2}{a_1},\beta_1]$. From the latest argument we know that $\Delta y =0$ implies $y_1=0$. But then all $y_i=0$. This proves Theorem \ref{thm:phase}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lemma:bounds}
    A model (\ref{eq:H2}) is  frustration free if and only if we have for all $i,j$:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:condlemma}
        \operatorname{tr}(\tau_i\tau_j)=\begin{cases} \lambda_i\cdot \lambda_j, \quad \text{if } u_{ij}<0\\
        \lambda_i \cdot P\lambda_j,\quad \text{if } u_{ij}>0
    \end{cases} \quad \text{and}\quad 
    \operatorname{tr}(\tau_i\tau_j^{\operatorname{T}})=\begin{cases} \lambda_i\cdot \lambda_j, \quad \text{if } v_{ij}<0\\
        \lambda_i \cdot P\lambda_j,\quad \text{if } v_{ij}>0 
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For a frustration free model we know that all $(u_{ij}\operatorname{tr} \tau_i\tau_j+v_{ij}\operatorname{tr}{\tau_i\tau_j}^{\operatorname{T}})$ are minimised with fixed $\lambda_i$. We can rewrite $\operatorname{tr}(\tau_i\tau_j)=\lambda_i \cdot |U_iU_j|^2 \lambda_j $, where $|U_iU_j|^2$ is a doubly stochastic matrix. By Birkoff's theorem and the rearrangement inequality we have the bounds:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bounds}
\begin{cases}
    \lambda_i \cdot P\lambda_j\leq \operatorname{tr} \tau_i \tau_j\leq \lambda_i \cdot \lambda_j\\
    \lambda_i \cdot P\lambda_j\leq \operatorname{tr} \tau_i \tau_j^{\operatorname{T}} \leq \lambda_i \cdot \lambda_j
\end{cases}   
\end{equation}
We claim that any pair of equalities can be attained simultaneously. The upper equalities can be attained by $U_i,U_j=I$. The lower equalities can be attained by $U_i=P$ and $U_j=I$. To get $\operatorname{tr} \tau_i\tau_j=\lambda_i\cdot \lambda_j$ and $\operatorname{tr} \tau_i\tau_j^{\operatorname{T}}=\lambda_i\cdot P\lambda_j$, take $U_i,U_j=Z$. And to get $\operatorname{tr} \tau_i\tau_j=\lambda_i\cdot P\lambda_j$ and $\operatorname{tr} \tau_i\tau_j^{\operatorname{T}}=\lambda_i\cdot \lambda_j$, take $U_i=Z$ and $U_j=\overline{Z}$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lemma:contra}
    If a model (\ref{eq:H2}) is  frustration free and cannot be partitioned into $V_1$ and $V_2$ as in Theorem \ref{thm:graph}, then, for large enough $\beta$, there exists a positive integer $l\leq \lceil s\rceil $, such that all $\lambda_i$ have precisely $l$ largest values and $l$ smallest values.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By Lemma \ref{lemma:bounds}, the free energy $f_\beta$ in equation (\ref{eq:freeO}) can be written in terms of $\lambda_i\cdot P\lambda_j$ and $\lambda_i\cdot \lambda_j$. Split $u_{ij}$ and $v_{ij}$ into positive and negative part: $u_{ij}=u_{ij}^++u_{ij}^-$ and $v_{ij}=v_{ij}^++v_{ij}^-$. Also denote $w^{\pm}_{ij}=a_ia_j(u_{ij}^{\pm} +v_{ij}^{\pm})$. Then for frustration free models:
\begin{equation}
     f_\beta(\lambda)= \sum_{i\neq j }^k\lambda_i\cdot\big(w_{ij}^+ P  +w_{ij}^-  \big)\lambda_j+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^k a_i\lambda_i\cdot\ln \lambda_i
\end{equation} 
Next let us show two properties of how repeated eigenvalues $\lambda_i=(\lambda_{i,-s},\dots,\lambda_{i,m},\dots,\lambda_{i,s})$ transfers to other vectors $\lambda_j$.

\textbf{Claim 1.} 
\textit{If $w_{ij}^-<0$ we have: 
$$ \lambda_{i,m}=\lambda_{i,m+1} \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \lambda_{j,m}=\lambda_{j,m+1}$$
and if $w_{ij}^+>0$ we have:}
$$ \lambda_{i,m}=\lambda_{i,m+1} \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \lambda_{j,-m}=\lambda_{j,-m-1} $$

\begin{Proof}
Consider $w_{ij}^+>0$ and $m=s$, the other cases are done similarly. Suppose the opposite that $\lambda_i=(\dots,y,z)$ where $y<z$ and $\lambda_j=(x,x,\dots)$. Firstly, $x>0$ for all $\beta<\infty$, since the derivative of the entropy is infinite at $x=0$. Compare the free energy of $\lambda_j$ with $(x-\epsilon,x+\epsilon,\dots)$ for some small $\epsilon>0$. Then the energy in the interaction $w_{ij}^+\lambda_i \cdot P \lambda_j$ is changed by $\epsilon w_{ij}^+(y-z)<0$. The energy in all other interactions is either unchanged or lowered since $(w_{ij}^+P+w_{ij}^-)\lambda_j$ is decreasing. The entropy is decreased by $\frac{a_j}{\beta}\frac{1}{x}\epsilon^2+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^4)$. The total change in free energy is:
$$ f_\beta(x-\epsilon,x+\epsilon)-f_\beta(x,x)\leq \epsilon w_{ij}^+(y-z)+\frac{a_j}{\beta}\frac{1}{x}\epsilon^2+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^4) $$
Which is negative for small enough $\epsilon$, which is a contradiction.
\end{Proof}
\textbf{Claim 2.}\textit{
    If $\lambda_i$ has precisely $l$ smallest and largest values, for some $i$, then that property is true for all $i=1,2,\dots,k$.}
    \begin{Proof}
    If $\lambda_i$ has precisely $l$ smallest values, then, by Claim 1, all neighbours $j$ with $w_{ij}^-<0$ also have $l$ smallest values while all neighbours with $w_{ij}^+>0$ have $l$ largest values. If $\lambda_i$ has both $l$ smallest and $l$ largest, this property transfers to all neighbours. And since the graph is connected this property must transfer to all $1,2,3,\dots,k$.
\end{Proof}
Consider the set $S$ of needles $i$ such that $w_{ij}^-<0$ for some $j$. In the limit $\beta\rightarrow\infty$ the free energy approaches $\sum_{i\neq j} w_{ij}^-<0$, which can only happen if $\lambda_i$ has a unique largest value for all $i\in S$.

Try to colour every needle in $V$ black or white such that: all needles in $S$ are white and if $w_{ij}^+>0$ then $i$ and $j$ have different colours. 

If such a colouring exists then let $V_1$ be the white needles and $V_2$ be the black needles. Then the weights in $E_1,E_2$ is $(--)$ and the weights in $E_{12}$ is $(++)$. This is a partition allowed by Theorem \ref{thm:graph}, and thus a contradiction. 

Hence such a colouring does not exists. If $S$ is non-empty, then there exists a path with $w_{ij}^+>0$ of odd steps from $S$ to $S$. Using Claim 1 on this path implies that some $i\in S$ have a unique smallest value as well. By Claim 2, all needles  $i\in V$ have a unique largest and smallest value. So here $l=1$.\newline \newline
If instead $S$ is empty and there is no colouring, there must exists an odd cycle with $w_{ij}^+>0$. By Claim 1, any $\lambda_i$ on this cycle must be on the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:form}
    \lambda_i=(\underbrace{b_i,\dots,b_i}_{l},c_i,\dots,d_i,\underbrace{e_i,\dots,e_i}_{l})
\end{equation}
with $b_i<c_i\leq d_i<e_i$ and $1\leq l \leq \lceil s \rceil $ or all $\lambda_i$ on this cycle is on the form $\lambda_i=(\frac{1}{2s+1},\dots,\frac{1}{2s+1})$. We can exclude the second case for large enough $\beta$, since then free energy would approach $\frac{1}{2s+1}\sum_{i\neq j}w_{ij}^+$ as $\beta\rightarrow\infty$, but we know the correct answer is $f_\beta\rightarrow 0$. By Claim 2, all $i=1,2,\dots,k$ are on the form (\ref{eq:form}) for large $\beta$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lemma:cond}
    If a model (\ref{eq:H2}) is frustration free and cannot be partitioned into $V_1$
and $V_2$ as in Theorem \ref{thm:graph}, then, for large enough $\beta$, there exists an integer $l$ and unitary matrices $U_i$ in (\ref{eq:freeO}) such that for all $i,j$:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cond}
    \begin{cases}
        U_i\sim_l   U_j,\quad \text{if }u_{ij}<0,\\
        U_i\sim_l PU_j \quad \text{if }u_{ij}>0.
    \end{cases} \quad \text{and}\quad \begin{cases}
        U_i\sim_l \overline{U}_j,\quad \text{if }v_{ij}<0,\\
        U_i\sim_l P\overline{U}_j \quad \text{if }v_{ij}>0.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Assume $\beta$ is large enough so we can define $l$ as in Lemma \ref{lemma:contra}. We assume a frustration free model, so we have conditions (\ref{eq:condlemma}) in Lemma \ref{lemma:bounds}. 

If $u_{ij}<0$ then $ \operatorname{tr} \tau_i\tau_j = \lambda_i\cdot\lambda_j $. Rewrite this equation:
$ \lambda_i |U_i U_j^\dagger|^2\lambda_j=\lambda_i\cdot\lambda_j $. By the rearrangement inequality, the $l$ largest and smallest values in $\lambda_j$ must stay in their positions after the mixture of permutations $|U_i U_j^\dagger|^2$ is applied to $\lambda_j$. This can only happen if $U_i\sim_l U_j$.

If instead $u_{ij}>0$ then $ \lambda_i |U_i U_j^\dagger|^2\lambda_j = \lambda_i\cdot P\lambda_j $. By the rearrangement inequality, the $l$ largest and smallest values in $\lambda_j$ must switch with each other after the mixture of permutations $|U_i U_j^\dagger|^2$ is applied to $\lambda_j$. This can only happen if $U_i\sim_l PU_j$.

Similar analysis for $v_{ij}$ leads to (\ref{eq:cond}). The only difference is that $|U_iU_j^\dagger|^2$ is replaced by $|U_iU_j^{\operatorname{T}}|^2$.

We start by showing that frustration free and not having the partition $V_1\sqcup V_2$ as in Theorem \ref{thm:graph} is impossible. Assume conditions in Lemma \ref{lemma:cond} so (\ref{eq:cond}) hold. 

Let us denote $A=U_1$ for clarity. Since our graph $G=(V,E)$ is connected there is a path from 1 to any $i\in V$. Using condition (\ref{eq:cond}) on this path results in some sequence of multiplication with $P$ and complex conjugation. For instance say $u_{12}>0$ and $v_{23}>0$ then: $U_1\sim_l PU_2\sim_l P(P\overline{U_3})=\overline{U}_3$. And in general, since multiplication by $P$ and complex conjugations are involutions that commute, there are only four possible equivalence classes:
$$ U_i\sim_l \,A,\, PA,\, \overline{A} \,\text{ or }\, P\overline{A}, \quad \forall i\in V $$
Let us divide into three cases: $A\sim_l \overline{A}$ or $ A\sim_lP\overline{A}$ or neither.

If $A\sim_l \overline{A}$, then there are only two equivalence classes $A$ and $PA$. Notice that $A\sim_l \overline{A}$ implies $\overline{U}_i\sim_l U_i$. In other words, condition (\ref{eq:cond}) is the same for $u_{ij}$ and $v_{ij}$. If $U_i\sim_l U_j $ then $u_{ij},v_{ij}\leq 0$. And if $ U_i\not  \sim_l U_j $ then $u_{ij},v_{ij}\geq 0$. Our graph can be partitioned into $V_1$ and $V_2$ being the two equivalence classes. The corresponding weights are $(--)$ in $E_1\sqcup E_1$ and $(++)$ in $E_{12}$.

If instead $A\sim_l P\overline{A}$, then the only equivalence classes are $A$ and $\overline{A}$. Notice that $A\sim_l P\overline{A}$ implies $\overline{U}_i\sim_l PU_i$. In other words, condition (\ref{eq:cond}) is the opposite for $u_{ij}$ and $v_{ij}$. If $U_i\sim_l U_j$ then $u_{ij}\leq 0$ while $v_{ij}\geq 0$. And if $U_i\not\sim_l U_j$ then $u_{ij}\geq 0$ while $v_{ij}\leq 0$. Our graph can be partitioned into $V_1$ and $V_2$ being the two equivalence classes. The corresponding weights are $(-+)$ in $E_1\sqcup E_1$ and $(+-)$ in $E_{12}$. \vspace{0.2cm}

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lemmaB}
    $\nu$-a.e. $A,A'\in \mathcal{A}$ are positive semidefinite.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
We focus on $A$ here ($A'$ can be done in a similar fashion). Take the partial trace of $\rho$ eliminating $A'^{\otimes N'}$. Notice that the partial trace of a density operator is still a density operator.
$$ \tilde{\rho}=\operatorname{tr}_{N'}\rho =\int  A^{\otimes N}d\nu(A,A') $$
Define a compact set where $A$ is not positive semidefinite.
$$ \mathcal{A}_\epsilon = \{ (A,A')\in \mathcal{A} : \min_{\|\psi\|=1} \langle \psi |A|\psi \rangle   \leq -\epsilon \}   $$
The compactness follows from $\mathcal{A}$ being bounded. Assume for the sake of contradiction $\nu(\mathcal{A}_\epsilon) > 0$ for some $\epsilon>0$. Around every point $(A_0,A_0')\in \mathcal{A}_\epsilon$ we can pick a minimiser $|\psi_0\rangle$ and by continuity we can find some small open ball $\mathcal{B}_0\subset \mathcal{A}$ around $A_0$ where 
$$ \langle \psi_0 |B|\psi_0 \rangle   \leq -\frac{\epsilon}{2},\quad \forall (B,B')\in \mathcal{B}_0. $$
This family of balls is an open cover of the compact set $\mathcal{B}_\epsilon$ so there exists a finite subcover and hence a ball $B_0$ with positive measure $\nu(\mathcal{B}_0)>0$. Define the projection operator: $\Pi_0=1-|\psi_0\rangle\langle \psi_0| $. Let $N$ be even. Apply $\Pi_0^{\otimes N}$ on $\tilde{\rho}$ and take the trace:
$$ \operatorname{tr} (\tilde{\rho}\Pi_0^{\otimes N} )\geq \int_{\mathcal{B}_0} \mathrm{d} \nu \, (1+\frac{\epsilon}{2})^{ N} + \int_{\mathcal{A}\setminus \mathcal{B}_0}\mathrm{d} \nu \,\operatorname{tr} (A\Pi_0)^{ N}\geq   \nu(\mathcal{B}_0)(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2})^{ N}  $$
This leads to a contradiction as $N\rightarrow\infty$, since the LHS $\leq 1$.
