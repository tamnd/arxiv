<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
There is a free immersion $F \colon P^3 \times S^1 \to \mathbf{R}^{14}$ of the form
\begin{align}
F(g, z) = R(g) \cdot v(z),
\end{align}
for some homomorphism $R \colon \mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathrm{GL}_{14}(\mathbf{R})$ and smooth map $v \colon S^1 \to \mathbf{R}^{14}$, once we identify $P^3$ with $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.

In fact, there is a five-parameter family of such maps $F$.
For any fixed choice of parameters, the determinant of the osculating matrix is constant in $(g, z) \in \mathrm{SO}(3) \times S^1$.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:main}
The analogue of \Cref{thm:main} holds with $S^3$ and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ in place of $P^3$ and $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.
<!-- end cor-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Via the quotient map $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- ex-1: body_md -->
\label{ex:r-mod-z}
Let $C = \mathbf{R}/2\pi \mathbf{Z}$.
For each integer $w$, there is a complex irrep $\psi_w \colon C \to \mathrm{GL}_1(\mathbf{C})$ given by 
\begin{align} 
\psi_w(x) \vcentcolon= \exp (\sqrt{-1}wx),
\end{align} 
called the irrep of \textcolor{sapphire}{\emph{winding number}} or \textcolor{sapphire}{\emph{weight}} $w$.
Note that $\psi_1$ identifies $C$ with $\mathrm{U}(1)$.
Up to isomorphism, these are the only complex irreps.
<!-- end ex-1 -->
<!-- ex-2: body_md -->
\label{ex:torus}
Every complex irrep of the $d$-fold power $C^d$ is a $d$-fold tensor product of complex irreps of $C$.
So by \Cref{ex:r-mod-z}, the complex irreps are again $1$-dimensional, but now classified by integer $d$-tuples $\vec{w} \in \mathbf{Z}^d$, still called their \textcolor{sapphire}{\emph{weights}}.
Explicitly, the complex irrep $\psi_{\vec{w}} \colon C^d \to \mathrm{GL}_1(\mathbf{C})$ is given by
\begin{align}
\psi_{\vec{w}}(\vec{x}) = \exp(\sqrt{-1}\vec{w} \cdot \vec{x}),
\end{align}
where $- \cdot -$ denotes the dot product.

For a complex representation of $C^d$ to be the complexification of a real one, it must decompose into irreps so that the resulting multiset of weights is stable under $\vec{w} \mapsto -\vec{w}$.
In particular, $\psi_{\vec{0}}$ complexifies the trivial real irrep, which we will denote by $\varphi_{\vec{0}}$, while for each weight $\vec{w} \neq \vec{0}$, the sum $\psi_{\vec{w}} \oplus \psi_{-\vec{w}}$ complexifies a real irrep $\varphi_{\vec{w}} \colon C^d \to \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbf{R})$.
Explicitly,
\begin{align} 
\varphi_{\vec{w}}(\vec{x}) \vcentcolon=
	\left(\begin{matrix}
		\cos \vec{w} \cdot \vec{x}
		& -{\sin \vec{w} \cdot \vec{x}}\\ 
		\sin \vec{w} \cdot \vec{x}
		& \cos \vec{w} \cdot \vec{x}
	\end{matrix}\right).
\end{align} 
Note that $\varphi_{\vec{w}}$ and $\varphi_{-\vec{w}}$ are conjugate, hence isomorphic, for any $\vec{w}$.
Up to isomorphism, the $\varphi_{\vec{w}}$ for $\vec{w} \in \mathbf{Z}^d/\langle \pm 1\rangle$ are the only real irreps.
Finally, note that in the $d = 1$ case, $\varphi_1$ identifies $C$ with $\mathrm{SO}(2)$.
<!-- end ex-2 -->
<!-- ex-3: body_md -->
\label{ex:su-2}
For each integer $k \geq 0$, there is a complex irrep \begin{align} 
\sigma_k \colon \mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{GL}(W_k),
\end{align} 
where $W_k$ is the $(k + 1)$-dimensional vector space of homogeneous polynomial functions on $\mathbf{C}^2$ of degree $k$.
Here, $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ acts on $W_k$ by pullback of functions along its action on $\mathbf{C}^2$.
We say that $\sigma_k$ has \textcolor{sapphire}{\emph{weight}} $k$.
Up to isomorphism, these are the only complex irreps.

For $\sigma_k$ to be the complexification of a real irrep, $k$ must be even.
In this case, $-I_2 \in \mathrm{SU}(2)$ acts trivially, so $\sigma_k$ factors through the quotient map $\varpi \colon \mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$ from \Cref{subsec:coords}.
Up to isomorphism, the real irreps of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ arising in this way are the only real irreps.
<!-- end ex-3 -->
<!-- ex-4: body_md -->
\label{ex:so-3}
Every complex irrep of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ arises from some complex irrep of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ of even weight.
That is, in the notation of \Cref{ex:su-2}, every complex irrep of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ takes the form $\bar{\sigma}_k \colon \mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathrm{GL}(W_k)$ for some even $k \geq 0$, in which case it is characterized by $\sigma_k = \bar{\sigma}_k \circ \varpi$.

Write $k = 2\ell$.
Then $\sigma_k$ complexifies an irrep of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ on some $(2\ell + 1)$-dimensional real vector space $V_\ell$.
Therefore, $\bar{\sigma}_k$ complexifies an irrep of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ of the form
\begin{align} 
\rho_\ell \colon \mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathrm{GL}(V_\ell).
\end{align} 
Explicitly, one may take $V_\ell$ to be the vector space of homogeneous, degree-$\ell$ harmonic polynomial functions on $\mathbf{R}^3$.
(The restrictions of these functions to $S^2$ are known as \textcolor{sapphire}{\emph{spherical harmonics}}.)
Up to isomorphism, the $\rho_\ell$ are the only real irreps of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.
<!-- end ex-4 -->

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:partials}
\begin{enumerate} 
\item 	$G \xrightarrow{R} \mathrm{GL}(V) \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is a free immersion if and only if the list of matrices
\begin{align}\label{eq:list-r}
	X_\alpha,\quad
	X_\alpha X_\beta
\end{align} 
is linearly independent.

\item 	$F$ is a free immersion if and only if the list of vectors 
\begin{align}\label{eq:list-f}
v',\quad 
v'',\quad 
X_\alpha \cdot v,\quad
X_\alpha \cdot v',\quad 
X_\alpha X_\beta \cdot v
\end{align} 
is linearly independent for all $z \in S^1$.

\end{enumerate}
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- cor-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:partials}
If $F$ is a free immersion, then $G \xrightarrow{R} \mathrm{GL}(V) \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is a free immersion.
<!-- end cor-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Immediate from comparing \eqref{eq:list-r} and \eqref{eq:list-f}.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- cor-3: body_md -->
\label{cor:dim-2}
If $G$ is compact, $F$ is a free immersion, and $\dim V = q_{d + 1}$, then the $G$-invariant subspace $V^G \subseteq V$ has dimension at most $2$.
<!-- end cor-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Since $G$ is compact, we can pick an invariant projection $V \to V^G$.
The operators $X_\alpha$ act by zero on $V^G$, so in the list of vectors \eqref{eq:list-f}, only $v'$ and $v''$ can possibly have nonzero projections to $V^G$.
But if $\dim V = q_{d + 1}$ and \eqref{eq:list-f} is linearly independent, then \eqref{eq:list-f} actually forms a basis for $V$.
Then the projections of $v'$ and $v''$ to $V^G$ must span $V^G$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- ex-6: body_md -->
\label{ex:quadratic}
In the notation of \Cref{ex:torus}, take $G = C^d$ and $V = \mathbf{R}^q$ and 
\begin{align} 
R = \varphi_{\vec{w}^{(1)}} \oplus \cdots \oplus \varphi_{\vec{w}^{(r)}} \oplus \varphi_{\vec{0}}^{\oplus s},
\end{align} 
for some $r, s$ such that $q = 2r + s$ and some weights $\vec{w}^{(i)} = (w_1^{(i)}, \ldots, w_d^{(i)}) \in \mathbf{Z}^d$.
                                                        
Taking $\xi_\alpha = \partial_{x_\alpha}$ for $1 \leq \alpha \leq d$, we compute
\begin{align}\label{eq:de-leo-x}
X_\alpha = w^{(1)}_\alpha J \oplus \cdots \oplus w^{(r)}_\alpha J \oplus 0_s,
	\quad\text{where $J = \left(\begin{matrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{matrix}\right)$},
\end{align}
from which
\begin{align}
X_\alpha X_\beta = -w_\alpha^{(1)}w_\beta^{(1)} I_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus -w_\alpha^{(r)}w_\beta^{(r)} I_2 \oplus 0_s.
\end{align} 
(Above, $0_s$ is the $s \times s$ zero matrix.)

We deduce that for the $X_\alpha X_\beta$ to be linearly independent, it is necessary that whenever a (real) quadratic $k$-form $Q(\vec{x}) = \sum_{\alpha, \beta} c_{\alpha\beta} x_\alpha x_\beta$ satisfies $Q(\vec{w}^{(1)}) = \cdots = Q(\vec{w}^{(r)}) = 0$, we must have $c_{\alpha\beta}= 0$ for all $\alpha, \beta$.
In other words, for $C^d \xrightarrow{R} \mathrm{GL}(V) \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ to be free, the evaluation map from the vector space of quadratic $d$-forms into $\mathbf{R}^r$ that sends
\begin{align}\label{eq:quadratic}
	Q \mapsto (Q(\vec{w}^{(1)}), \ldots, Q(\vec{w}^{(d)}))
\end{align}
must be injective.
In particular, we must have 
\begin{align} 
\binom{d + 1}{2} \leq r \leq \lfloor q/2\rfloor.
\end{align}
When $q = q_{d + 1}$, this inequality forces $d \leq 4$.

By \Cref{cor:partials}, the discussion above refines the discussion in~\cite[\S{2.5}]{deleo}, which showed directly that a map $F \colon (S^1)^m \to \mathbf{R}^{q_m}$ of the form \eqref{eq:factorization}, via $C \simeq S^1$, can be free only if $m \leq 5$.
<!-- end ex-6 -->
<!-- rem-1: body_md -->
In the setup of \Cref{ex:quadratic}, the freeness of $C^d \xrightarrow{R} \mathrm{GL}(V) \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ also forces the weights $\vec{w}^{(r)}, \ldots, \vec{w}^{(r)}$ to be linearly independent as vectors in $\mathbf{R}^d$.
For otherwise, they all vanish on some nonzero linear functional $\lambda$ on $\mathbf{R}^d$.
But then the symmetric square of $\lambda$ is a nonzero quadratic form that vanishes on each weight.

Note that the linear independence of the weights is a tighter constraint than the linear independence of the $X_\alpha$, due to \eqref{eq:de-leo-x} and the bound $\binom{d + 1}{2} \leq r$.
<!-- end rem-1 -->
<!-- ex-7: body_md -->
\label{ex:veronese-so-3}
Take $G = \mathrm{SO}(3)$.
In the notation of \Cref{ex:so-3}, take $V = V_1$ and $R = \rho_1$.
Here, one can check that the nine matrices $X_\alpha, X_\alpha X_\beta$ are indeed linearly independent, so $\rho_1$ gives rise to a free immersion 
\begin{align}
\mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathfrak{gl}(V_1) \simeq \mathbf{R}^9.
\end{align}
Since $q_3 = 9$, this is a free immersion in critical dimension.

In fact, after identifying $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ with $P^3$, one can check that this map is just a rescaled version of the free immersion of $P^3$ into $\mathbf{R}^9$ that arises from the Veronese embedding of $P^3$ into $P^9$, discussed in the introduction.
<!-- end ex-7 -->
<!-- rem-2: body_md -->
In the notation of \S\ref{subsec:rep-coords}, our choices for $v_2$ and $v_3$ are
\begin{align}
v_2(z) &= a\mathsf{v}_0^2 + \alpha ((\cos z) \mathsf{u}_2^2 + (\sin z) \mathsf{v}_2^2),\\
v_3(z) &= b\mathsf{v}_0^2 + \beta ((\cos z) \mathsf{u}_1^3 + (\sin z) \mathsf{v}_1^3).
\end{align}
There are other choices that would suffice for \Cref{thm:main}, replacing the pair of subscripts $2$ and $1$ by another pair of distinct subscripts $j_2 \in \{1,2\}$ and $j_3 \in \{1,2,3\}$.
In this generality, one checks that
\begin{align}\label{eq:x-3}
X_3 v_2 = j_2 v'_2,\qquad 
X_3 v_3 &= j_3 v'_3.
\end{align} 
These identities show why $j_2$ and $j_3$ must be distinct.
For if they are the same integer $j$, then $X_\alpha v' = j X_\alpha X_3 v$ for each $\alpha$, violating linear independence.

Let us give a conceptual explanation of \eqref{eq:x-3}.
Let $\iota \colon C \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$ be the homomorphism $\iota(z) = \exp(z \xi_3)$.
In the notation of \Cref{ex:torus}, we see that for any integers $\ell \geq k \geq 1$, the vectors $\mathsf{u}_k^\ell$ and $\mathsf{v}_k^\ell$ span an irreducible summand of 
\begin{align}
\rho_\ell \circ \iota \colon C \to \mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathrm{GL}(V_\ell)
\end{align}
of weight $k$.
Therefore, $X_3 = d\rho_\ell(\xi_3)$ acts on $\langle\mathsf{u}_k^\ell, \mathsf{v}_k^\ell\rangle$ by $kJ$, where $J$ is defined as in \eqref{eq:de-leo-x}.
Meanwhile, from \S\ref{subsec:rep-coords}, we know that $X_3$ sends $\mathsf{v}_0^\ell$ to zero.
Taking $(\ell, k) = (2, j_2), (3, j_3)$, we arrive at \eqref{eq:x-3}.

<!-- rem-3: body_md -->
The calculation of the $14 \times 14$ osculating determinant can be simplified through several observations.
First, it immediately decomposes as the product of a $12 \times 12$ determinant and the $2 \times 2$ determinant 
\begin{align} 
\det \left(\begin{matrix}-\gamma s &-\gamma c\\ \gamma c &-\gamma s\end{matrix}\right) = \gamma^2(c^2 + s^2) = \gamma^2.
\end{align}
The $12 \times 12$ determinant involves rows $11$ and $12$ of the original $14 \times 14$ matrix.
These rows only contain nonzero entries in columns $9$, $10$, and $12$.
So the $12 \times 12$ determinant becomes a sum of three $10 \times 10$ determinants.
Finally, the expansions of the latter involve many $2 \times 2$ determinants that simplify to scalar multiples of $c^2 + s^2 = 1$.

This does not completely explain why the osculating determinant is constant, as the original matrix includes several entries that are linear in $\sin z$ and survive into the $10\times 10$ determinants.
It is remarkable that all of these extra copies of $\sin z$ cancel in the end.
It would be pleasant to have a purely conceptual explanation for the constancy of the determinant.
