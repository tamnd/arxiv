<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:deloc}
 For any fixed $\kappa > 0$ and $D > 0$ there exists a constant $C \equiv C(D, \kappa) > 0$ such that the following holds. Suppose that $C (\log N)^{2} \leqslant Np \leqslant N^{1-\kappa}$. Then, for any deterministic $\bm v \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and any normalized eigenvector $\bm u$ of $H$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ satisfying $\lvert \lambda \rvert \leqslant 2 - \kappa$, we have
	\begin{equation} \label{deloc_main}
\langle \bm v, \bm u \rangle^2 \leqslant \frac{C \log N}{N} \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e}^2
	\end{equation}
	with probability at least $1 - N^{-D}$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:GOE}
Fix $E \in (-2,2)$ and $\kappa > 0$. Suppose that $(\log N)^{2+\kappa} \leqslant Np \leqslant N^{1-\kappa}$. Then, as $N \to \infty$, $\mu^E$ converges in distribution, with respect to the vague topology, to the $\mathrm{Sine}_1$ point process.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:deloc_optimal}
As explained before \eqref{optimal_deloc}, our delocalization bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:deloc} is optimal up to the precise dependence of $C$ on $D$. In the direction of $\bm e$, we obtain the improved delocalization bound
\begin{equation*}
\langle \bm e, \bm u \rangle^2 \leqslant \frac{1}{Np} \frac{C \log N}{N}\,.
\end{equation*}
Such an improvement by a factor $\frac{1}{Np}$ in the $\bm e$-direction was first observed in \cite[Corollary 1.5]{he2026extremal} in the regime $Np \geqslant N^c$. It stems from the near-alignement of the top eigenvector $\bm u_1$ with $\bm e$, constraining all other eigenvectors to lie in the (deterministic) orthogonal complement of $\bm e$. In \cite[Theorem 6.2]{EKYY1}, it was shown\relax{This result is proved for $Np \gg (\log N)^6$, but we expect it and its proof to carry over to the sparser regime of the current paper.} that the top eigenvector $\bm u_1$ of $H$ satisfies $\langle\bm e \mspace{2mu}, \bm u_1\rangle^2 = 1 - \frac{1 + o(1)}{Np}$, which implies that the squared projection of any other eigenvector in the direction of $\bm e$ is suppressed by the factor $\frac{1}{Np}$.  In our estimate \eqref{deloc_main}, this phenomenon is captured by the non-isotropic norm \eqref{eq:normdef}.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:digraphs}
Our method used to prove Theorem \ref{thm:deloc} also applies to directed Erd\H{o}s-Rényi graphs.  Define
	\begin{equation*}
\widehat{H} \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \theta \widehat{A}\,,
	\end{equation*}
where $\widehat{A}$ has i.i.d.~off-diagonal matrix elements. That is, $(\widehat{A}_{xy} : x \neq y)$ is a family of i.i.d.\ $\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)$ random variables, and $\widehat{A}_{xx} = 0$ for all $x$. It is well known that the asymptotic spectrum of $\widehat A$ is the unit disk in $\mathbb{C}$ (the circular law).  Under the conditions of Theorem \ref{thm:deloc}, for any normalized left-eigenvector $\bm l$ and right-eigenvector $\bm r$ of $\widehat H$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ satisfying $\lvert \lambda \rvert \leqslant 1 - \kappa$, we obtain
\begin{equation} \label{eq:directed_deloc}
\lvert \langle \bm v, \bm l \rangle \rvert^2 + \lvert \langle \bm v, \bm r \rangle \rvert^2 \leqslant \frac{C \log N}{N} \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e}^2
\end{equation}
	with probability at least $1 - N^{-D}$.  We refer to Remark \ref{rem:digraphs_proof} below for a proof.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:p_range}
The upper bound $Np \leqslant N^{1-\kappa}$ is made for convenience, and it can be relaxed without fundamental changes to our argument. We impose this bound to avoid extraneous complications, bearing in mind that the main focus of this paper is the sparse regime. While the optimal delocalization results from Theorem \ref{thm:deloc} and Remark \ref{rem:digraphs} are new as soon as $Np \ll 1$, the bulk universality result from Theorem \ref{thm:GOE} is new only in the very sparse regime $(\log N)^{2 + \kappa} \leqslant Np \leqslant N^{o(1)}$, since the case $Np \geqslant N^{o(1)}$ was previously treated in \cite{huang2015bulk}.

The fundamental assumptions on $p$ in Theorems \ref{thm:deloc} and \ref{thm:GOE} are the lower bounds. The lower bound on $p$ in Theorem \ref{thm:deloc} is an immediate consequence of the same lower bound in the local law, Theorem \ref{thm:lolaw}, of which Theorem \ref{thm:deloc} is an immediate consequence. Interestingly, although the two lower bounds, $C (\log N)^2$ and $(\log N)^{2 + \kappa}$ respectively, are almost identical, and although the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:GOE} relies on Theorem \ref{thm:lolaw}, the origins of these restrictions are very different.

The assumption $Np \geqslant C (\log N)^2$ in Theorems \ref{thm:deloc} and \ref{thm:lolaw} is required in many crucial steps of our proof, the most central of which is in the estimate of the Dynkin martingale in terms of its quadratic variation, where this condition is required to ensure that the jumps of the martingale are sufficiently small. Unlike in the classical Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality for Brownian motion, which always yields subgaussian tails, for Markov jump processes the tails can be subgaussian or subexponential, depending on the jump sizes and how far one goes into the tail. We refer to Remark \ref{rem:lowerbound_p} below for more details.

The assumption $(\log N)^{2 + \kappa}$ for Theorem \ref{thm:GOE} is unrelated to the analogous assumption in Theorem~\ref{thm:lolaw}. In fact, even if Theorem \ref{thm:lolaw} held under the weaker assumption $pN \geqslant C \log N$, it would not allow us to improve the lower bound on $p$ in Theorem \ref{thm:GOE}. Essentially, the latter arises from the third-order remainder term of a cumulant expansion used to verify the GOE loop equations, which balances a term $\theta \asymp \frac{1}{\sqrt{Np}}$ with a resolvent entry $G_{ab}$. Since the whole argument takes place at the microscopic spectral scale $\eta \asymp \frac{1}{N}$ and our local law controls the entries of $G$ with very high probability by $O(1)$ at the optimal scale $\eta \asymp \frac{\log N}{N}$, we have to resort to a monotonicity argument in $G$ to estimate $G_{ab}$ by $\log N$ at the microscopic scale, which leads to the error bound $\frac{\log N}{\sqrt{Np}}$. As explained in Section \ref{sec:GOE_overview} below, the actual argument is considerably more subtle and even obtaining the lower bound $(\log N)^{2 + \kappa}$ requires some care.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rmk:edge}
We expect our optimal delocalization result in Theorem \ref{thm:deloc} to extend to the edge as well, i.e.~\eqref{deloc_main} to hold for all but the top eigenvector $\bm u_1$; cf.~Remark \ref{rem:deloc_optimal}. Moreover, we expect edge universality to hold as well, up to a random shift of the edge locations $\pm2$; see \cite{EKYY2, lee2018local, HuangLandonYau, he2021fluctuations, JLeeedge, huang2026edge} for results in the bounded-diameter regime $Np \ge N^\varepsilon$ (see \eqref{diam_G} below). The missing ingredient for proving such results is a nontrivial extension of our optimal local law in Theorem \ref{thm:lolaw} to the edge, in particular also controlling concentration of the Green function around a suitable explicit random approximation, taking into account the randomly shifted edge locations. See Section \ref{subsec:edge} below for more details.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:Mprop}
For $|z| \lesssim 1$ and $\bm v, \bm w \in \mathbb{R}^N$, we have
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Mprop}
|M_{\bm v \bm w}(z)| \asymp |\langle \bm v, (\mathbf{1} - \Pi_{\bm e})\bm w \rangle| + \frac{|\langle \bm v, \Pi_{\bm e} \bm w \rangle|}{\sqrt{Np}} \,. 
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ImMprop}
|(\operatorname{Im} M(z))_{\bm v \bm w}| \lesssim  |\langle \bm v, (\mathbf{1} - \Pi_{\bm e})\bm w \rangle| + \frac{|\langle \bm v, \Pi_{\bm e} \bm w \rangle|}{Np} \lesssim \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e} \, \lVert \bm w \rVert_{\bm e} \,. 
\end{equation}

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:lolaw}
The following holds under the assumptions of Theorem \ref{thm:deloc}.
\begin{subequations}
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)]  \emph{The average local law:}
	\begin{equation} \label{eq:avLL}
		\mathbb{P} \Biggl( \bigcap_{z \in \mathbb{D} } \Biggl\{ \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left| \big\langle G(z) - M(z)\big\rangle \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right| \leqslant C \frac{1}{\sqrt{N \eta}}\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{ \log N}{N \eta}} + \sqrt{\frac{\log N}{N p}}  \Biggr)\Biggr\} \Biggr) \geqslant 1 - N^{-D} \,. 
	\end{equation}
	\item[(ii)] \emph{The isotropic local law}: For all deterministic $\bm v, \bm w \in \mathbb{R}^N$
	\begin{equation} \label{eq:isoLL}
		\mathbb{P} \Biggl(  \bigcap_{z \in \mathbb{D} } \Biggl\{ \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left| \big( G(z) - M(z)\big)_{\bm v \bm w} \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right| \leqslant C \,  \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e} \,  \lVert \bm w \rVert_{\bm e} \,  \Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{N \eta}} + \sqrt{\frac{\log N}{Np} } \Biggr)\Biggr\}\Biggr) \geqslant  1 - N^{-D}\,.
	\end{equation}
\end{itemize}
	\end{subequations}

To the best of our knowledge, the optimal factor $\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{N \eta}$ in the average law \eqref{eq:avLL} has previously been obtained only for $\beta$-ensembles \cite{bourgade2022optimal}, in particular in no independent Hermitian ensemble other than GOE, let alone sparse matrices. <!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Deducing delocalization from an entrywise or isotropic local law is standard (see, e.g., \cite[Theorem 2.10]{BenyachKnowles2017}). For the reader's convenience, we recall the simple argument here. First, note that the local law \eqref{eq:isoLL} obviously also holds when replacing $G$ and $M$ by $\operatorname{Im} G$ and $\operatorname{Im} M$, respectively. Denote by $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N$ the eigenvalues of $H$, with associated normalized eigenvectors $\bm u_i$. Take an eigenvalue $\lambda_i \in [-2+\kappa, 2-\kappa]$ and spectral parameter $z_i = \lambda_i + \mathrm i \eta  \in \mathbb{D}$ with $\eta = CN^{-1} \log N$. Then, for $\bm v \in \mathbb{R}^N$, we deduce from Lemma \ref{lem:Mprop} and Theorem \ref{thm:lolaw} that 
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
\frac{\langle \bm v, \bm u_i \rangle^2}{\eta} &\leqslant \sum_j \frac{\langle \bm v, \bm u_j \rangle^2 \, \eta}{(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)^2 + \eta^2} = (\operatorname{Im} G(z_i))_{\bm v \bm v} \\
&\leqslant (\operatorname{Im} M(z_i))_{\bm v \bm v} + 2 C^{1/2}\lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e}^2  \leqslant C_\kappa \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e}^2
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
with probability $\geqslant 1 - N^{-D}$,
for a constant $C_\kappa$ depending only on $\kappa$. 
We deduce that
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\originalleft( \langle \bm v, \bm u_i \rangle^2 \leqslant C_\kappa C \, \frac{ \log N}{N} \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e}^2 \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\originalright) \geqslant 1 - N^{-D} \,. 
\end{equation}
The claim now follows from a union bound.

<!-- remark-8: body_md -->
\label{rem:digraphs_proof}
	We now briefly explain how our proof of Theorem \ref{thm:deloc} has to be adapted in order to conclude the proof of the claimed optimal delocalization bound for the left- and right-eigenvectors of the Erd\H{o}s-Rényi digraph in Remark \ref{rem:digraphs}. We refer to  \cite[Corollary~2.6]{AEK2018} as well as \cite{cipolloni2025optimal, alt2021spectral, alt2021inhomogeneous, CEHS24, campbell2025spectral} for similar arguments and will hence be rather brief. 
	
	As usual, the argument proceeds by considering the \emph{Hermitization}
	\begin{equation*}
\widetilde{H}^w := \begin{pmatrix}
0 & \widehat{H} - w \\
\widehat{H}^* - \overline{w} & 0
\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^{2N \times 2N} \qquad \text{with} \qquad w \in \mathbb{C} 
	\end{equation*}
	of the non-Hermitian adjacency matrix $\widehat{H}$. By following the proof of our local law in Sections \ref{sec:Bernoulli}--\ref{sec:isolaw} with  only minor straightforward adjustments, we can show the following: Uniformly in $|w| \le 1 - \kappa$ and $\eta \in [C N^{-1} \log N, 1]$, the resolvent 
	\begin{equation*}
G^w(\eta) := \big(\widetilde{H}^w - \mathrm i \eta\big)^{-1}
	\end{equation*}
	concentrates around a deterministic matrix $M^w(z) \in \mathbb{C}^{2N \times 2N}$ given as the solution to 
	\begin{equation*}
- \frac{1}{M^w(\eta)} = \mathrm i \eta - p \theta \widetilde{S} + \begin{pmatrix}
0 & w \mathbf{1} \\ \overline{w} \mathbf{1} & 0 
\end{pmatrix} + \langle M^w(\eta) \rangle \quad \text{where} \quad \widetilde{S} := \begin{pmatrix}
0 & S \\ S & 0
\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^{2N \times 2N} \,, 
	\end{equation*}
	exactly as in Theorem \ref{thm:lolaw}. The only difference is that in the definition of the anisotropic norm \eqref{eq:normdef} the orthogonal projection $\Pi_{\bm e}$ is replaced by
$
\bigl(
\begin{smallmatrix}
\Pi_{\bm e} & 0
\\
0 & \Pi_{\bm e}
\end{smallmatrix}
\bigr)
$	
(a rank-two orthogonal projection). 
	
	Hence, completely analogously to the Hermitian setting, we obtain optimal delocalization for the eigenvectors of the Hermitization $\widetilde{H}^w$. To conclude the same statement fo the true left and right non-Hermitian eigenvectors of $\widehat{H}$, we observe that $0 \in \operatorname{spec}(\widetilde{H}^\lambda)$ if and only if $\lambda \in \operatorname{spec}(\widehat{H})$. Therefore, by uniformity of the local law in $|w| \le 1- \kappa$, we infer \eqref{eq:directed_deloc} (see \cite[Proof of Theorem~2.2]{cipolloni2025optimal} for more details).

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:Gron}
Adopt the notations and conventions from above, and assume that 
\begin{equation}

<!-- remark-9: body_md -->
\label{rem:lowerbound_p}
We comment on the importance of the lower bound $Np \geqslant C (\log N)^2$ in our proof. It is used in several crucial steps of our argument. Perhaps the most prominent one is in the estimate of Green function entries in terms of the parameter $\Lambda_{{\rm ent}}^{(1)}$ from \eqref{eq:Lambdaav}. We use the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy-type inequality from Proposition \ref{prop:DBG} below to estimate the size of Dynkin martingale $\mathcal{M}^{{\rm ent}, i}_{\bm a \bm b,t}$ from \eqref{eq:Ment} in terms of its quadratic variation. Since our entire argument is predicated on very high probability bounds, we require the right-hand side of \eqref{BDG} to be of oder $\mathrm e^{-D \log N}$, which in particular requires $a/K \geqslant 4 D \log N$. Our upper bound on the jump size of $\mathcal{M}^{{\rm ent}, i}_{\bm a \bm b,t}$ is $K = C (Np)^{-1/2} \Lambda_{{\rm ent}}^{(1)}$ (see \eqref{jump_sizes}), which essentially arises by resolvent expansion and is sharp. Since our goal in \eqref{BDG} is the bound $a = \Lambda_{{\rm ent}}^{(1)}$, we therefore immediately arrive at the condition $a/K = \frac{1}{C}\sqrt{Np}$, and hence $Np \geqslant (4CD \log N)^2$.

\end{equation}
with very high probability.

with very high probability.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Armed with Proposition \ref{prop:Groniso}, the proof of the isotropic local law \eqref{eq:isoLL} follows analogously to that of the average and isotropic laws in Section \ref{sec:Bernoulli}. The main difference is that, analogously in order to eventually go from $\widetilde{G}$ in \eqref{eq:tGiso} to the usual $G$, one has to replace up to $|\mathcal{I}|^2 \leqslant 4 (\log N)^{5/4}$ many matrix entries of size (up to) $(Np)^{-1/2}$. However, since $|\mathcal{I}|^2/\sqrt{Np}$ might be large, we cannot simply sum up the errors. 

To overcome this difficulty, we consider, for fixed $z \in \mathbb{D}$, 
\begin{equation*}
G(u) = G(u; z)\mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \big(\theta \big[\widetilde{X}(T) + u(X(T) - \widetilde{X}(T))\big] - z\big)^{-1} \quad \text{for} \quad u \in [0,1]\,,
\end{equation*}
which smoothly interpolates between $\widetilde{G} = G(0)$ and $G = G(1)$. 

Then, by differentiating in $u$, we obtain
\begin{equation} \label{eq:derivident}
\partial_{u} G_{\bm v \bm w}(u) = - \theta \sum_{x,y \in \mathcal{I}} X_{xy}(T) G_{\bm v x}(u) G_{y \bm w}(u)  
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation} \label{eq:partialest}
|\partial_{u} G_{\bm v \bm w}(u)| \preceq \frac{1}{\sqrt{Np}} \max_{x,y \in \mathcal{I}}  |G_{\bm v x}(u) G_{y \bm w}(u) | 
\end{equation}
uniformly in $\bm v, \bm w \in \mathcal{V}$ and $u \in [0,1]$, by application of \eqref{eq:IBennett}, yielding that 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:IXxy}
\sum_{x,y \in \mathcal{I}} X_{xy}(T) \preceq 1 \,. 
\end{equation}

We can then apply Grönwall's lemma to \eqref{eq:partialest} and find that\relax{To be precise, we first apply Grönwall for the case that $\bm v, \bm w$ are both standard basis vectors. Afterwards, we treat the case that one of the two vectors is not a standard basis vector in $\mathcal{V}$, and finally the case where both $\bm v, \bm w$ are not standard basis vectors. The outcome can be summarized in the following bound \eqref{eq:Gubound}.}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Gubound}
| G_{\bm v \bm w}(u)| \lesssim  |M_{\bm v \bm w}| + \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e} \, \lVert \bm w \rVert_{\bm e}
\end{equation}
with very high probability,
uniformly in $u \in [0,1]$ and $\bm v, \bm w \in \mathcal{V}$. 
By integrating \eqref{eq:derivident} and using \eqref{eq:Gubound} together with \eqref{eq:IXxy}, we conclude
\begin{equation*}
\max_{\bm v, \bm w \in \mathcal{I}} \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left| G_{\bm v \bm w} - \widetilde{G}_{\bm v \bm w} \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right| \preceq \frac{1}{\sqrt{Np}} \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e} \, \lVert \bm w \rVert_{\bm e}\,,
\end{equation*}
where we additionally used that $|M_{\bm v x} |\lesssim \lVert \bm v \rVert_{\bm e}$ and $|M_{y \bm w} |\lesssim \lVert \bm w \rVert_{\bm e}$, both uniformly in $x, y \in [N]$.  We have hence proven the isotropic local for $G$ as well.

\begin{equation} \label{eq:CK}

\end{equation}
Therefore, using the trivial resolvent bound $\Vert G^{(xy,\nu)}(\tilde{z}) \Vert + \Vert G(\tilde{z})\Vert \leqslant 2|\operatorname{Im} z|^{-1} \leqslant 2 C_K N$ for all $\tilde{z} \in \{z, z_2, ... , z_k\}$, we have that the contribution of $\Omega_N^{\mathsf{c}}$ to \emph{all} the estimates \eqref{eq:thirdorder}--\eqref{eq:diagonal} is negligible, simply because $\mathbb{P}[\Omega_N^{\mathsf{c}}] \leqslant N^{-D}$ for some $D$ chosen sufficiently large. We shall henceforth assume that all resolvents are controlled on the very-high-probability event $\Omega_N$. 

In fact, on the event $\Omega_N$, we have, by Lemma \ref{lem:monotone} and Lemma \ref{lem:stability} that, uniformly in $a,b,x,y \in [N]$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:resolventbound}

We proceed and record the standard resolvent derivative identities 
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\partial_\nu G^{(xy,\nu)}(z) = - G^{(xy,\nu)} (z)\Delta_{xy} G^{(xy,\nu)}(z) \\
\partial_{\nu}^2 G^{(xy,\nu)}(z) = 2 G^{(xy,\nu)}(z) \Delta_{xy} G^{(xy,\nu)}(z) \Delta_{xy} G^{(xy,\nu)}(z)
\end{split}
\end{equation}

For simplicity of the presentation, in the items below, we drop the superscript $(xy,\nu)$, the spectral parameters, as well as non-differentiated factors of $\langle G \rangle$ occurring in $f_{xy}$: 
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i.a)] both derivatives act on $G_{xy}$, one gets $G_{xy} G_{xx} G_{yy}$; 
\item[(i.b)] both derivatives act on $G_{xy}$, one gets $G_{xy} G_{xy} G_{xy}$; 
\item[(ii.a)] only one derivative acts on $G_{xy}$, the other on a copy of $\langle G \rangle$, one gets $N^{-1}G_{xy} G_{xy} (G^2)_{xy}$; 
\item[(ii.b)] only one derivative acts on $G_{xy}$, the other on a copy of $\langle G \rangle$, one gets $N^{-1}G_{xx} G_{yy} (G^2)_{xy}$; 
\item[(iii.a)] no derivative acts on $G_{xy}$, but both on one copy of $\langle G \rangle$, one gets $N^{-1}G_{xy} G_{yy} (G^2)_{xx}$; 
\item[(iii.b)] no derivative acts on $G_{xy}$, but both on one copy of $\langle G \rangle$, one gets $N^{-1}G_{xy} G_{xy} (G^2)_{xy}$;
\item[(iii.c)] no derivative acts on $G_{xy}$, but both on a fresh copy of $\langle G \rangle$, one gets $N^{-2}G_{xy} (G^2)_{xy} (G^2)_{xy}$. 
\end{itemize}

we have therefore shown that $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d t} \mathbb{E}[\lvert \mathcal M_t \rvert^2] = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d t} \mathbb{E}[A_t]$.
The same calculation combined with the Markov property of $(X_t)_{t \geqslant 0}$, like in the proof of the previous lemma, shows that $\lvert \mathcal M_t \rvert^2 - A_t$ is a martingale.

Next, we write
\begin{align*}
A_t &= \int_0^t \mathrm d s \, \sum_y L(X_s, y) \Bigl[\lvert \phi(y) \rvert^2 - \lvert \phi(X_s) \rvert^2 - 2 \operatorname{Re} \phi(X_s) (\phi(y) - \phi(X_s))\Bigr]
\\
&= \int_0^t \mathrm d s \, \sum_y L(X_s, y) \, \lvert \phi(y) - \phi(X_s) \rvert^2\,.
\end{align*}
In particular, $A_t$ is increasing and predictable (even continuous), and hence must be equal to $\langle \mathcal M \rangle_t$.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:DBG}
Suppose that the jumps of the martingale \eqref{dynkin} satisfy $|\mathcal{M}_t - \mathcal{M}_{t-}| \leqslant K$ almost surely for some constant $K > 0$. Then for any $a,b >0$ we have
\begin{equation} \label{BDG}
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\originalleft( \exists t \geqslant 0 :  |\mathcal{M}_t| \geqslant a, \langle \mathcal{M} \rangle_t \leqslant b^2 \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\originalright) \leqslant 4 \exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left[-\frac{a^2}{2(aK + b^2)}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right]}\,.
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
Suppose first that $\phi$ is real.
Fix $\lambda > 0$.
Applying Lemma \ref{lem:dynkin_mart} to $\psi \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \mathrm e^{\lambda \phi}$, we find that
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal N_t \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \psi(X_t) - \psi(X_0) - \int_0^t \mathrm d s \, \mathcal L \psi(X_s) =
\psi(X_t) - \psi(X_0) - \int_0^t \mathrm d s \, \kappa(X_s) \psi(X_s)
\end{equation*}
is a martingale, where we defined
\begin{equation*}
\kappa(x) \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \sum_y \mathcal L(x,y) \bigl(\mathrm e^{\lambda(\phi(y) - \phi(x))} - 1\bigr)\,.
\end{equation*}
We can solve the equation $\mathrm d \psi(X_t) = \kappa(X_t) \psi(X_t) + \mathrm d \mathcal N_t$ by variation of constants, which implies that
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal V_t \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \mathrm e^{-\int_0^t \mathrm d s \, \kappa(X_s)} \psi(X_t)
\end{equation*}
is a martingale, since
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm d \mathcal V_t = \mathrm e^{-\int_0^t \mathrm d s \, \kappa(X_s)} \mathrm d \mathcal N_t\,.
\end{equation*}
Defining
\begin{equation*}
\rho(x) \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \sum_y \mathcal L(x,y) \bigl(\mathrm e^{\lambda (\phi(y) - \phi(x))} - 1 - \lambda(\phi(y) - \phi(x))\bigr)\,,
\end{equation*}
we conclude that
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal Z_t \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \mathcal V_t / \psi(X_0) = \mathrm e^{\lambda \mathcal M_t - \int_0^t \mathrm d s \, \rho(X_s)}
\end{equation*}
is a martingale.
By power series expansion, we deduce that
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathrm e^u - 1 - u}{u^2} \leqslant \frac{\mathrm e^v - 1 - v}{v^2}
\end{equation*}
whenever $\lvert u \rvert \leqslant v$. By assumption on the jump sizes, $\lvert \phi(y) - \phi(x) \rvert \leqslant K$ whenever $\mathcal L(x,y) \neq 0$. We conclude that
\begin{equation*}
\rho(x) \leqslant h(\lambda) \sum_y \mathcal L(x,y) (\phi(y) - \phi(x))^2 \,, \qquad h(\lambda) \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \frac{\mathrm e^{\lambda K} -1 -\lambda K}{K^2}\,.
\end{equation*}
By Lemma \ref{lem:quadr_var}, we deduce that
\begin{equation} \label{Z_t_rho}
\int_0^t \mathrm d s \, \rho(X_s) \leqslant h(\lambda) \langle \mathcal M \rangle_t\,.
\end{equation}

Next, we define the stopping time (recall that $\mathcal M_t$ is right-continuous and $\langle \mathcal M \rangle_t$ is continuous)
\begin{equation*}
\tau \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \inf \{t \geqslant 0 : \mathcal M_t \geqslant a,  \langle \mathcal M \rangle_t \leqslant b^2\}\,.
\end{equation*}
Using \eqref{Z_t_rho}, we estimate 
\begin{equation} \label{tau_infty_estimate}
\mathbb{P}(\tau < \infty) \leqslant \mathbb{E} \Bigl[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}_{\tau < \infty} \, \mathrm e^{\lambda (\mathcal M_\tau - a)} \mathrm e^{-h(\lambda) (\langle \mathcal M \rangle_\tau - b^2)}\Bigr] \leqslant \mathrm e^{- \lambda a + h(\lambda) b^2} \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}_{\tau \leqslant \infty} \mathcal Z_\tau] \leqslant \mathrm e^{- \lambda a + h(\lambda) b^2}\,,
\end{equation}
where in the last step we used Fatou's lemma and optional stopping for the martingale $\mathcal Z_t$ to conclude that
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}_{\tau \leqslant \infty} \mathcal Z_\tau] = \mathbb{E} \Bigl[\lim_{n \to \infty} \boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}_{\tau \leqslant n} \mathcal Z_{\tau \wedge n}\Bigr] \leqslant \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}_{\tau \leqslant n} \mathcal Z_{\tau \wedge n}] \leqslant \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[\mathcal Z_{\tau \wedge n}] = 1\,.
\end{equation*}
To conclude the proof, by convexity of $\exp$ we obtain for $\lambda K < 1$ that $h(\lambda) \leqslant \frac{\lambda^2}{2 (1 - \lambda K)}$.
Using \eqref{tau_infty_estimate} with the choice $\lambda \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \frac{a}{b^2 + Ka}$, and repeating the same argument for $-\mathcal M_t$, we conclude from a union bound that
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\originalleft( \exists t \geqslant 0 :  |\mathcal{M}_t| \geqslant a, \langle \mathcal{M} \rangle_t \leqslant b^2 \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\originalright) \leqslant 2 \exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left[-\frac{a^2}{2(aK + b^2)}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right]}\,.
\end{equation*}

Finally, the claim for complex $\phi$ follows by applying the above estimate to the real and imaginary parts of $\phi = \phi^{(1)} + \mathrm i \phi^{(2)}$ separately, using that the resulting splitting $\mathcal M_t = \mathcal M_t^{(1)} + \mathrm i \mathcal M_t^{(2)}$ satisfies $\langle \mathcal M \rangle_t = \langle \mathcal M^{(1)} \rangle_t + \langle \mathcal M^{(2)} \rangle_t$.

<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
The overall structure of the argument is similar to those applied for the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lolaw}: That is, we consider a dynamics and argue that a suitably designed stopping time (see \eqref{eq:stoptime2} below) actually equals the maximal time of the evolution. Instead of the Bernoulli flow, we now consider the usual Brownian characteristic flow, governed by the equations
	\begin{equation} \label{eq:flow}
		\mathrm d W_t = \frac{\mathrm d B_t}{\sqrt{N}} \,, \qquad \partial_t z_t = - m_t(z_t)\,, 
	\end{equation}
	where we fix the time interval to be $t \in [0,1]$. Moreover, $B_t$ is a real symmetric or complex Hermitian matrix valued standard Brownian motion (depending on the symmetry class of $W$). We start with initial condition $W_0 = 0$ and end with terminal condition $z_{T=1} \in \mathbb{D}$. Finally, $m_t(z_t)$ solves the equation
	\begin{equation}
- \frac{1}{m_t(z_t)} = z_t + t m_t(z_t) \,,
	\end{equation}
	from which we infer that $|m_t(z_t)| \asymp 1$ and $|m_t'(z_t)| \leqslant C_\kappa$ where $m_t'(z_t) = \tfrac{\mathrm d }{\mathrm d w} m_t(w) \vert_{w = z_t}$
	
For fixed $z_T \in \mathbb{D}$, we define two observables 
\begin{equation}
\phi_t^{(1)} \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \langle G_t(z_t) - m_t(z_t) \rangle \,, \qquad \phi_t^{(2)} \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \langle G^2_t(z_t) - m_t'(z_t) \rangle
\end{equation}
and note that $\phi_0^{(i)} = 0$ for $i \in [2]$. Now, abbreviating $\eta_t \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \operatorname{Im} z_t$, for a constant $\mathfrak{C} > 0$, we define the stopping time
\begin{equation} \label{eq:stoptime2}
	\tau = \tau (\mathfrak{C}) \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= \inf \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{ t \in [0,1] : N \eta_t \big| \phi_t^{(1)} \big| \geqslant \mathfrak{C} (\log N)^{1/2}\quad \text{or} \quad  N \eta_t^2 \big| \phi_t^{(2)} \big|\geqslant \mathfrak{C}^2 (\log N)^{1/2} \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\} 
\end{equation}
and suppose that $N \eta_T \geqslant (1 + \mathfrak{C})^4 (\log N)^{1/2}$. By continuity and since the condition is trivial (identically zero) at time zero, we have that $\tau > 0$ with very high probability and shall henceforth assume that all times are in the interval $t \in [0,\tau]$. 

Next, dropping the $z$-arguments for brevity, we compute the differential of $\phi_t^{(i)}$ using Ito's formula together with \eqref{eq:flow} and obtain
\begin{equation} \label{eq:flows}
	\begin{split}
\mathrm d \phi_t^{(1)} &= m_t' \phi_t^{(1)} \mathrm d t+ \phi_t^{(1)} \phi_t^{(2)} \mathrm d t + \frac{\mathbf{1}_{\beta = 1}}{N} \mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left( \frac{\langle \operatorname{Im} G_t \rangle}{\eta_t^2} \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) \mathrm d t + \mathrm d \mathcal{M}_t^{(1)}\,,  \\
\mathrm d \phi_t^{(2)} &= 2m_t' \phi_t^{(2)} \mathrm d t+ \big( \phi_t^{(2)}\big)^2 \mathrm d t + 2 \phi_t^{(1)} \langle G_t^3\rangle + \frac{\mathbf{1}_{\beta = 1}}{N} \mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left( \frac{\langle \operatorname{Im} G_t \rangle}{\eta_t^3} \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) \mathrm d t +   \mathrm d \mathcal{M}_t^{(2)}\,,
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
where we abbreviated 
\begin{equation}
\mathrm d \mathcal{M}_t^{(1)} \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= - \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \langle G_t^2 \mathrm d B_t \rangle\,, \qquad \mathrm d \mathcal{M}_t^{(2)} \mathrel{\vcenter{\baselineskip0.65ex \lineskiplimit0pt \hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}= - \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \langle G_t^3 \mathrm d B_t \rangle\,,
\end{equation}
and the shorthand notation $\mathbf{1}_{\beta = 1}$ indicates that these terms are only present in the GOE case. The implicit constants in the $\mathcal{O}$-notation are bounded by, say, $10$, independent of any particular constant from above. 

Completely analogously to our proofs in Sections \ref{sec:Gronproof}--\ref{sec:lemproofs}, we now control the quadratic variation of the martingale terms in \eqref{eq:flows} and the size of the generator terms. The quadratic variations admit the bounds
\begin{equation} \label{eq:martest}
	\begin{split}
	[\mathcal{M}_t^{(1)}]^2 &\leqslant \int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \frac{\langle |G_s|^4 \rangle}{N^2} \leqslant \int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \frac{1}{N^2 \eta_s^3} \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(1 + \frac{\mathfrak{C} (\log N)^{1/2}}{N \eta_s}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\lesssim \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\frac{1}{N \eta_t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^2 \,, \\
	[\mathcal{M}_t^{(2)}]^2 &\leqslant \int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \frac{\langle |G_s|^6 \rangle}{N^2} \leqslant \int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \frac{1}{N^2 \eta_s^5} \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(1 + \frac{\mathfrak{C} (\log N)^{1/2}}{N \eta_s}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\lesssim \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\frac{1}{N \eta_t^2}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^2\,. 
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
The remaining terms in \eqref{eq:flows} can be bounded as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gen1}
	\begin{split}
& \int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \, \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left|m_s' \phi_s^{(1)} + \phi_s^{(1)} \phi_s^{(2)}  + \frac{\mathbf{1}_{\beta = 1}}{N} \mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left( \frac{\langle \operatorname{Im} G_s \rangle}{\eta_s^2} \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right| \\
& \qquad \qquad \leqslant C_\kappa \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(1 + \mathfrak{C}^2 \frac{(\log N)^{1/2}}{N \eta_s^2}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\big|\phi_s^{(1)}\big| + \frac{(\log N)^{1/2}}{N \eta_t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gen2}
	\begin{split}
		& \int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \, \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left|2m_s' \phi_s^{(2)} + \big( \phi_s^{(2)}\big)^2  + 2 \phi_s^{(1)} \langle G_s^3\rangle + \frac{\mathbf{1}_{\beta = 1}}{N} \mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left( \frac{\langle \operatorname{Im} G_s \rangle}{\eta_s^3} \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) \mathrm d t \aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right| \\
		& \hspace{6cm} \leqslant C_\kappa \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(1 + \mathfrak{C}^2 \frac{(\log N)^{1/2}}{N \eta_s^2}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\big|\phi_s^{(2)}\big| + \frac{(\log N)^{1/2}}{N \eta_t^2}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
where we used the integration rules
\begin{equation}
\int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s\frac{1}{\eta_s} \lesssim \log\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\frac{\eta_0}{\eta_t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) \quad \text{and} \quad \int_{0}^{t} \mathrm d s \frac{1}{\eta_s^\alpha} \lesssim \frac{1}{\eta_t^{\alpha - 1}} \quad \text{for} \quad \alpha > 1 
\end{equation}
and the definition of the stopping time \eqref{eq:stoptime2}. 

We can then obtain a similar Grönwall estimate as in Proposition \ref{prop:Gron} (see in particular \eqref{eq:avGron1}--\eqref{eq:avGron2}) by involving the Burholder-Davis-Gundy inequality for continuous martingales (corresponding to Proposition \ref{prop:DBG} with $K=0$); see e.g.\ \cite{vandegeer1995} or \cite[Eq.~(18) in Appendix B.6]{shorack2009empirical}. Deducing the validity of the local law in Proposition \ref{prop:Gauss} is then deduced by appropriately choosing the constant $\mathfrak{C}$ as done in \eqref{eq:Cchoice}, eventually showing that $\tau(\mathfrak{C}) = 1$ with probability at least $1 - N^{-\mathfrak{C}/C_\kappa}$ for some constant $C_\kappa > 0$ depending only on $\kappa$. This concludes the proof of Proposition \ref{prop:Gauss}.
