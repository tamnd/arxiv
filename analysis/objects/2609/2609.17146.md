<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:reprH} The following facts hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] For $\mathbf{x}(0)=0$, the limit $l= \lim\limits_{t\to +\infty}\mathbf{x}(t)$ exists and $x(t)=l$ for $t\geq n$;
\item[(b)] $\mathcal H=\left\{\mathbf{h}\in \mathbb{R}_+^{n}\,|\, \mathbf{x}(n)=\1\;\hbox{for}\, \mathbf{x}(0)=0\right\}$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Consider the standard partial order on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ whereby, for two vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, we write that $\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{y}$ if $x_i\leq y_i$ for every $1\le i\le n$ and notice that $F_{\mathbf{h}}$ is monotonically nondecreasing with respect to such order. This implies that, since $F_{\mathbf{h}}(\0)\geq \0$, then $\mathbf{x}(t)=F^t_{\mathbf{h}}(\0)$ is monotonically nondecreasing and thus converges to a limit. Moreover, since every entry of $x(t)$ changes value at most once, by time $t=n$, we have reached a point for which $F^t_{\mathbf{h}}(\0)=F^{t+1}_{\mathbf{h}}(\0)$. From that point on, the sequence is stationary. This proves point (a).

Since $F_{\mathbf{h}}$ is monotonically nondecreasing, we have that
$$F^t_{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{x}(0))\geq F^t_{\mathbf{h}}(\0)\quad\forall t,\,\forall \mathbf{x}(0)$$
This yields point (b).
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:optimal-cost} For every LTM $(\mathcal G,\theta)$, it holds that 
\begin{equation}\label{optimalcost}
C^*(\mathcal G,\theta)=\min\limits_{\sigma\in \mathcal{S} _{n}}C({\sigma})
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We first show that for every permutation $\sigma$ in $\mathcal S_{n}$, we have that $\mathbf{h}(\sigma)\in\mathcal H$. First, we prove by induction that, for every $0\le t\leq n$, 
\begin{equation}\label{monotone}x^{\mathbf{h}}(t)\geq \delta^{\sigma_1}+\cdots +  \delta^{\sigma_t}\,.\end{equation}
For $t=0$, relation \eqref{monotone} trivially holds true. Moreover, if \eqref{monotone} holds true for some $t\ge0$, then, by definition of $\mathbf{h}(\sigma)$,
$$x^{\mathbf{h}}(t+1)_{\sigma_{t+1}}=f_{w_{\sigma_{t+1}}(\sigma)}((Wx^{\mathbf{h}}(t))_{\sigma_{t+1}})\geq f_{w_{\sigma_{t+1}}(\sigma)}(w_{\sigma_{t+1}}(\sigma))=1\,. $$
Since $x^{\mathbf{h}}(t+1)\geq x^{\mathbf{h}}(t)$, this yields relation (\ref{monotone}) for $t+1$. Relation (\ref{monotone}) for $t=n$  implies that $\mathbf{h}(\sigma)\in\mathcal H$. By definition, this yields $\leq$ in formula (\ref{optimalcost}).

To see that the converse inequality holds true as well, for every $\mathbf{h}$ in $\mathcal H$, we construct a permutation $\sigma^\mathbf{h}$ in $\mathcal S_n$ as follows. Start with $\mathbf{x}(0)=\0$ and, for $t=1,\ldots,n$ choose $i_t$ in $\mathcal V$ such that $x_{i_t}(t-1)=0$ and $f_{w_{i_t}\theta_{i_t}+h_{i_t}}(\sum_jW_{{i_t}j}x_j(t-1))=1$ and put $$\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}(t-1)+\delta^{i_t}\,,\qquad \sigma^{\mathbf{h}}_t={i_t}\,.$$ 
Notice that such ${i_t}$ exists for every $1\le t\le n$ for otherwise $F_{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{x}(t-1))=\mathbf{x}(t-1)\ne\1$ and $\mathbf{h}\notin\mathcal H$. Moreover, by construction, $i_s\ne i_t$ for every $s\ne t$ so that $\sigma^{\mathbf{h}}$ is indeed a permutation in $\mathcal S_n$. Now, observe that $\mathbf{h}(\sigma^{\mathbf{h}})\le \mathbf{h}$, so that $C(\sigma^{\mathbf{h}})\le\sum_ih_i$. By the arbitrariness of $\mathbf{h}$ in $\mathcal H$ this implies that 
$$\min\limits_{\sigma\in \mathcal{S} _{n}}C({\sigma})\le\min\limits_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal H}C({\sigma}^{\mathbf{h}})\le\min\limits_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal H}\sum\nolimits_ih_i= C^*(\mathcal G,\theta)\,,$$
thus completing the proof.

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
Given a graph $\mathcal G$, a permutation $\sigma$ is called $\mathcal G$-\emph{greedy} if 
\begin{equation}\label{sigma-neigh}  \sigma_t\in\bigcup\limits_{s<t}N^-_{\sigma_s}\end{equation}
but for the first $q_{\mathcal G}$ values of $t$.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
Given a graph $\mathcal G$, the following facts hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathcal G$-{greedy} permutations exist
\item For every $\mathcal G$-{greedy} permutation $\sigma$, we have that $C(\sigma)\leq C^{max}(\mathcal G,\theta)$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
1. To prove the existence of $\mathcal G$-{greedy} permutations, it is sufficient to choose arbitrarily $\sigma_t\in\mathcal S_t$ for $t=1,\dots , q$ and then, successively, to choose the remaining elements imposing \eqref{sigma-neigh}. The fact that this is possible  is equivalent to the assumption that no connected component of $\mathcal G$ different from $\mathcal G_k$ for $k=1,\dots ,q$ is a sink component. 

2. If $\sigma$ is a $\mathcal G$-{greedy} permutation, then $w_t(\sigma)\geq w$ for every $t>q$ and thus
$$\begin{array}{rcl}C(\sigma)&=&\sum\limits_{t=1}^n(\theta_{\sigma_t}w_{\sigma_t}-w_t(\sigma))^+\\&=&\rho+\sum\limits_{t> q}(\theta_{\sigma_t}w_{\sigma_t}-w_t(\sigma))^+\\
&\leq &\rho+\sum\limits_{t>q}(\theta_{\sigma_t}w_{\sigma_t}-w)^+\\ &=&C^{max}(\mathcal G,\theta)\end{array}$$

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:conservation} For the LTM $(\mathcal G,\theta)$ on a graph $\mathcal G=(\mathcal V, \mathcal E, W)$ of order $n$ with $\theta_i>0$ for every $i$: 
\begin{equation}\label{basic-bound}C^{min}(\mathcal G,\theta)\leq C^*(\mathcal G, \theta)\leq C^{max}(\mathcal G,\theta)\,.\end{equation}
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We only need to prove the lower bound. This  follows from the observation that for any permutation $\sigma$, the external planner must pay in full for at least $q$ times, each time that it chooses for the first time a node in $\mathcal S_k$ for $k=1,\dots , q$. 
Precisely, define
$$t^*_k=min\{t\,|\, \sigma_t\in\mathcal S_k\},\quad \mathcal T=\{t^*_1,\dots , t^*_q\},\quad \mathcal T^*=\{1,\dots , q\}\setminus\mathcal T$$
                                                                                                                                                                                                                        
The following inequalities hold.
                                                                                                
$$\begin{array}{rcl}C(\sigma)&=&\sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal T}\theta_{\sigma_{t}}w_{\sigma_{t}}+\sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal T^*}(\theta_{\sigma_t}w_{\sigma_t}-w_t(\sigma))^+\\ 
&\geq&\sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal T}\theta_{\sigma_{t}}w_{\sigma_{t}}+\left(\sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal T^*}\theta_{\sigma_t}w_{\sigma_t}- \sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal T^*}w_t(\sigma))^+\right)\\ 
                                                                                                                          
                                                                                      
\end{array}$$
Since, by construction, $\sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal T}\theta_{\sigma_{t}}w_{\sigma_{t}}\geq \rho$ and 
$$\sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal T}\theta_{\sigma_{t}}w_{\sigma_{t}}+\sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal T^*}\theta_{\sigma_t}w_{\sigma_t}=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal V}\theta_iw_i=\rho+\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal V\setminus \mathcal V^*}\theta_iw_i$$
                                                                                                           
the left-hand side inequality in \eqref{basic-bound} follows.

\begin{equation}\label{absolute}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal V}|\theta_iw_i-w_i(\sigma)|=\sum\limits_{1\le k\le n}|\theta_{\sigma_k}(n-1)-(k-1)|\,,\end{equation}
and notice that if, for some $k$, it happens that $\theta_{\sigma_k}>\theta_{\sigma_{k+1}}$, then it holds
$$ |\theta_{\sigma_k}(n-1)-(k-1)|+|\theta_{\sigma_{k+1}}(n-1)-k|>
|\theta_{\sigma_{k+1}}(n-1)-(k-1)|+|\theta_{\sigma_{k}}(n-1)-k|\,.$$
In other words, if $\sigma$ does not satisfy condition (\ref{permutation-order}), then there exists another permutation that allows to lower the value (\ref{absolute}).
Since the cost can also be written as 
$$C(\sigma)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal V}[\theta_iw_i-w_i(\sigma)]^+=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal V}\theta_iw_i-W^*\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal V}|\theta_iw_i-w_i(\sigma)|\,,$$
this implies that if a permutation does not satisfy  (\ref{permutation-order}), it cannot be optimal. As optimal permutations surely exist, they must necessarily be of the form above. Notice moreover that, whenever $\theta_{\sigma_k}=\theta_{\sigma_{k+1}}$, inverting the two agents would not modify the cost. This says that all permutations for which the thresholds are in non-decreasing order in the sense of (\ref{permutation-order}) are optimal.
