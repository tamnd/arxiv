<!-- df-1: body_md -->
A set $S$ of points in an affine plane $A_q$ is said to \emph{determine} a direction $(d) \in \ell_\infty$ if there exist 2 distinct points $P,Q \in S$ such that the line $PQ$ has direction $(d)$.
<!-- end df-1 -->
<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
Let $p$ be a prime number, and $S$ a set of $kp-r$ points in $\operatorname{AG}(2,p)$ with $0 \leqslant r < p$ and $k \geqslant 1$.
 Then either $S$ is contained in the union of $k$ lines, or there are at least $\frac{p+k+2-r}{k+1}$ special directions.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- df-2: body_md -->
Suppose that $S$ is an integer multiset of points in an affine plane $A_q$ of order $q$.
 Let $(d)$ be a direction.
 Then $S$ is \emph{equidistributed} from direction $(d)$ if all lines with direction $(d)$ intersect $S$ in the same number of points (counted with multiplicity), which is necessarily $|S|/q$, and \emph{inequidistributed} otherwise.
 We will also say
 that direction $(d)$ is equidistributed, respectively inequidistributed,
 for $S$.
<!-- end df-2 -->
<!-- rmk-1: body_md -->
In previous articles \cite{Ghidelli, Kiss:Somlai, ASW}, the term special was used instead of inequidistributed. For point sets whose size is divisible by the order of the plane, the two notions coincide. 
 However, being equidistributed from a direction $(d)$ actually stands out, and being inequidistributed is rather unremarkable.
 Therefore, calling such directions special feels counterintuitive, and we avoid this terminology.

<!-- thm-2: body_md -->
\label{Thm:KS}
 Let $p$ be a prime number, and consider $\operatorname{AG}(2,p)$.
 Interpret the elements of the field $\mathbb F_p$ as the integers $0,\dots,p-1$, naturally equipped with the order relation $<$.
 The set
 \[
  K =  \left\{ (x,y) \in \mathbb F_p^2 \, \, || \, \,  x < y \right \} 
 \]
 is inequidistributed from only 3 directions: $(\infty)$, $(0)$, and $(1)$.
 Moreover, any set in $\operatorname{AG}(2,p)$ that is inequidistributed from exactly 3 directions is the image of $K$ or its complement under an affine transformation.

<!-- thm-4: body_md -->
\label{Thm:No shared directions}
 Let $A_q = (\mathcal P,\mathcal L)$ be an affine plane of order $q$.
 Let $S$ and $T$ be integer multisets of points in $A_q$.
 Suppose that there is no direction from which both $S$ and $T$ are inequidistributed.
 Then
 \[
  \sum_{P \in \mathcal P} \mu_S(P) \mu_T(P) = \frac{|S| |T|}{q^2}.
 \]
 If $S$ and $T$ are ordinary sets, this formula simplifies to $|S \cap T| = |S| |T| / q^2$.
 In particular, if $S$ and $T$ are disjoint point sets of size $q$, then there is a direction determined by both of them.

\label{Thm:Cross determine}
 Suppose that $S$ and $T$ are sets of points in an affine plane $A_q$ of order $q$ and the number of directions cross-determined by $(S,T)$ equals $c \leqslant \frac{q+1}2$.
 Then $|S| |T| \leqslant q^2$.

<!-- thm-6: body_md -->
\label{Thm:Main:Lift}
 Let $p$ be a prime number and $q = p^h$ a power of $p$.
 Let $\operatorname{Tr}:\mathbb F_q\to\mathbb F_p$ be the absolute trace function.
 Recall the natural total ordering $<$ on the elements of $\mathbb F_p$.
 Then the set 
 \[
  L =  \left\{ (x,y) \in \mathbb F_q^2 \, \, || \, \, \operatorname{Tr}(x) < \operatorname{Tr}(y) \right \} 
 \]
 of points in $\operatorname{AG}(2,q)$ is inequidistributed from exactly 3 directions: $(0)$, $(1)$, and $(\infty)$.
 Moreover, every set that is inequidistributed from exactly 3 directions in $\operatorname{AG}(2,q)$ is affinely equivalent to the union of $L$ and some parallel lines that are disjoint from $L$.

\label{Lm:Ortho}
 Let $A_q = (\mathcal P,\mathcal L)$ be an affine plane of order $q$, and $\ell_\infty$ be the line at infinity.
 For every $(d) \in \ell_\infty$ define $V_{(d)} = \left \langle \mu_\ell - \frac 1q \mathbf 1 \, \| \, \ell \in \mathcal L,\ \operatorname{dir}(\ell) = (d) \right \rangle$.
 \begin{enumerate}
  \item \label{Lm:Ortho:1} $\left \langle \mathbf 1 \right \rangle$ and the spaces $V_{(d)}$ for $(d) \in \ell_\infty$ form an orthogonal decomposition of $\mathbb Q^\mathcal P$.
  \item \label{Lm:Ortho:2} Let $S$ be an integer multiset of points and $(d)$ a direction.
  Then $\mu_S \perp V_{(d)}$ if and only if $S$ is equidistributed from $(d)$.
  Thus, if $D$ is the set of inequidistributed directions of $S$, then $\mu_S \in \left \langle \mathbf 1,V_{(d)} \, \| \, (d) \in D \right \rangle$.
 \end{enumerate}
<!-- end lm-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
(1) First let us check that these subspaces are mutually orthogonal.
 For every line $\ell$,
\[
 \left(\mu_\ell - \frac 1q \mathbf 1 \right) \cdot \mathbf 1 = q - \frac{q^2}q = 0,
\]
hence $\left \langle \mathbf 1 \right \rangle$ is orthogonal to all spaces $V_{(d)}$.
Now take two affine lines $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ with different directions.
Then they have one point in common, hence
\[
 \left(\mu_{\ell_1} - \frac 1q \mathbf 1 \right) \cdot \left(\mu_{\ell_2} - \frac 1q \mathbf 1 \right) = \mu_{\ell_1} \cdot \left(\mu_{\ell_2} - \frac 1q \mathbf 1 \right) - \frac 1 q \mathbf 1 \cdot \left(\mu_{\ell_2} - \frac 1q \mathbf 1 \right) = \left(1 - \frac 1 q q \right) - 0 = 0.
\]
This shows that the spaces $V_{(d)}$ are mutually orthogonal.

It remains to show that $\left \langle \mathbf 1, V_{(d)} \, \| \, (d) \in \ell_\infty \right \rangle = \mathbb Q^\mathcal P$. 
First note that $\left \langle \mathbf 1, V_{(d)} \, \| \, (d) \in \ell_\infty \right \rangle = \left \langle \mu_\ell \, \| \, \ell \in \mathcal L \right \rangle$ is the column space of the point-line incidence matrix $B$ of $A_q$.
It is well known that $B B^\top = q I + J$ with $J$ the all-one matrix, which implies that $B$ has rank $q^2$, thus its column space is $\mathbb Q^\mathcal P$.

\bigskip

(2) The integer multiset $S$ is equidistributed from direction $(d)$ if and only if for all affine lines $\ell \ni (d)$ we have
\[
 \left( \mu_\ell - \frac 1q \mathbf 1 \right) \cdot \mu_S = \frac{|S|}q - \frac 1 q |S| = 0,
\]
which is equivalent to $\mu_S \in V_{(d)}^\perp$.
The statement now follows from (1).
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $\mu_S^*$ and $\mu_T^*$ denote the orthogonal projections of $\mu_S$ and $\mu_T$ onto $\left \langle \mathbf 1 \right \rangle ^\perp$.
 Then
 \[
  \mu_S = \frac{\mu_S \cdot \mathbf 1}{\mathbf 1 \cdot \mathbf 1} \mathbf 1 + \mu_S^* = \frac{|S|}{q^2} \mathbf 1 + \mu_S^*.
 \]
 Similarly, $\mu_T = \frac{|T|}{q^2} \mathbf 1 + \mu_T^*$.
 By Lemma \ref{Lm:Ortho}, $\mu_S^*$ and $\mu_T^*$ are orthogonal.
 It follows that
 \[
  \sum_{P \in \mathcal P} \mu_S(P) \mu_T(P) = \mu_S \cdot \mu_T = \left(\frac{|S|}{q^2} \mathbf 1 + \mu_S^*\right) \cdot \left(\frac{|T|}{q^2} \mathbf 1 + \mu_T^*\right ) = \frac{|S| |T|}{q^2}.
 \]
 The remainder of the theorem statement is an immediate consequence.

<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{Prop:General CS}
 Let $B$ be a symmetric real matrix with distinct eigenvalues $\mu_1 > \dots > \mu_d$.
 Then for any two vectors $x$ and $y$, 
  \[
   |x^\top B y| \leqslant \max\{|\mu_1|, |\mu_d|\} \| x \| \| y \|.
  \]
 If $\mu_d< - \mu_1$, then equality holds if and only if $x$ and $y$ are linearly dependent $\mu_d$-eigenvectors of $B$ or one of the vectors is $\mathbf 0$.
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We will suppose that $x$ and $y$ are non-zero, otherwise the claim is trivially true.
 Since $B$ is symmetric, its eigenspaces form an orthogonal decomposition.
 Suppose that $x = \sum_{i=1}^d x_i$ and $y = \sum_{i=1}^d y_i$ with $x_i$ and $y_i$ in the $\mu_i$-eigenspace of $B$ for each $i$.
 Then
 \[
  |x^\top B y| = \left| \sum_{i=1}^d \mu_i x_i^\top y_i \right| \leqslant \sum_{i=1}^d |\mu_i| | x_i^\top y_i | \leqslant \left( \max_{i=1}^d |\mu_i| \right) \sum_{i=1}^d \| x_i \| \|y_i \|,
 \]
 where the last inequality holds by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
 Note that we have $\max_{i=1}^d |\mu_i| = \max\{|\mu_1|, |\mu_d|\}$.
 Since the vectors $x_i$ are pairwise orthogonal, we have that $\sum_{i=1}^d \|x_i\|^2 = \| x \|^2$ and likewise $\sum_{i=1}^d \|y_i\|^2 = \|y\|^2$.
 By applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the vectors $(\|x_1\|, \dots, \|x_d\|)$ and $(\|y_1\|, \dots, \|y_d\|)$, we see that $\sum_{i=1}^d \|x_i\| \|y_i\| \leqslant \|x\| \|y\|$.
 This proves the inequality from the statement.

 Now suppose that $\mu_d < - \mu_1$, which means that $\max\{|\mu_1|, |\mu_d|\} = - \mu_d$, and also assume that equality holds.
 Then we have $\sum_{i=1}^d |\mu_i| | x_i^\top y_i | = -\mu_d \sum_{i=1}^d \| x_i \| \|y_i \|$.
 This means that $\|x_i \| \| y_i\| = 0$ whenever $i < d$.
 Moreover, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality holds for the vectors $(\|x_1\|, \dots, \|x_d\|)$ and $(\|y_1\|, \dots, \|y_d\|)$ with equality, which means that $\|x_i\| = \|y_i\| = 0$ whenever $i < d$.
 It follows that $x$ and $y$ are $\mu_d$-eigenvectors of $B$, i.e.\ $x = x_d$ and $y = y_d$.
 Lastly, we must have that $| x_d^\top y_d | = \| x_d \| \| y_d \|$, which implies that $x_d$ and $y_d$ are linearly dependent.
Conversely, if $x$ and $y$ are linearly dependent $\mu_d$-eigenvectors, then clearly $|x^\top B y| = |\mu_d| \|x\| \|y\|$.

<!-- thm-7: body_md -->
\label{Thm:Cross coclique}
 Let $G$ be a $k$-regular graph on $n$ vertices, $k>0$, with eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix given by $\lambda_1 \geqslant \dots \geqslant \lambda_n$ (counted with multiplicity).
 Let $\lambda = \max\{|\lambda_2|, |\lambda_n|\}$.
 If $(S,T)$ is a cross-coclique in $G$ then
 \[
  |S| |T| \leqslant \left( \frac{n}{\frac{k}{\lambda } + 1} \right)^2.
 \]
 If $\lambda_n < -\lambda_2$ and equality holds in the bound, then $S = T$.
<!-- end thm-7 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $\mu_S$ and $\mu_T$ be the multiplicity functions of the sets $S$ and $T$ respectively, seen as subsets of the vertices of $G$.
 Interpret $\mu_S$ and $\mu_T$ as column vectors, with coordinates labelled by the vertices of $G$.
 We will assume that $S$ and $T$ are non-empty to avoid trivialities.
 Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of $G$.
 Then the fact that $(S,T)$ is a cross-coclique means that $\mu_S^\top A \mu_T = 0$.
 When we project everything onto the orthogonal complement of $\mathbf 1$, this becomes
 \begin{equation}
  \label{Eq:Projection}
  \left( \mu_S - \frac{|S|}n \mathbf 1 \right)^\top \left( A - \frac kn J \right) \left( \mu_T - \frac{|T|}n \mathbf 1 \right) = - \frac k n |S| |T|.
 \end{equation}
 Note that the only difference in the spectra of $A$ and $A - \frac kn J$ is that we change the eigenvalue of $\mathbf 1$ from $k$ to 0, hence $\lambda_1$ becomes 0, and the other eigenvalues stay the same.
 By applying Proposition \ref{Prop:General CS} to \eqref{Eq:Projection}, we obtain
 \begin{equation}
  \label{Eq:Cross proof}
  \frac kn |S| |T| \leqslant \lambda \left \| \mu_S - \frac{|S|}n \mathbf 1 \right \| \left \| \mu_T - \frac{|T|}n \mathbf 1 \right \| = \lambda \sqrt{ |S| \frac{n-|S|}n} \sqrt{|T| \frac{n-|T|}n}.
 \end{equation}
 Now we use the following inequality, which one can see as the AM-GM inequality.
 \begin{align*}
  \left(n-\sqrt{|S| |T|}\right)^2 - (n-|S|)(n-|T|) &= \left(n^2 - 2 \sqrt{|S| |T|} n + |S| |T|\right) - (n^2 - (|S|+|T|)n + |S| |T|) \\
  &= \left(|S| - 2 \sqrt{|S| |T|} + |T|\right)n = \left(\sqrt{|S|} - \sqrt{|T|}\right)^2 n \geqslant 0.
 \end{align*}
 We conclude that $(n-|S|)(n-|T|) \leqslant (n-\sqrt{|S| |T|})^2$ with equality if and only if $|S| = |T|$.
 Plugging this into \eqref{Eq:Cross proof}, we find that
 \[
  \frac kn |S| |T| \leqslant \frac \lambda n \sqrt{|S| |T|}(n-\sqrt{|S| |T|}).
 \]
 This inequality is equivalent to
 \[
  \sqrt{|S| |T|} \leqslant \frac n {\frac k \lambda + 1 },
 \]
 proving the inequality of the theorem.

 Now suppose that $\lambda_n < - \lambda_2$.
 If equality holds, then we must have $|S| = |T|$ and in (\ref{Eq:Cross proof}) we find equality for Proposition \ref{Prop:General CS}, hence $\mu_S - \frac{|S|}n \mathbf 1$ and $\mu_T - \frac{|T|}n \mathbf 1$ must be linearly dependent $\lambda_n$-eigenvectors of $A$.
 Since $|S| = |T|$, these vectors have the same magnitude, which means that they are either the same vector, or differ by a factor $-1$.
 Suppose that the latter holds, and write $\mu_S - \frac{|S|}n \mathbf 1 = x$.
 Then we have $\mu_S = \frac{|S|}n \mathbf 1 + x$ and $\mu_T = \frac{|S|}n \mathbf 1 - x$.
 Note that both of these vectors are $\{0,1\}$-vectors.
 If there exists a vertex $v$ with $x(v) = 0$, then $|S|/n$ is either $0$ or $1$.
The former contradicts the assumption that $S$ is non-empty. The latter implies that $S$ is the full vertex set.
 Since $T$ is non-empty, $G$ must have isolated vertices.
 But given that $G$ is $k$-regular, this means that $k=0$, hence $\lambda_2 = \lambda_n = 0$, contradicting that $\lambda_n < - \lambda_2$.
 This means that $x(v)$ is non-zero everywhere.
 Then we have $\{|S|/n \pm x(v)\} = \{0,1\}$, hence $|S| = n/2$ and $x(v) = \pm 1/2$.
 This immediately implies that $S$ and $T$ partition the vertex set.
 But since they form a cross-coclique, this implies that $G$ is disconnected.
 The multiplicity of $k$ as eigenvalue of $A$ equals the number of connected components.
 Hence we find that $\lambda_2 = k$.
 But then $\lambda_n < -\lambda_2 = -k$ cannot hold since a $k$-regular graph cannot have an eigenvalue whose absolute value exceeds $k$ by the Perron-Frobenius theorem.

 The other option is that $\mu_S - \frac{|S|}n \mathbf 1 = \mu_T - \frac{|T|}n \mathbf 1$, which together with $|S| = |T|$ implies that $S = T$.

Suppose that $q$ is a prime power and $A_q = \operatorname{AG}(2,q)$ is a Desarguesian affine plane.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item
 First suppose that $q$ is odd.
 We will build an example based on the projective triangle.
 Let $S_q$ denote the set of non-zero squares of $\mathbb F_q$ and $\overline S_q$ the set of non-squares.
 Define
 \begin{align*}
  S &= \{(0,0)\} \cup  \left\{ (x,0) \, \, || \, \, x \in S_q \right \}  \cup  \left\{ (0,y) \, \, || \, \, y \in S_q \right \}  \\
  T &= \{(0,0)\} \cup  \left\{ (x,0) \, \, || \, \, x \in \overline S_q \right \}  \cup  \left\{ (0,y) \, \, || \, \, y \in \overline S_q \right \} 
 \end{align*}
 Then $S$ and $T$ have size $q$ and $|S \cap T| = 1$.
 We will check that $(S,T)$  cross-determines $\frac{q+3}2$ directions.
 Indeed, the axes $X=0$ and $Y=0$ clearly both intersect $S$ and $T$ (in distinct points), hence $(0)$ and $(\infty)$ are cross-determined.
 If we take a point $P \in S$ and a different point $Q \in T$ such that the line $\ell$ spanned by $P$ and $Q$ has a different slope than $(0)$ or $(\infty)$, then the points we chose are $(x,0)$ and $(0,y)$ with one element of $\{x,y\}$ in $S_q$ and the other one in $\overline S_q$.
 Then $\ell$ has slope $(-y/x)$, and $y/x \in \overline S_q$.
 Every element of $-\overline S_q$ occurs as such a slope, so the directions cross-determined by $(S,T)$ are $(0)$, $(\infty)$, and all $(-d)$ with $d \in \overline S_q$, which indeed gives us $\frac{q+3}2$ directions.
 \item Now suppose that $q$ is even. We will build an example based on the projective triad.
 Consider the absolute trace function $\operatorname{Tr}: \mathbb F_q \to \mathbb F_2$ on $\mathbb F_q$.
 Define
 \begin{align*}
 S &= \{0,1\} \times  \left\{ y \in \mathbb F_q \, \, || \, \, \operatorname{Tr}(y) = 0 \right \}  \\
 T &= \{0,1\} \times  \left\{ y \in \mathbb F_q \, \, || \, \, \operatorname{Tr}(y) = 1 \right \} . 
\end{align*}
Then $S$ and $T$ both have size $q$ and are disjoint.
Clearly the lines $X=1$ and $X=0$ intersect both $S$ and $T$, hence $S$ and $T$ cross-determine $(\infty)$.
Now suppose that $P \in S$ and $Q \in T$ are joined by a non-vertical line $\ell$.
Then they have coordinates of the form $(0,y)$ and $(1,z)$ with $\operatorname{Tr}(y) \neq \operatorname{Tr}(z)$.
Hence, $\ell$ has slope $z-y$ which has trace 1.
Conversely, it is easy to check that any direction $(d)$ with $\operatorname{Tr}(d) = 1$ is cross-determined.
Hence, $(S,T)$ cross-determines $\frac q2 + 1$ directions.
\end{enumerate}

\label{df:multiset-lift}
Let $S$ be an integer multiset of points in $\operatorname{AG}(2,q)$, let $\mathbb F_{q^e}$ be a field
extension of $\mathbb F_q$, and consider the trace map
$\operatorname{Tr}\colon\mathbb F_{q^e}\to\mathbb F_q $.
$\operatorname{Lift}(S)$ in $\operatorname{AG}(2,q^e)$ is the integer multiset defined by
\[
    \mu_{\operatorname{Lift}(S)}(X,Y)
    =\mu_S\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(X),\operatorname{Tr}(Y)\bigr).
\]

<!-- lm-2: body_md -->
\label{lm:lift-equivariance}
Suppose that $M\in\operatorname{GL}(2,q)$. Then, for every integer multiset $S$ in
$\operatorname{AG}(2,q)$,
\[
    \operatorname{Lift}(S^M)=\operatorname{Lift}(S)^M.
\]
More generally, lifts of affinely equivalent integer multisets
are affinely equivalent.
<!-- end lm-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
For $v\in\mathbb F_{q^e}^2$, the multiplicity of $v$ in $\operatorname{Lift}(S)^M$ is
\[
    \mu_{\operatorname{Lift}(S)}(M^{-1}v)
    =\mu_S\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(M^{-1}v)\bigr).
\]
Since the entries of $M^{-1}$ belong to $\mathbb F_q$, the trace map commutes with
$M^{-1}$. Hence this equals
\[
    \mu_S\bigl(M^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}(v)\bigr)
    =\mu_{S^M}\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(v)\bigr),
\]
which is the multiplicity of $v$ in $\operatorname{Lift}(S^M)$.

For translations, let $b\in\mathbb F_q^2$ and choose
$\widetilde b\in\mathbb F_{q^e}^2$ with $\operatorname{Tr}(\widetilde b)=b$,
which is possible by the surjectivity of the trace map.
Then $\operatorname{Tr}(v-\widetilde b)=\operatorname{Tr}(v)-b$, so
\[
    \operatorname{Lift}(S+b)=\operatorname{Lift}(S)+\widetilde b.
\]
Together with the linear case, this proves the affine statement.

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:lift-directions}
Suppose that $S$ is an integer multiset in $\operatorname{AG}(2,q)$. Then $\operatorname{Lift}(S)$ in
$\operatorname{AG}(2,q^e)$ has the same inequidistributed directions as $S$. In particular, $\operatorname{Lift}(S)$ is equidistributed from every direction in
$\mathbb F_{q^e}\setminus\mathbb F_q$.
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
First let $(d)$ be a direction in $\mathbb F_q\cup\{\infty\}$. If $\ell$ has
direction $(\infty)$ and equation $X=a$, and $\ell'$ is the line
$X=\operatorname{Tr}(a)$ in $\operatorname{AG}(2,q)$, then
\[
    |\operatorname{Lift}(S)\cap\ell|=q^{e-1}|S\cap\ell'|.
\]
The assertion follows for $(\infty)$ and hence, by Lemma 
\ref{lm:lift-equivariance}, for every $(d)$ with $d\in\mathbb F_q$.

Now let  $\ell$ be a line  with slope $d \in \mathbb F_{q^e}\setminus\mathbb F_q$, and hence equation
$Y=dX+b$ for some $b\in\mathbb F_{q^e}$. Then $\ell$ is an
$e$-dimensional affine $\mathbb F_q$-subspace.
Define for $(\alpha,\beta) \in \mathbb F_q^2$, $B_{\alpha,\beta} = \operatorname{Lift}(\{(\alpha,\beta)\})$.
The equations
$\operatorname{Tr}(X)=\alpha$ and $\operatorname{Tr}(Y)=\beta$ define affine hyperplanes when we
view $\mathbb F_{q^e}^2$ as an $\mathbb F_q$-vector space. Neither of these
hyperplanes is parallel to $\ell$, since $\ell$ has points with every
$X$-coordinate and every $Y$-coordinate. Therefore, either
$\ell\cap B_{\alpha,\beta}$ is an $(e-2)$-dimensional affine
$\mathbb F_q$-subspace, or the restrictions of the equations
\[
    \operatorname{Tr}(X)=\alpha
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    \operatorname{Tr}(Y)=\operatorname{Tr}(dX+b)=\beta
\]
to $\ell$ define parallel, possibly equal, hyperplanes. The latter
happens if and only if $\operatorname{Tr}(X)$ and $\operatorname{Tr}(dX)$ are $\mathbb F_q$-linearly dependent
functionals. 
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         
This happens
if and only if $d\in\mathbb F_q$. We conclude that
\[
    |\ell\cap B_{\alpha,\beta}|=q^{e-2}.
\]

By definition, $\mu_{\operatorname{Lift}(S)}$ is constant on each block
$B_{\alpha,\beta}$, with value $\mu_S(\alpha,\beta)$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
    |\ell\cap\operatorname{Lift}(S)|
    &=
    \sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb F_q}
    \mu_S(\alpha,\beta)|\ell\cap B_{\alpha,\beta}| \\
    &=
    q^{e-2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb F_q}\mu_S(\alpha,\beta)
    =q^{e-2}|S|.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $\operatorname{Lift}(S)$ is equidistributed from $(d)$.

<!-- rmk-4: body_md -->
\label{rmk:shifting}
Suppose that $D$ is non-empty and let $(d)\in D$. Since the lines of
direction $(d)$ partition the affine plane,
\[
    \mathbf 1=\sum_{\substack{\ell\in\mathcal L\\\operatorname{dir}(\ell)=(d)}}\mu_\ell

\[

\label{thm:integer-three-directions}
Let $p$ be prime and $q=p^h$. Suppose that $S$ is an integer multiset
in $\operatorname{AG}(2,q)$ that is inequidistributed from exactly three directions.
Then there exists an affine transformation $\varphi$ of $\operatorname{AG}(2,q)$ such that either $\mu_{S^\varphi} \in \mathbb Z \mathcal L( \left \{ (\infty),(0),(1) \right \} )$ or
\[
 \mu_{S^\varphi} \in \mu_{\operatorname{Lift}(K)} + \mathbb Z \mathcal L( \left \{ (\infty),(0),(1) \right \} ).
\]
<!-- end thm-8 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
We may apply a first affine transformation so that $S$ is
inequidistributed from $(\infty)$, $(0)$, and $(1)$.
For $t\in\mathbb F_q$, let $\ell_\infty(t)$, $\ell_0(t)$, and $\ell_1(t)$ denote
the lines $X=t$, $Y=t$, and $X-Y=t$, respectively, and let $f_d^*(t)$ denote the intersection number of $S$ with $\ell_d(t)$, i.e. 
\[
    f_d^*(t)=\sum_{P\in\ell_d(t)}\mu_S(P),
    \qquad d\in\{\infty,0,1\}.
\]

All other directions are equidistributed and hence have intersection number $|S|/q$. Counting, with
multiplicity, the points of $S$ on the lines through $(x,y)$ gives
\[
    |S| + q\mu_S(x,y)
    =f_\infty^*(x)+f_0^*(y)+f_1^*(x-y) + (q-2) \frac{|S|}{q}.
             
                                   
\]
Let $\lambda: =\frac{2|S|}{q}$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qmu-relation}
    q\mu_S(x,y)
    =f_\infty^*(x)+f_0^*(y)+f_1^*(x-y)  
    -\lambda.
\end{equation}
Here $\lambda$ is an integer: if $q>2$, this follows from the existence of an equidistributed direction, while for $q=2$ we have $\lambda=|S|$.

For $a\in\mathbb Z$, write $[a]_q$ for its residue class in $\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z$.
Reducing \eqref{eq:qmu-relation} modulo $q$ and subtracting its special
case $x=y=0$ gives
\[
    [f_\infty^*(x)-f_\infty^*(0)]_q
    +[f_0^*(y)-f_0^*(0)]_q
    +[f_1^*(x-y)-f_1^*(0)]_q=[0]_q.
\]
Define $g_d(t)=[f_d^*(t)-f_d^*(0)]_q\in\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:g-relation}
    g_\infty(x)+g_0(y)+g_1(x-y)=[0]_q.
\end{equation}
Setting $y=0$ in \eqref{eq:g-relation} gives
$g_\infty(x)=-g_1(x)$, while setting $x=0$ gives
$g_0(y)=-g_1(-y)$. Substituting these identities back into
\eqref{eq:g-relation}, we obtain
\[
    g_1(x-y)=g_1(x)+g_1(-y)
\] 
for all $x,y\in\mathbb F_q$, which means that $g_1$ is a group homomorphism from $(\mathbb F_q,+)$ to $(\mathbb Z / q \mathbb Z,+)$. The additive group of $\mathbb F_q$ has exponent $p$, so $pg_1(t)=[0]_q$ for
every $t\in\mathbb F_q$. Therefore, every value of $g_1$ is of the form
$[\frac qp i]_q$ for a unique $i\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$. This defines an
additive, and hence $\mathbb F_p$-linear, map $F\colon\mathbb F_q\to\mathbb F_p$ satisfying
\[
    g_1(t)=\left[\frac qp\,\nu(F(t))\right]_q.
\]
Since $g_1$ is additive, we also have $g_\infty=-g_1$ and $g_0=g_1$.
Moreover, since $F$ is $\mathbb F_p$-linear, it must be of the form $F(t) = \operatorname{Tr}(at)$ for some $a \in \mathbb F_q$.
It follows that there are integer-valued functions
$A_\infty, A_0, A_1 \colon\mathbb F_q\to\mathbb Z$, vanishing at zero, such that
\begin{align}
  \label{Eq:f's and A's}
 \begin{aligned}
    f_\infty^*(x)&=f_\infty^*(0)-\frac qp\nu(\operatorname{Tr}(ax))+q A_\infty(x),\\
    f_0^*(y)&=f_0^*(0)+\frac qp\nu(\operatorname{Tr}(ay))+q A_0(y),\\
    f_1^*(z)&=f_1^*(0)+\frac qp\nu(\operatorname{Tr}(az))+q A_1(z).
\end{aligned}
\end{align}
Substituting these expressions into \eqref{eq:qmu-relation} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:muT}
    \mu_S(x,y)
    =\underbrace{\mu_S(0,0)+A_\infty(x)+A_0(y)+A_1(x-y)}_{\eqqcolon L(x,y)}
     +\frac{\nu(\operatorname{Tr}(ay))-\nu(\operatorname{Tr}(ax))+\nu(\operatorname{Tr}(a(x-y)))}{p}.
\end{equation}
Note that $L \in \mathbb Z \mathcal L(\{(\infty), (0), (1) \})$.
If $a = 0$, then $\mu_S = L$.
Otherwise, let $\varphi: \mathbb F_q^2 \to \mathbb F_q^2: (x,y) \mapsto a(x,y)$.
                                                                       
                                             
Then
\[
\mu_{S^\varphi}=L\circ\varphi^{-1}+\mu_{\operatorname{Lift}(K)}
\]
by Lemma~\ref{lm:KF-identity}.
Since $L\circ\varphi^{-1}\in\mathbb Z\mathcal L(\{(\infty),(0),(1)\})$,
the result follows.

be empty, and $T$ would have only one inequidistributed direction.

parallel to $\ell$ were removed, while every line not parallel to $\ell$

\label{lm:three-skew-subspaces}
The group $\operatorname{GL}(2k,q)$ acts transitively on triples of pairwise skew
$k$-spaces in $\mathbb F_q^{2k}$.
<!-- end lm-4 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Suppose that $K_1,K_2,K_3$ are $k$-spaces of $\mathbb F_q^{2k}$, any two of which
intersect trivially. Choose a basis $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ of $K_1$. For every
$i$, the $(k+1)$-spaces $\left \langle v_i,K_2 \right \rangle$ and $\left \langle v_i,K_3 \right \rangle$ intersect
in a $2$-space through $v_i$. Choose non-zero vectors $v_{k+i}\in K_2$ and $u_i\in K_3$
in this $2$-space. After rescaling $v_i$ and $v_{k+i}$, we may assume that
\[
    u_i=v_i+v_{k+i}.
\]
The vectors $u_1,\ldots,u_k$ are linearly independent: if
$\sum_i\gamma_i u_i=0$, then
\[
    \sum_i\gamma_i v_i=-\sum_i\gamma_i v_{k+i}.
\]
The left-hand side belongs to $K_1$ and the right-hand side to $K_2$; since
$K_1\cap K_2=\{0\}$, all $\gamma_i$ are zero. Similarly,
$v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{2k}$ are linearly independent. Consequently, there is a
basis $v_1,\ldots,v_{2k}$ such that
\[
\begin{aligned}
    K_1&=\left \langle v_1,\ldots,v_k \right \rangle,\\
    K_2&=\left \langle v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{2k} \right \rangle,\\
    K_3&=\left \langle v_1+v_{k+1},\ldots,v_k+v_{2k} \right \rangle.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $\operatorname{GL}(2k,q)$ acts transitively on bases, it acts transitively on such
triples.

\label{Lm:Cov}
 Given two integer multisets $S$ and $T$ of points in a $2-(v,k,\lambda)$ design $(\mathcal P,\mathcal B,I)$,
 \[
  \Gamma(S,T) = (r-\lambda) \sum_{P \in \mathcal P} \mu_S(P) \mu_T(P) - \frac rv \frac{v-k}{v-1} |S| |T|.
 \]
 If $S$ and $T$ are ordinary sets, this simplifies to $\Gamma(S,T) = (r - \lambda) |S \cap T| - \frac rv \frac{v-k}{v-1} |S| |T|$.
<!-- end lm-5 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
By expanding the sum in the definition of covariance, we find
 \begin{align*}
  \Gamma(S,T) = \sum_{B \in \mathcal B} |S \cap B| |T \cap B| - |S| \frac kv \sum_{B \in \mathcal B} |T \cap B| - |T| \frac kv \sum_{B \in \mathcal B} |S \cap B| + b |S| |T| \frac{k^2}{v^2}.
 \end{align*}
Note that $\sum_{B \in \mathcal B} |S \cap B|$ counts incident pairs $(P,B) \in I$, each with weight $\mu_S(P)$. 
Counting in another way, this gives $|S|r$.
Similarly, $\sum_{B \in \mathcal B} |T \cap B| = |T|r$.
In addition, $\frac kv = \frac rb$, hence $b \frac{k^2}{v^2} = \frac{rk}v$.
Lastly, 
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{B \in \mathcal B} |S \cap B| |T \cap B|
 &= \sum_{B \in \mathcal B} \left( \sum_{P \in B} \mu_S(P) \right) \left( \sum_{Q \in B} \mu_T(Q) \right) 
 = \sum_{P \in \mathcal P} \mu_S(P) \sum_{Q \in \mathcal P} \mu_T(Q) | \left\{ B \in \mathcal B \, \, || \, \, P,Q \; I \; B \right \} | \\
 &= \sum_{P \in \mathcal P} \mu_S(P) \left( \mu_T(P) r + \sum_{Q \in \mathcal P \setminus \{P\}} \mu_T(Q) \lambda \right)
 = \sum_{P \in \mathcal P} \mu_S(P) \left( \mu_T(P) (r- \lambda) + |T| \lambda \right) \\
 &= |S| |T| \lambda + (r-\lambda) \sum_{P \in \mathcal P} \mu_S(P) \mu_T(P)
\end{align*}
Combining this, we obtain
\[
 \Gamma(S,T) = (r-\lambda) \sum_{P \in \mathcal P} \mu_S(P) \mu_T(P) + \left( \lambda - \frac{rk}v \right) |S||T|.
\]
Note that $\lambda = \frac{r(k-1)}{v-1}$, so
\[
 \lambda - \frac{rk}v = r \left( \frac{k-1}{v-1} - \frac kv \right) = -\frac rv \frac{v-k}{v-1}.
\]
This yields the claimed equality.
