If both \(q,q' > 0\), there exists a ring isomorphism \(f : H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q'};\mathbb{Z}) \to H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q};\mathbb{Z})\) and a \(\mathbb{C}\)-algebra isomorphism \(f_{\mathbb{C}} : H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q'};\mathbb{C}) \to H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q};\mathbb{C})\) such that the natural square

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
We call \emph{generalized Calabi--Eckmann} threefold of parameter \(q \in \mathbb{N}\) the LVMB manifold \(N_q\) constructed as above. The natural projection \(\pi : N_q \to \mathbb{F}_{q}\) is a holomorphic principal \(\mathbb{T}^{1}\)-bundle.
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
According to Definition 1 in \cite{Thiella2026preprint}, all the LVMB manifolds \(N_q\) are regular. This is because the initial fan datum \(\Sigma_q\) is smooth for every \(q \in \mathbb{N}\).
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\(N_q \cong N_{q'}\) if and only if \(q = q' \in \mathbb{N}\).
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
There also exist anti-Dolbeault and Aeppli cohomologies. The first is isomorphic to Dolbeault up to conjugation: namely \(H^{s,t}_\partial(N_q) \cong \overline{H^{t,s}_{\bar\partial}(N_q)}\). Aeppli cohomology, instead, is defined in bidegree \((s,t)\) as
    \begin{equation*}
        H^{s,t}_\text{Ae}(N_q) = \frac{\ker(\partial{\bar\partial} : \mathcal{A}^{s,t}_{N_q} \to \mathcal{A}^{s+1,t+1}_{N_q})}{\operatorname{Im} (\partial: \mathcal{A}^{s-1,t}_{N_q} \to \mathcal{A}^{s,t}_{N_q}) + \operatorname{Im} ({\bar\partial}: \mathcal{A}^{s,t-1}_{N_q} \to \mathcal{A}^{s,t}_{N_q}) }.
    \end{equation*}
    By \cite{Schweitzer2007preprint}, in the compact case the antilinear Hodge-\(\star\)-operator induces an isomorphism
    \begin{equation*}
        H^{s,t}_\text{Ae}(N_q) \cong \overline{H^{3-s,3-t}_\text{BC}(N_q)}.
    \end{equation*}
    Therefore, anti-Dolbeault and Aeppli cohomologies do not carry more information about \(N_q\) than Dolbeault and Bott--Chern cohomologies.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{real_cohomology_model}
    Applying the Leray--Serre spectral sequence directly to \(\mathbb{T}^{1} \hookrightarrow N_q \to \mathbb{F}_{q}\), we obtain a model \((\mathcal{M}_\mathbb{Z}^\bullet,d_{\mathcal{M}})\) for the singular cohomology of \(N_q\). Namely, it is given by
    \begin{equation*}
        (\mathcal{M}_\mathbb{Z}^\bullet, d_{\mathcal{M}}) \coloneqq (\Lambda[\theta_1, \theta_2] \otimes H^*(\mathbb{F}_{q};\mathbb{Z}), d)
    \end{equation*}
    with \(\theta_1 = \frac{1}{2}(\xi + \bar\xi)\), \(\theta_2 = -\frac{i}{2} (\xi-\bar\xi)\) and \(d\theta_1 = w_1\), \(d\theta_2 = w_4\). Notice that tensoring this with \(\mathbb{C}\) and taking the projection onto bidegrees provides the bigraded model \(\mathcal{M}^{\bullet,\bullet}\), and \(\mathcal{M}_\mathbb{Z}^\bullet\) is then the restriction to integral classes.

    In particular, the classes \(\theta_1 \wedge w_1\) and \((q \theta_1 + \theta_2) \wedge w_4\) are integral; and for \(q > 0\) we can rewrite the generators of \(H^3(N_q;\mathbb{Z})\) as
    \begin{equation*}
        H^3(N_q;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \left\langle \operatorname{\mathrm{Re}}\left( \frac{q - 2i}{q} \alpha\right), \operatorname{\mathrm{Im}}\left(2\frac{q^2 +1}{q} \alpha\right)\right\rangle.
    \end{equation*}
<!-- end remark-3 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
One can analogously define the Bott--Chern cohomology with coefficients in any subring \(R \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}\)  by replacing \(\mathbb{Z}(s)\) with \(R\) and changing \(Z\) accordingly.  Of course, the inclusion of rings induces an injection of the Bott--Chern complexes \(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;R} \hookrightarrow \mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{C}}\) that, in turn, induces a natural map in cohomology \(H^{s,t}_\text{BC}(X;R) \to H^{s,t}_\text{BC}(X;\mathbb{C})\) that is not injective in general.
<!-- end remark-4 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{0_and_top_BC_cohomology}
    Let \(X\) be an \(n\)-dimensional compact complex manifold. If \(X\) is connected and \(H^{2n-1}(X;\mathbb{Z}) = 0\), then \(H^{0,0}_\text{BC}(X;\mathbb{Z}) \cong H^{n,n}_\text{BC}(X;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}\).
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Since \(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{0,0;\mathbb{Z}} = \mathbb{Z}\), we trivially have \(H^{0,0}_\text{BC}(X;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}\). On the other hand, as by compactness \(H^{2n}(X;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}\), in total degree \(2n\) the hypercohomology long exact sequence described above gives
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{tikzcd}
            0 \ar[r] & H^{n,n}_\text{BC}(X,\mathbb{Z}) \ar[r] & \mathbb{C} \ar[r] & \frac{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{Z}} \ar[r] & 0
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation*}
    as \(H^{2n-1}\left(X;\frac{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{Z}}\right) \cong H^{2n-1}(X;\mathbb{Z}) \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \frac{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{Z}} = 0\). The statement then follows immediately.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
In general, the top degree Bott--Chern cohomology is not isomorphic to \(\mathbb{Z}\). Indeed, if \(\kappa : H^{2n-1}(X;\mathbb{Z}) \otimes \frac{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{Z}} \to H^{n,n}_\text{BC}(X,\mathbb{Z})\) is the connecting morphism in degree \(2n-1\), we only have the extension of groups
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{tikzcd}
            0 \ar[r] & \operatorname{Im}\kappa \ar[r] & H^{n,n}_\text{BC}(X,\mathbb{Z}) \ar[r] & \mathbb{Z} \ar[r] & 0.
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation*}
    Since the first is a quotient of a divisible group, it is divisible as well; thus the extension is trivial and so
    \begin{equation*}
        H^{n,n}_\text{BC}(X,\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \operatorname{Im}\kappa.
    \end{equation*}
<!-- end remark-5 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
Let \(f : (A^\bullet,d_A) \to (B^\bullet,d_B)\) be a morphism of chain complexes. The \emph{mapping cone} of \(f\) is the chain complex \((\operatorname{cone}(f)^\bullet, d_f)\) with
    \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname{cone}(f)^n = A^{n+1} \oplus B^n = (A^\bullet[-1] \oplus B^\bullet)^n,
    \end{equation*}
    and the differential given by the matrix
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            -d_A & 0 \\
            -f & d_B
        \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation*}
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
We chose this sign convention to match that of bicomplexes. Indeed, as their squares \emph{anticommute}, this is the natural choice so that the mapping cone of a chain map \(f : A^\bullet \to B^\bullet\) defines a double complex
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{tikzcd}
            \cdots \ar[r] & A^\ell \ar[r,"-d_A^\ell"] \ar[d,"-f^{\ell+1}"'] & A^{\ell+1} \ar[d,"-f^{\ell+1}"] \ar[r] & \cdots\\
            \cdots \ar[r] & B^\ell \ar[r, "d_B^\ell"] & B^{\ell+1} \ar[r] &\cdots
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation*}
    Nevertheless, choosing anticommutative squares for double complexes breaks the trivial identification with chain complexes of chain complexes (whose squares are commutative instead). To restore it, we need to introduce alternating signs: namely, if \(((A^{\bullet_1}, d_{A^{\bullet_1}})^{\bullet_2}, f^{\bullet_2})\) is a chain complex of chain complexes, the associated bicomplex is \((A^{\bullet,\bullet}, d_h, d_v)\), with \(A^{k,\ell} = (A^{\ell})^k\) and the differentials 
    \begin{equation*}
        d_h^{k,\ell} = d_{A^k}^\ell : A^{k,\ell} \to A^{k,\ell+1}, \quad\text{while} \quad d_v^{k,\ell} = (-1)^\ell f^\ell : A^{k,\ell} \to A^{k+1,\ell}.
    \end{equation*}
    Keeping in mind this identification, henceforth we will not draw a sharp distinction between complexes (and so in particular morphisms) of chain complexes and bicomplexes.
<!-- end remark-6 -->
<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
A chain bicomplex \((C^{\bullet,\bullet}, d_h, d_v)\) determines two chain complexes:
    \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname{Tot}^\Pi(C)^k = \prod_{p+q = k} C^{p,q}, \qquad \operatorname{Tot}^\oplus(C)^k = \bigoplus_{p+q = k} C^{p,q}
    \end{equation*}
    endowed with the natural differentials. The identity \(d^2 = 0\) follows from \(d_h \circ d_v + d_v \circ d_h = 0\). Notice that, whenever the anti-diagonals of \(C^{\bullet,\bullet}\) are bounded, the two constructions of total complexes coincide. In particular this holds for bicomplexes contained in a quadrant, as well as for those contained in a strip.
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
This behavior has no analogue in de~Rham cohomology. Indeed, by the de~Rham Theorem and the Universal Coefficients Theorem, the de Rham cohomology of a manifold is isomorphic to the singular cohomology with coefficients in \(\mathbb{R}\) (or \(\mathbb{C}\) in the complex case); thus any class can be rescaled so that it lies in the singular cohomology with integral coefficients. Here, instead, we have the Bott--Chern class \([\alpha]_\text{BC}\) that cannot be rescaled to any integral class when \(q > 0\). This happens because the canonical map
    \begin{equation*}
        H^{2,1}_\text{BC}(N_q;\mathbb{C}) \to H^3(N_q;\mathbb{C}),
    \end{equation*}
    that is injective by \zcref{fig:the_model_q_not_0}, identifies the Bott--Chern cohomology group with a complex subspace of \(H^3(N_q;\mathbb{C})\) that has trivial intersection with the image of the inclusion \(H^3(N_q;\mathbb{Z}) \hookrightarrow H^3(N_q;\mathbb{C})\) induced by the Universal Coefficients Theorem. With a slight modification of this argument, the same behavior can be observed in Deligne cohomology as well. For this cohomological theory, see e.g. \cite{Brylinski2008}.
<!-- end remark-7 -->

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
Let \(N_q, N_{q'}\) be two generalized Calabi--Eckmann threefolds with \(q,q' > 0\). Then \(H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_q;\mathbb{Z}) \cong H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q'};\mathbb{Z})\) as bigraded rings.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
From \zcref[noname]{Hodge_diamond_integral_BC_N_q} and the compatibility between integral and usual Bott--Chern cohomologies, an isomorphism of bigraded \(\mathbb{Z}\)-modules \(f : H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q'};\mathbb{Z}) \to H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q};\mathbb{Z})\) defines a ring isomorphism if it preserves the restriction of the cup product map to:
    \begin{equation*}
         H^{1,1}_\text{BC}(N_q;\mathbb{Z}) \otimes H^{1,1}_\text{BC}(N_q;\mathbb{Z}) \to H^{2,2}_\text{BC}(N_q;\mathbb{Z}).
    \end{equation*}
    Identifying \(\mathbb{Z}(s)\) with \(\mathbb{Z}\) in each degree, \(H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_q;\mathbb{Z})\) can be represented as the diamond
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{array}{ccccccc}
            &&& 1 &&& \\
            && 0 && 0 && \\
            & 0 && \left\langle w_1,w_4\right\rangle_\mathbb{C} && 0 & \\
            0 && 0 && 0 && 0 \\
            & 0 && \left\langle x \right\rangle_\frac{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{Z}} \oplus \left\langle y \right\rangle_\frac{\mathbb{C}}{q\mathbb{Z}}  && 0 & \\
            && \left\langle \bar\nu \right\rangle_\mathbb{C} && \left\langle \nu \right\rangle_\mathbb{C} && \\
            &&& \left\langle \operatorname{vol} \right\rangle_\mathbb{Z} &&&
        \end{array}
    \end{equation*}
    where we have adopted the more compact notation \(\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_M \coloneqq \left\langle\cdot\right\rangle \otimes_\mathbb{Z} M\). For \(H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q'};\mathbb{Z})\) we take a similar set of generators \(\{1', w_1',w_4', x',y', \bar\nu',\operatorname{vol}'\}\) together with their complex conjugates. We define an isomorphism of \(\mathbb{Z}\)-modules \(f : H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q};\mathbb{Z}) \to H^{*,*}_\text{BC}(N_{q'};\mathbb{Z})\), on generators of bidegree \((s,t)\) with \(s \geq t\) as
    \begin{gather*}
        f(1) = 1',\quad f(w_1) = p w_1', \quad f(w_4) = p w_4',\\
        f(x) = x', \quad f(y) = p^2 y',\quad f(\bar\nu) = \bar\nu',\quad f(\operatorname{vol}) = \operatorname{vol}',
    \end{gather*}
    where \(p\) is such that \(p^2 = \frac{q'}{q}\). The non-trivial algebraic relations among the generators of both sets are:
    \begin{gather*}
        w_1\smile w_1 = 0,\quad  w_1 \smile w_4 = y, \quad w_4 \smile w_4 = -q y = 0 \in \left\langle y\right\rangle \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \frac{\mathbb{C}}{q \mathbb{Z}}.
    \end{gather*}
    The conclusion then follows observing that these are preserved by \(f\), as we now verify:
    \begin{align*}
        f(w_1 \smile w_4) &= f(y) = p^2 y' = p^2 w_1' \smile w_4' = f(w_1) \smile f(w_4),\\
        f(w_1 \smile w_1) &= 0 = p^2 w_1' \smile w_1' = f(w_1) \smile f(w_1),\\
        f(w_4 \smile w_4) &= 0 = p^2 w_4' \smile w_4' = f(w_4) \smile f(w_4).\qedhere
    \end{align*}

<!-- remark-8: body_md -->
We may ask whether a refined version of the Universal Coefficients Theorem can be exploited to recover information about the map
    \begin{equation*}
        H^{s,t}_\text{BC}(X;\mathbb{Z}) \longrightarrow H^{s,t}_\text{BC}(X;\mathbb{C})
    \end{equation*}
    connecting the integral Bott--Chern cohomology of a complex manifold \(X\) with the usual Bott--Chern cohomology. The answer is surprisingly nontrivial. Indeed, on a compact complex manifold the sheaf (hyper)cohomology functor commutes with all filtered colimits \cite[Thm.~4.12.1]{Godement1998}, thus we can apply the result in \cite{Kahn2024preprint} to obtain such a generalization of the Universal Coefficients Theorem. Nevertheless, this does not behave as one might naively expect. Indeed, for the Bott--Chern complex, we obtain the short exact sequence
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{tikzcd}
            0 \ar[r] & \mathbb{H}^k(X, \mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}}) \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C} \ar[r] &  \mathbb{H}^k(X, \mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}} \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C} ) \ar[r] & \mathbb{H}^{k+2}(X,\operatorname{Tor}(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C})) = 0
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation*}
    where the last term vanishes as \(\mathbb{C}\) is flat over \(\mathbb{Z}\). However, \(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}} \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C}\) is very far from being quasi-isomorphic to \(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{C}}\). This can be observed by computing, for example, the first cohomology sheaf:
    \begin{equation*}
        \mathcal{H}^1(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{C}}) = \frac{\ker (\mathcal{O} \oplus \overline{\mathcal{O}} \xrightarrow{\partial \oplus {\bar\partial}} \Omega^1 \oplus \overline{\Omega^1})}{\operatorname{Im} (\underline\mathbb{C} \xrightarrow{(1,-1)} \mathcal{O} \oplus \overline{\mathcal{O}})} \cong \frac{\underline{\mathbb{C}}^2}{\underline\mathbb{C}(e_1 - e_2)} \cong \underline\mathbb{C};
    \end{equation*}
    whose stalks are thus \(1\)-dimensional complex vector spaces. On the other hand,
    \begin{equation*}
        \mathcal{H}^1(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}}) = \frac{\ker (\mathcal{O} \oplus \overline{\mathcal{O}} \xrightarrow{\partial \oplus {\bar\partial}} \Omega^1 \oplus \overline{\Omega^1})}{\operatorname{Im} (\underline{\mathbb{Z}(s)} \xrightarrow{(1,-1)}\mathcal{O} \oplus \overline{\mathcal{O}})} \cong \frac{\underline\mathbb{C}^2}{\underline\mathbb{Z}(e_1-e_2)}
    \end{equation*}
    as \(\mathbb{Z}\)-modules. Since \((-) \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C}\) is exact, one has
    \begin{equation*}
        \mathcal{H}^1(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}} \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C}) \cong \mathcal{H}^1(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}}) \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C} \cong \frac{\underline\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C}}{\underline\mathbb{Z}(e_1-e_2) \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C}}.
    \end{equation*}
    Since \(\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{C}\) is a complex vector space whose dimension is the cardinality of the continuum, while \(\mathbb{Z}(e_1-e_2) \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C}\) is a complex line, the stalks of \(\mathcal{H}^1(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}} \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C})\) cannot be isomorphic to those of \(\mathcal{H}^1(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{C}})\); this obstructs any quasi-isomorphism between \(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{Z}} \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C}\) and \(\mathcal{B}^\bullet_{s,t;\mathbb{C}}\).

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{GCE_are_distinct_cohomological}
    Let \(N_q\) and \(N_{q'}\) be two generalized Calabi--Eckmann threefolds; let \(c,c'\) be the maps in cohomology constructed as above. Then \(c \cong c'\) if and only if \(q = q'\).

<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We consider the short exact sequence
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{tikzcd}
            0 \ar[r] & \operatorname{Coim}c \ar[r] & H^{1,2}_{{\bar\partial}}(N_q) \oplus H^{2,1}_{\partial}(N_q) \ar[r] & H^{2,2}_\text{BC}(N_q;\mathbb{Z}) \ar[r] & 0,
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation*}
    where 
    \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname{Coim}c = \frac{H^3(N_q;\mathbb{Z}(2))}{\ker c} \cong
        \begin{cases}
            \left\langle \theta_1 \wedge w_1 \right\rangle & \text{if } q = 0,\\
            \left\langle \theta_1 \wedge w_1, (q\theta_1 + \theta_2) \wedge w_4 \right\rangle & \text{if } q > 0.
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    Since \(c = (\pi^{1,2}, - \pi^{2,1})\) and \(\theta_1 \wedge w_4 - \theta_2 \wedge w_1\) is \(d\)-exact, the coimage is mapped through \(c\) to the group
    \begin{equation*}
        c(\operatorname{Coim} c) = \left\langle \frac{1}{2} \bar\xi \wedge w_1 - \frac{1}{2} \xi \wedge w_1, \frac{1}{2}(iq -1) \bar\xi \wedge w_1 + \frac{1}{2} (iq + 1) \xi \wedge w_1\right\rangle \subseteq H^{1,2}_{{\bar\partial}}(N_q) \oplus H^{2,1}_{\partial}(N_q).
    \end{equation*}
    We fix \(\{\frac{1}{2} \bar\xi \wedge w_1, \frac{1}{2} \xi \wedge w_1\}\) as a basis for \(H^{1,2}_{{\bar\partial}}(N_q) \oplus H^{2,1}_{\partial}(N_q) \cong \mathbb{C}^2\); with respect to it, we can see \(H^{2,2}_\text{BC}(N_q,\mathbb{Z})\) as the quotient \(\mathbb{C}^2/\Gamma_q\) where \(\Gamma_q\) is the \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module generated by \( \{e_1 - e_2, (iq-1) e_1 + (iq + 1) e_2\}\). The isomorphism of maps \(c \cong c'\), yields an isomorphism of their images \(\Gamma_q\) and \(\Gamma_{q'}\) as subgroups of \(\mathbb{C}^2\). This may happen only if there exists \(A \in \operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z}[i])\) such that
    \begin{equation*}
        M_{\Gamma_{q'}} \coloneqq
        \begin{bmatrix}
            1 & iq' - 1\\
            -1 & iq' + 1
        \end{bmatrix}
        =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            1 & iq - 1\\
            -1 & iq + 1
        \end{bmatrix}
        A \eqqcolon M_{\Gamma_q} A.
    \end{equation*}
    Since \(|\det A| = 1\), 
    \begin{equation*}
        4 q'^2 = |\det M_{\Gamma_{q'}}|^2 = |\det M_{\Gamma_{q}}|^2 = 4 q^2,
    \end{equation*}
    whence \(q = q'\) since they are both taken non-negative. The other implication is trivial.
