<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:explicit-sequence}
For every $n\ge3$, the maps \eqref{eq:intro-family} are closed connected homogeneous minimal embeddings, linearly full in $\mathbb S^{2n+1}$. Their fundamental matrices have constant spectrum
\[
\left\{n(1-n\rho),(2n\rho)^{[n-1]},0\right\},
\]
where the bracketed superscript denotes multiplicity. In particular,
\[
S=n+n(n-2)\rho,\qquad Q=n+n^2\rho.

The additional eigenvalues of the fundamental matrix are zero.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:rank-one}
Let $n\ge3$. If a connected minimal immersion has $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal A=1$ everywhere, its image lies in a fixed totally geodesic $\mathbb S^{n+1}$. If the immersion is complete and $S\equiv n$, it is a covering immersion of a standard Clifford hypersurface
\[
C_p=\mathbb S^p\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right)\times
   \mathbb S^{n-p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n-p}{n}}\right),\qquad 1\le p\le n-1.
\]
In particular, if $M$ is closed, $q\ge1$, $\lambda_2\equiv0$, and $Q>0$ is constant, the classical constant-$S$ hypersurface second gap theorem applies.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Write $h=A\nu$ locally, where $A\ne0$ is trace-free and $\nu$ spans the first normal line. If $\eta\perp\nu$ and $\theta(X)=\langle \nabla_X^\perp\nu,\eta\rangle$, the Codazzi equation in the $\eta$ direction gives $A_{ij}\theta_k=A_{ik}\theta_j$. If $\theta\ne0$ at a point, choose the first coframe vector in its direction. All columns of $A$ except the first then vanish. Symmetry and the trace-free condition give $A=0$, a contradiction. Thus the first normal line is parallel. The rank-$(n+2)$ subbundle $E=\operatorname{span}\{F,dF(TM),\nu\}$ of the trivial Euclidean ambient bundle is preserved by $D$, by the Gauss and Weingarten formulas. Its orthogonal projection is therefore constant. Since the domain is connected, $E$ is a fixed ambient subspace. Assume now that the immersion is complete and $S\equiv n$. Since the fundamental matrix has rank one, the scalar Simons identity reduces to
\[
0=|\nabla h|^2+nS-S^2=|\nabla h|^2.
\]
Thus $h$ is parallel. On the complete simply connected cover choose a unit normal $\eta$. Its shape operator $A$ is parallel and trace-free, with squared norm $n$. The eigendistributions of $A$ are parallel, so a plane spanned by vectors in distinct eigendistributions has zero sectional curvature. The Gauss equation gives $\lambda\mu=-1$ for every pair of distinct eigenvalues. There must be exactly two, with multiplicities $p,m=n-p$ and values $\lambda=-\sqrt{\frac{m}{p}}$, $\mu=\sqrt{\frac{p}{m}}$, after choosing the sign of $\eta$. Let $E_\lambda,E_\mu$ denote the corresponding eigendistributions. Define
\[
F_1=\frac{\eta+\mu F}{\mu-\lambda},\qquad
F_2=\frac{-\eta-\lambda F}{\mu-\lambda}.
\]
Their differentials are the tangent projections onto the two eigenbundles. Moreover, the two maps are pointwise orthogonal, with $|F_1|^2=\frac{p}{n}$, $|F_2|^2=\frac{m}{n}$. The spaces $\operatorname{span}\{F_1,E_\lambda\}$ and $\operatorname{span}\{F_2,E_\mu\}$ are fixed orthogonal Euclidean subspaces: for example,
\[
D_UZ=\nabla_UZ+\langle U,Z\rangle(\lambda\eta-F),\qquad
\lambda\eta-F=-(1+\lambda^2)F_1
\quad (Z\in E_\lambda).
\]
Thus the lifted immersion is a local isometry onto $C_p$. A local isometry from a complete connected manifold into a connected Riemannian manifold is a surjective covering, as follows by extending geodesic and path lifts. Since the original immersion has the same image and is also a complete local isometry into $C_p$, it too is a surjective covering. The simply connected lifted source is therefore the universal cover of $C_p$. If both factors have dimension at least two, this is the compact product itself. Otherwise it is the full cylinder $\mathbb R\times\mathbb S^{n-1}(\sqrt{(n-1)/n})$.

 =\Delta a-2(a-b)|\omega|^2,
\end{equation}
where the second term arises from the commutator of the normal connection and the off-diagonal components of $\mathcal{A}$.
On the other hand, applying \eqref{eq:simons-tensor} to the first normal direction gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq2}
\frac12(\Delta^\perp\mathcal A)_{11}
   =|T_1|^2+na-a^2-|[A,B]|^2.
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq1} with \eqref{eq2} yields \eqref{eq:eigenvalue-laplacian-general}.

For \eqref{eq:tangent-simons-general}, calculate at a tangent geodesic frame:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta h^1}
(\Delta h)^1=\sum_i\nabla_iT_{1;i}-\sum_i\omega_iT_{2;i}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:commutator-bound}
|[A,B]|^2=\sum_{i,j}(A_{ii}-A_{jj})^2B_{ij}^2\le\operatorname{osc}(A)^2|B|^2\le2ab,

By \eqref{eq:gauss}, the immersion is minimal. To prove injectivity, suppose that two image points agree and put $\delta=t-t'$. Then $e^{i\ell_0\delta}=1$ and $e^{i\ell_1\delta}x=x'$. The latter equality implies that $e^{i\ell_1\delta}=\pm1$, and hence $e^{2i\ell_1\delta}=1$. Since $\gcd(\ell_0,2\ell_1)=1$, $\delta\in2\pi\mathbb Z$ and $x=x'$. The immersion is therefore an embedding. The ambient action
\[
(R,z)(w_0,w)=(z^{\ell_0}w_0,z^{\ell_1}Rw),\qquad (R,z)\in SO(n)\times\mathbb S^1,
\]
is transitive on the image, so all scalar invariants are constant. For linear fullness, suppose that a real linear functional vanishes on the image. Applying it to $F(t,x)$ and $F(t,-x)$, we see that it vanishes on the first complex line and on $\mathbb C^n$. Hence it vanishes on the whole ambient space. We now compute the second fundamental form and the fundamental matrix. Write 
\[u=e^{i\ell_0t},\quad v=e^{i\ell_1t}x,\quad d=\frac{r}{s}, \quad c=\sqrt n\frac{\ell_1}{\ell_0}.\]
 For a local unit frame $Y_j$ on $\mathbb S^{n-1}$, put
\[
e_0=(i\sqrt n r u,i\sqrt n\frac{\ell_1}{\ell_0}s v),\quad e_j=(0,e^{i\ell_1t}Y_j).
\]
These vectors are orthonormal since $(\sqrt n r)^2+(\sqrt n\frac{\ell_1}{\ell_0}s)^2=1$. An orthonormal spherical normal frame is
\[
\eta=(su,-rv),\quad \zeta=(-i\sqrt n\frac{\ell_1}{\ell_0}s u,i\sqrt n r v),\quad
\xi_j=(0,ie^{i\ell_1t}Y_j),\qquad 1\le j\le n-1.
\]
Choose $Y_j$ to be geodesic at the point under consideration. Direct differentiation and the Gauss formula \eqref{eq:gauss} give
\[
\begin{aligned}
h(e_j,e_k)&=d\delta_{jk}\eta, &h(e_0,e_j)&=c\xi_j, &
h(e_0,e_0)&=-(n-1)d\eta.
\end{aligned}
\]
Indeed, $D_{e_j}e_k=-(0,\delta_{jk}\frac{v}{s})$ and $D_{e_0}e_0=(-nru,-n\rho sv)$. Adding the spherical correction $g_{ij}F$ gives the above normal components. Their trace is zero, as required by minimality. Let $E_{ij}$ be the matrix unit with $(E_{ij})_{k\ell}=\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}$, where $i,j,k,\ell\in\{0,\cdots,n-1\}$. Consequently
\[
A_\eta=d\operatorname{diag}(-(n-1),1,\cdots,1),\quad
A_{\xi_j}=c(E_{0j}+E_{j0}),\quad A_\zeta=0.
\]
These shape operators are mutually orthogonal. Since $d^2=\frac{1-n\rho}{n-1}$, the spectrum of $\mathcal{A}$ is
\[
\operatorname{spec}\mathcal A=\{n(1-n\rho),(2n\rho)^{[n-1]},0\}.
\]
Since $2n\rho$ has multiplicity at least two, the second largest eigenvalue is $2n\rho$ throughout the parameter interval, also when $n(1-n\rho)=2n\rho$. Therefore
\[
S=n+n(n-2)\rho,\qquad Q=n+n^2\rho.
\]
The normal bundle is nonflat because $|[A_\eta,A_{\xi_j}]|^2=2n^2c^2d^2>0$. Although one shape operator vanishes, its normal line bundle is not parallel. Indeed,
\[
\nabla^\perp_{e_j}\zeta=d\sqrt n\xi_j\ne0.
\]

\tau=\frac{\sqrt{Q_*-n}}n\in(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt n}).

\[
1\le k_j<\frac{p_j}{\sqrt n}<p_j,\qquad \gcd(p_j,k_j)=1.
\]
Thus $(\ell_0,\ell_1)=(p_j,k_j)$ is admissible, and
\[
Q_j=n+n^2\left(\frac{k_j}{p_j}\right)^2\rightarrow Q_*.
\]
This proves density in $(n,2n)$ while preserving the fixed domain $\mathbb S^1\times\mathbb S^{n-1}$. A totally geodesic inclusion preserves the second fundamental form and adds zero eigenvalues, which proves the assertion for every $q>n+1$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:immersion-compactness}
Fix $n\ge2$, $q\ge1$, and $C_0<\infty$. Let $F_j:(M_j,g_j,x_j)\to\mathbb S^{n+q}(1)$ be complete connected pointed minimal immersions with $|h_j|^2\le C_0$, where $g_j$ is the induced metric. After passing to a subsequence, the immersions converge smoothly on an exhaustion to a complete connected minimal immersion $F_\infty:(M_\infty,g_\infty,x_\infty)\to\mathbb S^{n+q}(1)$. More precisely, there are embeddings $\phi_j:W_j\to M_j$ of relatively compact open sets exhausting $M_\infty$, with $\phi_j(x_\infty)=x_j$, and
\[
\phi_j^*g_j\longrightarrow g_\infty,
\qquad F_j\circ\phi_j\longrightarrow F_\infty
\quad\hbox{in }C^\infty_{\mathrm{loc}}.
\]
For each fixed $R$, the images of these embeddings contain $B_{g_j}(x_j,R)$ for all sufficiently large $j$. In local coordinates obtained by projection onto the tangent spaces, the immersions have uniform derivative estimates of every order, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intrinsic-injectivity-bound}
\operatorname{inj}(M_j,g_j)\ge(C_0+n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.
\end{equation}
If $M_\infty$ is compact, the convergence is eventually global through diffeomorphisms. If the intrinsic diameters of the $M_j$ are uniformly bounded, the limit is compact and the same conclusion holds.

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Put $\Lambda=\sqrt{C_0+n}$. For the immersion into Euclidean space, we have $|\mathrm{II}^E_j|^2=|h_j|^2+n\le C_0+n$, hence $|\mathrm{II}^E_j|\le\Lambda$. The Euclidean Gauss equation implies $\sec_{g_j}\le\Lambda^2$, by the Rauch comparison theorem, the conjugate radius is at least $\frac{\pi}{\Lambda}$. If $\operatorname{inj}_{g_j}(x_0)<\frac{\pi}{\Lambda}$ at a point $x_0\in M_j$, the nearest-cut-point alternative gives a unit-speed geodesic loop $\gamma:[0,L]\to M_j$, smooth away from its basepoint, with $L=2\operatorname{inj}_{g_j}(x_0)$; see \cite[Chapter~5]{Petersen}. For $z=F_j\circ\gamma$ in Euclidean space, since $\gamma$ is a unit-speed geodesic, $|z'| = 1$. Moreover, $z'' = \Pi_j^E(\gamma', \gamma')$ is purely normal, so $|z''| \le \Lambda$ on $(0, L)$. The identity $z(L)=z(0)$ gives
\[
0=\langle z(L)-z(0),z'(0)\rangle
=\int_0^L\langle z'(t),z'(0)\rangle dt.
\]
Using the fundamental theorem of calculus, $z'(t) = z'(0) + \int_0^t z''(s) ds$. Substituting this and swapping the order of integration (Fubini's theorem) yields:
\[
0 = L + \int_0^L (L-s) \langle z''(s), z'(0) \rangle ds.
\]
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, $\langle z''(s), z'(0) \rangle \ge -|z''(s)||z'(0)| \ge -\Lambda$. Therefore, 
\[0 \ge L - \Lambda \int_0^L (L-s) ds = L - \frac{\Lambda L^2}{2},\]
 which implies $L \ge \frac{2}{\Lambda}$. Substituting $L = 2 \text{inj}_{g_j}(x_0)$ gives $\text{inj}_{g_j}(x_0) \ge \frac{1}{\Lambda}= (C_0+n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}$, proving \eqref{eq:intrinsic-injectivity-bound} at every point. This argument uses neither compactness nor embeddedness.

 We next construct graph coordinates of uniform size. Fix $x_0\in M_j$ and identify $T=dF_j(T_{x_0}M_j)$ with $\mathbb R^n$. Let $\pi:\mathbb R^{n+q+1}\to T$ be orthogonal projection and set $P=\pi(F_j-F_j(x_0))$. Along a minimizing geodesic of length at most $R_0=(4\Lambda)^{-1}$, a parallel unit tangent vector, viewed in Euclidean space, changes by at most $\Lambda R_0$. It follows that $dP$ is invertible on $B_{g_j}(x_0,R_0)$ and $\|(dP)^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le2$. Put $\rho=\frac{R_0}{4}$ and $D_\rho=\{x\in T:|x|<\rho\}$. For $x\in D_\rho$, solve
\[
\dot\gamma_x(t)=(dP_{\gamma_x(t)})^{-1}x,
\qquad \gamma_x(0)=x_0,\qquad 0\le t\le1.
\]
Its length is at most $2|x|<\frac{R_0}{2}$. By completeness, the local solution extends to $t=1$. Smooth dependence on the parameter gives $\Psi(x)=\gamma_x(1)$, with $P\circ\Psi=x$. Thus $\Psi$ is a smooth embedding, and $F_j\circ\Psi$ is a graph with uniformly bounded slope. Its image in $M_j$ contains $B_{g_j}(x_0,\rho)$: project a minimizing path of length less than $\rho$ and use uniqueness of its lift through the local inverse of $P$. Thus each graph chart contains an intrinsic neighborhood, even if other sheets of the immersion meet its image. After a rotation about the sphere center, write this graph as $F_j(x)=(x,c_j+u_j(x))$, with $u_j(0)=Du_j(0)=0$ and $|c_j|=1$. Here $c_j,u_j$ take values in $\mathbb R^{q+1}$, and $\alpha=1,\ldots,q+1$ in the graph equation below labels Euclidean vertical coordinates. If $g_{ik}=\delta_{ik}+\partial_i u_j\cdot\partial_k u_j$, the Euclidean graph formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{graph estimate}
|D^2u_j|^2\le(1+|Du_j|^2)^3|\mathrm{II}^E_j|^2.
\end{equation}
Indeed, for the graph frames $T_i=(e_i,\partial_i u_j)$ and $N_\alpha=(-Du_j^Te_\alpha,e_\alpha)$, we have
\[
\langle \mathrm{II}^E_j(T_i,T_k),N_\alpha\rangle=\partial_{ik}u_j^\alpha.
\]
To derive the estimate, let
\[
g=(\langle T_i,T_k\rangle)_{i,k}=I_n+(Du_j)^TDu_j,
\qquad
G=(\langle N_\alpha,N_\beta\rangle)_{\alpha,\beta}
=I_{q+1}+Du_j(Du_j)^T.
\]
Writing $(g^{ik})=g^{-1}$ and $(G^{\alpha\beta})=G^{-1}$, we obtain
\[
|\mathrm{II}^E_j|^2
=\sum_{i,k,r,s=1}^n\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{q+1}
g^{ir}g^{ks}G^{\alpha\beta}
(\partial_{ik}u_j^\alpha)(\partial_{rs}u_j^\beta).
\]
Both $g^{-1}$ and $G^{-1}$ are bounded below by $(1+|Du_j|^2)^{-1}I$ as quadratic forms. Since $g^{-1}$ occurs twice and $G^{-1}$ once in this contraction, it follows that
\[
|\mathrm{II}^E_j|^2\ge(1+|Du_j|^2)^{-3}|D^2u_j|^2,
\]
which proves \eqref{graph estimate}. Subtracting $Du_j$ times the horizontal components of $\Delta_gF_j=-nF_j$ from its vertical components yields
\[
g^{ik}(Du_j)\partial_{ik}u_j^\alpha
=-n\big(c_j^\alpha+u_j^\alpha-x^k\partial_k u_j^\alpha\big).
\]
The bounds of the first-order derivative and the second-order derivative give uniform ellipticity and uniform H\"older bounds for the coefficients and the right-hand side. Interior Schauder estimates, applied successively after differentiation, give bounds of every order on a smaller graph ball of fixed radius. They also bound every covariant derivative of $h_j$ and the coordinate derivatives of $g_j$. The transition maps are obtained by tangent projection of the Euclidean graphs. They therefore satisfy the same derivative estimates on the overlaps. The metric estimates give smooth pointed convergence to a complete limit by \cite[Chapter 11]{Petersen}. The local coordinate construction and a diagonal subsequence give embeddings on an exhaustion, with convergence in every derivative order. In these coordinates the components of $F_j\circ\phi_j$ are bounded by one and solve
\[
\Delta_{\phi_j^*g_j}(F_j\circ\phi_j)+n(F_j\circ\phi_j)=0.
\]
Interior elliptic estimates and a further diagonal subsequence give smooth convergence to $F_\infty$. Passing to the limit in $(F_j\circ\phi_j)^*g_{\mathbb S}=\phi_j^*g_j$ proves $F_\infty^*g_{\mathbb S}=g_\infty$, so the limit is an isometric immersion. The limiting equation then proves its minimality. 

Finally, we verify the assertion about intrinsic balls. For fixed $R>0$, choose a precompact open $\Omega\subset M_\infty$ containing $\overline B_{g_\infty}(x_\infty,3R)$, with its closure inside an exhaustion region. For large $j$, $\phi_j^*g_j\ge \frac{g_\infty}{4}$ on $\overline\Omega$. A path from $x_j$ first exiting $\phi_j(\Omega)$ has length at least $\frac{3R}{2}$, by lifting it up to its first exit. Every minimizing path of length less than $R$ therefore stays in $\phi_j(\Omega)$, proving the claimed containment. If $M_\infty$ is compact, the exhaustion eventually contains the whole limit. Its image under $\phi_j$ is both compact and open in the connected manifold $M_j$, and hence equals $M_j$. These maps are global diffeomorphisms and the metric convergence bounds the intrinsic diameters. Conversely, if $\operatorname{diam}(M_j)\le D$, choose $R>D$. The containment of the intrinsic balls implies that $\phi_j|_\Omega$ is surjective onto $M_j$, and hence a diffeomorphism. By completeness and the diameter bound, $M_j$ is compact. Thus $\Omega$ is compact and open in connected $M_\infty$, so $\Omega=M_\infty$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:jacobi}
Let $f$ be a smooth scalar solution of $(\Delta_{g_0}+n)f=0$ on the entire cylinder. If
\[
\sup_{t\in\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb S^m(s)}|B(t,x)|^2\,d\mu_s(x)<\infty,
\]
then for constants $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R$ and $v,w\in\mathbb R^{m+1}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobi-classification}
f(t,x)=\alpha\cos(\sqrt n t)+\beta\sin(\sqrt n t)+(v+tw)\cdot x.
\end{equation}
The projected tensor satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobi-norm}
|B|^2=\frac2{s^2}\left(|w|^2-(w\cdot x)^2\right).
\end{equation}

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Use $e_0=\partial_t$ and a local orthonormal frame $e_1,\cdots,e_m$ for $g_s$. The indices $a,b$ range from $1$ to $m$ and refer to sphere directions. All sphere derivatives in this proof use $g_s$. Writing $B_0=\operatorname{Hess}_{g_0}f+fg_0$, we have
\[
(B_0)_{00}=f_{tt}+f,\qquad
(B_0)_{0a}=\nabla_a f_t,\qquad
(B_0)_{ab}=(\operatorname{Hess}_{g_s}f)_{ab}+f\delta_{ab}.
\]
Taking the inner product with $A$ and using the Jacobi equation, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle B_0,A\rangle
&=-\sqrt m(f_{tt}+f)
+\frac1{\sqrt m}\bigl(\Delta_{g_s}f+mf\bigr)\\
&=-\frac n{\sqrt m}(f_{tt}+f).
\end{aligned}
\]
Substituting into \eqref{B}, we have
\[
B_{00}=0,\qquad B_{0a}=\nabla_a f_t,\qquad
B_{ab}=(\operatorname{Hess}^0 f)_{ab}.
\]
Here, for any smooth function $Y$ on $\mathbb S^m(s)$, the trace-free sphere Hessian is defined by
\[
\operatorname{Hess}^0Y
\coloneqq\operatorname{Hess}_{g_s}Y-\frac{\Delta_{g_s}Y}{m}\,g_s,
\qquad m=n-1.
\]
For $f=f(t,x)$ this definition is applied to $f(t,\cdot)$ at each fixed $t$. Expanding $f$ in spherical harmonics orthonormal in $L^2(\mathbb S^m(s),d\mu_s)$, we write 
$f(t,x) = \sum_{d=0}^\infty f_d(t)Y_d(x)$,
where $Y_d$ are spherical harmonics of degree $d$ satisfying $-\Delta_{g_s}Y_d = \kappa_d Y_d$ with eigenvalues $\kappa_d = \frac{d(d+m-1)}{s^2}$. Recall that the product metric \(g_0 = dt^2 + g_s\) allows the Laplace operator to decompose as \(\Delta_{g_0} = \partial_t^2 + \Delta_{g_s}\). Substituting this expansion into the Jacobi equation $(\Delta_{g_0} + n)f = 0$, and using the orthogonality of the harmonics, we obtain the ordinary differential equation for each coefficient:
\begin{equation}\label{ODE}
	f_d''+(n-\kappa_d)f_d=0.
\end{equation} 
For a spherical eigenfunction $Y$ satisfying $-\Delta_{g_s}Y=\kappa Y$, applying the classical Bochner formula on the sphere, 
\[
\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{g_s}|\nabla Y|^2 = |\text{Hess} Y|^2 + \langle \nabla \Delta_{g_s}Y, \nabla Y \rangle + \text{Ric}_{g_s}(\nabla Y, \nabla Y),
\]
and integrating over the closed manifold $\mathbb{S}^m(s)$ yields:
\[
0 = \int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |\text{Hess} Y|^2 d\mu_s - \kappa \int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |\nabla Y|^2 d\mu_s + (m-1)s^{-2} \int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |\nabla Y|^2 d\mu_s.
\]
Since $\int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |\nabla Y|^2 \,d\mu_s= \kappa \int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |Y|^2\,d\mu_s$, this reduces to \[\int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |\text{Hess} Y|^2\,d\mu_s = \left(\kappa^2 - \frac{(m-1)\kappa}{s^2}\right)\int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |Y|^2\,d\mu_s.\] Subtracting $m^{-1}\int_{\mathbb S^m(s)} (\Delta_{g_s}Y)^2\,d\mu_s = \frac{\kappa^2}{m}\int |Y|^2$ from this integrated squared Hessian norm gives the trace-free estimate:
\[
\int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |\text{Hess}^0 Y|^2_{g_s} d\mu_s = \frac{m-1}{m}\kappa(\kappa - n) \int_{\mathbb{S}^m(s)} |Y|^2 d\mu_s,
\]
where we used $s^2 = \frac{m}{n}$.
 By polarization, the corresponding Hessian tensors are orthogonal whenever the spherical harmonics are orthogonal. For $d\ge2$, we have $\kappa_d>n$. The integral bound for $B$ bounds the trace-free Hessian in the sphere directions and hence each harmonic coefficient on $\mathbb R$. Since the solutions to \eqref{ODE} are exponential, $f_d(t) = c_1 e^{\sqrt{\kappa_d - n}t} + c_2 e^{-\sqrt{\kappa_d - n}t}$, boundedness on the whole line implies $c_1 = c_2 = 0$, so $f_d \equiv 0$ for $d \ge 2$. For $d=0$, we have $\kappa_0=0$ and $f_0''+nf_0=0$, which gives the sine and cosine terms in \eqref{eq:jacobi-classification}. For $d=1$, we have $\kappa_1=n$, so the coefficients are affine functions of $t$. This proves the expansion \eqref{eq:jacobi-classification}. The only possibly nonzero components of $B$ are the mixed components, which are given by $\nabla(w\cdot x)$. The squared norm of this gradient is $s^{-2}(|w|^2-(w\cdot x)^2)$, which proves \eqref{eq:jacobi-norm}.

\label{lem:local-elliptic}
Let $n\ge3$. On a fixed coordinate domain $U$, suppose that $g_j\to g$ and $A_j\to A$ smoothly, where each $A_j$ is symmetric and trace-free with respect to $g_j$, and $A$ is invertible. For the $j$-th system, all covariant derivatives, contractions, and tensor norms are taken with respect to $g_j$. Let symmetric tensors $C_j$ and one-forms $\theta_j$ satisfy
\[
\operatorname{Tr}_{g_j}C_j=\langle A_j,C_j\rangle=0,
\]
\[
(\nabla_XC_j)Y-(\nabla_YC_j)X
   +\theta_j(X)A_jY-\theta_j(Y)A_jX=0.
\]
If $\sup_U|C_j|$ is bounded independently of $j$, then all local derivatives of $C_j$ and $\theta_j$ are uniformly bounded. More precisely, for $U'\Subset U$, $k\ge0$, and all sufficiently large $j$,
\[
\|C_j\|_{C^{k+1}(U')}+\|\theta_j\|_{C^k(U')}
   \le C_k\|C_j\|_{L^2(U)}.
\]
The constants depend on the regions, uniform metric and coefficient bounds, and a uniform bound for $A_j^{-1}$, but not on a positive lower bound for $|C_j|$.

Tracing the first-order equation gives $\operatorname{div}C_j=-A_j\theta_j$. Eliminating $\theta_j$, consider the operator on

given by

\begin{aligned}
(P_AC)_{ikl}={}&\nabla_iC_{kl}-\nabla_kC_{il}

\label{lem:q2-global-potential}
Let $F:M^n\to C_p\subset\mathbb S^{n+1}$, $n\ge3$, be a connected covering of a standard Clifford hypersurface, with global unit normal $\eta$ and shape operator $A$. Suppose that a symmetric tensor $C$ and a one-form $\theta$ satisfy \eqref{eq:normalized-constraints}-\eqref{eq:normalized-ricci}. Then the $\mathbb R^{n+2}$-valued one-form
\[
\Omega(X)=-dF(CX)-\theta(X)\eta
\]
is closed. If $\Omega$ is exact, in particular if $M$ is simply connected, there is a smooth function $f$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:global-jacobi-potential}
\begin{aligned}
(\Delta+n)f&=0,\\
C&=\operatorname{Hess}f+fg-\psi A,\\
\psi&=\frac{\langle \operatorname{Hess}f+fg,A\rangle}n,\\
\theta&=d\psi+A(\nabla f,\cdot).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
If $M$ is compact and $\Omega$ is exact, then $C=0$.

The tangential component of $d\Omega$ vanishes by \eqref{eq:normalized-codazzi}, its radial component by symmetry of $C$, and its $\eta$ component is $\langle [A,C]X,Y\rangle-d\theta(X,Y)=0$. If $\Omega$ is exact, choose $W:M\to\mathbb R^{n+2}$ with $dW=\Omega$ and put $f=-\langle W,F\rangle$, $\psi=-\langle W,\eta\rangle$. Differentiating $f$ gives $df(X)=-\langle W,dF(X)\rangle$, so
\[
W=-fF-dF(\nabla f)-\psi\eta.
\]
Comparing the tangential and normal components of $dW=\Omega$, and then using $\operatorname{Tr} C=\langle A,C\rangle=0$ and $|A|^2=n$, gives \eqref{eq:global-jacobi-potential}. Suppose now that $M$ is compact. If both factors of $C_p$ have dimension at least two, $C_p$ is simply connected and $M=C_p$ as a Riemannian covering. Its Laplace eigenvalues are
\[
\frac np d(d+p-1)+\frac n{n-p}e(e+n-p-1),
\qquad d,e\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.
\]
The eigenvalue $n$ occurs only for $(d,e)=(1,0),(0,1)$. If one factor is a circle, a compact connected cover has some degree $k$. Its circle eigenvalues are $\frac{nj^2}{k^2}$, $j\in\mathbb Z$, and its transverse sphere eigenvalues are $\frac{n\ell(\ell+n-2)}{n-1}$, $\ell\ge0$. By the Gauss formula on the sphere, \(\text{Hess} f = \langle v, \eta \rangle A - fg\). Substituting this into the definition of \(\psi\) yields \(\psi = \frac{\langle \langle v, \eta \rangle A, A \rangle}{n} = \langle v, \eta \rangle\). 
Consequently, plugging both expressions into \(C = \text{Hess} f + fg - \psi A\) gives \(C = \langle v, \eta \rangle A - \langle v, \eta \rangle A = 0\). This completes the proof.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{higher:endpoint}
Let $n\ge3$ and $q\ge2$.  Every complete connected minimal immersion
$F:M^n\to\mathbb S^{n+q}(1)$ satisfying $Q\le n$ is a covering
immersion of a totally geodesic sphere or a standard Clifford
hypersurface in a fixed totally geodesic $\mathbb S^{n+1}$.
In particular, if $Q\equiv n$, then
\[
 \lambda_2\equiv0,\qquad S\equiv n,\qquad \nabla h\equiv0,
\]
and the immersion is a Clifford covering.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
If $M$ is compact, this is Lu's pinching classification
\cite[Theorem~6]{Lu11}.  Suppose henceforth that $M$ is noncompact.
Write $a=\lambda_1$, $b=\lambda_2$, and
$T=\sum_{\alpha=2}^q\lambda_\alpha$.  We have
\begin{equation}\label{higher:endpoint-elementary}
 S=a+T,\qquad Q=S+b\le n,\qquad
 b\le T\le(q-1)b,\qquad 0\le a\le S\le n.
\end{equation}

We first verify the curvature conditions for linear volume growth.
For any orthonormal normal frame set
$\mathcal C=\sum_\alpha(A^\alpha)^2$, a positive semidefinite
tangent endomorphism with $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal C=S$.
The Gauss equation gives
\[
 \operatorname{Ric}=(n-1)I-\mathcal C.
\]
The trace-free estimate
$\|A^\alpha\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2\le\frac{n-1}{n}|A^\alpha|^2$
yields
\begin{equation}\label{higher:endpoint-Ricci}
 \operatorname{Ric}\ge\frac{n-1}{n}(n-S)g
 \ge\frac{n-1}{n}b\,g\ge0.
\end{equation}
Let $\rho_1\le\rho_2\le\cdots\le\rho_n$ be the eigenvalues of
the Ricci tensor and let $c_1\ge c_2\ge\cdots\ge c_n\ge0$ be
the eigenvalues of $\mathcal C$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{higher:endpoint-two-Ricci}
 \rho_1+\rho_2
 =2(n-1)-(c_1+c_2)
 \ge2(n-1)-\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal C
 \ge n-2\ge1.
\end{equation}
Consequently condition~(i) of Cucinotta and Mondino
\cite[Theorem~1.3(1)]{CucinottaMondino26} holds: taking their
truncation level to be $L=1$, the normalized integral on every unit
ball of the truncated sum of the lowest two Ricci eigenvalues is
exactly one.  Their lower-Ricci hypothesis holds because
$\operatorname{Ric}\ge0$.  Hence there is a constant $C$ such that,
for a fixed $o\in M$,
\begin{equation}\label{higher:endpoint-linear-volume}
 \operatorname{Vol}(B_R(o))\le CR\qquad(R\ge1).
\end{equation}
No restriction on the codimension enters these curvature estimates.

We next show that the locally Lipschitz function $a$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{higher:top-weak-subharmonic}
 \Delta a\ge2a(n-Q)\ge0
 \quad\text{in the distributional sense}.
\end{equation}
At a point, choose a normal eigenbasis of the fundamental matrix
$\mathcal A$ and denote the shape operators by $A_1,\ldots,A_q$.
Set $C_{\alpha\beta}=|[A_\alpha,A_\beta]|^2$.
Lu's matrix inequality \cite[Lemma~2]{Lu11}, applied after placing
any chosen normal index first, gives
\begin{equation}\label{higher:Lu-every-index}
 \sum_{\beta\ne\alpha}C_{\alpha\beta}
 \le\lambda_\alpha
 \left(S-\lambda_\alpha+
       \max_{\beta\ne\alpha}\lambda_\beta\right).
\end{equation}
Only the inequality is used; no assertion about its equality cases
is needed.

For an even integer $p\ge2$ and $\tau>0$, put
\[
 f_p=(\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal A^p)^{1/p},\qquad
 f_{p,\tau}=(\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal A^p+\tau^p)^{1/p}.
\]
The map $X\mapsto(\operatorname{Tr}X^p+\tau^p)^{1/p}$ is the
Schatten $p$-norm of the block diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(X,\tau)$.
It is convex in $X$ and smooth because $\tau>0$.
The diagonal entries of the matrix Simons identity are
\[
 (\Delta^\perp\mathcal A)_{\alpha\alpha}
 =2|T_\alpha|^2+2n\lambda_\alpha-2\lambda_\alpha^2
       -2\sum_\beta C_{\alpha\beta}.
\]
Convexity and the chain rule with the induced normal-bundle
connection therefore imply
\begin{align}
 \Delta f_{p,\tau}
 &\ge2f_{p,\tau}^{1-p}\sum_\alpha\lambda_\alpha^p
       \left(n-\lambda_\alpha-
        \frac{\sum_\beta C_{\alpha\beta}}{\lambda_\alpha}\right)
       \notag\\
 &\ge
 2\frac{f_p^p}{f_{p,\tau}^{p-1}}(n-Q)
 -2f_{p,\tau}^{1-p}(a-b)
                   \sum_{\alpha=2}^q\lambda_\alpha^p.
 \label{higher:regularized-power-laplacian}
\end{align}
Terms containing $\lambda_\alpha=0$ in the first line are
interpreted as zero.  Before division they have the factor
$\lambda_\alpha^{p-1}$, and $A_\alpha=0$ also gives
$C_{\alpha\beta}=0$.  The second line follows from
\eqref{higher:Lu-every-index}: the expression in parentheses is
at least $n-Q$ for $\alpha=1$, and at least
$(n-Q)-(a-b)$ for every $\alpha\ge2$.

Where $a>0$, write $r=b/a\in[0,1]$ and
$w_1=a^p/\sum_\alpha\lambda_\alpha^p$.  Since
$f_{p,\tau}\ge f_p$, $f_p\le S\le n$, and
$1-w_1\le(q-1)r^p$, the magnitude of the last term in
\eqref{higher:regularized-power-laplacian} is at most
\[
 2f_p(a-b)(1-w_1)
 \le2n^2(q-1)r^p(1-r)
 \le\frac{2n^2(q-1)}{p+1}.
\]
The last inequality follows by maximizing $r^p(1-r)$ on $[0,1]$.
At a point where $a=0$, the same estimate is immediate because
all normal eigenvalues vanish.

Letting $\tau\downarrow0$ in the distributional inequality,
using $f_{p,\tau}\to f_p$ uniformly and
$0\le f_p^p/f_{p,\tau}^{p-1}\le f_p\le n$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{higher:power-almost-subharmonic}
 \Delta f_p\ge2f_p(n-Q)-\frac{2n^2(q-1)}{p+1}
 \quad\text{weakly}.
\end{equation}
Finally $a\le f_p\le q^{1/p}a$ and $a\le n$ show that
$f_p\to a$ uniformly as $p\to\infty$ through even integers.
Testing \eqref{higher:power-almost-subharmonic} against any
nonnegative compactly supported smooth function and passing to the
limit proves \eqref{higher:top-weak-subharmonic}.

We include the cutoff argument that forces $a$ to be constant.
Set $v=n+1-a\ge1$.  It is a positive locally Lipschitz weakly
superharmonic function.  Let $\chi_R$ be a compactly supported
Lipschitz cutoff equal to one on $B_R(o)$, zero outside
$B_{2R}(o)$, and satisfying $|\nabla\chi_R|\le1/R$.
Testing the weak inequality for $v$ with $\chi_R^2/v$ and applying
Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
 \int_M\chi_R^2|\nabla\log v|^2
 \le4\int_M|\nabla\chi_R|^2

Suppose a limit $F_0:M_0\to\mathbb S^{n+q}(1)$ is compact. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:immersion-compactness}, convergence is global through
diffeomorphisms, and the diameters are uniformly bounded. Lu's closed
first pinching theorem identifies $F_0$ as a compact covering of a
Clifford hypersurface in a fixed great $\mathbb S^{n+1}$; see
\cite[Theorem~6]{Lu11} and Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-one}. Let $\eta$
be its global first normal. The first normal eigenspace of the nearby
immersions is globally oriented by projecting $\eta$, so we can use
the notation above globally as bundle-valued notation.

Put $b_j=\lambda_{2,j}$ and $\beta_j=\max_{M_j}b_j$. If
$\beta_j=0$ along a subsequence, Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-one}
reduces the sequence to hypersurfaces, contradicting the hypersurface
second gap theorem \cite{PengTerng}. We may assume $\beta_j>0$ and
normalize with $\sigma_j=\sqrt{\beta_j}$. The largest eigenvalue of
the Gram matrix $(\langle C_{\alpha,j},C_{\gamma,j}\rangle)$ is at most one,
and has global maximum one. In particular
\[
 |C_j|^2=\sum_{\alpha=2}^q|C_{\alpha,j}|^2\le q-1.
\]
Lemma~\ref{higher:vector-elliptic} gives uniform global bounds on all
derivatives of $C_j$ and $\theta_j$.

We next choose a global complementary normal frame whose internal
connection has the required rate. Let $P_j$ be the orthogonal
projection, in the trivial Euclidean ambient bundle, onto
$\mathcal E_j$. Differentiating $P_j$ using the Weingarten formula
gives, for any local orthonormal frame of $\mathcal E_j$,
\[
 dP_j(X)=-\sum_{\alpha=2}^q
 \bigl(dF_j(B_{\alpha,j}X)+\omega_{\alpha,j}(X)\nu_{1,j}\bigr)
                 \mathbin{\odot}\nu_{\alpha,j},
\]
where $u\mathbin{\odot}v=u\otimes v+v\otimes u$. Internal connection
terms cancel in this formula. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{higher:projection-derivative}
 \|dP_j\|_{L^\infty}\le C\sqrt{\beta_j}.
\end{equation}
Choose a point $p_j$ and an ambient constant orthonormal matrix $Z_j$
whose columns span $\mathcal E_j(p_j)$. The uniform diameter bound and
\eqref{higher:projection-derivative} imply
\[
 \sup_{M_j}|P_j-P_j(p_j)|\le C\sqrt{\beta_j}.
\]
For large $j$, the columns of
\[
 V_j=P_jZ_j(Z_j^TP_jZ_j)^{-1/2}
\]
therefore give a global orthonormal frame of $\mathcal E_j$.
Its internal connection matrix $\Sigma_j=V_j^TdV_j$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{higher:small-internal-connection}
 |\Sigma_j|\le C|P_j-P_j(p_j)|\,|dP_j|
                  \le C\beta_j.
\end{equation}
Indeed, $P_j(dP_j)P_j=0$, so both
$Z_j^T(dP_j)Z_j$ and $V_j^T(dP_j)Z_j$ contain a factor bounded by
$|P_j-P_j(p_j)|$. Differentiating the displayed formula for $V_j$
then proves \eqref{higher:small-internal-connection}; the matrix
inverse square root and its derivative stay uniformly bounded.

Use the columns of $V_j$ as $\nu_{\alpha,j}$. They converge, after a
subsequence, to an ambient constant orthonormal frame
$\zeta_2,\ldots,\zeta_q$ of the complement of the fixed
$\mathbb R^{n+2}$ containing the limiting immersion. The normalized fields
converge smoothly globally in this frame. Their limits
$(C_\alpha,\theta_\alpha)$ satisfy the scalar equations
\eqref{eq:normalized-constraints}--\eqref{eq:normalized-ricci}
for each $\alpha$. Define
\[
 \Omega_{\alpha,j}(X)
   =-dF_j(C_{\alpha,j}X)-\theta_{\alpha,j}(X)\nu_{1,j}.
\]
The Weingarten formula and
\eqref{higher:small-internal-connection} give
\[
 \Omega_{\alpha,j}
 =d\left(\frac{\nu_{\alpha,j}}{\sqrt{\beta_j}}\right)
   -\frac1{\sqrt{\beta_j}}
        \sum_{\gamma=2}^q(\Sigma_j)_{\gamma\alpha}\nu_{\gamma,j},
 \qquad
 \left|\frac{\Sigma_j}{\sqrt{\beta_j}}\right|
       \longrightarrow0.
\]
For every fixed closed loop in $M_0$, smooth global metric convergence bounds the lengths of its corresponding loops in $M_j$. The error in its period is therefore $O(\sqrt{\beta_j})$. Consequently, the integral of the limit $\Omega_\alpha$ along every closed loop is zero. It is an exact $\mathbb R^{n+2}$-valued one-form on
$M_0$. The compact-cover conclusion of
Lemma~\ref{lem:q2-global-potential} gives $C_\alpha=0$ for every
$\alpha$. This contradicts the normalization: smooth global
convergence preserves the maximum one of the largest Gram
eigenvalue. The contradiction proves the proposition.

 \mathcal M_A(Z)_{ikr}

\label{higher:rank}
Let $n\ge3$ and let $H$ be a positive semidefinite symmetric
$n\times n$ matrix. For $x\in\mathbb S^{n-1}(1)$, set
\[
 h_H(x)=x^THx,\qquad
 r_H(x)=\lambda_{\max}\!
 \left(\left.(I-xx^T)H(I-xx^T)\right|_{x^\perp}\right).
\]
Suppose that, for a constant $K\in\mathbb R$,
\begin{equation}\label{higher:rank-master}
 \Delta_{\mathbb S^{n-1}}r_H
 +2(n-1)r_H+(n^2+n+2)h_H=K
\end{equation}
holds in the sense of distributions. Then $H=cI_n$ for some $c\ge0$.
In particular, if $H\ne0$, then $\operatorname{rank}H=n$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
The function $r_H$ is Lipschitz. Indeed, since $H\ge0$, its value
is also the largest eigenvalue of the full symmetric matrix
$(I-xx^T)H(I-xx^T)$; the latter matrix depends smoothly on $x$,
and the largest eigenvalue is Lipschitz with respect to the
operator norm. Equation~\eqref{higher:rank-master} is a linear
elliptic equation with smooth coefficients and smooth right-hand
side for $r_H$. Elliptic regularity therefore gives
$r_H\in C^\infty(\mathbb S^{n-1})$. Thus the pointwise maximum
principle can be applied without assuming differentiability of
the largest eigenvalue in advance.

Write the eigenvalues of $H$ as
\[
 h_1\ge h_2\ge\cdots\ge h_n\ge0
\]
and choose an orthonormal eigenbasis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$.
For every unit $x$, the two-dimensional space
$\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2\}$ has a nonzero intersection with
$x^\perp$. The Rayleigh quotient on this intersection, together
with the maximality of $h_1$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{higher:rank-interlacing}
 h_2\le r_H(x)\le h_1.
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\[
 h_H(e_1)=h_1,\qquad r_H(e_1)=h_2,\qquad
 h_H(e_n)=h_n,\qquad r_H(e_n)=h_1.
\]
These identities remain valid when eigenvalues have multiplicity.
Consequently $e_1$ is a global minimum point of $r_H$ and $e_n$
is a global maximum point. Evaluating
\eqref{higher:rank-master} at these points yields
\[
 K\ge2(n-1)h_2+(n^2+n+2)h_1,
 \qquad
 K\le2(n-1)h_1+(n^2+n+2)h_n.
\]
It follows that
\[
 (n^2+n+2)(h_1-h_n)
 \le2(n-1)(h_1-h_2)
 \le2(n-1)(h_1-h_n).
\]
Since $n^2+n+2-2(n-1)=n^2-n+4>0$, we obtain
$h_1=h_n$. Thus all eigenvalues of $H$ are equal, which proves
the assertion.

\label{higher:rank-quantitative}
The same two-point argument has an explicit stability estimate.
Suppose that $r_H\in C^2(\mathbb S^{n-1})$ and that, for constants
$K\in\mathbb R$ and $e\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{higher:rank-residual}
 \left\|
 \Delta_{\mathbb S^{n-1}}r_H+2(n-1)r_H
 +(n^2+n+2)h_H-K
 \right\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb S^{n-1})}\le e.
\end{equation}
At the minimum point $e_1$ and the maximum point $e_n$ used in
the proof, the corresponding inequalities now have errors at
most $e$. Subtracting them gives
\begin{equation}\label{higher:rank-stability}
 (n^2-n+4)(h_1-h_n)\le2e.
\end{equation}
If, in addition, $\operatorname{rank}H\le n-1$ and
\[
 \max_{x\in\mathbb S^{n-1}}
 \frac{2n}{n-1}r_H(x)=1,
\]
then $h_n=0$ and $h_1=(n-1)/(2n)$ by
\eqref{higher:rank-interlacing}. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{higher:rank-margin}
 e\ge\frac{(n^2-n+4)(n-1)}{4n}\ge\frac53.
\end{equation}
In particular, a residual smaller than $1$ is impossible under
these rank and normalization assumptions. The $C^2$ hypothesis
in this quantitative statement is explicit; it is not inferred
merely from the fact that $r_H$ is a largest eigenvalue.

 \rho:=\lambda_{\max}G=\frac{2n}{m}r_H.
\end{equation}
The formula also holds when this largest eigenvalue is zero.

We derive the equation forced by constancy of $Q$ before applying
the rank obstruction. After a subsequence,
\[
 \frac{\varepsilon_j}{\beta_j}\longrightarrow\kappa
\]
by Lemma~\ref{higher:first-shape-rate}. The exact Laplacian formula
for the simple largest normal eigenvalue, written without
diagonalizing the lower block, is
\begin{equation}\label{higher:top-eigenvalue-simons}
 \frac12\Delta a_j
  =|T_{1,j}|^2+a_j(n-a_j)
    -\beta_j\sum_{\alpha=2}^q|[A_j,C_{\alpha,j}]|^2
    +\beta_j\sum_i
       \langle (a_jI-\beta_jG_j)\theta_{j,i},\theta_{j,i}\rangle.
\end{equation}
For example, it follows by choosing an eigenframe of the lower
block at the point in question and applying the simple-eigenvalue
second derivative formula. The off-diagonal derivative of the
fundamental matrix is $(a_jI-\beta_jG_j)\omega_j$; the resulting
eigenvector correction is the last term above. This also shows
that the expression is unchanged at lower-eigenvalue crossings.

Use $a_j=n+\varepsilon_j-\beta_j(T_j+\rho_j)$ and divide
\eqref{higher:top-eigenvalue-simons} by $\beta_j$. The first term
tends to zero, since $|T_{1,j}|^2/\beta_j=O(\beta_j)$.
The difference between the two tangent eigenvalues of $A_0$ is
$-n/\sqrt m$, and $C_\alpha$ has only mixed components, so
\[
 \sum_\alpha|[A_0,C_\alpha]|^2=\frac{n^2}{m}T.
\]
All terms on the right therefore have locally uniform limits.
On the left, $T_j\to T$ smoothly and $\rho_j\to\rho$ uniformly,
the latter by the eigenvalue Lipschitz inequality. Testing
against a compactly supported smooth function and integrating
by parts transfers the Laplacian to the test function. Smooth
metric convergence then passes the left-hand side to
$-\frac12\Delta_{g_0}(T+\rho)$ in the sense of distributions.
No uniform second derivative bound for $\rho_j$ is used.
We obtain
\[
 -\frac12\Delta_{g_0}(T+\rho)
      =n(T+\rho-\kappa)-\frac{n^2}{m}T+nV,
\]
or equivalently
\begin{equation}\label{higher:constant-Q-master}
 \kappa=\frac{\Delta_{g_0}(T+\rho)}{2n}
                    -\frac{T}{m}+\rho+V.
\end{equation}
In particular, both the trace $T$ of the lower Gram matrix and
its largest eigenvalue $\rho$ enter the equation.

All the quantities in \eqref{higher:constant-Q-master} are
independent of $t$, and
$\Delta_{g_0}=(n/m)\Delta_{\mathbb S^{n-1}(1)}$ on such functions.
Substitute \eqref{higher:limit-gram} and
\eqref{higher:rho-compression}, and use
\[
 \Delta_{\mathbb S^{n-1}(1)}h_H=2t_H-2n h_H.
\]
After multiplying by $m^2/n$, the result is
\begin{equation}\label{higher:compression-equation}
 \Delta_{\mathbb S^{n-1}(1)}r_H
       +2m r_H+(n^2+n+2)h_H
                   =4t_H+\frac{m^2}{n}\kappa.
\end{equation}
The equation holds distributionally. Lemma~\ref{higher:rank}
first makes $r_H$ smooth by elliptic regularity and then gives
$H=c_0I_n$. Since $H\ne0$, $c_0>0$ and
\[
 n=\operatorname{rank} H\le q-1.
\]
This contradicts $q\le n$. Thus the sequence cannot exist, and
the asserted positive gap follows.

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:joint-spectral-bound}
For $n \ge 3$, let $A$ be a trace-free symmetric endomorphism of an $n$-dimensional Euclidean space,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint-spectral-bound}
\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2+
\frac{\operatorname{osc}(A)^2}{2n}
\le\frac{n+\sqrt{n^2-2n+4}}{2n}|A|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{osc}(A)$ is the difference between the largest and smallest eigenvalues of $A$.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
Let $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ be the eigenvalue vector of $A$, so $\lambda\perp\mathbf1=(1,\ldots,1)$. Let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb R^n$.
For indices $i,j,k$ with $j\ne k$, put
$v=e_i-\frac{\mathbf1}{n}$ and $d=e_j-e_k$.
Then a direct computation yields 
\[|v|^2=\frac{n-1}{n}, \quad |d|^2=2, \quad |\langle v,d\rangle|\le1.\]
Consider the symmetric operator \(\hat{S} = v \otimes v + \frac{1}{2n} d \otimes d\), which acts as \(\hat{S} z = \langle v, z \rangle v + \frac{1}{2n} \langle d, z \rangle d\). Thus, we have
\[
\lambda_i^2 + \frac{1}{2n}(\lambda_j - \lambda_k)^2 = \langle \lambda, \hat{S} \lambda \rangle \le \|\hat{S}\|_{op} |\lambda|^2 = \|\hat{S}\|_{op} |A|^2.
\]
 Since \(\hat{S}\) acts nontrivially only on the subspace spanned by \(v\) and \(d\), its matrix representation in this basis is:
\[
\begin{pmatrix} |v|^2 & \langle v, d \rangle \\ \frac{1}{2n}\langle v, d \rangle & \frac{1}{2n}|d|^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{n-1}{n} & \langle v, d \rangle \\ \frac{1}{2n}\langle v, d \rangle & \frac{1}{n} \end{pmatrix},
\]
where \(|\langle v, d \rangle|\le 1\). Hence, the largest eigenvalue of
$\hat{S}$ is at most
\[
\frac12\left(1+
\sqrt{\left(\frac{n-2}{n}\right)^2+\frac2n}\right)
=\frac{n+\sqrt{n^2-2n+4}}{2n}.
\] 
Apply this bound to
$\lambda_i^2+(2n)^{-1}(\lambda_j-\lambda_k)^2$
and maximize over $i,j,k$ yields the desired result \eqref{eq:joint-spectral-bound}.

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{quant:local-conformal-radius}
Let \((M^n,g)\) be closed and connected, and let \(\Omega\) be a
nonempty open subset, possibly equal to \(M\).  Suppose that \(u\) is
smooth and positive on a neighbourhood of \(\overline\Omega\), and that
\begin{equation}\label{quant:local-weighted-ricci}
 \operatorname{Ric}-k\frac{\Delta u}{u}g\ge c_0g
 \quad\hbox{on }\Omega,
 \qquad
 a_k:=k-\frac{n-1}{4}k^2>0,
 \qquad c_0>0.
\end{equation}
Put
\begin{equation}\label{quant:local-radius-value}
 R(k,c_0,n)=\pi\left[
 \frac{(n-1)+(n-3)^2k^2/(4a_k)}{c_0}
 \right]^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
If \(\Omega\ne M\), every point of \(\Omega\) can be joined to
\(\partial\Omega\) by a curve of \(g\)-length at most
\(R(k,c_0,n)\) whose interior is in \(\Omega\).  If \(\Omega=M\),
then \(\operatorname{diam}(M,g)\le R(k,c_0,n)\).
In particular, for \(k=1/(2n)\) and \(c_0=1/12\), both conclusions
hold with the strict upper bound \(12\sqrt n\).

<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
Write \(f=\log u\) and \(\widetilde g=e^{2kf}g\).  In the proper-domain
case, extend \(f\) smoothly from a neighbourhood of
\(\overline\Omega\) to \(M\).  The resulting metric is positive and
complete because \(M\) is compact.  A shortest \(\widetilde g\)-curve
from a fixed point of \(\Omega\) to the closed set
\(\partial\Omega\) exists.  Its interior lies in \(\Omega\), since
otherwise its first intersection with the boundary would shorten it.
Thus no regularity of the level set \(\partial\Omega\) is required.
If \(\Omega=M\), take instead a shortest \(\widetilde g\)-curve
between any two given points.  Denote either curve by \(\gamma\),
parametrize it by \(g\)-arclength \(s\in[0,\ell]\), and put
\(T=\gamma'(s)\).

The conformal geodesic equation and the conformal Ricci formula give
\[
 \nabla_TT=k(\nabla f-f'T),\qquad
 \operatorname{Hess}f(T,T)=f''-k(|\nabla f|^2-(f')^2),
\]
and, using \eqref{quant:local-weighted-ricci},
\begin{equation}\label{quant:conformal-ricci-along-curve}
 \operatorname{Ric}_{\widetilde g}(T,T)
 \ge c_0-(n-2)k f''+k|\nabla f|^2.
\end{equation}
Choose a \(\widetilde g\)-parallel orthonormal frame normal to the
curve.  Sum the index forms of the normal variation fields with
coefficient \(\phi=e^{kf/2}\psi\), where
\(\psi(0)=\psi(\ell)=0\).  Since
\(d\widetilde s=e^{kf}ds\), nonnegativity of the index form implies
\[
 0\le\int_0^\ell e^{-kf}
 \left((n-1)(\phi')^2
       -\operatorname{Ric}_{\widetilde g}(T,T)\phi^2\right)ds.
\]
Substitute \eqref{quant:conformal-ricci-along-curve}, integrate the
\(f''\)-term by parts, and use \(|\nabla f|^2\ge(f')^2\).  This gives
\begin{align*}
 0&\le\int_0^\ell
 \left((n-1)(\psi')^2-c_0\psi^2
       -(n-3)k f'\psi\psi'-a_k(f'\psi)^2\right)ds\\
 &\le\int_0^\ell
 \left(\left(n-1+\frac{(n-3)^2k^2}{4a_k}\right)(\psi')^2
             -c_0\psi^2\right)ds.
\end{align*}
The last inequality is completion of a square, with positive
coefficient \(a_k\).  The choice
\(\psi(s)=\sin(\pi s/\ell)\) proves
\(\ell\le R(k,c_0,n)\).  The original \(g\)-distance is no larger
than this \(g\)-length, proving both assertions.

For the stated numerical specialization,
\[
 a_k=\frac{7n+1}{16n^2},\qquad
 R(k,c_0,n)^2
 =12\pi^2\left(n-1+\frac{(n-3)^2}{7n+1}\right)<144n.
\]
Indeed, \((n-3)^2/(7n+1)\le n/7\) for \(n\ge3\), and
\(\pi^2<10\).

here.  Set \(G=|\nabla h|^2\) and \(H=|\nabla^2h|^2\).

 \Delta G\ge2H-100n^2G,\qquad

\end{equation}
For completeness, these numerical coefficients follow directly from
the tensor formulas as follows.  The derivatives of the Gram cubic
and double-commutator cubic in \eqref{eq:simons-tensor} have norm at
most \(3S\sqrt G\) and \(12S\sqrt G\), respectively.  Hence

 |\nabla\Delta h|\le(n+15S)\sqrt G\le21n\sqrt G.

The Gauss and Ricci equations give

 |\operatorname{Rm}|\le5n,\quad
 |R^\perp|\le3n,\quad
 |\nabla\operatorname{Rm}|, |\nabla R^\perp|
       \le4\sqrt S\sqrt G,\quad

The bundle containing \(h\) has two covariant tangent slots and one

\(2|\operatorname{Rm}|+|R^\perp|\le13n\).  In the commutator

 \tfrac12\Delta G
 \ge H-\bigl(49n+12\sqrt n(n+1)\bigr)G

The second inequality in \eqref{quant:entry-coarse-bochner} follows

If necessary, pass to a connected finite cover which orients both

At the point in question diagonalize \(A\), and write its eigenvalues as
\(\lambda+\delta_i\) in the \(P\)-block and
\(\mu+\delta_i\) in the \(P^\perp\)-block.
Since the roots of \(t^2-ct-1\) are simple,
\[
 |\delta_i|\le\frac{2|E_i|}{D},\qquad
 \sum_i\delta_i=0.
\]
Consequently, with \(t=\operatorname{tr}E\),
\begin{equation}\label{quant:block-trace-identity}
 \operatorname{tr}(PE)-\operatorname{tr}(P^\perp E)
      =\sum_{P}\delta_i^2-\sum_{P^\perp}\delta_i^2,
 \qquad
 \left|\operatorname{tr}(PE)-\operatorname{tr}(P^\perp E)\right|
      \le|E|^2 .
\end{equation}
Here the linear terms cancel because \(\sum_i\delta_i=0\), and the last
inequality uses \(D\ge2\).
Set
\[
 W=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(PE)}pP
       +\frac{\operatorname{tr}(P^\perp E)}mP^\perp,
 \qquad Z=E-W.
\]
Thus \(Z\) has zero trace in each block.  The orthogonality of the two
blocks and \eqref{quant:block-trace-identity} give
\begin{equation}\label{quant:traceless-block-bound}
 |W|\le|t|+|E|^2,\qquad
 |Z|^2\ge(1-2d^2)|E|^2-2t^2.
\end{equation}

Let \(\operatorname{Rm}_*\) be the algebraic curvature tensor obtained
from the Gauss equation with shape operators \(A_*,0\).
Its sectional curvatures are \(n/p\) in the \(P\)-block,
\(n/m\) in the \(P^\perp\)-block, and zero in mixed planes.
Summing over ordered pairs \(i,j\), direct diagonalization of each
trace-free block yields
\begin{equation}\label{quant:model-curvature-action}
 \begin{split}
 \sum_{i,j}|[R^*_{ij},Z]|^2
 &=4p\left(\frac np\right)^2|Z_P|^2
      +4m\left(\frac nm\right)^2|Z_{P^\perp}|^2\\
 &\ge4n|Z|^2 .
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
For instance, on a block of dimension \(r\) and curvature \(k\), the
sum is
\[
 4k^2\sum_{i<j}(z_i-z_j)^2=4rk^2\sum_i z_i^2
\]
when \(\sum_i z_i=0\).
The block-scalar tensor \(W\) commutes with every \(R^*_{ij}\).

The Gauss equation and \eqref{quant:clifford-neighborhood} imply
\[
 |\operatorname{Rm}-\operatorname{Rm}_*|
      \le16\sqrt n\,\eta.
\]
Its action on \(E\) therefore has norm at most
\(32\sqrt n\,\eta|E|\).
Using \(|X+Y|^2\ge|X|^2/2-|Y|^2\), followed by
\eqref{quant:traceless-block-bound}, gives
\[
 \begin{split}
 \sum_{i,j}|R_{ij}\cdot E|^2
 &\ge2n|Z|^2-1024n\eta^2|E|^2\\
 &\ge\bigl(2n-4nd^2-1024n\eta^2\bigr)|E|^2-4nt^2\\
 &\ge n|E|^2-4nt^2.
 \end{split}
\]
For the last step it is enough that
\(\eta\le2^{-10}/n\) and \(d\le4\sqrt n\,\eta\), both consequences of
our hypotheses.
Finally, the Ricci commutation identity gives
\[

   =\sum_{i,j}|(\nabla_i\nabla_j-\nabla_j\nabla_i)E|^2
   \le4|\nabla^2E|^2.
\]
Since \(|t|\le\varepsilon+2\beta\), this proves
\eqref{quant:curvature-coercivity}.

      \ge\frac{D^2}{D^2+3}\ge\frac47.

\(\Pi_P([A,[A,C]])\); therefore
\[
 |\Pi_P([A,[A,C]])|\le4|H|^2|C|\le4d^2 .
\]
Moreover, \(|\Pi_P(A)|\le d\), and
\eqref{quant:projector-derivatives-end}--\eqref{quant:projector-laplacian-end}
give
\[
 |\nabla\Pi_P|\le18L^{12}\beta,\qquad
 |\Delta\Pi_P|\le6L^5\beta+72L^{24}\beta^2\le L^7\beta .
\]
Applying \(\Pi_P\) to \eqref{quant:projected-c-equation}, including
the two derivatives of the projection, now gives
\begin{equation}\label{quant:diagonal-coercive-equation}
 |\Delta Y-nY|\le L^{15}\beta .
\end{equation}
For example, the derivative terms are bounded by
\(36NL^{12}\beta+L^7\beta\), the normal divergence term by
\(\sqrt n\,Nd\), and the remaining terms by
\(\beta+4d^2+2NL^{12}\beta+N^2\beta\).
Their sum is at most \(L^{15}\beta\).
At a maximum of \(|Y|^2\),
\[
 0\ge|\nabla Y|^2+n|Y|^2-L^{15}\beta|Y|,
\]
so \(|Y|\le L^{16}\beta\).

The traces discarded by \(\Pi_P\) are small as well.  Writing
\(\alpha=\operatorname{tr}(PC)\), the identities
\(\operatorname{tr}C=\langle A,C\rangle=0\) give
\[
 \operatorname{tr}(P^\perp C)=-\alpha,\qquad
 \alpha=\frac{\langle H,C\rangle}{D}.
\]
The norm of the scalar block part is exactly
\[
 \left|\frac\alpha pP-\frac\alpha mP^\perp\right|
 =|\alpha|\sqrt{\frac1p+\frac1m}
 =\frac{|\langle H,C\rangle|}{\sqrt n}\le\frac d{\sqrt n}.
\]
We conclude that
\begin{equation}\label{quant:block-c-small}
 |C_{\mathrm{diag}}|\le L^{17}\beta .
\end{equation}

We shall use the following elementary tensor interpolation estimate
on a complete manifold:
\begin{equation}\label{quant:geodesic-interpolation}
 \sup|\nabla T|
 \le2\sqrt{n\,\sup|T|\,\sup|\nabla^2T|}.
\end{equation}
To prove it, parallel transport \(T\) along a complete unit-speed
geodesic.  Taylor's estimate along this geodesic,
\(|f'(0)|\le2\sqrt{\sup|f|\,\sup|f''|}\) controls every directional
derivative; summing over an orthonormal frame gives the factor
\(\sqrt n\).
Differentiating the definition of \(C_{\mathrm{diag}}\) twice gives
\[
 |\nabla^2C_{\mathrm{diag}}|
 \le N+4|\nabla P|N+2|\nabla^2P|+4|\nabla P|^2\le4N.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{quant:block-c-gradient}
\begin{gathered}
 |\nabla C_{\mathrm{diag}}|\le L^{10}\sqrt\beta,\\
 |P(\nabla C)P+P^\perp(\nabla C)P^\perp|
 \le|\nabla C_{\mathrm{diag}}|+2|\nabla P||C|
 \le L^{11}\sqrt\beta .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Both multiplicities are at least two.}
Suppose first that \(p,m\ge2\).  Restrict all three tangent indices
of the normalized Codazzi equation to the \(P\) block:
\[
 \lambda(\theta_i\delta_{kr}-\theta_k\delta_{ir})
 =-(\nabla_iC_{kr}-\nabla_kC_{ir})
   -\theta_iH_{kr}+\theta_kH_{ir}.
\]
The elementary identity
\[
 \sum_{i,k,r\in P}
   (\theta_i\delta_{kr}-\theta_k\delta_{ir})^2
 =2(p-1)|P\theta|^2
\]
and \eqref{quant:block-c-gradient} imply
\[
 |\lambda|\sqrt{2(p-1)}\,|P\theta|
 \le2L^{11}\sqrt\beta+2NL^{12}\beta
 \le3L^{11}\sqrt\beta .
\]
Since \(|\lambda|\ge n^{-1/2}\), we have
\(|P\theta|\le L^{12}\sqrt\beta\).  The same argument in the
\(P^\perp\) block, using \(m\ge2\), gives
\begin{equation}\label{quant:theta-multiple-blocks}
 |\theta|\le L^{13}\sqrt\beta,\qquad
 |\nabla\theta|
 \le2\sqrt{nNL^{13}\sqrt\beta}
 \le L^{10}\beta^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
In matrix components the Ricci equation says
\([A,C]_{ij}=-(\nabla_i\theta_j-\nabla_j\theta_i)\).  Hence, with
the full matrix norm,
\[
 |[A,C]|\le2|\nabla\theta|.
\]
The two tangent spectral clusters of \(A\) are separated by
\(D-2\eta\ge1\), so
\[
 |PCP^\perp+P^\perp CP|
 \le|[A,C]|\le2L^{10}\beta^{1/4}.
\]
At a point where \(|C|=1\), this contradicts
\[
 1\le L^{17}\beta+2L^{10}\beta^{1/4}
 \le L^{-1983}+2L^{-490}<1.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\emph{One multiplicity is one.}
Changing the sign of the first normal if necessary, assume
\(p=1\), \(m=n-1\).  Thus
\[
 \lambda=-\sqrt m,\qquad \mu=\frac1{\sqrt m},\qquad
 D=\frac n{\sqrt m}.
\]
Orient the rank-one tangent bundle of \(P\) on an additional double
cover and let \(e_0\) be its unit section.  Define
\[
 z=P^\perp Ce_0,\qquad \tau=\theta(e_0),\qquad
 \theta_\perp=P^\perp\theta .
\]
We have the exact decomposition
\begin{equation}\label{quant:cylinder-decomposition}
 C=e_0\otimes z+z\otimes e_0+C_{\mathrm{diag}},\qquad
 u=2|z|^2+|C_{\mathrm{diag}}|^2,\qquad
 v=\tau^2+|\theta_\perp|^2 .
\end{equation}
The preceding pure-block argument applies to the \(m\)-dimensional
block and gives
\[
 |\theta_\perp|\le L^{12}\sqrt\beta.
\]
Since \(|\nabla^2\theta_\perp|\le4N\), interpolation gives
\[
 |\nabla\theta_\perp|\le L^{10}\beta^{1/4}.
\]
Here and below the covariant derivatives are taken on the original
manifold, rather than on a product model.

The mixed Codazzi equations and the Ricci equation now give
\begin{equation}\label{quant:cylinder-first-order}
 \begin{split}
 |\nabla z-\mu\tau P^\perp|&\le L^{15}\sqrt\beta,\\
 |P^\perp\nabla\tau+Dz|&\le L^{15}\beta^{1/4},\qquad
 |\nabla_{e_0}\tau|\le2N .
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Here are the component details.  In an adapted orthonormal frame,
with \(a,b\) in the \(P^\perp\) block, Codazzi gives
\[
 \nabla_aC_{0b}=\nabla_0C_{ab}+\tau A_{ab},
 \qquad
 \nabla_0C_{a0}=\nabla_aC_{00}+\theta_a A_{00}.
\]
Passing from these components to the derivative of
\(z=P^\perp Ce_0\) adds at most \(2|\nabla P||C|\).
Thus the first error in \eqref{quant:cylinder-first-order} is at most
\[
 2L^{11}\sqrt\beta+Nd+
 2\sqrt n\,|\theta_\perp|+2|\nabla P|
 \le L^{15}\sqrt\beta .
\]
Also,
\[
 d\theta(e_0,e_a)=Dz_a+[H,C]_{a0}.
\]
Differentiating \(\tau=\theta(e_0)\) and separating
\(\theta_\perp\) shows that the second error is at most
\[
 |\nabla\theta_\perp|+2N|\nabla P|+2d
 \le L^{15}\beta^{1/4}.
\]
The final bound follows from
\(|\nabla\theta|\le N\) and \(|\nabla e_0|\le|\nabla P|\).

Put \(\kappa=\varepsilon/\beta\).  Constancy of \(Q=a+2b\)
gives the following exact weighted identity:
\begin{equation}\label{quant:q-weighted-identity}
 \begin{split}
 Q\kappa={}&\frac{|T_1|^2}{\beta}
 +2|\nabla C+\theta\otimes A|^2
 -(a-b)v+4au+2\beta u^2-3|[A,C]|^2 .
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Indeed, adding the \(a\) eigenvalue equation to twice the \(b\)
eigenvalue equation gives
\[
 |T_1|^2+2|T_2|^2-(a-b)|\omega|^2
 =Q\varepsilon+2(a-b)b-3(2ab-|[A,B]|^2);
\]
divide by \(\beta\) and use
\(T_2/\sqrt\beta=\nabla C+\theta\otimes A\).

We record explicitly the leading terms and the error in
\eqref{quant:q-weighted-identity}.  By
\eqref{quant:terminal-inputs},
\[
 |T_1|=|\nabla A-\beta\theta\otimes C|
 \le2L^{12}\beta,\qquad
 \kappa\le130N^2<L^2,\qquad
 \varepsilon\kappa\le L^4\beta .
\]
Let \(\mathcal T\) be the symmetric three-tensor whose only nonzero
adapted components are
\[
 \mathcal T_{000}=\lambda\tau,\qquad
 \mathcal T_{0ab}=\mathcal T_{a0b}=\mathcal T_{ab0}
 =\mu\tau\delta_{ab}.
\]
Then \(|\mathcal T|^2=(\lambda^2+3m\mu^2)\tau^2
=(n+2)\tau^2\).
Equations \eqref{quant:cylinder-decomposition}-- 
\eqref{quant:cylinder-first-order} give
\[
 |\nabla C+\theta\otimes A-\mathcal T|
 \le L^{17}\sqrt\beta,\qquad
 |\nabla C+\theta\otimes A|+|\mathcal T|\le2L.
\]
Furthermore,
\[
 \left||[A,C]|^2-\frac{n^2}{m}u\right|\le L^{15}\beta,
 \qquad |v-\tau^2|\le L^{24}\beta,
 \qquad |a-n|+|a-b-n|\le L^3\beta .
\]
For the commutator estimate, use
\([A_*,C_{\mathrm{diag}}]=0\),
\(|[H,C]|\le2d\), and
\(|[A_*,C-C_{\mathrm{diag}}]|^2
=D^2(u-|C_{\mathrm{diag}}|^2)\).
Substituting these bounds into \eqref{quant:q-weighted-identity},
and replacing \(Q\kappa\) by \(n\kappa\) using the displayed
bound on \(\varepsilon\kappa\), yields
\begin{equation}\label{quant:q-leading}
 \left|\kappa-\frac{n-4}{m}u-\frac{n+4}{n}v\right|
 \le L^{30}\sqrt\beta .
\end{equation}
In particular, this replacement does not discard an uncontrolled
factor \(\varepsilon/\beta\).

Choose \(x_0\) with \(u(x_0)=1\).  At this point
\(|z(x_0)|^2=(1-|C_{\mathrm{diag}}(x_0)|^2)/2\ge1/4\).
Write
\[
 E_z=\nabla z-\mu\tau P^\perp .
\]
Differentiating \(u\) in the unit direction
\(X=z(x_0)/|z(x_0)|\), and using \(\nabla u(x_0)=0\), gives
\[
 0=4\mu\tau(x_0)|z(x_0)|
   +4\langle E_z(X),z(x_0)\rangle
   +2\langle\nabla_XC_{\mathrm{diag}},C_{\mathrm{diag}}\rangle .
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{quant:cylinder-start}
 |\tau(x_0)|\le L^{17}\sqrt\beta,\qquad
 v(x_0)\le L^{35}\beta,\qquad
 \left|\kappa-\frac{n-4}{m}\right|\le L^{37}\sqrt\beta .
\end{equation}

Let \(\gamma\) be the unit-speed geodesic with
\(\gamma(0)=x_0\), \(\gamma'(0)=X\), and write \(T=\gamma'\).
Along \(\gamma\), set
\[
 a_0(s)=\langle T,e_0\rangle,\qquad
 \ell(s)=\langle z,T\rangle,\qquad
 y(s)=\frac{\tau}{\sqrt n},\qquad
 \varpi=\sqrt{\frac nm}.
\]
The subscript on \(a_0\) distinguishes this scalar angle from the
normal eigenvalue \(a\).  Since
\(\nabla_Te_0=(\nabla_TP)e_0\) and \(a_0(0)=0\),
\[
 |a_0(s)|\le3L^{12}\beta s\le6L^{12}\beta
 \qquad(0\le s\le2).
\]
The first-order equations \eqref{quant:cylinder-first-order}
imply
\begin{equation}\label{quant:cylinder-oscillator}
 \ell'=\varpi y+e_1,\qquad
 y'=-\varpi\ell+e_2,\qquad
 |e_1|+|e_2|\le L^{28}\beta^{1/4},
\end{equation}
where, explicitly,
\[
 \begin{split}
 e_1&=\langle E_z(T),T\rangle-\varpi y\,a_0^2,\\
 e_2&=\frac1{\sqrt n}
 \left(\langle P^\perp\nabla\tau+Dz,P^\perp T\rangle
       +a_0\nabla_{e_0}\tau\right).
 \end{split}
\]
Put \(s_*=\pi/(2\varpi)<2\).  The unperturbed system is an
orthogonal rotation, so variation of constants gives
\[
 |(\ell(s_*),y(s_*))-(y(0),-\ell(0))|
 \le2L^{28}\beta^{1/4}.
\]
Using \eqref{quant:cylinder-start},
\begin{equation}\label{quant:cylinder-rotation-end}
 |\ell(s_*)|\le L^{30}\beta^{1/4},\qquad
 |y(s_*)+\ell(0)|\le L^{29}\beta^{1/4}.
\end{equation}

It remains to control the whole vector \(z\), rather than just its
component along \(T\).  Let \(w=z-\ell T\).  Then \(w(0)=0\), and
\[
 \nabla_Tw
 =\mu\tau(a_0^2T-a_0e_0)
   +E_z(T)-\langle E_z(T),T\rangle T.
\]
Since \(|a_0^2T-a_0e_0|\le|a_0|\), this gives
\[
 |\nabla_Tw|\le6NL^{12}\beta+L^{15}\sqrt\beta
 \le L^{16}\sqrt\beta,\qquad
 |w(s_*)|\le L^{17}\sqrt\beta .
\]
Together with \eqref{quant:cylinder-decomposition} and
\eqref{quant:cylinder-rotation-end}, it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{quant:cylinder-end}
 u(\gamma(s_*))\le L^{64}\beta^{1/2},\qquad
 \left|v(\gamma(s_*))-\frac n2\right|
 \le L^{32}\beta^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
For the second estimate, use
\(v=ny^2+|\theta_\perp|^2\) and
\(\ell(0)^2=(1-|C_{\mathrm{diag}}(x_0)|^2)/2\).
Applying \eqref{quant:q-leading} at the endpoint yields
\[
 \left|\kappa-\frac{n+4}{2}\right|
 \le L^{66}\beta^{1/4}.
\]
The two leading values of \(\kappa\) are separated by
\[
 \frac{n+4}{2}-\frac{n-4}{n-1}
 =\frac{n^2+n+4}{2(n-1)}\ge4
 \qquad(n\ge3).
\]
But their combined error is at most
\[
 L^{37}\sqrt\beta+L^{66}\beta^{1/4}
 \le L^{70}\beta^{1/4}
 \le L^{-430}<1.
\]
This final contradiction excludes \(\beta>0\) and proves the lemma.

For $n\ge3$, retain the constants $\Theta_n$ and $B_n$ from
\eqref{quant:entry-constants}, and put
\begin{equation}\label{quant:explicit-threshold}
 \begin{split}
 x&=100n,\qquad K=x^{10000},\qquad
 \eta_n=x^{-10^{14}},\\
 \widehat\gamma_n
 &=\min\left\{\Theta_n,
       B_n^{-1}x^{-(2\cdot10^{14}+20002)}\right\}.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Suppose that $Q=n+\varepsilon$ is constant and
$0<\varepsilon\le\widehat\gamma_n$.
Proposition~\ref{quant:global-entry} gives
$\beta\le B_n\varepsilon$. Set $t=B_n\varepsilon>0$.
Then $\varepsilon\le t$, $\beta\le t$, and
\[
 t\le x^{-(2\cdot10^{14}+20002)}\le x^{-30000}.
\]
Lemma~\ref{quant:jets-clifford-entry} supplies a fixed Clifford orbit
$\mathcal O_p$ on a connected finite covering, and gives
\[
 \sqrt\beta+
 \sup_M\operatorname{dist}(A,\mathcal O_p)
 +\sum_{r=1}^6\sup_M|\nabla^rA|
 \le K\sqrt t
 \le x^{-10^{14}-1}<\eta_n.
\]
With $L=x^{100001}$ as in the quantitative rigidity lemma,
\[
 \eta_n=x^{-10^{14}}
       \le x^{-100001000}=L^{-1000}.
\]
Thus every hypothesis of
Lemma~\ref{quant:near-clifford-rigidity} is satisfied.
That lemma gives $\varepsilon=\beta=0$, a contradiction.
We have proved the exclusion of all
$0<\varepsilon\le\widehat\gamma_n$.

For clarity, the comparison with the simpler formula
\eqref{eq:explicit-main-gap} is entirely numerical. Since
$\log(3\cdot10^6)<15$, $\log n\le n$, and $n\ge3$, the definition
of $\Theta_n$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{quant:threshold-log-comparison}
 \begin{split}
 -\log\Theta_n
 &=\max\left\{
 (n+2)\log(3\cdot10^6)+(11n+20)\log2
                  +(3n+4)\log n,\right.\\[-2pt]
 &\hspace{39mm}\left.\log(10000n^2)\right\}
 <50n^2.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Also $\log(100n)\le2n\le n^2$, so
\[
 \begin{split}
 \log B_n+(2\cdot10^{14}+20002)\log x
 &=5000n^{3/2}+(2\cdot10^{14}+20002)\log(100n)\\
 &<10^{15}n^2.
 \end{split}
\]
Consequently
\[
 -\log\widehat\gamma_n<10^{15}n^2<10^{16}n^2,
 \qquad
 \exp(-10^{16}n^2)<\widehat\gamma_n.
\]
The exclusion just proved therefore applies whenever
$0<Q-n\le\exp(-10^{16}n^2)$, and implies the stated gap.
