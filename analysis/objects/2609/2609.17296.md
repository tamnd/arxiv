<!-- head-1: body_md -->
Generative AI is emerging as a new class of intervention in the social sciences, with applications designed to change attitudes and behaviors through scalable, personalized interactions \citep[e.g.,][]{costello2024durably, bai2025llm}. At the same time, recent empirical studies highlight an important risk: while such AI interventions may benefit many individuals and tasks, they may also harm others. For example, a randomized experiment in a global consulting firm \citep{dell2023navigating} found that access to AI can harm the productivity of high-skilled consultants when working on challenging intellectual tasks. Similarly, \cite{bastani2025generative} found that generative AI without guardrails can harm the learning of high school math students. As interventions are powered by black-box AI, controlling the harm is fundamental for safety, public trust, and efficient deployment of AI-powered treatment.
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- head-2: body_md -->
Precision medicine---prevention and treatment strategies that account for individual variability---is central in modern medicine \citep{kosorok2019precision}. The problem of controlling individual risk while maximizing benefit has long been recognized.                                                       
It is especially relevant when efficacious medications may also lead to a higher risk for certain individuals, such as opioid treatment of chronic pain~\citep{laber2018identifying} and type-2 diabetes with insulin therapies~\citep{wang2018learning}.
<!-- end head-2 -->
<!-- head-3: body_md -->
In the US criminal justice system, how to safely use risk scores to evaluate an individual's likelihood of reoffending and identify their criminogenic needs is a major topic of interest \citep{skeem2020impact}. For example, \cite{ben2025safe} analyze a field experiment to estimate the causal effect of algorithmic recommendations on judges' decisions at a criminal first appearance hearing. Here, a treatment rule with the safety guarantee can  prevent arrestees from committing a new crime or failing to appear in court, while avoiding unnecessarily harsh decisions.
<!-- end head-3 -->
<!-- assumption-1: body_md -->
\label{assump:uncon}
    The treatment assignments $\{T_i\}_{i=1}^n$ are mutually independent and obey $T_i\perp \!\!\! \perp (Y_i(0),Y_i(1)){\,|\,} X_i$, with  $\mathbb{P}(T_i=1{\,|\,} X_i=x) \coloneq e(x)$, and we call $e(x)\in (0,1)$ the propensity score.
<!-- end assumption-1 -->
<!-- assumption-2: body_md -->
\label{assump:iid}
    The test point is drawn from $(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}(1),Y_{n+1}(0))\sim \mathbb{P}_{X,Y(1),Y(0)}$  independently of the labeled data.
<!-- end assumption-2 -->
<!-- head-4: body_md -->
One example is the clipped score function~\citep{jin2023selection}, $V(x,y) = M\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{y>0\} + (1 - \hat{\mu}_1(x))$, where $\hat{\mu}_1(x)$ is an estimator for the conditional expectation function ${\mathbb E}[Y_i \mid T_i = 1, X_i = x]$ and $M>2\sup_x|\hat\mu_1(x)|$ is a sufficiently large constant so that~\eqref{eq:cs_pval} reduces to 
\@\label{eq:cs_pval_clip}
p_{n+1}^{(1)} = \frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^n T_i  \cdot \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{Y_i= 0\} \cdot \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{ \hat{\mu}_1(X_i)\geq  \hat{\mu}_1(X_{n+1})\}}{1 + \sum_{i=1}^n T_i}.
\@ 
Intuitively, this p-value focuses on potentially unsafe cases (i.e., $Y_i(1) = 0$) among treated units and examines whether the test unit is extreme with respect to $\hat{\mu}_1(x)$. When this p-value is small, the test unit is unlikely to come from the distribution of potentially unsafe units, and thus is likely to be safe. \qed  \\

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:validity_rct}
    Assume Assumption~\ref{assump:uncon} with $e(x) \equiv 0.5$ and Assumption~\ref{assump:iid}.
    Then, for any score function $V \colon \mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}$ that is non-decreasing in the second argument and $\hat{g}\colon \mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}\to [0,1]$ whose training process is independent of $\{(X_i,T_i,Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\cup \{X_{n+1}\}$, and obey $\hat{g}(x,1)+\hat{g}(x,0)=a$ for a constant $a>0$ for any $x\in \mathcal{X}$, it holds that $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}(1) = 0, Y_{n+1}(0) = 1, p_{n+1} \leq \alpha) \leq \alpha$ for any $\alpha\in (0,1)$. That is, the finite-sample safety guarantee~\eqref{eq:safety_guarantee} holds for  $\hat\pi(X_{n+1}) =\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{p_{n+1}\leq \alpha\}$.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rmk:conservative}
Our conformal p-value is subject to two sources of conservativeness compared with $p_{n+1}^*$: (i) the ``oracle'' labels $Y_i^*$ are replaced by the proxy labels $Y_i^\dagger$, and (ii) the test label $Y_{n+1}^*$ is replaced by the threshold $0$. As in conformal selection~\citep{jin2023selection}, the issue (ii) will be addressed by a tailored ``clipped'' score function. 
On the other hand, (i) is rooted in the partial identification nature of the harm rate, and specific choices of $\hat{g}$ makes our p-value sharp; we shall discuss this in more details once the optimality results in the next section are ready.

    \text{where}\quad r^* := \sup\left\{ \tilde r\leq 0: \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X) \mathbf{1}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)\leq \tilde r\} \right] \leq \alpha \right\}.

<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We aim to show that 
\@\label{eq:target_prf}
\mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}\leq \alpha, Y_{n+1}^* =0 {\,|\,} \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n ) \leq \alpha. 
\@
Note that by definition, $Y_i^\dagger = T_iY_i+(1-T_i)(1-Y_i) \leq \max\{Y_i(1),1-Y_i(0)\}=Y_i^*$. Thus, by the monotonicity of $V(x,y)$ in $y$, on the event $\{Y_{n+1}^*=0\}$, it holds deterministically that 
\$
p_{n+1} \geq p_{n+1}^* :=  \frac{1+ \sum_{i=1}^n G_i   \cdot \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{V(X_i,Y_i^*) \leq V(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}^*)\} }{1+\sum_{i=1}^n G_i}.
\$
We thus have 
\@\label{eq:monotone_bd}
\mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}\leq \alpha, Y_{n+1}^* =0 {\,|\,} \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n )
\leq \mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}^*\leq \alpha, Y_{n+1}^* =0 {\,|\,} \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n ) 
\leq \mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}^*\leq \alpha  {\,|\,} \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n ).
\@
Now, conditional on $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^n$, we denote $\mathcal{I}:=\{i\in [n]\colon G_i=1\}$ as the index set of the selected calibration data. 
Let $P^{\sup}_{X,Y^*}$ be the (unknown) joint distribution of $(X,Y^*)$ induced by the unknown super-population $P^{\sup}$ of $(X_i,Y_i(1),Y_i(0))$. We then have $(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}^*)\sim P^{\sup}_{X,Y^*}$. 
For any measurable subset $A$ of $\mathcal{X}\times \{0,1\}$, by the Bayes' rule, we have  
\$
\frac{\mathbb{P}((X_i,Y_i^*)\in A {\,|\,} G_i=1)}{\mathbb{P}((X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}^*)\in A)}
= \frac{\mathbb{P}(G_i=1 {\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A) }{  \mathbb{P}(G_i=1)}.
\$
Furthermore, we know 
\$
\mathbb{P}(G_i=1 {\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A )
&= \mathbb{P}(G_i=1, T_i=1 {\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A ) + \mathbb{P}(G_i=1,T_i=0{\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A)) \\ 
&= \mathbb{P}(G_i=1 {\,|\,}   T_i=1, (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A ) \cdot \mathbb{P}(T_i = 1{\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A ) \\ 
&\qquad + \mathbb{P}(G_i=1 {\,|\,}   T_i=0, (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A ) \cdot \mathbb{P}(T_i = 0{\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A ) \\ 
&= 1/2\cdot \mathbb{E}[\hat{g}(X_i,1){\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A] + 1/2\cdot \mathbb{E}[\hat{g}(X_i,0){\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A].
\$
Since $\hat{g}(x,1)+\hat{g}(x,0)\equiv a$ for a constant $a>0$, we have 
\$
\mathbb{P}(G_i=1 {\,|\,} (X_i,Y_i^*)\in A ) = a/2, 
\$
and by the tower property, 
\$
\mathbb{P}(G_i=1) = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{P}(G_i=1{\,|\,} X_i)\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{P}(G_i=1,T_i=1{\,|\,} X_i) +\mathbb{P}(G_i=1,T_i=0{\,|\,} X_i) \big] =a/2.
\$
Putting things together, we know that 
\$
(X_i,Y_i^*){\,|\,} G_i=1 ~~ \stackrel{d}{=} ~~(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}^*).
\$
Thus, conditional on $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^n$, the samples $\{(X_i,Y_i)\}_{i\in \mathcal{I}_0\cup\{n+1\}}$ are exchangeable. 
Noting that 
\$
p_{n+1}^* = \frac{1+\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_0}\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{V(X_i,Y_i^*)\leq V(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}^*)\}}{1+|\mathcal{I}_0|},
\$
we know that $\mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}^*\leq \alpha {\,|\,} \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n )\leq \alpha$ in~\eqref{eq:monotone_bd}; see, e.g.,~\cite{jin2023selection} or~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic}. 
Marginalizing over $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^n$, we complete the proof of~\eqref{eq:target_prf}. Finally, taking $T_{n+1}=\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{p_{n+1}\leq \alpha\}$, we have 
\$
\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}(T_{n+1})<Y_{n+1}(0)) = \mathbb{P}(T_{n+1}=1,~Y_{n+1}^*=0) = \mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}\leq \alpha, ~Y_{n+1}^*=0)\leq \alpha,
\$
thereby concluding the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:validity_rct}.

Write  $\tau(x):=\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x)$, 
$\hat\tau(x):=\hat\mu_1(x)-\hat\mu_0(x)$, and recall that $\gamma(x)=\min\{1-\mu_1(x),\mu_0(x)\}$ and $\hat\gamma(x) =\min\{1-\hat\mu_1(x),\hat\mu_0(x)\}$. We simplify the notation to
\[

\] Let $\mathcal T_n$ denote the sigma-field generated by the independent training process for $\hat\mu_0$ and $\hat\mu_1$. Conditional on $\mathcal T_n$, the estimated functions $\hat\mu_t$, $\hat\gamma$, $\hat s$, and the inclusion rule in (3.10) are fixed. We first show that selective calibration consistently recovers the sharp worst-case harm-rate function. Define \[ q_1(x):=1-\mu_1(x),\qquad q_0(x):=\mu_0(x), \] and analogously $\hat q_1(x):=1-\hat\mu_1(x)$ and $\hat q_0(x):=\hat\mu_0(x)$. Let $\hat a(x)\in\{0,1\}$ denote the arm selected by (3.10), so that $\hat a(x)=1$ when $\hat q_1(x)\leq\hat q_0(x)$ and $\hat a(x)=0$ otherwise. 
Define the true proxy-label risk of the selected arm by 
\[ 
\overline\gamma_n(x) := q_1(x) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat a(x)=1\} + q_0(x) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat a(x)=0\}. 
\] 
Since $\gamma(x)=\min\{q_0(x),q_1(x)\}$, we have $\overline\gamma_n(x)\geq\gamma(x)$. Moreover, if $a^*(x)\in\arg\min_{a\in\{0,1\}}q_a(x)$, the optimality of $\hat a(x)$ for the estimated risks gives 
\$ 
0 \leq \overline\gamma_n(x)-\gamma(x) &= q_{\hat a(x)}(x)-q_{a^*(x)}(x)\\ &\leq \left| q_{\hat a(x)}(x) -\hat q_{\hat a(x)}(x) \right| + \left| \hat q_{a^*(x)}(x)-q_{a^*(x)}(x) \right|\\ &\leq 2\left\{ |\hat\mu_1(x)-\mu_1(x)| + |\hat\mu_0(x)-\mu_0(x)| \right\}. 
\$
The assumed $L_2(P_X)$ convergence and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality therefore imply \[ \|\overline\gamma_n-\gamma\|_{L_1(P_X)} = o_P(1). \] 

We next establish convergence of the estimated ranking. The outcome-model consistency implies $\|\hat\tau-\tau\|_{L_2(P_X)}=o_P(1)$ and $\|\hat\gamma-\gamma\|_{L_2(P_X)} =o_P(1)$, where the latter follows from the Lipschitz property of the minimum function. Under the ratio conventions in~\eqref{eq:s_welfare_optimal}, if $\gamma(X)=0$, then $s^*(X)$ must take one of the three values $-\infty$, $0$, or $+\infty$. By the no-point-mass assumption for $s^*(X)$, we have $\mathbb{P}\{\gamma(X)=0\}=0$. 
The continuous mapping theorem then gives $\hat s(X)-s^*(X) = o_P(1)$,  where $X\sim P_X$ is independent of the training process. 
The preceding convergence also holds uniformly for threshold indicators. Indeed, for every $\varepsilon>0$, 
\$
&\sup_{t\in\mathbb R} \mathbb{P}\!\left(  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\} \neq  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s^*(X)\leq t\} \,\middle|\,\mathcal T_n \right)\\ &\qquad\leq \mathbb{P}\!\left( |\hat s(X)-s^*(X)|>\varepsilon \,\middle|\,\mathcal T_n \right) + \sup_{t\in\mathbb R} \mathbb{P}\{|s^*(X)-t|\leq\varepsilon\}. 
\$
The first term is $o_P(1)$. The second term converges to zero as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ because $s^*(X)$ has no point mass. It follows that 
\[ 
\sup_{t\in\mathbb R} \mathbb{P}\!\left(  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\} \neq  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s^*(X)\leq t\} \,\middle|\,\mathcal T_n \right) = o_P(1).
\] 
Now define the empirical  curve 
\[ 
F_n(t) := \frac{ 1+\sum_{i=1}^n G_i \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{Y_i^\dagger=0\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X_i)\leq t\} }{ 1+\sum_{i=1}^nG_i }, \qquad t\in\mathbb R, 
\] 
so that $p^{\mathrm{welfare}}_{n+1} =F_n\{\hat s(X_{n+1})\}$. 
Conditional on $\mathcal T_n$, the summands are i.i.d. and bounded, thus Lemma~\ref{lem:wcdf_conv} gives
\[ 
\sup_{t\in \mathbb{R}}\bigg| F_n(t) - \frac{ \mathbb{E}\!\left[ G \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{Y^\dagger=0\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\} \,\middle|\,\mathcal T_n \right] }{ \mathbb{E}[G\mid\mathcal T_n] } \bigg| = o_P(1). 
\] 
Under balanced randomization and the given inclusion rule, we know $\mathbb{E}[G\mid X,\mathcal T_n]=1/2$. Furthermore, conditional on $X=x$, the probability that the selected proxy label is zero is $q_1(x)$ when $\hat a(x)=1$ and $q_0(x)$ when $\hat a(x)=0$. Consequently, 
\[ 
\mathbb{E}\!\left[ G \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{Y^\dagger=0\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\} \,\middle|\,\mathcal T_n \right] = \frac12 \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \overline\gamma_n(X)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\} \,\middle|\,\mathcal T_n \right]. 
\] 
Therefore, 
\[ 
\sup_{t\in\mathbb R} \left| F_n(t)-\overline H_n(t) \right| = o_{\mathbb P}(1), \qquad \overline H_n(t) := \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \overline\gamma_n(X)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\} \,\middle|\,\mathcal T_n \right]. 
\] 
Define the oracle harm-cost curve \[ H(t) := \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s^*(X)\leq t\} \right]. \] Using $0\leq\gamma\leq1$, we have
\$ 
\sup_{t\in\mathbb R} |\overline H_n(t)-H(t)| &\leq \|\overline\gamma_n-\gamma\|_{L_1(P_X)} + \sup_{t\in\mathbb R} \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X) \left|  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\} -  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s^*(X)\leq t\} \right| \,\middle|\,\mathcal T_n \right] = o_P(1). 
\$
Combining the preceding displays gives  
$\sup_{t\in\mathbb R}|F_n(t)-H(t)| = o_P(1)$.  
The function $H$ is continuous because its jump at any $t\in\mathbb R$ is $\mathbb{E}[\gamma(X) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s^*(X)=t\}]=0$. Thus, for the independent test point, 
\$ 
\left| p^{\mathrm{welfare}}_{n+1} - H\{s^*(X_{n+1})\} \right| &\leq \sup_{t\in\mathbb R}|F_n(t)-H(t)| + \left| H\{\hat s(X_{n+1})\} - H\{s^*(X_{n+1})\} \right| = 0_P(1). 
\$ 
We next translate this convergence into convergence of treatment decisions. 
Since $\tau(X)=0$ implies $s^*(X)=0$, the no-point-mass condition gives $\mathbb{P}\{\tau(X)=0\}=0$. Hence,  $ \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat\tau(X_{n+1})>0\} -  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\tau(X_{n+1})>0\}\to 0$ in probability. 
We also claim that \[ \mathbb{P}\!\left( H\{s^*(X)\}=\alpha,\, \tau(X)>0 \right)=0. \] 
To see this, let $J_\alpha := \{t<0:H(t)=\alpha\}$.  Since $H$ is nondecreasing, $J_\alpha$ is an interval. 
If $a<b$ lie in the interior of this interval, then $0 = H(b)-H(a) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{a<s^*(X)\leq b\} \right]$. 
Since $\gamma(X)>0$ almost surely, it follows that $\mathbb{P}\{a<s^*(X)\leq b\}=0$. 
The endpoints of $J_\alpha$ also have probability zero because $s^*(X)$ has no point mass. This proves the claim. 
Recall that  $\hat\pi_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) = \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{p^{\mathrm{welfare}}_{n+1}\leq\alpha\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat\tau(X_{n+1})>0\}$. 
The convergence $p^{\mathrm{welfare}}_{n+1} - H\{s^*(X_{n+1})\} = o_{\mathbb P}(1)$, the convergence of the treatment-effect indicator, and the preceding zero-probability claim imply  $\mathbb{P}\!\left\{ \hat\pi_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) \neq \pi_0(X_{n+1}) \right\}\to 0$, where \[ \pi_0(x) := \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{H(s^*(x))\leq\alpha\} \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\tau(x)>0\}. \] 

It remains to identify $\pi_0$ with the oracle policy in Theorem~\ref{thm:welfare_optimal}. Let $r^*\leq0$ be the cutoff in that theorem. Suppose first that the safety constraint is nonbinding. Then $r^*=0$ and, since $\mathbb{P}\{\tau(X)=0\}=0$, \[ H(0) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\tau(X)>0\} \right] \leq\alpha. \] For every $x$ such that $\tau(x)>0$, we have $s^*(x)<0$, and therefore $ H\{s^*(x)\} \leq H(0) \leq\alpha$. 
It follows that \[ \pi_0(X) =  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\tau(X)>0\} =  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s^*(X)\leq0\} = \pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X) \quad\text{almost surely}. \] Suppose instead that the safety constraint is binding. Then $r^*<0$. By the continuity of $H$ and the definition of $r^*$, we have $H(r^*)=\alpha$.  By the monotonicity of $H$ and the preceding zero-probability result for the level set $\{t<0:H(t)=\alpha\}$, we know 
\[  
\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{H(s^*(X))\leq\alpha\} =  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s^*(X)\leq r^*\} \quad\text{almost surely}. 
\] 
Moreover, the indicator $ \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\tau(X)>0\}$ is redundant on the event $\{s^*(X)\leq r^*\}$ because $r^*<0$. Consequently, \[ \pi_0(X) =  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s^*(X)\leq r^*\} =  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)\leq r^*\} = \pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X) \quad\text{almost surely}. \] 
We have therefore shown that 
$\mathbb{P}\!\left\{ \hat\pi_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) \neq \pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) \right\}\to 0$, which implies  
\$
& \left| \mathbb{E}\!\left[ Y\{\hat\pi_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1})\} \right] - \operatorname{Welfare} (\pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}};P) \right| \leq \mathbb{P}\!\left\{ \hat\pi_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) \neq \pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) \right\} \to 0. 
\$
This completes the proof.

The p-value~\eqref{eq:def_pval_weight} can be  equivalently written as

p_{n+1} = \frac{w(X_{n+1}) +\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{\textrm{calib}}} w(X_i)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{V(X_i,Y_i^\dagger) \leq V(X_{n+1},0)\}}{\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_\textrm{calib}} w(X_i) + w(X_{n+1})}.

By definition, we have $Y_i^\dagger = T_iY_i+(1-T_i)(1-Y_i) \leq \max\{Y_i(1),1-Y_i(0)\}=Y_i^*$. Thus, by the monotonicity of $V(x,y)$ in $y$, on the event $\{Y_{n+1}^*=0\}$, it holds deterministically that 
\$
p_{n+1} \geq p_{n+1}^* :=  \frac{w(X_{n+1}) +\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{\textrm{calib}}} w(X_i)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{V(X_i,Y_i^*) \leq V(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}^*)\}}{\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_\textrm{calib}} w(X_i) + w(X_{n+1})}.
\$
We thus have 
\@\label{eq:monotone_bd_weight}
\mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}\leq \alpha, Y_{n+1}^* =0 {\,|\,} \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n )
\leq \mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}^*\leq \alpha, Y_{n+1}^* =0 {\,|\,} \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n ) \leq \mathbb{P}(p_{n+1}^*\leq \alpha  {\,|\,} \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n ).
\@
The goal is then to prove the RHS of~\eqref{eq:monotone_bd_weight} is upper bounded by $\alpha$.

Conditional on $\{G_i\}_{i=1}^n$, the   data $(X_i,Y_i^*)$ for  $i\in\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{calib}}$ are i.i.d.~and follow the distribution 
\$
P^{\textrm{calib}} :\stackrel{d}{=} P_{(X_i,Y_i^*){\,|\,} G_i=1}, 
\$
whereas the test point $(X_i,Y_i^*)$ follows the distribution 
\$
P^{\textrm{test}} := P_{X_i,Y_i^*},
\$
and we recall that $P$ denotes the true underlying super-population distribution. Let $p(x,y^*)$  be the density function of $P_{X,Y^*}$  with respect to some base measure. The density ratio between the two distributions is 
\$
\frac{{\,\mathrm{d}} P^{\textrm{test}} }{{\,\mathrm{d}} P^{\textrm{calib}}} (x,y) = \frac{p(x,y^*)}{p(x,y^*{\,|\,} G=1)} = \frac{p(x) p(y^*{\,|\,} x)}{p(x{\,|\,} G=1)p(y^*{\,|\,} x,G=1)},
\$
where we define the random variable $G=\hat{g}(X,T)$, and $p(y^*{\,|\,} x,G=1)$ is the conditional density of $Y_i^*$ given $X_i=x$ and $G_i=1$, etc. 
By unconfoundedness, $Y^*$ is independent of $T$ conditional on $X$. This leads to 
\$
\frac{{\,\mathrm{d}} P^{\textrm{test}} }{{\,\mathrm{d}} P^{\textrm{calib}}} (x,y)   = \frac{p(x) p(y^*{\,|\,} x)}{p(x{\,|\,} G=1)p(y^*{\,|\,} x)} = \frac{p(x)  }{p(x{\,|\,} G=1) }  = \frac{\mathbb{P}(G=1)}{\mathbb{P}(G=1{\,|\,} X=x)}.
\$
Now, by the definition of the $G_i$'s, we know 
\$
\mathbb{P}(G=1{\,|\,} X=x)                                       
& = 
\mathbb{P}(G=1,T=1{\,|\,} X=x) + \mathbb{P}(G=1,T=0{\,|\,} X=x) \\ 
&= \mathbb{P}(G=1{\,|\,} T=1, X=x) \mathbb{P}(T=1{\,|\,} X=x) + \mathbb{P}(G=1{\,|\,} T=0,X=x)\mathbb{P}(T=0{\,|\,} X=x) \\  
&= e(x) \hat{g}(x,1) + (1-e(x))\hat{g}(x,0).
\$
                                                                                                                                                                                                                 
This implies the density ratio between $P^\textrm{test}$ and $P^\textrm{calib}$ is given by 
\$
\frac{{\,\mathrm{d}} P^{\textrm{test}} }{{\,\mathrm{d}} P^{\textrm{calib}}} (x,y) = \mathbb{P}(G=1)\cdot w(x).
\$
In other words, $\{(X_i,Y_i^*\}_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{\textrm{calib}}\cup\{n+1\}}$ are weighted exchangeable~\citep{TibshiraniBCR19}, and thus following~\cite{TibshiraniBCR19}, we know that 
\$
\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\frac{w(X_{n+1}) +\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{\textrm{calib}}} w(X_i)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{V(X_i,Y_i^*) \leq V(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1}^*)\}}{\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_\textrm{calib}} w(X_i) + w(X_{n+1})} \leq \alpha \,\Bigg{|}\, \{G_i\}_{i=1}^n\Bigg) \leq \alpha.
\$
This proves the desired upper bound on the RHS of~\eqref{eq:monotone_bd_weight} hence Theorem~\ref{thm:valid_weighted}.

Let \[ \hat\pi_{\mathrm{obs,raw}}(X_{n+1}) :=  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{p^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n+1}\leq\alpha\}. \]
Fix $0<\varepsilon\leq\eta$. The preceding  results imply that, with probability tending to
one,
\$ 
\hat s(X_{n+1}) \leq t_n^\circ-\varepsilon \quad\Rightarrow\quad p^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n+1}\leq\alpha, \\
\hat s(X_{n+1}) \geq t_n^\circ+\varepsilon \quad\Rightarrow\quad p^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n+1}>\alpha.  
\$
Therefore, we have 
\[
\left| \hat\pi_{\mathrm{obs,raw}}(X_{n+1}) -  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X_{n+1})\leq t_n^\circ\} \right| \leq  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{ |\hat s(X_{n+1})-t_n^\circ| \leq\varepsilon \} + o_{\mathbb P}(1).
\]
Assumption~\ref{assump:bal_reg}(iv) and the arbitrariness of $\varepsilon>0$ then imply 
$\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \left| \hat\pi_{\mathrm{obs,raw}}(X_{n+1}) -  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X_{n+1})\leq t_n^\circ\} \right| \right] \to 0$. Since multiplication by an indicator cannot increase the absolute difference, the preceding display gives
\$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \left| \hat\pi_{\mathrm{obs-welfare}}(X_{n+1}) -  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X_{n+1})\leq t_n^\circ\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X_{n+1})<0\} \right| \right] \to 0.
\$
We next pass to the population limit. The assumptions $\|\hat s-s_{\mathrm{welfare}}\|_{L_2(P_X)} =o_{\mathbb P}(1)$ and $t_n^\circ-t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*=o_{\mathbb P}(1)$, together with the no-point-mass condition on $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)$, imply 
\$ 
\mathbb{E}\Big[ \big| &  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X_{n+1})\leq t_n^\circ\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X_{n+1})<0\}  -  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) \leq t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1})<0\} \big| \Big] \to 0.
\$ 
Indeed, for any $\varepsilon>0$, disagreement between the first threshold indicators is contained in the union of $\{|\hat s-s_{\mathrm{welfare}}|>\varepsilon\}$, $\{|t_n^\circ-t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*|>\varepsilon\}$, and $\{|s_{\mathrm{welfare}}-t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*|\leq2\varepsilon\}$. The corresponding argument at the threshold zero establishes convergence of the second indicators. 

It remains to identify the limiting rule with the oracle policy in Theorem~\ref{thm:welfare_optimal}. Let $r^*\leq0$ denote the cutoff therein. We consider two cases:
\begin{itemize} 
\item If the safety constraint is binding, then $H_0(0)>\alpha$, so $t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*<0$ and $t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*=r^*$. In this case, $ \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}<0\}$ is redundant on $\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}\leq t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*\}$, and hence $\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X) \leq t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)<0\} =  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)\leq r^*\} = \pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)$  almost surely. 
\item 
If the safety constraint is nonbinding, then $H_0(0)\leq\alpha$, so $r^*=0$ and $t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*\geq0$. Therefore,  $\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X) \leq t_{\mathrm{raw}}^*\}  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)<0\} =  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)<0\}$. Since $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)$ has no point mass at zero, this equals $ \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)\leq0\} = \pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)$ almost surely. 
\end{itemize}
Combining the preceding results gives  $\mathbb{E} [\left| \hat\pi_{\mathrm{obs-welfare}}(X_{n+1}) - \pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) \right|] \to 0 $. Finally, since the outcomes are binary, we know 
\$ 
\left| \mathbb{E}\!\left[ Y\{\hat\pi_{\mathrm{obs-welfare}}(X_{n+1})\} \right] - \operatorname{Welfare}(\pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}};P) \right| &\leq \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \left| \hat\pi_{\mathrm{obs-welfare}}(X_{n+1}) - \pi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X_{n+1}) \right| \right] \to 0,
\$
which completes the proof.

\[

\[

\leq
\alpha

\]
The bounded-density condition, $\hat\tau_0^*-t_n^\circ=o_P(1)$, and

$H_n^\gamma(\hat\tau_0^*)=\alpha+o_P(1)$ and 
$F_n^\gamma(\hat\tau_0^*)=\alpha+o_P(1)$. 
Consequently, $R_n\leq\alpha+o_P(1)$. Marginalizing over $\mathcal{A}_n$
completes the proof in this case.

We reuse the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dr_obs} up to~\eqref{eq:risk_bd23}  to prove the rate double robustness result. Note that the results up to~\eqref{eq:risk_bd23} did not use any assumptions on the fitted models.  Define
\[
\hat\omega^\dagger(x):=\frac{\hat\omega(x)}{p_G}.
\]
Since $q(x)\geq c$, we have $p_G=\mathbb{E}[q(X)\mid{\mathcal{T}}_n]\geq c$.
Therefore, Assumption~\ref{assump:obs_slow} implies
\[
\|\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w\|_{L_\infty(\mathbb{P}_X)}
= 
\|\hat\omega-w^\circ\|_{L_\infty(\mathbb{P}_X)} / p_G
=
O_P(n^{-1/4}).
\]
Moreover, replacing $\hat\omega$ by $\hat{\omega}^\dagger$ does not change
any weighted empirical or population ratio.
Recall that $\mathcal{A}_n$ is the $\sigma$-field that includes the randomness in the labeled data, the training process, and generating $\{G_i\}$, and we denote the $\mathcal{A}_n$-conditional harm rate  
$R_n := \mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}(\pi_{\text{obs}}(X_{n+1}))<Y_{n+1}(0){\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n)$. 
The balancing condition in Assumption~\ref{assump:obs_bal} with $r_n=O(n^{-1/2})$, together with~\eqref{eq:risk_bd23}, implies 
\$
R_n &\leq \alpha + \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X_{n+1})\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_{n+1})\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}{\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n] - \frac{  \sum_{i=1}^n G_i \hat\omega^\dagger(X_i)\gamma(X_i)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_i)\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}}{\sum_{i=1}^n G_i \hat\omega^\dagger(X_i)  }  \\ 
&\qquad + \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n G_i \hat\omega^\dagger(X_i) \hat\gamma(X_i) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_i)\leq \hat{t}\}}{\sum_{i=1}^n G_i\hat\omega^\dagger(X_i)} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat\gamma(X_i) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_i)\leq \hat{t}\} +O_P(1/\sqrt{n}),
\$
where the second and the third terms invoked Lemma~\ref{lem:w_to_wx} twice. 
By Assumption~\ref{assump:obs_bal}, we have
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nG_i\hat\omega(X_i)
&=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nG_i\hat w_i
+
O_P(1/\sqrt{n}) =
\frac{m_n}{n}\{1+O_P(1/\sqrt{n})\}
+
O_P(1/\sqrt{n}) =
p_G+O_P(1/\sqrt{n}).
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently, 
$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nG_i\hat\omega^\dagger(X_i)
=
1+O_P(1/\sqrt{n})$, which further yields
\$
R_n &\leq \alpha + \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X_{n+1})\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_{n+1})\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}{\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n] - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat\gamma(X_i) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_i)\leq \hat{t}\}  \\ 
&\quad + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n G_i \hat\omega^\dagger(X_i) \hat\gamma(X_i) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_i)\leq \hat{t}\}  - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n G_i \hat\omega^\dagger(X_i)\gamma(X_i)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_i)\leq \hat\tau_0^*\} +O_P(1/\sqrt{n}) \\
 &\leq \alpha + \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X)\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}{\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n] - \mathbb{E}[\hat\gamma(X ) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X )\leq \hat{t}\}{\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n ] \\ 
&\quad + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{g}(X_i,T_i) \hat\omega^\dagger(X_i) \hat\gamma(X_i) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_i)\leq \hat{t}\}  - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{g}(X_i,T_i)  \hat\omega^\dagger(X_i)\gamma(X_i)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_i)\leq \hat\tau_0^*\} +O_P(1/\sqrt{n}),
\$
where $X\sim \mathbb{P}_X$ is an independent copy. 
Here, the second inequality uses Lemma~\ref{lem:wcdf_conv} for $Z_i=(\hat{G}_i-\hat{g}(X_i,T_i))\hat{\omega}^\dagger(X_i)\gamma(X_i)$ or $Z_i =(\hat{G}_i-\hat{g}(X_i,T_i))\hat{\omega}^\dagger(X_i)\hat\gamma(X_i)$, as well as Lemma~\ref{lem:wcdf_conv} for $Z_i=\hat\gamma(X_i)$ and $H_n(t)=\mathbb{E}[\hat\gamma(X)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X)\leq t\}{\,|\,} {\mathcal{T}}_n]$ evaluated at $t=\hat{t}$ which is adapted to the $\sigma$-field $\mathcal{A}_n$. 

For the oracle weight $w(X)$, we know $\mathbb{E}[G_iw(X_i)]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}(X,T)w(X)]=1$, and due to the covariate shift between calibration data conditional on $G_i=1$ and the test data, 
\$
& \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X_{n+1})\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_{n+1})\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}{\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n] =  \mathbb{E}[\hat{g}(X,T) w(X)\gamma(X)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X )\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}{\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n],\\
 & \mathbb{E}[\hat\gamma(X_{n+1})\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X_{n+1})\leq \hat t\}{\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n] =  \mathbb{E}[\hat{g}(X,T) w(X)\hat\gamma(X)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(X )\leq \hat t\}{\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n],
\$
where the expectations are over an independent copy $(X,T)\sim \mathbb{P}_{X,T}$. Thus, denoting 
\$
\hat{r}(x,t) = \hat{g}(x,t) \cdot \big\{ \hat\gamma(x)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(x)\leq \hat{t}\} - \gamma(x) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(x)\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}\big\}
\$
which is a function adapted to $\mathcal{A}_n$ and obeys $\hat{r}(x,t)\in [-1,1]$ for any value of $(x,t)$, since $\hat{g}(x,t)\in [0,1]$ and $\hat\gamma(x),\gamma(x)\in [0,1]$. 
Then, for an independent copy $(X,T)\sim \mathbb{P}_{X,T}$, 
\$
R_n & \leq \alpha + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n  \hat{\omega}^\dagger(X_i) \hat{r}(X_i,T_i) - \mathbb{E}[w(X)\hat{r}(X,T){\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n] + O_P(1/\sqrt{n}) \\
&\leq \alpha + \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \big\{\hat{\omega}^\dagger(X_i)-w(X_i)\big\} \hat{r}(X_i,T_i)}_{\text{(a)}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \Big\{ w(X_i)\hat{r}(X_i,T_i) - \mathbb{E}[w(X)\hat{r}(X,T){\,|\,} \mathcal{A}_n] \Big\}}_{\text{(b)}} + O_P(1/\sqrt{n}).
\$
We now proceed to control the two terms separately via uniform convergence. 
Let ${\mathcal{T}}_n$ denote the $\sigma$-field generated by the
training processes, conditional on which $\hat g$, $\hat\gamma$, and
$\hat s$ are fixed. Writing $P_n$ for the empirical measure of
$(X_i,T_i)_{i=1}^n$ and $P$ for the expectation over an independent copy,
define
\[
f_{1,u}(x,t)
:=
w(x)\hat g(x,t)\hat\gamma(x)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(x)\leq u\},
\qquad
f_{2,v}(x,t)
:=
w(x)\hat g(x,t)\gamma(x)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(x)\leq v\}.
\]
By definition, as the cutoffs $\hat\tau_0^*$ and $\hat{t}$ in the definition of $\hat{r}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is fixed given $\mathcal{A}_n$, we  know 
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\text{(b)}|
&=
\left|
(P_n-P)f_{1,\hat t}
-
(P_n-P)f_{2,\hat\tau_0^*}
\right| \leq
\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}|(P_n-P)f_{1,u}|
+
\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}}|(P_n-P)f_{2,v}| = O_P(1/\sqrt{n}),
\end{aligned}
\]
where the last equality follows from applying Lemma~\ref{lem:wcdf_conv} twice,  
provided that
\( \|w\hat g\hat\gamma\|_{L_2(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})}
+
\|w\hat g\gamma\|_{L_2(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})}
=
O_P(1)
\)
based on the boundedness of $w(\cdot)$, $\hat{g}$, $\gamma$, and $\hat\gamma$. 
For term $(a)$, we define for any $u,v\in\mathbb{R}$ the function 
\[
r_{u,v}(x,t)
:=
\hat g(x,t)\left\{
\hat\gamma(x)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(x)\leq u\}
-
\gamma(x)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(x)\leq v\}
\right\},
\]
so that $\hat r=r_{\hat t,\hat\tau_0^*}$. We then have 
\$
|\text{(a)}|
&=
\big|P_n\{(\hat{\omega}^\dagger - w)r_{\hat t,\hat\tau_0^*}\}\big|
\leq
\big|P\{(\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w)r_{\hat t,\hat\tau_0^*}\}\big|
+
\sup_{u,v\in\mathbb{R}}
\big|
(P_n-P)\{(\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w)r_{u,v}\}
\big|.
\$
By the H\"older's inequality,
\[
\left|P\{(\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w)r_{\hat t,\hat\tau_0^*}\}\right|
\leq
\|\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w\|_{L_\infty(\mathbb{P}_X)}
\|\hat r\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})}.
\]
Moreover,
\$
&\sup_{u,v\in\mathbb{R}}
\big|
(P_n-P)\{(\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w)r_{u,v}\}
\big| \\ 
&\leq
\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}
\big|
(P_n-P)
\left[
(\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w)\hat g\hat\gamma
\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s\leq u\}
\right]
\big|+
\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}}
\big|
(P_n-P)
\left[
(\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w)\hat g\gamma
\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s\leq v\}
\right]
\big|.
\$
Conditional on ${\mathcal{T}}_n$, both terms are indexed by nested threshold
classes. Since $\hat g,\hat\gamma,\gamma\in[0,1]$ and
$\|\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w\|_{L_2(\mathbb{P}_X)}=O_P(1)$, applying
Lemma~\ref{lem:wcdf_conv} twice gives
$\sup_{u,v\in\mathbb{R}}
\left|
(P_n-P)\{(\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w)r_{u,v}\}
\right|
=
O_P(n^{-1/2})$. 
Thus,
\[
|(a)|
\leq
\|\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w\|_{L_\infty(\mathbb{P}_X)}
\|\hat r\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})}
+
O_P(n^{-1/2}).
\]
Combining the above two bounds gives 
\@\label{eq:R_bd_5}
R_n \leq \alpha + \|\hat{\omega}^\dagger-w\|_{L_\infty(\mathbb{P}_X)}
\|\hat r\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})}
+
O_P(n^{-1/2}).
\@


We now proceed to show $\|\hat{r}\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})}=O_P(n^{-1/4})$. By definition, as $\hat{g}\in [0,1]$ and $\gamma\in [0,1]$, 
\$
\|\hat{r}\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})} &\leq \big\| \hat\gamma(\cdot)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(\cdot)\leq \hat{t}\} - \gamma(\cdot) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(\cdot)\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}\big\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})} \\ 
&\leq \big\| \hat\gamma(\cdot)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(\cdot)\leq \hat{t}\} - \gamma(\cdot) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(\cdot)\leq \hat t\}\big\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})} + \big\| \gamma(\cdot)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(\cdot)\leq \hat{t}\} - \gamma(\cdot) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(\cdot)\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}\big\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})} \\ 
&\leq \|\hat\gamma-\gamma\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})} +\big\|  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(\cdot)\leq \hat{t}\} -  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat{s}(\cdot)\leq \hat\tau_0^*\}\big\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_{X,T})} 
\leq O_P(n^{-1/4}) + O(|\hat\tau_0^*-\hat{t}|),
\$
where the last inequality uses the bounded-density condition on $\hat{s}(\cdot)$. 

In the following, we show that $|\hat\tau_0^*-\hat{t}|=O_P(n^{-1/4})$ under the given conditions. 
Recall that 
$H_n(t)
:=
\mathbb{E}[
\hat\gamma(X)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\}
\mid{\mathcal{T}}_n
]$ and  
$t_n^\circ
:=
\sup\{t\in\mathbb{R}:H_n(t)\leq\alpha\}$. 
By Lemma~\ref{lem:wecdf_hat},
\[
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\hat\gamma(X_i)\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X_i)\leq t\}
-
H_n(t)
\right|
=
O_P(n^{-1/2}).
\]
Together with condition~(ii) of
Theorem~\ref{thm:dr_obs_rate_general}, the cutoff conclusion of
Lemma~\ref{lem:wecdf_hat} gives 
$|\hat t-t_n^\circ|=O_P(n^{-1/2})$. 
 
Since Assumption~\ref{assump:obs_bal} holds with
$r_n=O(n^{-1/2})$, Lemmas~\ref{lem:w_to_wx}
and~\ref{lem:wecdf_hat}, together with
$\mathbb{P}(Y_i^{**}=0\mid X_i,T_i)=\gamma(X_i)$, give
\[
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
|\hat F_{n,0}^*(t)-F_n^\circ(t)|
=
O_P(n^{-1/2}),\quad \text{where}\quad F_n^\circ(t)
:=
\frac{
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\hat\omega^\dagger(X)\hat g(X,T)\gamma(X)
\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\}
\,\middle|\,{\mathcal{T}}_n
\right]
}{
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\hat\omega^\dagger(X)\hat g(X,T)
\,\middle|\,{\mathcal{T}}_n
\right]
}.
\]
Write 
$D_n:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\hat\omega^\dagger(X)\hat g(X,T)
{\,|\,}{\mathcal{T}}_n
\right]$. 
For every integrable function $h$, we have
$\mathbb{E}\!\left[
w(X)\hat g(X,T)h(X)
{\,|\,} {\mathcal{T}}_n
\right]
=
\mathbb{E}[h(X){\,|\,}{\mathcal{T}}_n]$. 
It follows that 
$|D_n-1|
\leq
\|\hat\omega^\dagger-w\|_{L_\infty(\mathbb{P}_X)}
=
O_P(n^{-1/4})$, 
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
\bigg|
&\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\hat\omega^\dagger(X)\hat g(X,T)\gamma(X)
\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\hat s(X)\leq t\}
\,\middle|\,{\mathcal{T}}_n
\right]
-
H_n(t)
\bigg| \leq
\|\hat\omega^\dagger-w\|_{L_\infty(\mathbb{P}_X)}
+
\|\hat\gamma-\gamma\|_{L_1(\mathbb{P}_X)}
=
O_P(n^{-1/4}).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $D_n=1+o_P(1)$ and $0\leq H_n(t)\leq1$, on the event
$D_n\geq1/2$, it holds that  
$\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|F_n^\circ(t)-H_n(t)|
=
O_P(n^{-1/4})$. 
Therefore, by the triangle inequality,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_n
&:=
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
|\hat F_{n,0}^*(t)-H_n(t)|  \leq
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
|\hat F_{n,0}^*(t)-F_n^\circ(t)|
+
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
|F_n^\circ(t)-H_n(t)| =
O_P(n^{-1/4}).
\end{aligned}
\]

Let $\mathcal E_n$ denote the event on which 
$H_n(t_n^\circ-u)\leq\alpha-cu$ and 
$H_n(t_n^\circ+u)\geq\alpha+cu$ 
for every $0<u\leq\eta$. By condition~(ii) of
Theorem~\ref{thm:dr_obs_rate_general}, we know 
$\mathbb{P}(\mathcal E_n)\to1$.
  
Set 
$\bar\varepsilon_n:=\varepsilon_n+n^{-1}$ and 
$u_n:=\frac{2\bar\varepsilon_n}{c}$. 
Since $\varepsilon_n=O_P(n^{-1/4})$, we have
$u_n=O_P(n^{-1/4})$ and 
$\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal E_n\cap\{u_n\leq\eta\}\}\to1$. 
On this event,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\hat F_{n,0}^*(t_n^\circ-u_n)
&\leq
H_n(t_n^\circ-u_n)+\varepsilon_n \leq
\alpha-cu_n+\varepsilon_n
<
\alpha, \\
\hat F_{n,0}^*(t_n^\circ+u_n)
&\geq
H_n(t_n^\circ+u_n)-\varepsilon_n \geq
\alpha+cu_n-\varepsilon_n
>
\alpha.
\end{aligned}
\]
Because $\hat F_{n,0}^*$ is non-decreasing, it follows that
$t_n^\circ-u_n
\leq
\hat\tau_0^*
\leq
t_n^\circ+u_n$. 
Therefore, we have  
$|\hat\tau_0^*-t_n^\circ|
=
O_P(n^{-1/4})$, 
and hence
\[
|\hat\tau_0^*-\hat t|
\leq
|\hat\tau_0^*-t_n^\circ|
+
|\hat t-t_n^\circ|
=
O_P(n^{-1/4}).
\]
Putting it back to~\eqref{eq:R_bd_5}, we conclude the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dr_obs_rate_general}.

Let $\mathcal{N}:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:|t-t^\circ|\leq\eta\}$ and write

Following~\cite{kallus2022s} and~\cite{li2023trustworthy},  for any decision rule $\phi\colon \mathcal{X}\to \{0,1\}$, 
    \$
\max_{P\in \mathcal{P}} ~\text{Err}(\phi;P)  = \mathbb{E}[\phi(X)\gamma(X)],
    \$
    where $\gamma(x)=\min\{\mathbb{P}(Y(1)=0{\,|\,} X=x), \mathbb{P}(Y(0)=1{\,|\,} X=x)\}$ solely relies on the observed distribution, and $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ is with respect to the observed distribution. On the other hand, the power is the expectation of $\phi(X)$ under the observed covariate distribution. The optimization problem (with randomized policy) is 
    \$
    \mathop{\mathrm{maximize}}_{\phi\colon \mathcal{X}\to [0,1]} ~~&\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]\\ 
    \text{subject to} ~~& \mathbb{E}[\phi(X)\gamma(X)] \leq \alpha.
    \$
    If $\mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)]\leq \alpha$, then $\phi_{\text{power}}^*=1$ maximizes the power since its power is one. 
    
    Otherwise, suppose $\mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)]>\alpha$. 
    Consider any decision rule $\psi(\cdot)$ obeying $\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)\gamma(X)]\leq \alpha$, and write $\phi^*(\cdot)=\phi_{\text{power}}^*(\cdot)$ for notational convenience. Note that 
    \$
     &\gamma^*\cdot \big(\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\phi^*(X)]\big)\\  
    &= \mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma^*\cdot (\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)<\gamma^*\} \big]\\ 
    &\qquad \quad + \mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma^*\cdot (\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)>\gamma^*\} \big] + \mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma^*\cdot (\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)=\gamma^*\} \big] .
    \$
    The first term, as $\phi^*(X)=1$ when $\gamma(X)<\gamma^*$ and hence $\psi(X)-\phi^*(X)\leq 0$, obeys 
    \$
    \mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma^*\cdot (\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)<\gamma^*\} \big] \leq \mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma(X)(\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)<\gamma^*\} \big].
    \$
    Similar arguments yield 
    \$
    &\mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma^*\cdot (\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)>\gamma^*\} \big] \leq \mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma(X)(\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)>\gamma^*\} \big], \\ 
    &\mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma^*\cdot (\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)=\gamma^*\} \big] = \mathbb{E}\big[ \gamma(X)(\psi(X)-\phi^*(X))\operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\gamma(X)=\gamma^*\} \big].
    \$
    Adding the three terms up, we know 
    \$
\gamma^*\cdot \big(\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\phi^*(X)]\big) 
\leq \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\psi(X)] - \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\phi^*(X)] \leq \alpha-\alpha=0
    \$
    since $\psi(X)$ is a feasible solution and the satefy constraint is binding for $\phi^*$. Finally, as $\gamma^*\geq 0$, this implies $\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)]\leq \mathbb{E}[\phi^*(X)]$. The arbitrariness of $\psi$ implies the optimality of $\phi^*$.

We first characterize the worst-case safety constraint. For any $P'\in\mathcal P$, define \[ f_{P'}(x) := P'\{Y(1)=0,Y(0)=1\mid X=x\}. \] For a randomized policy $\phi$, whose randomization is independent of the potential outcomes conditional on $X$, its harm rate under $P'$ is $\operatorname{Err}(\phi;P') =\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)f_{P'}(X)]$. Since $f_{P'}(x)\leq P'\{Y(1)=0\mid X=x\}=1-\mu_1(x)$ and $f_{P'}(x)\leq P'\{Y(0)=1\mid X=x\}=\mu_0(x)$, we have $f_{P'}(x)\leq\gamma(x)$ for every $P'\in\mathcal P$. This upper bound is sharp. Indeed, for every $x$, consider the following conditional distribution of the potential outcomes: \[ \begin{aligned} P'\{Y(1)=0,Y(0)=1\mid X=x\} &=\gamma(x),\\ P'\{Y(1)=0,Y(0)=0\mid X=x\} &=1-\mu_1(x)-\gamma(x),\\ P'\{Y(1)=1,Y(0)=1\mid X=x\} &=\mu_0(x)-\gamma(x),\\ P'\{Y(1)=1,Y(0)=0\mid X=x\} &=\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x)+\gamma(x). \end{aligned} \] All four probabilities are nonnegative. 
The first three nonnegativity claims follow directly from $\gamma(x)=\min\{1-\mu_1(x),\mu_0(x)\}$, while the last follows from $\gamma(x)\geq\mu_0(x)-\mu_1(x)$. They sum to one and yield the conditional marginals $P'\{Y(1)=1\mid X=x\}=\mu_1(x)$ and $P'\{Y(0)=1\mid X=x\}=\mu_0(x)$. 
Combining this conditional coupling with the original distribution of $X$ and the same treatment-assignment mechanism therefore defines a distribution in $\mathcal P$. Consequently, for every randomized policy $\phi$, \[ \max_{P'\in\mathcal P}\operatorname{Err}(\phi;P') = \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\phi(X)]. \] Write $\tau(x):=\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x)$. 
The welfare of a randomized policy is 
\$ 
\operatorname{Welfare}(\phi;P) &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \mu_1(X)\phi(X) + \mu_0(X)\{1-\phi(X)\} \right]\\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_0(X)] + \mathbb{E}[\tau(X)\phi(X)]. 
\$
Thus, up to the constant $\mathbb{E}[\mu_0(X)]$, the randomized extension of~\eqref{eq:def_opt_welfare_phi} is equivalent to 
\[ 
\underset{\phi:\mathcal X\to[0,1]}{\operatorname{maximize}} \quad \mathbb{E}[\tau(X)\phi(X)] \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\phi(X)]\leq\alpha. 
\] 
We next verify that the cutoff and randomization probability in the theorem are well defined. Recall that  $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x) = -\frac{\tau(x)}{\gamma(x)}$  under the stated ratio conventions, and define, for $r\leq0$, 
\[ 
H(r) := \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)<r\} \right]. 
\] 
The function $H$ is nondecreasing and left-continuous on $(-\infty,0]$. Indeed, if $r_k\uparrow r$, then $ \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)<r_k\}$ increases pointwise to $ \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)<r\}$, so the conclusion follows from the dominated convergence theorem. Moreover, $H(r)\to0$ as $r\to-\infty$. To see this, if $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)$ is finite, then $ \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)<r\}\to0$. If $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)=-\infty$, then necessarily $\gamma(X)=0$, so $\gamma(X) \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)<r\}=0$ for every $r$. The dominated convergence theorem therefore gives the claim, and hence the set defining $r^*$ is nonempty. Suppose first that $H(0)>\alpha$. Since $H(r)\uparrow H(0)$ as $r\uparrow0$, there exists some $r_0<0$ such that $H(r_0)>\alpha$. It follows that $r^*<0$. By the definition of $r^*$, there exists a sequence $r_k\uparrow r^*$ such that $H(r_k)\leq\alpha$. The left-continuity of $H$ therefore implies $H(r^*)\leq\alpha$. 
On the other hand, the right limit of $H$ at $r^*$ is \[ \lim_{r\downarrow r^*}H(r) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)\leq r^*\} \right] = H(r^*) + \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)=r^*\} \right]. \] This limit must be at least $\alpha$. 
Otherwise, there would exist some $r>r^*$ sufficiently close to $r^*$ such that $H(r)<\alpha$, contradicting the definition of $r^*$ as the supremum. Therefore, \[ H(r^*) \leq \alpha \leq H(r^*) + \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X)  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)=r^*\} \right]. \] This establishes the existence of $\eta^*\in[0,1]$ satisfying the equality in the theorem. If the expectation multiplying $\eta^*$ is zero, the preceding inequalities imply $H(r^*)=\alpha$, and we may take $\eta^*=0$. If instead $H(0)\leq\alpha$, the theorem sets $r^*=0$ and $\eta^*=0$. Under the stated ratio conventions, $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x)<0$ if and only if $\tau(x)>0$. Hence, in this case, \[ \phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x) =  \operatorname{\mathds{1}}\{\tau(x)>0\}, \qquad \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X)\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X) \right] = H(0) \leq\alpha. \] It follows in both cases that $\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}$ is feasible and satisfies  $(-r^*) \left\{ \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X)\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X) \right] -\alpha \right\} =0$ by complementary slackness. 

It remains to establish optimality. Let $\psi:\mathcal X\to[0,1]$ be any feasible randomized policy. By the definition of $\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}$, it holds pointwise that \[ \{\tau(x)+r^*\gamma(x)\} \{\psi(x)-\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x)\} \leq0. \] Indeed, if $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x)<r^*$, then $\tau(x)+r^*\gamma(x)\geq0$ and $\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x)=1$, so the second factor is nonpositive. If $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x)>r^*$, then $\tau(x)+r^*\gamma(x)\leq0$ and $\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x)=0$, so the second factor is nonnegative. If $s_{\mathrm{welfare}}(x)=r^*$, the first factor is zero. The same conclusions continue to hold when $\gamma(x)=0$ under the stated ratio conventions. Consequently, 
\$ 
& \operatorname{Welfare}(\psi;P) - \operatorname{Welfare}(\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}};P)\\ &\quad= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \tau(X) \{\psi(X)-\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)\} \right]\\ &\quad= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \{\tau(X)+r^*\gamma(X)\} \{\psi(X)-\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)\} \right]  -r^* \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \gamma(X) \{\psi(X)-\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)\} \right]\\ &\quad\leq -r^* \left\{ \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\psi(X)] - \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)] \right\}\\ &\quad\leq -r^* \left\{ \alpha - \mathbb{E}[\gamma(X)\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}(X)] \right\} =0. 
\$ 
The first inequality follows from the preceding pointwise inequality. The second follows from the feasibility of $\psi$ and the fact that $-r^*\geq0$. The final equality follows from complementary slackness. Since $\psi$ was arbitrary, this proves the optimality of $\phi^*_{\mathrm{welfare}}$.

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\hat H_n(t)-\widetilde H_n(t)|
\leq A_n+C_n=o_P(1).
\]
Moreover,
\[
\Delta_n\leq C_n+D_n=o_P(1),\qquad
\hat\Delta_n\leq A_n+C_n+D_n=o_P(1).
\]

Finally, fix $\varepsilon>0$ and define
\[
\gamma_\varepsilon:=\frac12\min\left\{\alpha-H\left(t^*-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right),
H\left(t^*+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)-\alpha\right\}>0.
\]
On the event $\{\hat\Delta_n<\gamma_\varepsilon\}$,
\[
\hat H_n\left(t^*-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)
\leq H\left(t^*-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)+\hat\Delta_n<\alpha
\]
and
\[
\hat H_n\left(t^*+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)
\geq H\left(t^*+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)-\hat\Delta_n>\alpha.
\]
Since $\hat f_n\geq0$, the function $\hat H_n$ is nondecreasing. Hence
\[
t^*-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\leq\hat t\leq t^*+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},
\]
so
\[
\mathbb{P}(|\hat t-t^*|\geq\varepsilon)
\leq \mathbb{P}(\hat\Delta_n\geq\gamma_\varepsilon)\to0.
\]
Therefore, $\hat t-t^*=o_P(1)$.
