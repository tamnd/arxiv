\label{rem:An-alternative-naive}An alternative naive way to obtain
the expression of the total force, $\boldsymbol{f}'$, is a direct application
of the Lorentz force to each of the particles above. Thus,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}\boldsymbol{f}' & =q\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r}+d\boldsymbol{r})+q(-\boldsymbol{v})\times\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r}),\\
 & =q\boldsymbol{v}\times(\nabla\boldsymbol{B}(d\boldsymbol{r})),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
or in index notation
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}f'_{i} & =q\varepsilon_{ilm}v_{l}B_{m,k}dr_{k}.\end{split}
\label{eq:f-directly_from_B}
\end{equation}
As ((\ref{eq:force_from_U})) is widely accepted, we propose that
the inconsistency follows from the interaction of the two particles,
issues customarily associated with ``hidden momentum'' (see \cite[p. 189]{Jackson},
\cite[p. 521]{Zangwill}).

<!-- assumption-1: body_md -->
There is a $C^{1}$ bounded vector field $\boldsymbol{H}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, the
current density potential, such that the total potential energy of
the current density in $\varOmega$ is given by ~
\begin{equation}
U=-m(\boldsymbol{A})=\int_{\varOmega}\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{H}\times\boldsymbol{A})\,\mathop{dV}=\int_{\varOmega}\varepsilon_{ijk}(H_{j}A_{k})_{,i}\,\mathop{dV.}\label{eq:U_reg-1}
\end{equation}

<!-- rem-2: body_md -->
\emph{Magnetostatics as polar elasticity}\\
In classical continuum mechanics in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, one has for the
power, 
\begin{equation}
P_{\varOmega}=F(\boldsymbol{w})=\int_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\,\mathop{dV}+\int_{\partial\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathsf{T}}(\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\mathop{dA},\quad\text{as}\ \boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{\nu}).
\end{equation}
Here, $\boldsymbol{b}$ and $\boldsymbol{t}$ are the body and surface forces, respectively.
Usually, $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is symmetric. However, let us consider the case
where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is anti-symmetric. Define 
\begin{equation}
H_{p}={\scriptstyle \frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon_{pjk}\sigma_{jk},\qquad\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathsf{T}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{H}\times\boldsymbol{w}.
\end{equation}
Thus, assuming that $\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{0}$, one has 
\begin{equation}
P_{\varOmega}(\boldsymbol{w})=\int_{\partial\varOmega}(\boldsymbol{H}\times\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\mathop{dA}.
\end{equation}
Using Gauss's theorem and $\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{H}\times\boldsymbol{w})=(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{H})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{H}\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{w})$,
\begin{equation}
P_{\varOmega}(\boldsymbol{w})=\int_{\varOmega}(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{H})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\,\mathop{dV}-\int_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{H}\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{w})\,\mathop{dV}.
\end{equation}
Evidently, the last equation is analogous to (\ref{eq:Ucontinuous})
above, where $\boldsymbol{w}$ replaces the vector potential.
