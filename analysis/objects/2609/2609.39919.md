<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:CET}
Under the assumptions above,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:CET}
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl[\int_0^\infty \alpha_s\sum_{k=0}^n (u^k_s)^2\,ds\Bigr]
\le
e\cdot
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl[\sum_{k=0}^n (u^k_\infty)^2\Bigr].
\tag{CET}
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
The term \(2(n-1)(a^0)^2\) in~\eqref{eq:Q-pos} is the only place where the dimension enters the algebraic identity. It is nonnegative precisely when \(n\ge 1\), which is the range relevant for Riesz transforms on \(\mathbb{R}^n\).
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:riesz-CE}
Under the assumptions above,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:riesz-CE}
\begin{aligned}
&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}
\Delta\varphi(x,y)\Bigl((P_yf)^2+\sum_{k=1}^n(P_y R_kf)^2\Bigr)\,2y\,dy\,dx
\\
&\qquad\le
e\cdot
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl[M_f(\tau)^2+\sum_{k=1}^n M_f^{(k)}(\tau)^2\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:occupation}
Let \(\varphi\) be smooth on \(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+\) with \(\Delta\varphi\ge 0\). Then, for each \(k=1,\dots,n\),
\begin{align*}
&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}
\Delta\varphi(x,y)\Bigl((P_yf)^2+(P_y R_kf)^2\Bigr)\,2y\,dy\,dx
\\
&\qquad\le
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau
\Delta\varphi(B_s)\Bigl(M_f(s)^2+M_f^{(k)}(s)^2\Bigr)\,ds,
\end{align*}
whenever the left-hand side is finite. For \(n=1\) the two sides are equal. For \(n\ge 2\) the inequality is generally strict.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:pairing}
Let \(g\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+})\). Then
\[
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau M_f^{(k)}(s)\,g(B_s)\,ds
=
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau v(B_s)\,g(B_s)\,ds.
\]
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We first work on the left-hand side.
Let \(U\) be the Dirichlet Green potential of \(g\) for the generator \(\tfrac12\Delta\), defined by
\[
U(z)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}^z\int_0^\tau g(B_s)\,ds,
\]
with \(B\) Brownian motion of generator \(\tfrac12\Delta\), started at \(z\), killed when it hits \(\{y=0\}\). Equivalently,
\[
\tfrac12\Delta U=-g\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+,
\qquad U=0\text{ on }\{y=0\}.
\]
Write \(d=n+1\) and let \(w^*\) be the reflection of \(w\) across \(\{y=0\}\).
The Dirichlet Green function of the half-space for \(\tfrac12\Delta\) is
\(G(z,w)=2\bigl(\Gamma(z-w)-\Gamma(z-w^*)\bigr)\), where \(\Gamma\) is the
free-space fundamental solution of \(\Delta\Gamma=-\delta\), that is, \(\Gamma(z)=c_d\lvertz\rvert^{2-d}\) for \(d\ge 3\), and
\(\Gamma(z)=(1/2\pi)\log(1/\lvertz\rvert)\) for \(d=2\).
Thus
\[
U(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}G(z,w)\,g(w)\,dw.
\]
The support of \(g\) is compact in \(\{y>0\}\), so it is enough to expand
\(G(z,w)\) for \(w\) in a fixed compact set and \(\lvertz\rvert\to\infty\).

If \(d\ge 3\), Taylor expansion of \(\Gamma\) about \(z\) gives
\[
\Gamma(z-w)-\Gamma(z-w^*)
=\nabla\Gamma(z)\cdot(w^*-w)+O\bigl(\lvertz\rvert^{-d}\bigr).
\]
Here \(w^*-w=-2\eta\,e_0\) if \(w=(\xi,\eta)\), and
\(\nabla\Gamma(z)=c\,z/\lvertz\rvert^d\), so the leading term is a multiple of
\(\eta y/\lvertz\rvert^{n+1}\). Hence \(G(z,w)=O\bigl(y/\lvertz\rvert^{n+1}\bigr)\), uniformly
for \(w\in\operatorname{supp}g\).

If \(d=2\), that is \(n=1\),
\[
G(z,w)=\frac1\pi\log\frac{\lvertz-w^*\rvert}{\lvertz-w\rvert}.
\]
The expansion
\(\log\lvertz-\zeta\rvert=\log\lvertz\rvert-z\cdot\zeta/\lvertz\rvert^2+O(\lvertz\rvert^{-2})\)
makes the two copies of \(\log\lvertz\rvert\) cancel, and one is left with
\[
\log\frac{\lvertz-w^*\rvert}{\lvertz-w\rvert}
=\frac{2\eta y}{\lvertz\rvert^2}+O\bigl(\lvertz\rvert^{-2}\bigr).
\]
Again \(G(z,w)=O\bigl(y/\lvertz\rvert^{2}\bigr)=O\bigl(y/\lvertz\rvert^{n+1}\bigr)\), with no residual logarithm.
Differentiating the same expansions in \(z\) yields, for \(w\in\operatorname{supp}g\),
\[
\lvert\nabla_z G(z,w)\rvert=O\bigl(\lvertz\rvert^{-(n+1)}\bigr).
\]
Integrating against \(g\) we obtain
\[
U(z)=O\bigl(y/\lvertz\rvert^{n+1}\bigr),
\qquad
\lvert\nabla U(z)\rvert=O\bigl(\lvertz\rvert^{-(n+1)}\bigr)
\qquad(\lvertz\rvert\to\infty).
\]

We apply It\^o formula along a path to the process \(t\mapsto U(B_t)\) up to \(\tau\). Because the generator is \(\tfrac12\Delta\),
\[
dU(B_t)=\nabla U(B_t)\cdot dB_t+\tfrac12\Delta U(B_t)\,dt.
\]
Substitute \(\tfrac12\Delta U=-g\):
\[
dU(B_t)=\nabla U(B_t)\cdot dB_t-g(B_t)\,dt.
\]
Integrate from a time \(r\le\tau\) to \(\tau\):
\[
U(B_\tau)-U(B_r)
=\int_r^\tau\nabla U(B_s)\cdot dB_s-\int_r^\tau g(B_s)\,ds.
\]
On the boundary one has \(U=0\), and \(B_\tau\) is on the boundary, so \(U(B_\tau)=0\). Rearranging gives
\[
\int_r^\tau g(B_s)\,ds
=U(B_r)+\int_r^\tau\nabla U(B_s)\cdot dB_s.
\]

We now pair martingales. Recalling \(M_f^{(k)}(s)=\int_0^s V(B_r)\cdot dB_r\), with
\(V=A_k\nabla u\), stochastic Fubini \cite{Protter2003} and the identity above give
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau M_f^{(k)}(s)\,g(B_s)\,ds
&= \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau\Bigl(\int_0^s V(B_r)\cdot dB_r\Bigr)g(B_s)\,ds \\
&= \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau V(B_r)\cdot dB_r\cdot\Bigl(\int_r^\tau g(B_s)\,ds\Bigr)\\
&=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau V(B_r)\cdot dB_r\cdot
\Bigl(U(B_r)+\int_r^\tau\nabla U(B_s)\cdot dB_s\Bigr)\\
&=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau V(B_r)\cdot\nabla U(B_r)\,dr\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+} 2y\,V(x,y)\cdot\nabla U(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
\end{align*}

We now use the divergence theorem on a compact half-ball to write this last integral. Write \(z=(x,y)\) and
\[
\Omega_R=\bigl\{(x,y):y>0,\ \lvertz\rvert<R\bigr\}.
\]
Write \(dw=dx\, dy\), then
\[
\int_{\Omega_R}2y\,V\cdot\nabla U\,dw
=-\int_{\Omega_R}U\,\operatorname{div}(2y\,V)\,dw
+\int_{\partial\Omega_R}U\,(2y\,V\cdot\nu)\,dS,
\]
where \(\nu\) is the outward unit normal to \(\Omega_R\). The boundary has two pieces.
On the bottom disk \(D_R=\{y=0,\ \lvertx\rvert<R\}\),
the outward normal is \(\nu=-e_y\), so
\[
2y\,V\cdot\nu=2y\,V\cdot(-e_y).
\]
The prefactor \(y\) is identically zero on \(D_R\), hence this flux is zero.
On the hemisphere \(S_R=\{\lvertz\rvert=R,\ y>0\}\),
the outward normal is \(\nu=z/\lvertz\rvert\), so the flux is
\[
\int_{S_R}U(z)\,2y\,V(z)\cdot\frac{z}{\lvertz\rvert}\,dS(z).
\]
That is the only remaining boundary term:
\[
\int_{\Omega_R}2y\,V\cdot\nabla U\,dw
=-2\int_{\Omega_R}U\,\operatorname{div}(yV)\,dw
+\int_{S_R}2y\,U\bigl(V\cdot\nu\bigr)\,dS.
\]
Let \(R\to\infty\). On \(S_R\) the bounds recorded above give
\(\lvertU\rvert=O(y/R^{n+1})\). Since \(f\) is Schwartz, the Poisson extension \(u\)
satisfies \(\lvert\nabla u(z)\rvert=O(\lvertz\rvert^{-(n+1)})\), hence \(\lvertV\rvert=O(R^{-(n+1)})\).
Taking into account that \(y\le R\) and the fact that the $n$-dimensional volume of $S_{R}$ is  \(O(R^n)\), we see that the integrand of the
hemisphere flux is \(O(R^{-n})\), so the integral tends to \(0\).

Therefore
\[
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}2y\,V\cdot\nabla U\,dw
=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}U\,\operatorname{div}(yV)\,dw
=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}U\,\partial_{x_k}u\,dw,
\]
since \(\operatorname{div} V=0\) and \(V_y=\partial_{x_k}u\), so \(\operatorname{div}(yV)=\partial_{x_k}u\). In summary, for the left-hand side we obtain
\[
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau M_f^{(k)}(s)\,g(B_s)\,ds=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}U\,\partial_{x_k}u\,dw.
\]

We turn to the right-hand side. The occupation formula~\ref{eq:occupation} and \(\tfrac12\Delta U=-g\) give
\[
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau v(B_s)\,g(B_s)\,ds
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+} 2y\,v\bigl(-\tfrac12\Delta U\bigr)\,dw
=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+} yv\,\Delta U\,dw.
\]
Green's identity for the pair \((U,yv)\) on the same truncated region \(\Omega_R\) reads
\[
\int_{\Omega_R}\bigl(U\,\Delta(yv)-yv\,\Delta U\bigr)\,dw
=\int_{\partial\Omega_R}\bigl(U\,\partial_\nu(yv)-yv\,\partial_\nu U\bigr)\,dS.
\]
On the bottom disk \(D_R=\{y=0,\,\lvertx\rvert<R\}\) one has \(U=0\) and \(yv=0\) as traces, so the integrand vanishes.
On the hemisphere \(S_R\), the same bounds give
\(\lvertU\rvert=O(R^{-n})\) and \(\lvert\nabla U\rvert=O(R^{-(n+1)})\).
Since \(f\) is Schwartz, \(R_kf(x)=O(\lvertx\rvert^{-n})\) at infinity, and the
Poisson extension therefore satisfies
\(\lvertv(z)\rvert+ \lvertz\rvert\cdot\lvert\nabla v(z)\rvert=O(\lvertz\rvert^{-n})\).
Thus \(\lvertyv\rvert=O(R^{1-n})\) and \(\lvert\nabla(yv)\rvert=O(R^{-n})\) on \(S_R\).
The integrand \(U\,\partial_\nu(yv)-yv\,\partial_\nu U\) is \(O(R^{-2n})\),
the $n$-dimensional area is \(O(R^n)\), and the flux tends to \(0\) as \(R\to\infty\).

Therefore
\[
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}yv\,\Delta U\,dw
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}U\,\Delta(yv)\,dw.
\]
The product rule and harmonicity of \(v\) give \(\Delta(yv)=2\partial_yv\). Combined with occupation and \(\tfrac12\Delta U=-g\), we summarize for the right-hand side
\[
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\int_0^\tau v(B_s)\,g(B_s)\,ds
=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_+}U\,\partial_yv\,dw.
\]

To compare the left- and right-hand sides, note the identity \(\partial_yv=\partial_{x_k}u\), which is immediately seen to be true by checking it on the Fourier side. Indeed: with the multiplier \(\widehat{R_kf}(\xi)=-i\xi_k/\lvert\xi\rvert\,\hat f(\xi)\) one has
\[
\hat u(\xi,y)=e^{-y\lvert\xi\rvert}\hat f(\xi),
\qquad
\hat v(\xi,y)=e^{-y\lvert\xi\rvert}\Bigl(-i\frac{\xi_k}{\lvert\xi\rvert}\Bigr)\hat f(\xi),
\]
hence
\[
\widehat{\partial_{x_k}u}(\xi,y)=i\xi_k\,e^{-y\lvert\xi\rvert}\hat f(\xi)
=\widehat{\partial_yv}(\xi,y).
\]
Equivalently, if \(Q_y^{(k)}\) denotes the conjugate Poisson kernel, then \(\partial_{x_k}P_y=\partial_y Q_y^{(k)}\) pointwise, and \(v=Q_y^{(k)}*f\) is the choice of conjugate that matches the matrix \(A_k\).
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
That identity is the only Cauchy--Riemann relation used. The remaining slots of \(\nabla v\) do not match those of \(A_k\nabla u\) when \(n\ge 2\), and are never invoked.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
If \(\Delta\varphi\) is itself compactly supported in \(\{y>0\}\), the cutoff is unnecessary. The hypothesis that the left-hand side is finite may be replaced by the assumption that both sides are well-defined in \([0,+\infty]\); if the right-hand side is infinite there is nothing to prove.
