    Let $G$ be a graph with average degree greater than $k-2$.  Then $G$ contains a copy of every tree $T$ on $k$ vertices.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{th:extremal}
    Let $T$ be a tree with $k$ vertices, and let $G$ be a graph with average degree $k-2$.  If $G$ does not contain a copy of $T$ then:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $T=K_{1,k-1}$, then $G$ is $(k-2)$-regular.
    \item If $T \ne K_{1,k-1}$, then $G$ is a vertex-disjoint union of copies of $K_{k-1}$. 
\end{itemize}
In both cases, every graph of the given form is extremal.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{th1}
    Let $G$ be a graph with average degree $d$, and let $T$ be a rooted tree.  Then the expected number of $T$-jumping edges in a uniformly random ordering of the vertices of $G$ is at least $d-|T|+1$.
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We proceed by induction on $|T|$.  For $|T|=1$ the statement is immediate: every edge incident with $v_1$ is $T$-jumping, and the expected number of such edges is $d$.

    Now suppose that $T$ is a tree with root $r$, and we have proved the result for smaller values of $|T|$.  There are two cases, depending on the degree of $r$.

    \medskip

    \noindent{\em Case 1: $r$ has degree 1.}  In this case let $s$ be the neighbour of $r$ in $T$, and let $A$ be the tree obtained from $T$ by deleting $r$ and declaring $s$ to be the new root.
    By induction, the expected number of $A$-jumping edges is at least $d-|A|+1=d-|T|+2$.  

    Call an edge of $G$ {\em $A$-spanning} if it joins $v_1$ to some vertex $v_i$, and there is a copy of $A$ rooted at $v_i$ with all its vertices contained in $\{v_2,\dots,v_i\}$. A given edge $xy$ is $A$-jumping in an ordering $\pi$ if and only if it is $A$-spanning in the ordering $\pi'$ obtained by swapping the positions of $x$ and $y$. It follows that the probabilities (in a random ordering) that $e$ is $A$-spanning and that $e$ is $A$-jumping are equal. Summing over $e$, the expected number of $A$-spanning edges is thus at least  $d-|T|+2$.

For a fixed ordering, if $v_i<v_j$ and the edges $v_1v_i$ and $v_1v_j$ are both $A$-spanning then the edge $v_1v_j$ is $T$-jumping, since $v_1v_i$ and the copy of $A$ guaranteed by $v_1v_i$ being $A$-spanning give a copy of $T$ rooted at $v_1$ (see Fig \ref{f1}).  Thus, in every ordering, all but at most one $A$-spanning edge is $T$-jumping.  It follows that the expected number of $T$-jumping edges in a random ordering is at least $d-|T|+1$, as required.

    \noindent{\em Case 2: $r$ has degree at least 2.}  We divide $T$ into two trees $A$ and $B$, each with at least two vertices, overlapping only at the root $r$.
Given an ordering $\pi$ of the vertices of $G$, call an edge of $G$ {\em $B^*$-jumping} if it is $B$-jumping in the ordering obtained from $\pi$ by reversing the order of $v_2,\dots,v_n$ (we can think of this as looking to the left of $v_1$ in the cyclic ordering).  Let $L_\pi$ be the set of $A$-jumping edges in $\pi$,  let $R_\pi$ be the set of $B^*$-jumping edges, and let $I_\pi=L_\pi\cap R_\pi$ be the set of edges that are jumping in both directions.  Since $L_\pi$ and $R_\pi$ are subsets of a set of size $d_G(v_1)$, we have
$|I_\pi|\geqslant |L_\pi|+|R_\pi|-d_G(v_1)$, so for a uniformly random permutation $\pi$ it follows by induction that 
\begin{align*}
\mathbb E|I_\pi|
&\geqslant \mathbb E|L_\pi|+\mathbb E|R_\pi|-\mathbb E d_G(v_1)\\
&\geqslant (d-|A|+1)+(d-|B|+1)-d\\
&=d-|T|+1.
\end{align*}

To convert this into a bound on the number of $T$-jumping edges, we apply an involution to the set of orderings.  Given an ordering $\pi: v_1<\cdots<v_n$, let $i$ be maximal such that $G$ contains a copy of $B$ rooted at $v_1$ and with all other vertices in $\{v_i,\dots,v_n\}$; if no such $i$ exists then set $i=2$.  Let $\pi'$ be the ordering obtained from $\pi$ by concatenating $v_1$ and $v_n,v_{n-1},\dots,v_i$ and $v_2,\dots,v_{i-1}$ (thus $v_i,\dots,v_n$ reverse order and are inserted between $v_1$ and $v_2$).  This is a bijection: for the inverse map, let $i$ be minimal such that $G$ contains a copy of $B$ rooted at $v_1$ and with all other vertices in $\{v_2,\dots,v_i\}$, or take $i=n$ if there is no such $i$; we then reverse the order of $\{v_2,\dots,v_i\}$ and move them to the end.  Thus $\pi'$ is also uniformly random.

Finally, we note that if $e$ belongs to $I_\pi$, then $e$ is a $T$-jumping edge in $\pi'$. Indeed, if $e\in I_\pi$ joins $v_1$ to $w$, then in $\pi$ there are copies $A_0$, $B_0$ of $A$, $B$ rooted at $v_1$ such that the rest of $A_0$ lies to the left of $w$ and the rest of $B_0$ lies to the right. Choosing the `right-most' such copy $B_0$, in $\pi'$ all vertices of $A_0\cup B_0$ lie to the left of $w$ (see Fig \ref{f2}).  Thus the expected number of $T$-jumping edges in a uniformly random ordering of the vertices of $G$ is at least $d-|T|+1$.

We divide $T$ into two trees $A$ and $B$, each with at least two vertices, overlapping only at the root $r$.
Given an ordering $\pi$ of the vertices of $G$, call an edge of $G$ {\em $B^*$-jumping} if it is $B$-jumping in the ordering obtained from $\pi$ by reversing the order of $v_2,\dots,v_n$ (we can think of this as looking to the left of $v_1$ in the cyclic ordering).  Let $L_\pi$ be the set of $A$-jumping edges in $\pi$,  let $R_\pi$ be the set of $B^*$-jumping edges, and let $I_\pi=L_\pi\cap R_\pi$ be the set of edges that are jumping in both directions.  Since $L_\pi$ and $R_\pi$ are subsets of a set of size $d_G(v_1)$, we have
$|I_\pi|\geqslant |L_\pi|+|R_\pi|-d_G(v_1)$, so for a uniformly random permutation $\pi$ it follows by induction that 
\begin{align*}
\mathbb E|I_\pi|
&\geqslant \mathbb E|L_\pi|+\mathbb E|R_\pi|-\mathbb E d_G(v_1)\\
&\geqslant (d-|A|+1)+(d-|B|+1)-d\\
&=d-|T|+1.
\end{align*}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{prefix}
Let $J,K\subseteq U$ satisfy $|J|=|K|$, and let
$\mathcal{F}$ be an increasing family of subsets of $U$. If $\sigma$ is a uniformly random ordering
of $U$, then
\begin{equation}\label{v0}
\mathbb{E}\left[
\sum_{u\in J}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{\leqslant u}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
-
\sum_{w\in K}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{<w}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
\right]
\leqslant 1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- claim-1: body_md -->
\label{Claimuw}
For any distinct $u,w\in U$ we have
\begin{equation}\label{v1}
 \mathbb P\bigl(S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma) \in \mathcal{F}\bigr) \geqslant \mathbb P\bigl(S_{<u}(\sigma) \in \mathcal{F}\bigr).
\end{equation}
<!-- end claim-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $\sigma'$ be the ordering in which $u$ and $w$ are swapped, so the position of $u$ in $\sigma$ is the position of $w$ in $\sigma'$. Let $R=U\setminus\{u,w\}$. Since all elements of $R$ have the same position in $\sigma$ and in $\sigma'$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{Suw}
 S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma)\cap R = S_{<u}(\sigma')\cap R.
\end{equation}
Since $S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma)$ always contains $w$, and $S_{<u}(\sigma')$ never contains $u$, it follows that $S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma) \supset S_{<u}(\sigma')$. If the latter is in $\mathcal{F}$, then so is the former, completing the proof.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We may assume that $\mathcal{F}$ is non-trivial. Note first that, for any $v$,
\begin{equation}\label{v2}
\mathbb{E}\left[
\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{\leqslant v}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
-
\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{< v}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
\right]
= p_v,
\end{equation}
where $p_v$ is the probability that, as we add elements in the order given by $\sigma$, the point when we add $v$ is the point where we enter $\mathcal{F}$. For a given $\sigma$, this occurs for exactly one $v$, so $\sum_vp_v=1$.

Now consider the expectation \eqref{v0}.  For $v\in J\cap K$, both sums contain $v$ and by \eqref{v2} the terms containing $v$ contribute $p_v$ to the expectation.  For the remaining terms, as $|J|=|K|$ we can fix a bijection between elements $u\in J\setminus K$ and $w\in K\setminus J$.  For any pair $u,w$, we have (using \eqref{v2} again)
\begin{align*}
    \mathbb{E}\left[
\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{\leqslant u}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
-
\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{< w}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
\right]
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{< u}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
-
\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
\right]
 + p_u + p_w.
\end{align*}
But the Claim~\ref{Claimuw} says exactly that the expectation on the right is at most 0, so 
\begin{align}\label{v3}
    \mathbb{E}\left[
\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{\leqslant u}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
-
\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{< w}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}\}}
\right]
\leqslant
p_u + p_w.
\end{align}
Summing \eqref{v2} over $J\cap K$ and \eqref{v3} over the pairs in our bijection between $J\setminus K$ and $K\setminus J$, it follows that the expectation in \eqref{v0} is at most $\sum_{v\in J\cup K}p_v \leqslant 1$.

We say that an edge $e=v_1v_i$ is \emph{$\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning} there is a copy of $\overrightarrow{A}$ rooted at $v_i$ with vertices contained in $\{v_2,\ldots,v_i\}$ and the edge $e$ is directed from $v_1$ to $v_i$ (and thus into the root of the copy of $\overrightarrow{A}$).  An edge is $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning if and only if it is $\overrightarrow{A}$-{in}-jumping in the order obtained by swapping its vertices.  Arguing as before, we see that the expected number of $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning edges is the same as the expected number of $\overrightarrow{A}$-{in}-jumping edges; and, by induction, this expectation is at least $d-|\overrightarrow{A}|+1=d-|\overrightarrow{T}|+2$. 

To bound the expected number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping edges, we note (as before) that if we consider the $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning edges in any ordering, then all but the first is $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping.  So the expected number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping edges is at least $d-|\overrightarrow{T}|+1$.

For $\overrightarrow{T}$-in-jumping edges, we need to do a little more, as having many $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning edges gives many $\overrightarrow{T}$-{\em out}-jumping edges but does not imply that we have a $\overrightarrow{T}$-{\em in}-jumping edge.  So let us fix a vertex $v_1$ and consider orderings starting with $v_1$. We will show that taking the rest of the ordering random, the expected number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-in-jumping edges is at most $1$ smaller than the expected number of $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning edges; the result then follows.
    
To see this, let $U=V\setminus\{v_1\}$ be the set we are randomly ordering, let $I$ be the in-neighbours and $O$ the out-neighbours of $v_1$, so $|I|=|O|$.
Call a subset $W$ of $U$ \emph{rich} if $\{v_1\}\cup W$ contains a copy of $\overrightarrow{T}$ rooted at $v_1$. Clearly the family $\mathcal{F}$ of rich sets is increasing. For a given ordering $<$, if an out-neighbour $u$ of $v$ is $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning then $S_{\leqslant u}\in \mathcal{F}$; it follows that the expected number of out-neighbours $u$ with $S_{\leqslant u}\in \mathcal{F}$ is at least the expected number of $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning edges. On the other hand, an in-neighbour $w$ of $v_1$ is $\overrightarrow{T}$-in-jumping if and only if $S_{<w}\in \mathcal{F}$.  Lemma \ref{prefix} thus implies that expected number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-in-jumping edges is at least the expected number of $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning edges minus~1.

Finally, we remove the conditioning on the first vertex: since the expected number of $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning edges is at least $d-|\overrightarrow{T}|+2$, the expected number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-{out}-jumping edges is at least $d-|\overrightarrow{T}|+1$.

We break the proof into three steps.

\medskip
{\em (1) Every ordering has exactly $s$ edges that are $A$-spanning.}
\smallskip

 By Theorem \ref{th1}, the expected number of $A$-jumping edges in a random ordering of the vertices of $G$ is at least $s$.  It follows (as in the proof of Case 1 in Section~\ref{sec:graphs}) that the expected number of $A$-spanning edges is at least $s$.  If there is an ordering with at least $s+1$ $A$-spanning edges, then we obtain a copy of $T$: take any $s+1$ such edges, and add the copy of $A$ guaranteed by the earliest edge.  It follows that every ordering has exactly $s$ edges that are $A$-spanning.

\medskip
{\em (2) $G$ is $(k-2)$-regular. }
\smallskip

For any vertex $v$, consider any ordering with $v$ as first vertex, followed by its neighbours and then the remaining vertices: since $s$ edges are $A$-spanning then the copy of $A$ guaranteed by the earliest of these edges is contained in the neighbourhood of $v$ and is disjoint from the other $A$-spanning edges, so $v$ has at least $|A|+s-1=k-2$ neighbours.  It follows that $G$ is $(k-2)$-regular.

\medskip
{\em (3) Every component of $G$ is a complete graph.}
\smallskip

Suppose not. We will show for a contradiction that $G$ contains a copy of $T$, by constructing a suitable embedding $\phi:V(T)\to V(G)$. 

Since $G$ has a non-complete component it contains vertices $x$, $y$ and $z$ such that $xy$, $yz\in E(G)$ but $xz\not\in E(G)$. Let $v_1v_2\cdots$ be a longest path in $T$, which has at least $4$ vertices, since $T$ is not a star. Set $\phi(v_2)=x$, $\phi(v_3)=y$ and $\phi(v_4)=z$. Now continue greedily step-by-step, each time embedding a vertex $u$ of $T$ with a (necessarily unique) already embedded neighbour $v$. At each step, the only constraint is that $\phi(u)$ should be an unused neighbour of $\phi(v)$. Since $\phi(v)$ has $k-2$ neighbours this is always possible, unless $u$ is the last vertex to be embedded, and all vertices other than $u$, $v$ are mapped to neighbours of $\phi(v)$. To avoid this, we simply embed $v_1$ last (which is possible since it's a leaf), noting that $\phi(v_4)=z$ is not a neighbour of $\phi(v_2)=x$.

Now suppose that $D$ is a digraph with $n$ vertices and $(k-2)n$ edges, and $\overrightarrow{T}$ is an antidirected tree with $k$ vertices.  If $D$ does not contain a copy of $\overrightarrow{T}$ then there are two cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $\overrightarrow{T}$ is a star then the antidirected condition implies that the centre of the star is either a source or a sink.  In the first case, any $(k-2)$-out-regular digraph is extremal; and in the second, any $(k-2)$-in-regular digraph is extremal.  It is clear that these are the only extremal digraphs.
    \item If $\overrightarrow{T}$ is not a star, then any vertex-disjoint union of copies of complete digraphs on $k-1$ vertices, with all edges directed in both directions, is extremal.  We will show that every extremal digraph has this form. 
\end{itemize}



In the graph case, the key step was to show that $G$ is regular. In the digraph case, we need biregularity. We start by showing that this is sufficient.

\medskip
{\em (1) It suffices to show that $D$ is both in- and out-regular. }
\smallskip

Suppose that $D$ is bi-regular, so every vertex has in-degree and out-degree equal to $k-2$. Let $v_1v_2v_3v_4$ be a path in $\overrightarrow{T}$ with $v_1$ a leaf (which exists since $\overrightarrow{T}$ is not a star), and without loss of generality suppose the edges are directed $v_1\leftarrow v_2\to v_3\leftarrow v_4$. Suppose first that $D$ contains a vertex $y$ whose in-neighbourhood is not bi-complete, so $y$ has in-neighbours $x$ and $z$ with $x\not\to z$. Set $\phi(v_2)=x$, $\phi(v_3)=y$ and $\phi(v_4)=z$, and embed greedily as before. When embedding $u$ with a unique already-embedded neighbour $v$, we need either an unused in- or unused out-neighbour of $\phi(v)$. The only possible problem is at the last step, but we take $v_1$ last, and since $\phi(v_4)$ is not an out-neighbour of $\phi(v_2)$, there is at least one unused out-neighbour of $\phi(v_2)$ to map $v_1$ to.

Thus we may assume that every in-neighbourhood is bi-complete. But this easily implies that each component of $D$ is bi-complete. Let $S$ be the in-neighbourhood of some vertex $v$, so $|S|=k-2$. Then every vertex in $S$ has in- and out- degree $k-3$ within $S$, so exactly one in-edge from outside $S$. If all such edges come from $v$, then the component containing $v$ is bicomplete. Otherwise, there is an edge $x\to s$ for some $s\in S$ and $x\notin S\cup \{v\}$. There is at least one other vertex $t\in S$ (since $k\geqslant 4$ since $\overrightarrow{T}$ is not a star), and now by bi-completeness of the in-neighbourhood of $s$, the edge $t\to x$ is present. But then the out-degree of $t$ is at least $k-1$, a contradiction.

{\em (2) Every ordering has exactly $s$ edges that are $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning.}
\smallskip

Consider first the subtree $\overrightarrow{A}$ rooted at $y$, noting that $y$ is a sink.  As in the graph case, by induction the expected number of $\overrightarrow{A}$-jumping edges is at least $s$, and so the expected number of $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning edges is at least $s$; if there are at least $s+1$ $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning edges then we obtain a (correctly oriented) copy of $\overrightarrow{T}$.  It follows that every ordering has exactly $s$ $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning edges.  

\medskip
{\em (3) $D$ is $(k-2)$-out-regular. }
\smallskip

For any vertex $v$, consider any ordering with $v$ as first vertex, followed by its out-neighbours and then the remaining vertices: since $s$ edges are $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning the copy of $\overrightarrow{A}$ guaranteed by the earliest of these edges is contained in the out-neighbourhood $\Gamma^+(v)$ of $v$ and is disjoint from the other $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning edges, so $v$ has at least $|\overrightarrow{A}|+s-1=k-2$ out-neighbours.  Since this holds for every vertex, it follows that $D$ is $(k-2)$-out-regular.

\medskip
{\em (4) We may suppose that $\overrightarrow{A}$ contains vertices $a, b $ such that $a$ is a source and $b$ is a leaf of $\overrightarrow{T}$ adjacent to $a$.}
\smallskip

As in the undirected case, consider the tree $\overrightarrow{T}'$ obtained from $\overrightarrow{T}$ by deleting all its leaves.  Then $\overrightarrow{T}'$ has at least two leaves, one of which is $x$; let $a$ be another leaf.  If $a$ is a sink, then using $a$ in place of $x$, we get a decomposition of $\overrightarrow{T}$ as in Fig \ref{f4}, except with a sink at $a$ in place of the source at $x$.  Arguing as above, we get that $D$ is $(k-2)$-in-regular; by (1) and (3), this is enough.  Otherwise, $a$ is a source. Since $a$ is a vertex of $\overrightarrow{T}'$, there is some leaf $b$ of $\overrightarrow{T}$ adjacent to $a$, and $a$, $b$ both belong to $\overrightarrow{A}$.

{\em (5) For every vertex $v$, every set $W$ of $|\overrightarrow{A}|=k-s-1$ out-neighbours of $v$, and every $z$ in $W$, there is a copy of $\overrightarrow{A}$ rooted at $z$ and with vertex set $W$.}

As in (3), consider an ordering with first vertex $v$, followed by its out-neighbours $w_1,\dots,w_{k-2}$ and then the remaining vertices.  If $vw_i$ is $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning then $i\geqslant|\overrightarrow{A}|=k-s-1$ (as we need to fit in a copy of $\overrightarrow{A}$); and since there are exactly $s$ $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning edges, it follows that $vw_i$ is $\overrightarrow{A}$-spanning for all $i\geqslant k-s-1$. \smallskip

By (5) there is a copy $\overrightarrow{A_0}$ of $\overrightarrow{A}$ in $\Gamma^+(u)$. Now by (4), $\overrightarrow{A}$ contains a source $a$ and a leaf $b$ of $\overrightarrow{T}$ adjacent to $a$ (so $b$ is not the root of $\overrightarrow{A}$). Let $v$, $w$ be the images of $a$ and $b$ in $\overrightarrow{A_0}$. Suppose $v$ has an out-neighbour $w'$ outside $\Gamma^+(u)\cup\{u\}$. Then we may replace $w$ by $w'$ to obtain a new copy of $\overrightarrow{A}$, and then add $u$, $w$, and the $s-1$ vertices of $\Gamma^+(u)$ outside $\overrightarrow{A_0}$ to make a copy of $\overrightarrow{T}$. Hence $\Gamma^+(v)\subset \Gamma^+(u)\cup\{u\}\setminus\{v\}$, a set of size $k-2$. Thus $\Gamma^+(v)$ is equal to this set.

\medskip
{\em (8) $D$ is in-regular. }
\smallskip

If not, then since the average in-degree is $k-2$, there is some vertex $v$ whose in-neighbourhood $I$ has $|I|\geqslant k-1$. If $I$ is an independent set, then by (6) all vertices in $I$ have the same set $J$ of out-neighbours which is necessarily disjoint from $I$. We thus have a complete bipartite graph with both vertex classes of size at least $k-2$ and all edges directed from the first class to the second class.  As $\overrightarrow{T}$ is not a star, this embeds $\overrightarrow{T}$. We may thus suppose that $I$ is not independent.

By (6) each pair in $I$ is either non-adjacent or bi-adjacent. Moreover, if $u,v$, and $v,w$ are non-adjacent, then (applying (6) to $u$, $v$, which shows that $w\notin \Gamma^+(u)$), so are $u,w$. Hence the subgraph induced by $I$ is complete multipartite with all edges bidirectional. Since $I$ is not independent, there are at least two vertex classes.

Now by (7), every vertex is in a bi-directed edge, so there is $u\in I$ with $v\to u$. Suppose $w$ is in a different class within $I$, and that $v\not\to w$. Since $u\leftrightarrow w$, $v$ and $w$ have a common out-neighbour $u$, so we obtain a contradiction to (6). Hence $v$ sends an edge to every vertex not in the same class as $u$. But applying this once more with $w$ in place of $u$, $v$ sends and edge to every vertex in $I$, and so has out-degree at least $k-1$, a contradiction.

\bigskip

We have shown that $D$ is both in- and out-regular, and so we are done by (1).

{\bf Case (ii):} $x$ is neither a source nor a sink in $\overrightarrow{T}$. Without loss of generality we may assume that the edge $xy$ is directed from $x$ to $y$. Thus there is some $s\in S$ with $sx$ directed from $s$ to $x$. We start with a simple claim: if $\overrightarrow{T}$ is a single edge rooted at the sink and $D$ contains some unidirectional edge, then the expected number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping edges is strictly more than $d-|\overrightarrow{T}|+1=d-1$, where $d=k-2$ is the average degree of $D$. To see this, consider orders with some particular vertex $v_1$ first, with in- and out-degree $t$, taking the rest of the order random. Consider any $u\in \Gamma^+(v_1)$. Then $u$ fails to be $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping if and only if no $w\in \Gamma^-(v_1)$ strictly preceeds $u$, i.e., $u$ is the first element in the set $\{u\}\cup\Gamma^-(v_1)$. This event has probability $1/t$ if $u\in \Gamma^-(v_1)$ and probability $1/(t+1)$ otherwise. It follows that the expected number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping edges in orders starting with $v_1$ is at least $d^+(v_1)-1$, with strict equality if there is some $u\in \Gamma^+(v_1)\setminus \Gamma^-(v_1)$. Averaging over $v_1$, the claim follows.

At this point we need to consider the details of how the inductive bound on in- and out-jumping edges works. By an \emph{augmented tree} we mean a rooted tree $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ with a distinguished leaf $\ell$ adjacent to the root $r$. We call $\ell$ the \emph{extra vertex} of $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ and $r\ell$ the \emph{extra edge}. An edge $v_1v_i$ in a particular ordering of the vertices of $D$ is \emph{a $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ edge} if there is a copy of $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ contained in $\{v_1,\ldots,v_i\}$ with root $v_1$ and with $v_1v_i$ playing the role of the extra edge $e$.
Thus a $\overrightarrow{T}$-in-jumping edge is exactly a  $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ edge where $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ is formed from $\overrightarrow{T}$ by adding a new edge $e$ directed to the root of $\overrightarrow{T}$, and similarly for $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping, reversing the direction of $e$. In this language, Theorem~\ref{eulerian} asserts lower bounds on the expected numbers of $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ edges for all augmented trees $\overrightarrow{T^*}$.
Note that this is simply a restatement of what we have already proved; here, unlike in previous sections, it is convenient to argue with the tree \emph{including} the extra/jumping edge.

Recall that we assume $D$ contains some unidirectional edge.

We say that a leaf $v$ in an augmented tree $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ is \emph{special} if the unique path $v=v_1v_2v_3\cdots$ from $v$ to the extra vertex contains at least two edges, and the first two edges are directed consistently, i.e., $v_1\to v_2\to v_3$ or $v_1\leftarrow v_2\leftarrow v_3$.
We claim that we have \emph{strict} inequality in Theorem~\ref{eulerian} whenever our augmented tree $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ contains a special leaf. We prove this by induction on $|\overrightarrow{T^*}|$, noting that any $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ containing a special leaf has $|\overrightarrow{T^*}|\geqslant 3$.

The base case $|\overrightarrow{T^*}|=3$ is the claim above: $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ consists only of the path $v_1\to v_2\to v_3$ (or the same with arrows reversed), with $v_2v_3$ the extra edge, so $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ edges are just $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping edges where $\overrightarrow{T}$ is the edge $v_1\to v_2$ rooted at $v_2$. 

For the induction step, suppose $|\overrightarrow{T^*}|\geqslant 4$. In Case 2 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{eulerian}, as before we bound the number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping edges by considering $\overrightarrow{A}$-out-jumping and $\overrightarrow{B}$-out-jumping edges, and similarly for in-jumping. The corresponding augmented trees $\overrightarrow{T^*}$, $\overrightarrow{A^*}$ and $\overrightarrow{B^*}$ are simply $T$, $A$ and $B$ with an extra distinguised edge from their root. If $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ contains a special leaf $v$, then without loss of generality $\overrightarrow{A^*}$ contains $v$. Since the entire path from $v$ to the extra vertex is also contained in $\overrightarrow{A^*}$, then $v$ is also special in $\overrightarrow{A^*}$, so by induction we get strict inequality for $\overrightarrow{A^*}$ and thus for $\overrightarrow{T^*}$.

In Case 1 of the induction step,
recall that the root $r$ of $\overrightarrow{T}$ is joined to a single vertex $s$ in $\overrightarrow{T}$, with the edge oriented $r\to s$ (without loss of generality); we consider both $\overrightarrow{T}$-in-jumping and $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping edges, corresponding to the two possible $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ extending $\overrightarrow{T}$.
Since $\overrightarrow{A}$ is defined by removing the root $r$ of $\overrightarrow{T}$, now $\overrightarrow{A^*}$ is simply $\overrightarrow{T}$ with the root moved and the edge $r\to s$ declared the extra edge (corresponding to considering $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-jumping edges). Now by assumption $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ contains a special leaf $v$. The path $P'$ from $v$ to the extra vertex in $\overrightarrow{A^*}$ is simply the corresponding path $P$ in $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ with the final vertex removed. If $v$ is not special, it follows that {\color{red}$P$} contains only three vertices, so $P=vrx$ where $r$ is the root of $\overrightarrow{T}$ and $\overrightarrow{T^*}$ and $x$ is the extra vertex of $\overrightarrow{T^*}$. But since $v$ is a leaf and $r$ has degree 1 in this case, then $\overrightarrow{T}$ is just a single edge, contradicting our assumption that $|\overrightarrow{T^*}|\geqslant 4$. Thus we may assume that $\overrightarrow{A^*}$ contains a special leaf.

As before the expected numbers of $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-spanning and $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-jumping edges are equal, and the expected number of $\overrightarrow{T}$-out-jumping or $\overrightarrow{T}$-in-jumping edges is at most one less. But now we have strict inequality for $\overrightarrow{A}$-in-jumping by induction, and we are done.

Finally, we deduce the presence of a copy of $\overrightarrow{T_0}$ by removing a leaf from $\overrightarrow{T_0}$, choosing one that is not in $S$, and so is in $\overrightarrow{A}$. Then the corresponding augmented tree contains the path $s\to x\to y$, so $s$ is a special leaf and we obtain strict inequality in Theorem~\ref{eulerian} and hence a copy of $\overrightarrow{T_0}$.

We may suppose that $\mathcal{F}$ is non-trivial, otherwise the left-hand side of \eqref{v0} is 0 and equality does not hold.

The proof of Lemma~\ref{prefix} proceeded by summing the inequality \eqref{v3} over pairs $u\in J\setminus K$ and $w\in K\setminus J$, and combining this with the inequality $\sum_{v\in J\cup K}p_v \leqslant \sum_{v\in U}p_v=1$.

Call an element $u\in U$ \emph{irrelevant} if no minimal element of $\mathcal{F}$ contains $u$, so $R\in \mathcal{F}$ if and only if $R\cup\{u\}\in \mathcal{F}$. Now (i) holds if and only if for any $u\in J\setminus K$, $w\in K\setminus J$ and any $\sigma$ we have $S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma)\in \mathcal{F}$ if and only if $S_{<u}(\sigma')\in \mathcal{F}$, where $\sigma'$ is the order obtained by swapping $u$ and $w$. Recalling \eqref{Suw}, this certainly holds if $u$ and $w$ are irrelevant. Conversely, if $S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma)\in \mathcal{F}$ if and only if $S_{<u}(\sigma')\in \mathcal{F}$, for any $R\subset U\setminus\{u,w\}$ we may choose $\sigma$ to start with $R$, then $w$, then $u$. Then $S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma)=R\cup\{w\}$ and $S_{<u}(\sigma')=R$. Swapping $u$ and $w$ in the order, we obtain $S_{\leqslant w}(\sigma)=R\cup \{u,w\}$ and $S_{<u}(\sigma')=R\cup\{w\}$, so $R\in \mathcal{F}$ if and only if $R\cup\{u,w\}\in \mathcal{F}$, which implies that $u$ and $w$ are irrelevant. Hence (i) holds exactly when all elements of $(J\setminus K)\cup (K\setminus J)$ are irrelevant. On the other hand, (ii) holds exactly when all $v\in U\setminus (J\cup K)$ are irrelevant, completing the proof.
