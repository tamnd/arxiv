<!-- head-1: body_md -->
The following statements holds true for the morphism $f\colon\mathfrak{Y}\to\mathfrak{X}$: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item The Radon-Nikodym derivative takes the value
\[
\frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})}
{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})}
(y)
=
\frac{\left\|\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}}\right\|}
{\left\|f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}}\right\|}
\]
for $\nu_{\mathfrak{Y},\operatorname{can}}$-almost all $y\in\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$, where $\nu_{\mathfrak{Y},\operatorname{can}}$ is the canonical measure on $\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$.
\item There is an equality
\[
\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})}{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})}\right)
= R
\]
of divisors on $\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$.
\item The map
\[
\mathfrak{Y}(O_K) \to\mathfrak{R},\;
y\mapsto \frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})}{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})}
\]
is regular, i.e.\ does not contain any poles.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Let us remark here that what Serre proved in \cite{Serre1965} is that compact $p$-adic analytic varieties seem somewhat uninteresting,  the only interesting quantity apart from their dimension being the number of $p$-adic balls it decomposes into modulo $(q-1)$. However, it is extra structure unearthed from them which makes them much more interesting, e.g.\ them being open submanifolds of algebraic varieties (like here), or through an integral structure and a compatible atlas, cf.\ e.g.\ \cite{diffMfp,HearingSerre,brad_habil,BKL2026}.

\]
for any Borel set $A\subset\mathfrak{X}(O_K)$.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Cf.\ \cite[Theorem 2.4]{Oesterle1984}.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{RNderOmega}
The Radon-Nikodym derivative
\[
\frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega)}{d\nu_{\operatorname{can}}}
\]
exists on $\mathfrak{X}(O_K)$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The measure ${\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega)$ associated with the algebraic differential $n$-form is described locally in (\ref{locallyModulus}).
 For the canonical measure $\nu_{\operatorname{can}}$ on $\mathcal{X}(O_K)$, take its  description via a $K$-analytic differential form denoted as $\omega_{X}$. Locally it is of the form
\[
\omega_{X}(x)=\phi(x)\,dx\,,
\]
with $\phi(x)$ a $K$-valued $K$-analytic function nowhere vanishing on the local chart. Hence, 
\[
\left\lvert\omega_X(x)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\phi(x)\right\rvert\left\lvertdx\right\rvert\,,
\]
where $\left\lvertdx\right\rvert$ is yet another way of expressing the Haar measure $\mu_{K^n}$ on $K^n$. But $\left\lvert\phi(x)\right\rvert$ coincides with the absolute value of a polynomial on this local chart. Hence, we can argue as follows: Let $A\subset\mathfrak{X}(O_K)$ be a Borel set, and assume that $A$ is contained inside some closed-open local chart $U$ of $\mathfrak{X}(O_K)$. Then
\begin{align*}
0=\nu_{\operatorname{can}}(A)&=\int_A\left\lvert\omega_X(x)\right\rvert=\int_A\left\lvert\phi(x)\right\rvert\left\lvertdx\right\rvert
\\
&\Rightarrow\;\mu_{K^n}(A)=0\hspace*{2cm}\text{[as $\left\lvert\phi(\cdot)\right\rvert\in\mathcal{D}(U)$ is somewhere $>0$]}
\\
&\Rightarrow\;{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega)(A)=\int_A\left\lvertg_U(x)\right\rvert\left\lvertdx\right\rvert
\\
&\qquad\le\max\limits_{x\in U}\left\lvertg_U(x)\right\rvert\mu_{K^n}(A)=0\,,
\end{align*}
where the latter inequality holds true, because $\left\lvertg_U\right\rvert$ is continuous on the set $U$ which is compact by compactness of $\mathfrak{X}(O_K)$.
Thus ${\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega)$ is strongly continuous w.r.t.\ $\nu_{\operatorname{can}}$. Hence, the Radon-Nikodym Theorem can be applied, and this  proves the existence of the Radon-Nikodym derivative 
\[
\frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega)}{d\nu_{\operatorname{can}}}\,,
\]
as asserted.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{effectiveRamification}
The ramification divisor $R$ is effective.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
This is well-known. A sketch of the proof is given here: the definition of $R$ as
\[
R=\sum\limits_{P\in\mathfrak{X}\;\text{of codim $1$}}\text{length}(\Omega_{\mathfrak{Y}/\mathfrak{X}})_P[P]
\]
obviously reveals the divisor $R$ as being effective. However, it is by proving the isomorphism (\ref{canonicalIsomorphism}) which makes its effectivity effectively meaningful. For this, the exact sequence (\ref{exactSequence_QCSh}) yields, via taking $\wedge^n$, the isomorphism
\[
\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}/O_K}\cong f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}/O_K}\otimes\det(\Omega_{\mathfrak{Y}/\mathfrak{X}})\,,
\]
because that exact sequence is one of locally free sheaves of finite rank on a scheme, cf.\ \cite[Corollary 4.2]{Liu2002}.
By smoothness of the morphism $f$, the latter equals
\[
\det(\Omega_{\mathfrak{X}/\mathfrak{Y}})\cong\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}/\mathfrak{X}}\,,
\]
and the isomorphism now follows from Duality Theory, cf.\ \cite[Chapter 6.4]{Liu2002}.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{RN_genEtale}
The following Radon-Nikodym derivative
\[
\frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})}{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})}
\]
exists, and is of the form
\[
\frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})}{d\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}}
\frac{d\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}}{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})}
\]
on $\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$, where the canonical measure on $\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$ is denoted as $\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
This is the chain rule for Radon-Nikodym derivatives, and is valid if
\[
{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})<<\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}<<{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})
\]
holds true, where $\nu<<\nu'$ denotes  strong continuity of the measure $\nu$ with respect to the measure $\nu'$.

\smallskip
The first strong continuity relation is seen as follows:
 let $A\subset\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$ be a Borel set with
\[
\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}(A)=0\,.
\]
Hence, as in the proof of Lemma \ref{RNderOmega}, it follows that $\mu_{K^n}(A)=0$, and thus
\begin{align*}
\int_A\left\lvertf^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}}\right\rvert
&=\int_A\left\lvertg_U(f(y))\right\rvert\left\lvert\det(T_yf)\right\rvert\left\lvertdy\right\rvert
\stackrel{(*)}{\le}\max\limits_{y\in \mathfrak{Y}(O_K)}\mu_{K^n}(A)=0\,,
\end{align*}
where the inequality $(*)$ follows in this way: first, since $f$ is a morphism of $O_K$-schemes, it holds true that 
\[
\left\lvert\det(T_yf)\right\rvert=1
\]
for $\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}$-almost every $y\in\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$, i.e.\ more precisely, in the locus of $f$ being \'etale. Next, the map
\[
y\mapsto\left\lvertg_U(f(y))\right\rvert
\]
is continuous on the closed-open chart $V$ in the compact $\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$ and containing $A$, where $U$ is a chart in $\mathfrak{X}(O_K)$ containing $f(V)$. This proves ${\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})<<\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}$.

\smallskip
The second one is seen as follows: let $A\subseteq\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$ be a Borel set, contained in some closed-open chart $U$, and such that
\[
\int_A d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathcal{Y}})=0\,.
\]
Then
\[
0=\int_A\left\lvert\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}}(y)\right\rvert
=\int_A\left\lvertg_U(y)\right\rvert
\left\lvertdy\right\rvert\,,
\]
where locally
\[
\omega_X|_U=g_U\,dx
\]
for the chart $U$ of $\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$ containing $A$. The set $U$ being a compact subset of $\mathcal{Y}(O_K)$, observe that the
map
\[
U\to\mathds{R},\;y\mapsto \left\lvertg_U(y)\right\rvert
\]
is continuous, and thus takes a minimum $m$ (and maximum) on $U$. Hence,
\[
0=\int_A d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})\ge m\,\mu(A)\ge0
\]
implies that $\mu(A)=0$ for the Haar measure $\mu$ on $K^n$. But this clearly implies that $\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}(A)=0$, again by compactness of $U$. Hence, $\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}<<{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathcal{Y}})$.

\smallskip 
By the Radon-Nikodym Theorem, it now follows that both derivatives
\[
\frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})}{d\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}}\,,\quad\frac{d\nu_{Y,\operatorname{can}}}{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})}
\]
exist, and the assertion follows by the chain rule for Radon-Nikodym derivatives.

The following statements holds true for the morphism $f\colon\mathfrak{Y}\to\mathfrak{X}$: 
\begin{enumerate}

\item There is an equality

\]
of divisors on $\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)$.

\item The map 
\[
\mathfrak{Y}(O_K)\to \mathds{R},\;
y\mapsto\frac{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(\omega_{\mathfrak{Y}})}{d{\rm\;\,\,\!\!\! mod}(f^*\omega_{\mathfrak{X}})}(y)
\]
is regular, i.e.\ does not contain any poles.
\end{enumerate}

Since the maximal possible rank of this approximately linear system is the number of distinct values of the $a_i$'s, taking enough samples of $a$ with coverage of enough faces of the intersection complex associated with $\operatorname{div}(\omega)$, yields a set of approximate linear equations with this rank. This yields the values of $a_1,\dots,a_s\in\mathds{Q}$.

\smallskip
Having established the knowledge of the $a_i$'s, the question of where on the intersection complex a given ball $B_k(a)$ is taken from, can now be answered as follows: The  volume of $B_k(a)$ can now be obtained with various sets of $a_{j_1},\dots,a_{j_m}$ with sets ${\left\{j_1,\dots,j_m\right\}}$ of varying size.
Collect all of these sets into a set $K_0$. Now, sample a ball of equal size from a neighbouring face in the intersection complex. This amounts to either adding an $a_i$ to, or subtracting an $a_i$ to these sets. Collect all the new possible subsets of $I$ in a new set $K_1$, and discard 
from $K_0$ all sets which do not have a counterpart in $K_1$ be either adding or removing an element. Do the same with $K_1$. Now sample a ball of equal radius from a neighbouring face to  the previously sampled ball, and collect all the possible subsets of $I$ in $K_{n+1}$. Remove from $K_n$ any set which does not have a match in $K_{n+1}$ by adding or removing an element. Likewise with $K_{n+1}$. From there backtrack all previous sets $K_0,\dots,K_n$ by removing items not forming edit-neighbour pairs. Eventually, $K_0$ has a minimal size. We need to prove that the minimal size of $1$ is attainable, and this then yields the position of the initially sampled ball $B_k(a)$ in the intersection complex.

\smallskip
The divisor $\operatorname{div}(\omega)$ is built up by attaching to the  known values of $a_1,\dots,a_s$ the fibres of all points of the reduction $\mathfrak{D}(\mathds{F}_q)$, taking into account the faces of the intersection complex they are attached to. These are not known per se, but two fibres belonging to the same face can be reached by a trivial path on that simplicial complex.
