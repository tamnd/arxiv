<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{easyLemma}

If $(E,|\!|\cdot|\!|)$ is a two-dimensional normed space with a sign-symmetric basis  $\{f_1,f_2\}$, then
\[{\rm (i)}\quad \hbox{For every $y$, if $|s|\leq |t|$ then $|\!|sf_1+yf_2|\!|\leq |\!|tf_1+yf_2|\!|$}, \]
and
\[{\rm (ii)}\quad \hbox{For every $x$, if $|s|\leq |t|$ then $|\!|xf_1+sf_2|\!|\leq |\!|xf_1+tf_2|\!|$}.\]
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We prove (i). The proof of (ii) is similar.

\medskip

Put $\nu(x,y)=|\!|xf_1+yf_2|\!|$.
Fix $y$, and let $s,t$ be such that $|s|\leq |t|$. Convexity of the norm combined with sign-symmetry implies, for every~$x$,
\begin{equation}\label{fconvex}
\nu(0,y)\leq\frac{1}{2}(\nu(-x,y)+\nu(x,y))=\nu(x,y).
\end{equation}
Pick a point $p=(t,y)$, with $t>0$. Sign-symmetry allows us to assume that $0\leq s<t$. The point $p$ belongs
to the convex set $B=\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}: |\!|q|\!|\leq|\!|p|\!|\}$, and by (\ref{fconvex}), so does $(0,y)$. Hence the line segment joining the point $p$ to $(0,y)$ also belongs to the set $B$, so for every $0\leq\lambda\leq 1$, the point
$(\lambda t,y)$ belongs to $B$ as well. In particular, (take $\lambda=s/t$),
the point $(s,y)$ belongs to $B$. Hence,
\[ \nu(s,y)\leq \nu(p)=\nu(t,y), \]
and (i) is proved.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{distanceProposition} Let $E$ be a two dimensional normed space  with a  sign-symmetric basis 
${\beta=\{f_1,f_2\}}$.
Put
\[ \textsc{H}\colonequals\textsc{H}(E,\beta) \ \  \mbox{and} \ \  \textsc{H}_{n+1}\colonequals\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E,\beta).\] 
The space $\textsc{H}$ is isomorphic to $\ell_2$, and for every $n\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}\label{equalDistances}
d(\textsc{H}_{n+1})= d(\textsc{H})=d(E).
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
One direction of inequalities follows from the inclusions
\[ E\cong\textsc{H}_2\subset\textsc{H}_{n+1}\subset\textsc{H},\] 
valid for all $n\geq 1$. Hence
\[ d(E)\leq d(\textsc{H}_{n+1})\leq d(\textsc{H}).\]
To complete the proof, it remains to show that $\textsc{H}$ is isomorphic to $\ell_2$ and that ${d(\textsc{H})\leq d(E)}$.

\medskip

If $B_{\scriptscriptstyle E}$ is the unit ball of $E$ and $B_2$ the unit ball of $\ell_2^2$, then there exists a linear isomorphism ${L:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to E}$  such that
\begin{equation}\label{distanceEllipsoidInclusion}
B_{\scriptscriptstyle E}\subset L(B_2)\subset d(E)B_{\scriptscriptstyle E},
\end{equation}
where $L(B_2)$ is the distance ellipse of $E$. Put $u_1=L^{-1}f_1$ and $u_2=L^{-1}f_2$. A vector $x_1f_1+x_2f_2$ belongs to $L(B_2)$ if and only if $x_1u_1+x_2u_2\in B_2$, that is, if and only if in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ the point $(x_1,x_2)$
belongs to some linear image of the ball $B_2$. Therefore, there exist real numbers $a,b,c$ such that $a$ and $c$ are nonzero and
\[ x_1f_1+x_2f_2\in\hbox{boundary of  $L(B_2)$}\enspace\hbox{if and only if}\enspace a^2x_1^2+b\,x_1x_2+c^2x_2^2=1. \]


By the uniqueness of the distance ellipse, $L(B_2)$  is invariant under the  sign-symmetries of~$E$.  Hence $b=0$.  
Thus, by (\ref{distanceEllipsoidInclusion}), for every choice of real scalars $x_1,x_2$,
\begin{equation}\label{distance}
(a^2x_1^2+c^2x_2^2)^{1/2}\leq |\!|x_1 f_1+x_2 f_2|\!|\leq d(E) (a^2x_1^2+c^2x_2^2)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}

Define a map $T:\textsc{H}\to\ell_2$ by
\[ T(t,\eta_1,\eta_2,\dots)=(c t,a \eta_1,a \eta_2,\dots).\]
$T$ is clearly an isomorphism onto, and by (\ref{distance}),
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\!|T(t,\eta_1,\eta_2,\dots)|\!|_{\ell_2}&=(c^{2}t^2+a^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\eta_i^2)^{1/2}\leq  |\!|(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\eta_i^2)^{1/2}f_1+tf_2|\!|\\
&=|\!|(t,\eta_1,\eta_2,\dots)|\!|_{\textsc{H}}. \\
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence, $|\!|T|\!|\leq 1$. The inverse $T^{-1}$ maps a vector $\{x_k\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\in\ell_2$ to the vector
\[(\frac{x_1}{c},\frac{x_2}{a},\frac{x_3}{a},\dots), \]
whose norm in $\textsc{H}$, according to (\ref{distance}), satisfies:
\[|\!|\frac{1}{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}x_k^2\bigg)^{1/2}f_1+\frac{x_1}{c}f_2|\!|\leq d(E)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_k^2\bigg)^{1/2},\]
whence $|\!|T^{-1}|\!|\leq d(E)$.
Therefore $\textsc{H}$ is  isomorphic to $\ell_2$, and  $d(\textsc{H})\leq d(E)$.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{dualSpaceProposition}
 Let $E$ be a two dimensional normed space  with a  sign-symmetric basis $\beta=\{f_1,f_2\}$.
 
 \medskip 
 
If $E^*$ is the dual space of $E$, then the algebraic dual basis~$\beta^*=\{f_1^*,f_2^*\}$ is also sign-symmetric 

\medskip

Moreover, for every ${n\geq 1}$
the dual space of $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E,\beta)$ is isometrically isomorphic to $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E^*,\beta^*)$ and the dual space of $\textsc{H}(E,\beta)$ is isometrically isomorphic to $\textsc{H}(E^*,\beta^*)$.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For every choice of signs $\varepsilon_1=\pm 1,\varepsilon_2=\pm 1$, the map 
\[ T(x_1f_1+x_2f_2)=\varepsilon_1x_1f_1+\varepsilon_2x_2f_2 \]
is a linear isometry of $E$ onto itself, hence for every choice of scalars $a_1,a_2$ and every $\varepsilon_i=\pm 1$
\[
|\!|a_1\varepsilon_1f_1^*+a_2\varepsilon_2f_2^*|\!|=|\!|a_1f_1^*+a_2 f_2^*|\!|.
\]
Thus $\{f_1^*,f_2^*\}$ is a sign-symmetric basis for the dual space $E^*$.
\vskip 6pt
We prove the infinite-dimensional case. The finite-dimensional case has a similar proof. To avoid
cluttered notation, we  temporarily replace the symbol $\textsc{H}(E,\beta)$ by $\textsc{H}(E)$ and $\textsc{H}(E^*,\beta^*)$ by $\textsc{H}(E^*)$.  Elements of the underlying vector-space $\mathbb{R}\oplus\ell_2$ are denoted by pairs $(t,v)$, and the standard inner product in $\ell_2$ is denoted by $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$.

\medskip
 
  For each element $(s,v)\in\textsc{H}(E^*)$, let $\Lambda_{s,v}$ denote the linear functional defined on $\textsc{H}(E)$ by
\[ \Lambda_{s,v}(t,u)=st+\langle v,u\rangle.\]
 Each $\Lambda_{s,v}$ is bounded, hence the map $(s,v)\to\Lambda_{s,v}$ maps
$\textsc{H}(E^*)$ into $(\textsc{H}(E))^*$. It is clearly linear. It is also surjective, because if $e_0$ denotes the vector in $\textsc{H}(E)$ whose first coordinate is $1$ and all the rest are zero, then it is straightforward to verify that every linear functional $\Lambda\in(\textsc{H}(E))^*$ has the form $\Lambda=\Lambda_{s,v}$ for $s=\Lambda(e_0)$ and a uniquely determined vector $v\in\ell_2$.

\medskip

To prove that our map is an isometry, we must  prove that for every $s\in\mathbb{R}$ and every $v\in\ell_2$,
\begin{equation}\label{isometryToProve}
|\!|\Lambda_{s,v}|\!|=|\!|(s,v)|\!|_{\textsc{H}(E^*)} .
\end{equation}

\medskip

Fix a pair $(s,v)\in\textsc{H}(E)$. Let $e_1$ denote first vector  of the standard basis of~$\ell_2$. For each $v\in\ell_2$,  let $|v|$ denote its norm. 

\medskip

Now we use the fact that the group of isometries of a Hilbert Space acts transitively on the unit sphere.
Pick an isometry $U$ of $\ell_2$ such that $Uv=|v|e_1$. Then,
\[\begin{aligned}
|\!|\Lambda_{s,v}|\!|&=\sup\{|st+\langle u,v\rangle|: (t,u)\in\textsc{H}(E),\ \ |\!|t f_1+|u|f_2|\!|=1\}\\
&=\sup\{|st+|v|\langle Uu,e_1\rangle|:(t,u)\in\textsc{H}(E),\ \  |\!|t f_1+|u|f_2|\!|=1\}\\
&\leq \sup\{|st+|v|\langle Uu,e_1\rangle|:(t,u)\in\textsc{H}(E),\ \  |\!|t f_1+\langle Uu,e_1\rangle f_2|\!|\leq 1\}\\
&=\sup\{|st+|v|a|:a,t\in\mathbb{R},\  |\!|t f_1+a f_2|\!|\leq 1\}\\
&=|\!|s f_1^*+|v|f_2^*|\!|_{E^*} = |\!|(s,v)|\!|_{\textsc{H}(E^*)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The inequality on the third line follows from  the inequality $|\langle Uu,e_1\rangle|\leq|u|$, valid for every $u\in\ell_2$.  The monotonicity of the basis $\{f_1,f_2\}$, as described in Lemma~\ref{easyLemma}, implies that  the supremum in the third line is taken on a larger set of elements $(t,u)$.
\medskip

So far we have the inequality $|\!|\Lambda_{s,v}|\!|\leq  |\!|(s,v)|\!|_{\textsc{H}(E^*)}$. To conclude the proof of ~(\ref{isometryToProve}), we show that for our already fixed $(s,v)$ there exists a norm-$1$ vector $(t,u)$ in $\textsc{H}(E)$, for which 
$\Lambda_{s,v}(t,u)=|\!|(s,v)|\!|_{\textsc{H}(E^*)}$.

\medskip

Choose an element $a_1f_1 + a_2f_2\in E$ whose norm is~$1$, such that the functional $sf_1^*+|v|f_2^*$ attains at it its norm. That is,
\begin{equation}\label{linearFunctionalAttainsNorm}
sa_1+a_2|v|_2=|\!|sf_1^*+|v|f_2^*|\!|_{E^*}=|\!|(s,v)|\!|_{\textsc{H}(E^*)}. 
\end{equation}
If $v=0$, then $(a_1,0)$ has norm $1$ in $\textsc{H}(E)$, and by (\ref{linearFunctionalAttainsNorm}), $\Lambda_{s,0}(a_1,0)=|\!|(s,0)|\!|_{\textsc{H}(E^*)}$.
If $v\neq 0$, then the element $(a_1,a_2v/|v|)$ has norm $1$ in $\textsc{H}(E)$, because by the sign-symmetry of the basis $\{f_1,f_2\}$,
\[
|\!|(a_1,a_2v/|v|)|\!|_{\textsc{H}(E)}=|\!|a_1f_1+|a_2|f_2|\!|=|\!|a_1f_1+a_2f_2|\!|_E =1.
\]
Moreover, 
\[\Lambda_{s,v}(a_1,a_2v/|v|)=sa_1+a_2|v|=|\!|(s,v)|\!|_{\textsc{H}(E^*)}.\]
This completes the proof of  (\ref{isometryToProve}).

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->

\end{aligned}
\]

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{myTheorem} Let $\mu$ be  a symmetric probability measure on $\mathbb{R}$, not concentrated at zero,
 with a finite first moment. If $E=\text{span}\{x,1\}$, then  $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E,\{x,1\})$  is an $L_1$-subspace if and only
 if $\mu$ is a marginal distribution of an isotropic vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The identity function of $\mathbb{R}$ belongs to $L_1(\mathbb{R},\mu)$ because $\mu$ has a finite first moment. The set $\{x,1\}$ is linearly independent, because in $L_1(\mathbb{R},\mu)$ the function~$x$ is proportional to some constant~$c$ if and only if 
$\mu\{x\neq c\}=0$, which by symmetry of $\mu$ implies $c=0$ and $\mu$ must then be concentrated at zero.
The functions $\{x,1\}$ form a sign-symmetric basis for their span in $L_1(\mathbb{R},\mu)$ because $\mu$ is symmetric.

 Assume  $J:\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)\hookrightarrow L_1$ is an isometric linear embedding.  The underlying vector space of
  $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)$ is $\mathbb{R}\oplus\ell_2^n$, with the standard basis $\{e_k\}_{k=1}^{n+1}$, where according to (\ref{normDefinition}) 
 \[ |\!|(t,a_1,\dots, a_n)|\!|_{\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^na_k^2\bigg)^{1/2}x+t|\,d\mu(x).\]
 We proceed under the assumption that $J(e_1)= 1$. A justification for this assumption is deferred to avoid disrupting the main argument.
 
\medskip

Put $X_{k-1}=Je_k$ for $k=2,\dots n+1$.  If $(a_1,\dots,a_n) \in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a unit vector, then since $J(e_1)=1$, 
\[ \int_0^1|\sum_{k=1}^na_kX_k(\omega)-t|\,d\omega=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^na_k^2\bigg)^{1/2}x-t|\,d\mu=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-t|\,d\mu, \]
 for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$.
Hence the probability distribution of $\sum_{k=1}^na_kX_k$ coincides with the probability distribution of $\mu$. This holds for every unit vector $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$, so that
the random vector $(X_1,\dots X_n)$ is isotropic, and its marginal distribution is $\mu$.

\medskip

Conversely, if $\mu$ is a marginal distribution of an isotropic vector $X$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, then  the map 
\[ J:\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)\hookrightarrow L_1,\]
defined by
\[ J(t,a_1,\dots, a_n)=\sum_{k=1}^na_kX_k+t , \]
is an isometric linear embedding, because
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\!|J(t,a_1,\dots,a_n)|\!|_{L_1}&=|\!|\sum_{k=1}^na_kX_k+t|\!|_{L_1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^na_k^2\bigg)^{1/2}x+t|\,d\mu\\
&=|\!|(t,a_1,\dots,a_n)|\!|_{\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)}.\\
\end{aligned}
\] 

\medskip

It remains to justify the assumption that  $J(e_1)=1$, a justification that turns out to be much more involved than the main argument.
That $J(e_1)$ can assumed to be the constant function~$1$ was mentioned as a seemingly innocent normalization in Dor's paper (\cite{Dor}, p. 264) with no justification. However, not every subspace of $L_1$ is unital, that is, contains constant functions, and so some kind of justification is necessary.  It follows from Lemma 1.2 of \cite{DJP} that every nonzero subspace of $L_1$ is isometrically isomorphic to a unital subspace of $L_1$. In particular, if $F$ denotes the image of $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)$ under $J$, then 
there is an isometry from $L_1$ onto itself that maps $F$ onto a unital subspace. 
The proof of Lemma 1.2 of \cite{DJP} reveals that in order to make sure that $J(e_1)$ is transformed to the constant function $1$ under such an isometry,
it suffices to check that $J(e_1)$ has full support in $F$, which means that if $f_1=J(e_1)$, then for every $g\in F$, 
\begin{equation}\label{fullSupport}
\lambda(\text{supp}(g)\backslash\text{supp}(f_1))=0,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the Lebesgue measure on $(0,1)$. Once we know that $f_1$ has full support in $F$, we can argue as in the proof of Lemma 1.2 of \cite{DJP} to produce an isometry $S:F\to L_1$ such that $S(f_1)= 1$, and then replace~$J$ by $S\circ J$. 

\medskip

To prove (\ref{fullSupport}), pick  $g\in F$, and scalars $\{a_i\}_{i=1}^{n+1}$, not all zero, such that 
\[ g=a_1f_1+J(\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}a_ie_i). \]
If $a_i=0$ for all $2\leq i\leq n+1$ then  (\ref{fullSupport}) is valid because $a_1\neq 0$. Otherwise, the vector
$v=\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}a_ie_i$ is nonzero,  so if $u=v/|v|$, then $g$ belongs to the span of $\{f_1,J(u)\}$.
By the definition of the norm in $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)$, 
there is an isometric isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{twoDimIsometry}
T:E\to\text{span}\{e_1,u\},\qquad T(s\cdot x+t\cdot 1)=su+te_1.
\end{equation}
If $J_{u}$ denotes the restriction of $J$ to the subspace $\text{span}\{e_1,u\}$, then $J_{u}\circ T$ is an isometry from $E$ onto $\text{span}\{f_1,J(u)\}$, that maps the constant function $1$ to the function $f_1$. 
By Lemma~3.4 of \cite{Hardin}, the function $f_1$ has full support in 
$\text{span}\{f_1,J(u)\}$. Since $g$ belongs to this two dimensional space, (\ref{fullSupport}) holds for every $g\in F$.

 Assume that for every $n\geq1$ the space  $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)$ is an $L_1$-subspace. \end{equation}

 for every choice of scalars $\{\alpha_i\}_{i=0}^{\infty}$ and positive integers $m<n$, the definition of the norm in

It follows from  \cite{LT}, proposition 1.a.3,  that $\{e_i\}_{i=0}^{\infty}$ is a Schauder basis in $\textsc{H}(E)$.

 |\!|a_1e_1+a_2e_2|\!|=\frac{1}{2}\int_\mathbb{R} |a_1x-a_2|\,\varphi''(x)\,dx, \qquad (a_1,a_2\in\mathbb{R}).

The space  $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)$ is an $L_1$-subspace, so by Lemma~\ref{myTheorem}, $\mu$ is a marginal distribution of an isotropic vector $X$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. If $\Phi_X$ is the characteristic function of $X$ and $\Phi_\mu$ is the characteristic function of~$\mu$, then orthogonal invariance implies:
\[ \Phi_X(Y)=E(e^{i Y\cdot X})=E(e^{i|Y|X_1})=\Phi_\mu(|Y|),\quad (Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}).\]
By a result of Schoenberg, (\cite{Schoenberg} , Theorem~$1$),   there exists a non-decreasing and bounded function $\alpha_n(u)$ defined on $[0,\infty)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{finiteDimCFunctionRep}
\Phi_\mu(t)=\int_0^\infty \Omega_n(tu)\,d\alpha_n(u),\qquad (t\in\mathbb{R}),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_n$ is the characteristic function of the marginal distribution of a unit vector uniformly distributed over the sphere $S^{n-1}$. 
                                                
                                                                                          

These considerations hold for all $n$. By another result of Schoenberg (\cite{Schoenberg}, Theorem~$2$),  there exists a non-decreasing and bounded function $\sigma(u)$ defined on $[0,\infty)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{infiniteDimCFunctionRep}
\Phi_\mu(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t^2u}\,d\sigma(u), \qquad (t\in\mathbb{R}).
\end{equation}

The characteristic function of $\mu$ is the Fourier transform of its  density. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{fundEq}
(\varphi'')^{\wedge}(t)=2\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t^2u}\,d\sigma(u), \qquad (t\in\mathbb{R}).
\end{equation}
As a function of $t$, the right hand side of (\ref{fundEq}) is in $L_1(\mathbb{R})$. Take the Fourier transforms of both sides and
use the fact that $\varphi''$ is continuous and even to deduce that 
\[ \varphi''(t)=2\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t^2/4x}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{x}}\,d\sigma(x), \qquad (t\in\mathbb{R}).\]
It follows that $\varphi''(\sqrt{t})$ is completely monotone in $[0,\infty)$. (See also \cite{BN}, p~$223$).

\medskip

These implications can be reversed. If $\varphi''(\sqrt{t})$ is completely monotone in $[0,\infty)$, then $(\varphi'')^{\wedge}(\sqrt{t})$ is also completely monotone in $[0,\infty)$.
By Bernstein's theorem, (\cite{Bernstein1928}, p.$56$), there exists a non-decreasing bounded function $\sigma$ such that
(\ref{infiniteDimCFunctionRep}) and (\ref{fundEq}) are valid. For every $n\in\mathbb{N}$, Schoneberg's theorem can now be
applied in the reverse direction, to deduce  from (\ref{infiniteDimCFunctionRep}) a representation of the form (\ref{finiteDimCFunctionRep}). As a result,  for every $n$ the measure $\mu$ is a marginal distribution of an isotropic vector in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.  By Lemma~\ref{myTheorem}, the space $\textsc{H}_{n+1}(E)$ is an $L_1$-subspace for all $n$, and by Lemma~\ref{subspaceLemma}, so is $\textsc{H}(E)$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{strictlyConvexAndSmooth}
The unit ball $B_1$ of the One Gaussian Norm is sign-symmetric, smooth and strictly convex.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->

That $B_1$ is sign-symmetric follows at once from~(\ref{oneGaussian}).  Smoothness will follow from a direct computation of the derivative of $\gamma_1$ in subsection 3.1 below. To prove that $B_1$ is strictly convex, i.e., that the boundary $\partial B_1$ contains no intervals, pick any two  points $(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2)$ in $\partial B_1$. 
If $x_1=0$, then $|y|=\pi^{-1/2}$.
Consider the functions $f_1(t)=x_1t-y_1$, $f_2(t)=x_2t-y_2$ defined in $\mathbb{R}$. We have 
\[ 
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_1(\frac{(x_1,y_1)+(x_2,y_2)}{2})=1&\Longleftrightarrow \int_\mathbb{R} |f_1(t)+f_2(t)|e^{-t^2}\,dt\\
&=\int_\mathbb{R} |f_1(t)|e^{-t^2}\,dt+\int_\mathbb{R} |f_2(t)|e^{-t^2}\,dt 
\end{aligned}
\]
Equality in the triangle inequality in $L_1(e^{-t^2}\,dt)$ for the continuous functions $f_1,f_2$, implies that they must have the same sign everywhere in $\mathbb{R}$:
\[ (x_1t-y_1)(x_2t-y_2)\geq 0\qquad (\forall t\in\mathbb{R}) \]
If $x_1=0$, then $|y_1|=\pi^{-1/2}$, which implies that $x_2t-y_2$ has a constant sign in $\mathbb{R}$, which is possible only if $x_2=0$, whence $|y_2|=\pi^{-1/2}$. Consequently,
the two points are equal or antipodal. They can't be antipodal because their average was supposed to have norm $1$, so they must be equal. Thus we may assume that both $x_1,x_2$ are nonzero. Examining the  quadratic above we deduce that $x_1x_2\geq 0$, $y_1y_2\geq 0$, and  $\frac{y_1}{x_1}=\frac{y_2}{x_2}$, which implies that $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ are proportional. Since both have norm one, and they are not antipodal, they must be equal. This proves that there are no intervals in the boundary of $B_1$.
