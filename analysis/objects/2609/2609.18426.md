<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:ball}
For every $n\geq2$, no ball of positive radius in $\mathbb R^n$ admits a Riesz basis of exponentials.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:bumps}
The system $\{e_\lambda:\lambda\in\Lambda\}$ is a Riesz basis of
$L^2(\Omega)$ if and only if there are $\delta,r>0$, a real nonnegative
$b\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n)$ and $0\leq\gamma<1$ such that $\Lambda$ is
$\delta$-separated,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bump}
 \|b\|_2=1,\quad \operatorname{supp} b\subset B(0,r),\quad 2r<\delta,
 \quad \inf_{x\in\overline\Omega}|\mathcal F b(x)|>0,
\end{equation}
and the orthogonal projection $Q_\Lambda$ onto the closed span of
$\{b(\cdot-\lambda):\lambda\in\Lambda\}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:initial-distance-bound}
 \|P-Q_\Lambda\|\leq\gamma<1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Suppose first that the system is a Riesz basis. Apply the lower Riesz bound to coefficients $1$ and $-1$ at distinct
frequencies. Since $|e^{iu}-e^{iv}|\leq|u-v|$,
\[
 2A\leq\|e_\lambda-e_\eta\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2
 \leq4\pi^2|\lambda-\eta|^2\int_\Omega|x|^2\,dx.
\]
The integral is finite and positive. This gives a positive separation
constant $\delta$. Comparing the volumes of disjoint balls of radius
$\delta/2$ also gives a uniform bound on the number of frequencies in any
ball of fixed radius.

Choose a real nonnegative smooth compactly supported $b_0$ with
$\|b_0\|_2=1$. For small $\varepsilon>0$ put
$b(\xi)=\varepsilon^{-n/2}b_0(\xi/\varepsilon)$. Its support can be made arbitrarily small, and
\[
 (\mathcal F b)(x)=\varepsilon^{n/2}(\mathcal F b_0)(\varepsilon x).
\]
Since $(\mathcal F b_0)(0)>0$ and $\overline\Omega$ is compact, this proves
\eqref{eq:bump} for a sufficiently small fixed $\varepsilon$.

For either direction, the conditions on $b$ and $\Lambda$ imply that the
translates $b_\lambda=b(\cdot-\lambda)$ have disjoint supports and
norm one. Consequently
\[
 W_\Lambda:\ell^2(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ran} Q_\Lambda,
 \qquad W_\Lambda c=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}c_\lambda b_\lambda,
\]
is unitary. For finitely supported $c$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bump-synthesis}
 \mathcal F(PW_\Lambda c)
 =\mathbf 1_\Omega(\mathcal F b)\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}c_\lambda e_\lambda.
\end{equation}
Multiplication by $\mathcal F b$ is an isomorphism of $L^2(\Omega)$.
If the exponential system is a Riesz basis, its synthesis operator is
also an isomorphism onto $L^2(\Omega)$. Thus \eqref{eq:bump-synthesis}
extends to $\ell^2(\Lambda)$ and shows that
$P:\operatorname{Ran} Q_\Lambda\to\operatorname{Ran} P$ is an isomorphism.
Lemma~\ref{lem:projection-distance} below gives
\eqref{eq:initial-distance-bound}.

Conversely, suppose the stated separation, bump and distance conditions
hold. The same lemma shows that
$P:\operatorname{Ran} Q_\Lambda\to\operatorname{Ran} P$ is an isomorphism. Hence
\[
 c\longmapsto (\mathcal F b)^{-1}\mathcal F(PW_\Lambda c)|_\Omega
\]
is a bounded isomorphism from $\ell^2(\Lambda)$ onto $L^2(\Omega)$.
By \eqref{eq:bump-synthesis}, it agrees with exponential synthesis on
finitely supported sequences. The exponential system is therefore a
Riesz basis.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:projection-distance}
Let $P$ and $Q$ be orthogonal projections on a Hilbert space.  The map
\[
 P:\operatorname{Ran} Q\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ran} P
\]
is a bounded isomorphism if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projection-distance}
 \|P-Q\|<1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Set $A=P|_{\operatorname{Ran} Q}:\operatorname{Ran} Q\to\operatorname{Ran} P$. Its adjoint is
$A^*=Q|_{\operatorname{Ran} P}$. We first record the identity
\[
 \|P-Q\|
 =\max\{\|(I-P)Q\|,\|(I-Q)P\|\}.
\]
Indeed, for every $x$,
\[
 (P-Q)x=P(I-Q)x-(I-P)Qx.
\]
The two terms on the right are orthogonal, as are $(I-Q)x$ and $Qx$.
Since $\|P(I-Q)\|=\|(I-Q)P\|$, Pythagoras gives the upper bound in
the identity. The reverse bound follows by multiplying $P-Q$ on the left
by $I-P$ and $I-Q$.


Suppose first that $\|P-Q\|=\gamma<1$. For $u\in\operatorname{Ran} Q$ and
$y\in\operatorname{Ran} P$, Pythagoras gives
\[
 \|Au\|^2\geq(1-\gamma^2)\|u\|^2,
 \qquad
 \|A^*y\|^2\geq(1-\gamma^2)\|y\|^2.
\]
Thus $A$ is injective with closed range, while
$(\operatorname{Ran} A)^\perp=\ker A^*=\{0\}$ makes its range dense in $\operatorname{Ran} P$.
Hence $A$ is onto and has a bounded inverse.

Conversely, suppose $A$ is an isomorphism. If both ranges are zero, the
conclusion is immediate. Otherwise, put
$c=\|A^{-1}\|^{-1}\in(0,1]$. Since
$(A^*)^{-1}=(A^{-1})^*$, both $A$ and $A^*$ are bounded below by $c$.
Pythagoras therefore yields
\[
 \|(I-P)Q\|\leq\sqrt{1-c^2},
 \qquad
 \|(I-Q)P\|\leq\sqrt{1-c^2}.
\]
The identity above gives $\|P-Q\|\leq\sqrt{1-c^2}<1$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:inclusion}
Let $R_-$ and $R_+$ be orthogonal projections with
\[
 \operatorname{Ran} R_-\subsetneq\operatorname{Ran} R_+.
\]
There is no orthogonal projection $M$ such that
\[
 \|R_--M\|<1,
 \qquad
 \|R_+-M\|<1.
\]
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{lem:projection-distance}, $M$ maps each of the two ranges isomorphically onto
$\operatorname{Ran} M$.  Choose $w\in\operatorname{Ran} R_+\setminus\operatorname{Ran} R_-$.  Surjectivity on the
smaller range gives $u\in\operatorname{Ran} R_-$ with $Mu=Mw$.  Then
$0\neq w-u\in\operatorname{Ran} R_+$ and $M(w-u)=0$, contradicting injectivity on
$\operatorname{Ran} R_+$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:hull}
The space of $\delta$-separated subsets of $\mathbb R^n$, including the empty
set, is compact and metrizable in the topology \eqref{eq:weak-sets}.
The translation action is jointly continuous in the translation parameter
and the configuration.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
The separation bound gives a uniform bound on the mass of the counting
measures on each compact set. A diagonal extraction against a countable
dense collection of continuous compactly supported functions gives a
vaguely convergent subsequence, with a locally finite positive limit
measure $\eta$.


We verify that $\eta$ is again a counting measure. If
$x\in\operatorname{supp}\eta$, choose arbitrarily small balls $B(x,\varepsilon)$ of
diameter less than $\delta$ whose boundaries have $\eta$-measure zero.
Vague convergence gives
\[
 \eta_{\Gamma_j}(B(x,\varepsilon))
 \longrightarrow\eta(B(x,\varepsilon)).
\]
The left-hand side takes values in $\{0,1\}$, while the limit is positive
because $x\in\operatorname{supp}\eta$. Hence every such ball has mass one. Letting
$\varepsilon\downarrow0$ gives $\eta(\{x\})=1$. Thus $\eta$ is the
counting measure of its support. Two distinct support points at distance
less than $\delta$ would, for large $j$, force two points of $\Gamma_j$
to be less than $\delta$ apart. The support is therefore
$\delta$-separated; the zero measure corresponds to the empty set.


The same countable test functions metrize vague convergence on this
uniformly locally bounded family. Sequential compactness therefore gives
compactness. Finally, if $\Gamma_j\to\Gamma$ and $y_j\to y$, the local
matching criterion gives $\Gamma_j-y_j\to\Gamma-y$.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:strong-Q}
If $\Gamma_j\to\Gamma$ in $X$, then $Q_{\Gamma_j}\to Q_\Gamma$
strongly on $L^2(\mathbb R^n)$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hull-distance-bound}
 \|P-Q_\Gamma\|\leq\gamma\qquad(\Gamma\in X).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
First take a compactly supported $a\in L^2(\mathbb R^n)$. Choose a ball
containing the closed $r$-neighborhood of its support in its interior
and having no point of $\Gamma$ on its boundary. Within this ball,
weak convergence and separation give, for large $j$, a bijection
between the finitely many centers of $\Gamma_j$ and $\Gamma$, with
matched centers converging. Only centers in this ball can contribute
to \eqref{eq:Q}. Translations of $b$ are continuous in $L^2$, so each
term of this finite sum converges in $L^2$. Thus
$Q_{\Gamma_j}a\to Q_\Gamma a$. Compactly supported functions are dense,
and all these projections have norm at most one, proving strong
convergence.

Conjugating by translations preserves $P$ and sends $Q_\Lambda$ to
the projection for the translated set. Thus every translate satisfies
\eqref{eq:initial-distance-bound}. Taking a strong limit and testing
on an arbitrary vector gives \eqref{eq:hull-distance-bound}.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:cutoffs}
The set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:good-t}
 \mathcal G=\{t\in\mathbb R^n:\sigma(\partial\Omega-t)=0\}
\end{equation}
is measurable and has full Lebesgue measure. For every $t\in\mathcal G$,
$\Pi_{t,R}$ converges strongly as $R\to\infty$ to an orthogonal
projection $\Pi_t$. For $s,t\in\mathcal G$ and $f\in\mathcal H$,
\begin{align}
 \|\Pi_tf\|^2
 &=\int_{\mathbb R^n}\mathbf 1_\Omega(t+\theta)\,d\sigma_f(\theta),
 \label{eq:Pi-norm}\\
 \|(\Pi_t-\Pi_s)f\|^2
 &=\int_{\mathbb R^n}|\mathbf 1_\Omega(t+\theta)-\mathbf 1_\Omega(s+\theta)|^2
       \,d\sigma_f(\theta).
 \label{eq:Pi-difference}
\end{align}
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
The function $(t,\theta)\mapsto\mathbf 1_{\partial\Omega}(t+\theta)$ is
Borel measurable, so its integral against $\sigma$ is measurable.
Tonelli's theorem and the assumption $|\partial\Omega|=0$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:good-ae}
 \int_{\mathbb R^n}\sigma(\partial\Omega-t)\,dt
 =\int_{\mathbb R^n}|\partial\Omega-\theta|\,d\sigma(\theta)=0.
\end{equation}
Thus $\mathcal G$ is conull. Fix $t\in\mathcal G$. By
\eqref{eq:control-null}, the boundary exception is null for every
$\sigma_f$. The difference norm formula for the integrable kernels gives
\[
 \|(\Pi_{t,R}-\Pi_{t,S})f\|^2
 =\int|m_R(t+\theta)-m_S(t+\theta)|^2\,d\sigma_f(\theta).
\]
The bounded symbols converge in $L^2(\sigma_f)$ by dominated convergence.
Hence $\Pi_{t,R}f$ is Cauchy and has a limit $\Pi_tf$. Uniform boundedness
makes $\Pi_t$ a bounded linear contraction. Similarly,
\[
 \|(\Pi_{t,R}^2-\Pi_{t,R})f\|^2
 =\int|m_R(t+\theta)^2-m_R(t+\theta)|^2\,d\sigma_f(\theta)
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
By \eqref{eq:strong-products}, $\Pi_{t,R}^2\to\Pi_t^2$ strongly,
so the preceding limit implies $\Pi_t^2=\Pi_t$. Self-adjointness passes
through inner products, so $\Pi_t$ is orthogonal. Passing to limits in the norm identities for
$\Pi_{t,R}$ and $\Pi_{t,R}-\Pi_{s,R}$ proves the asserted formulas.
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:M}
Every $M_t$ is an orthogonal projection. The family is Lipschitz continuous
in operator norm:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M-continuity}
 \|M_t-M_s\|\leq L|t-s|,\qquad
 L=2\pi\int_{\mathbb R^n}|y|\,\|k_y\|_\infty\,dy<\infty.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
For bounded $f,g$, invariance of $\mu$ and the change of variables
$\Gamma'=\Gamma+y$ give
\begin{align*}
 \langle B_yU_yf,g\rangle
 &=\int_X q_\Gamma(0,-y)f(\Gamma+y)\overline{g(\Gamma)}\,d\mu(\Gamma)\\
 &=\int_X q_{\Gamma'}(y,0)f(\Gamma')
                  \overline{g(\Gamma'-y)}\,d\mu(\Gamma')\\
 &=\langle f,B_{-y}U_{-y}g\rangle.
\end{align*}
Density gives $(B_yU_y)^*=B_{-y}U_{-y}$. Taking the adjoint in
\eqref{eq:M} and changing $y$ to $-y$ therefore gives $M_t^*=M_t$.

For idempotence, covariance implies
$k_u(\Gamma+y)=q_\Gamma(-y,-y-u)$. First let $f$ be bounded.
Expanding $M_t^2$ and putting $v=y+u$ yields
\begin{align*}
 (M_t^2f)(\Gamma)
 &=\int e^{2\pi it\cdot v}
       \left[\int q_\Gamma(0,-y)q_\Gamma(-y,-v)\,dy\right]
       f(\Gamma+v)\,dv\\
 &=\int e^{2\pi it\cdot v}q_\Gamma(0,-v)f(\Gamma+v)\,dv
 =(M_tf)(\Gamma),
\end{align*}
where the second equality is \eqref{eq:q-projection}, with the change
of variable $u=-y$. The original double integral has $|y|,|u|\leq2r$
and uniformly bounded coefficients, so Fubini applies. The equality
extends to every $f\in\mathcal H$ by density and boundedness. Hence $M_t^2=M_t$,
and $M_t$ is an orthogonal projection.

Finally $|e^{2\pi it\cdot y}-e^{2\pi is\cdot y}|\leq2\pi|t-s||y|$.
Applying the triangle inequality to the vector integral proves
\eqref{eq:M-continuity}. Finiteness follows from \eqref{eq:q-bounds}.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:distance}
For every $t\in\mathcal G$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationary-distance-bound}
 \|\Pi_t-M_t\|\leq\gamma<1.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Subtracting \eqref{eq:average-Q} from \eqref{eq:average-P} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compression-identity}
 \langle(\Pi_{t,R}-M_{t,R})f,g\rangle
 =\int_X\langle(P-Q_\Gamma)a_f(\Gamma),a_g(\Gamma)\rangle\,d\mu.
\end{equation}
Apply \eqref{eq:hull-distance-bound} for almost every $\Gamma$, followed
by Cauchy--Schwarz in $X$ and \eqref{eq:finite-energy}:
\begin{align*}
 |\langle(\Pi_{t,R}-M_{t,R})f,g\rangle|
 &\leq\gamma\int_X\|a_f(\Gamma)\|_2\|a_g(\Gamma)\|_2\,d\mu\\
 &\leq\gamma\|f\|\|g\|.
\end{align*}
Thus $\|\Pi_{t,R}-M_{t,R}\|\leq\gamma$ for every $t$ and $R$.
By \eqref{eq:q-bounds} and dominated convergence,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M-norm-limit}
 \|M_{t,R}-M_t\|
 \leq\int_{|y|\leq2r}|1-w_R(y)|\,\|k_y\|_\infty\,dy
 \longrightarrow0,
\end{equation}
uniformly in $t$. For $t\in\mathcal G$, Proposition~\ref{prop:cutoffs}
gives strong convergence $\Pi_{t,R}\to\Pi_t$. Testing the distance
bound on each vector and taking the limit proves
\eqref{eq:stationary-distance-bound}.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:sphere}
For every nonzero $\theta\in\mathbb R^n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sphere-intersection}
 \nu\bigl(S\cap(S-\theta)\bigr)=0.
\end{equation}
Moreover, $S$ has Lebesgue measure zero.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
If $x\in S\cap(S-\theta)$, then $|x-a|^2=\rho^2$ and
$|x+\theta-a|^2=\rho^2$. Subtraction gives
\[
 2(x-a)\cdot\theta=-|\theta|^2.
\]
For $\theta\ne0$, this is a hyperplane. Its intersection with the sphere
is empty, a point, or an $(n-2)$-dimensional sphere, hence has zero
$\mathcal H^{n-1}$-measure. Finally a sphere in $\mathbb R^n$ has zero
$n$-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:stationary-space}
The invariant probability measure supplies a nonzero constant vector
with spectral measure $\delta_0$. The ordinary translation representation
$(V_ya)(v)=a(v-y)$ on $L^2(\mathbb R^n)$ has no nonzero invariant vector.
With our Fourier convention, its scalar spectral measures are
$|\mathcal F a(\theta)|^2\,d\theta$, so changing a cutoff at the single point
zero has no effect on that space. On $\mathcal H$, the same change gives
$R_-\mathbf 1=0$ and $R_+\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1$. The averaging argument brings both this
invariant vector and the original distance bound into the same stationary
space.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:interval}
A sphere has no overlap of positive surface measure with a
nontrivial translate. This fails for the one-dimensional interval:
a translation can send one endpoint to the other. It also fails for
opposite sides of a rectangle. A boundary crossing can then exchange
spectral components, and the contradiction from strict inclusion does not follow.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:general-boundary}
Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^n$ be a nonempty bounded open set with
$|\partial\Omega|=0$. Suppose its boundary carries a finite nonzero
positive Borel measure $\nu$ such that
\[
 \nu\bigl(\partial\Omega\cap(\partial\Omega-\theta)\bigr)=0
 \qquad(\theta\ne0).
\]
Assume also that, for $\nu$-almost every $t\in\partial\Omega$, every
neighborhood of $t$ meets both $\Omega$ and
$\mathbb R^n\setminus\overline\Omega$ in sets of positive Lebesgue measure.
Then $L^2(\Omega)$ admits no Riesz basis of exponentials.
Indeed, Sections~\ref{sec:bumps}--\ref{sec:distance} apply under these
hypotheses. Tonelli's theorem, applied with $\nu$, supplies a point satisfying both
the spectral boundary-null condition and the stated two-sided
neighborhood condition. The latter provides the two sequences of good
parameters. Their symbols
have exactly the limits \eqref{eq:mminus}--\eqref{eq:mplus}, and the same
arguments using strong limits and norm continuity give a contradiction.
In particular one may use surface measure whenever it is finite and
nonzero and satisfies the displayed overlap condition and the two-sided neighborhood condition.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:triangle}
No nondegenerate triangle in $\mathbb R^2$ admits a Riesz basis of exponentials.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
An invertible affine change of variables preserves the Riesz basis
property. Indeed, the pullback under $x=a+Ay$ sends
$e_\lambda$ to $e^{2\pi i\lambda\cdot a}e_{A^{\mathsf T}\lambda}$
and multiplies the $L^2$ norm by $|\det A|^{-1/2}$. The phase factors
have modulus one, and the frequency map is invertible. Thus we may assume
\[
 \Omega=\{x_1>0,\ x_2>0,\ x_1+x_2<1\}.
\]
Suppose it has an exponential Riesz basis. Since $\Omega$ is bounded
and its boundary has area zero, Sections~\ref{sec:bumps}--\ref{sec:distance}
give the control measure $\sigma$, the conull set $\mathcal G$,
and the projections with \eqref{eq:stationary-distance-bound}.

\par\smallskip\noindent\leavevmodeWe first choose a point in the interior of the horizontal edge such
that the other two edges carry no translated spectral mass. Their
nonparallel supporting lines make the required Tonelli argument possible.\par\smallskip


Let $L=\{(a,0):0<a<1\}$ and $F=\partial\Omega\setminus L$.
The set $F$ consists of the two other closed edges, including all
vertices. For $\nu=\mathcal H^1|_L$ and every $\theta\in\mathbb R^2$,
\[
 \nu\bigl(L\cap(F-\theta)\bigr)=0,
\]
because a horizontal segment meets a translated vertical or diagonal
supporting line in at most one point. Tonelli's theorem therefore gives
\[
 \int_L\sigma(F-t)\,d\nu(t)
 =\int_{\mathbb R^2}\nu\bigl(L\cap(F-\theta)\bigr)\,d\sigma(\theta)=0.
\]
Choose $t_0\in L$ with $\sigma(F-t_0)=0$. This set is null for every
$\sigma_f$ by \eqref{eq:control-null}.

\par\smallskip\noindent\leavevmodeOnce the other edges have been removed as spectral null sets, the
sign of one normal coordinate controls every surviving boundary point.
Good parameters approaching from opposite sides therefore have ordered
limiting symbols.\par\smallskip


Near $t_0$, the triangle coincides with the half-plane $x_2>0$.
Choose good parameters $t_j^+\in\mathcal G\cap\Omega$ and
$t_j^-\in\mathcal G\cap(\mathbb R^2\setminus\overline\Omega)$ tending to
$t_0$ inside such a neighborhood. Their second coordinates are positive
and negative, respectively. For each point $t_0+\theta\in L$, membership
in the triangle is likewise determined locally by positivity of the second
coordinate. Therefore
$\mathbf 1_\Omega(t_j^++\theta)\to1$ and
$\mathbf 1_\Omega(t_j^-+\theta)\to0$ on $L-t_0$.
Away from the boundary both symbols converge to
$\mathbf 1_\Omega(t_0+\theta)$. All other boundary points belong to the
common null set $F-t_0$.

The limiting symbols are consequently
\[
 m_-=\mathbf 1_{\Omega-t_0},\qquad
 m_+=\mathbf 1_{\Omega-t_0}+\mathbf 1_{L-t_0}.
\]
They are indicators of nested sets.


\par\smallskip\noindent\leavevmodeThe remaining steps are the same as for the ball: the difference
formula gives strong limits, the constant vector makes their ranges
strictly nested, and norm continuity supplies a common nearby projection.\par\smallskip


Formula~\eqref{eq:Pi-difference}
and dominated convergence make $(\Pi_{t_j^\pm}f)_j$ Cauchy for every
$f$. As in Section~\ref{sec:sphere}, the resulting strong limits
$R_-,R_+$ are orthogonal projections with
\[
 \|R_\pm f\|^2=\int m_\pm\,d\sigma_f.
\]
Thus $\|R_-f\|\leq\|R_+f\|$ for every $f$, which gives
$\operatorname{Ran} R_-\subset\operatorname{Ran} R_+$ by taking kernels and orthogonal complements.
Since $0\in L-t_0$ and $\sigma_{\mathbf 1}=\delta_0$, we have
$R_-\mathbf 1=0$ and $R_+\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1$; the inclusion is strict.
Finally $M_{t_j^\pm}\to M_{t_0}$ in norm, so
\eqref{eq:stationary-distance-bound} gives
$\|R_\pm-M_{t_0}\|\leq\gamma<1$, contradicting
Lemma~\ref{lem:inclusion}.

In this case, $\|(R_+-R_-)f\|^2=\sigma_f(L-t_0)$. The jump may
contain nonzero tangential frequencies, but the constant vector still
guarantees that the jump is nonzero.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rem:odd-polygons}
The same proof excludes an exponential Riesz basis on the interior of a
convex polygon having an edge with no parallel partner. Here edges are
maximal boundary segments. In particular the conclusion holds for
every convex polygon with an odd number of sides: a bounded convex polygon
has at most two maximal edges in each unoriented direction, so an odd
number of sides cannot be partitioned into parallel pairs.

To verify the extension, let $L$ be the relative interior of an unpaired
edge and put $F=\partial\Omega\setminus L$. Every other edge is
nonparallel to $L$, so for $\nu=\mathcal H^1|_L$,
\[
 \nu\bigl(L\cap(F-\theta)\bigr)=0\qquad(\theta\in\mathbb R^2).
\]
The intersections with the translated supporting lines are finite;
the two endpoints contribute only finitely many further points.
Tonelli's theorem gives $t_0\in L$ with $\sigma(F-t_0)=0$.
Fix the inward unit normal to $L$. At each point of $L$, membership in
$\Omega$ is locally determined by positive displacement
in this normal direction. Choose good parameters approaching $t_0$
from the two local half-planes. For every $t_0+\theta\in L$, the same
normal sign determines their eventual membership. The symbols are
therefore the same $m_-,m_+$ as in the triangle proof, with this edge $L$.
Their limiting projections satisfy $\operatorname{Ran} R_-\subsetneq\operatorname{Ran} R_+$
because $0\in L-t_0$.
Norm continuity of $M_t$ again supplies the common comparison projection
$M_{t_0}$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:inclusion} gives the contradiction.

\label{cor:convex}
Let $n\geq2$ and let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^n$ be a nonempty bounded convex
open set with $C^2$ boundary. Then $L^2(\Omega)$ admits no Riesz basis of
exponentials.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:convex-patch}
Under the hypotheses of Corollary~\ref{cor:convex}, put
$K=\overline\Omega$ and $S=\partial\Omega$. There is a nonempty relatively
open patch $U\subset S$ on which the second fundamental form is positive
definite, with the convention that convex principal curvatures are
nonnegative. Moreover, if $N(x)$ denotes the outward unit normal, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convex-support}
 K\cap\{z:N(x)\cdot(z-x)=0\}=\{x\}
 \qquad(x\in U).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Choose $c\in\Omega$ and let
\[
 R=\max_{z\in K}|z-c|>0.
\]
Then $K\subset\overline B(c,R)$, with contact at some $p\in S$.
The tangent hyperplane to the ball at $p$ supports $K$ and, since $S$
is $C^2$, is also the tangent hyperplane to $S$. After a translation
and rotation, we may assume that
\[
 p=0,\qquad c=-Re_n,\qquad N(p)=e_n.
\]
Locally write $S$ as $x_n=f(x')$, with $f\in C^2$,
$f(0)=0$ and $Df(0)=0$. Containment in the ball gives
\[
 f(x')\leq -R+\sqrt{R^2-|x'|^2},
\]
with equality at $x'=0$. Taking second derivatives at this contact point
in the sense of quadratic forms yields
\[
 D^2f(0)\leq-\frac1R I.
\]
Thus the second fundamental form at $p$, represented in these coordinates
by $-D^2f(0)$, is positive definite. By continuity it remains positive
definite on a sufficiently small relatively open patch $U$.

To prove \eqref{eq:convex-support}, fix $x\in U$ and suppose that another
point $z\in K$ lies in the supporting hyperplane at $x$. Convexity places
the segment $[x,z]$ in $K$ and in that hyperplane, hence in $S$.
In coordinates centered at $x$ with horizontal tangent hyperplane, the
boundary is the graph of a function $g$ with $g(0)=0$, $Dg(0)=0$ and
$D^2g(0)$ negative definite. The segment would give
$g(tv)=0$ for a nonzero tangent vector $v$ and all sufficiently small
$t>0$, contradicting
\[
 g(tv)=\frac{t^2}{2}\,D^2g(0)[v,v]+o(t^2)<0.
\]
This proves the singleton supporting-face property.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:convex-translates}
Let $U$ be the patch in Lemma~\ref{lem:convex-patch}. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convex-intersection}
 \mathcal H^{n-1}\bigl(U\cap(S-\theta)\bigr)=0
 \qquad(\theta\in\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}).
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
Fix $\theta\ne0$. At any $x\in U\cap(S-\theta)$, put $y=x+\theta\in S$.
The normals to $S$ and $S-\theta$ at $x$ are $N(x)$ and $N(y)$,
respectively.

If these normals are not parallel, the implicit function theorem makes
the intersection locally a $C^1$ submanifold of dimension $n-2$.
The set of all such transverse intersection points is covered by
countably many of these neighborhoods, since $\mathbb R^n$ is second countable.
It therefore has zero $\mathcal H^{n-1}$-measure.

The equality $N(y)=N(x)$ is impossible. Indeed, $x$ and $y$ would
maximize the same linear functional on $K$ and so lie in the same
supporting hyperplane, contrary to \eqref{eq:convex-support} and $x\ne y$.

Finally, suppose $N(y)=-N(x)$ and write $v=N(x)$. The supporting
inequalities at $x$ and $y$ give
\[
 K\subset\{z:v\cdot(z-x)\leq0\},\qquad
 K-\theta\subset\{z:v\cdot(z-x)\geq0\}.
\]
Consequently, \eqref{eq:convex-support} implies
\[
 K\cap(K-\theta)
 \subset K\cap\{z:v\cdot(z-x)=0\}
 =\{x\}.
\]
Since $x$ belongs to both boundaries, $S\cap(S-\theta)=\{x\}$ in this
case. A singleton has zero $\mathcal H^{n-1}$-measure because $n\geq2$.
If this case does not occur, every intersection point in $U$ is
transverse. This proves \eqref{eq:convex-intersection}.
<!-- end proof-12 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
The compact $C^2$ hypersurface $S$ has zero $n$-dimensional Lebesgue
measure and finite $(n-1)$-dimensional surface measure. Since $U$ is a
nonempty relatively open patch, the positive Borel measure
\[
 \nu=\mathcal H^{n-1}|_U
\]
is finite and nonzero. Lemma~\ref{lem:convex-translates} gives
\[
 \nu\bigl(S\cap(S-\theta)\bigr)
 =\mathcal H^{n-1}\bigl(U\cap(S-\theta)\bigr)=0
 \qquad(\theta\ne0).
\]
Every neighborhood of a point $t\in S$ meets $\Omega$ in a nonempty
open set, because $t$ is a boundary point. It also contains a nonempty
open subset of the strict exterior: the supporting inequality
$N(t)\cdot(z-t)\leq0$ holds throughout $K$, so points sufficiently close
to $t$ on the other side of this hyperplane lie outside $K$.
Both intersections therefore have positive Lebesgue measure.
All the hypotheses of Remark~\ref{rem:general-boundary} hold, and its
conclusion proves the corollary.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{cm:thm:main}
Let $n\geq1$ and let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^n$ be nonempty, bounded,
open, and convex. There is a locally finite sequence $\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^n$ such that
$\{e_\lambda:\lambda\in\Lambda\}$ is complete and minimal in
$L^2(\Omega)$. Its biorthogonal functions are supported in
the John ellipsoid of $\overline\Omega$.

\label{cm:lem:ellipse}
After an invertible affine change of variables, $\overline B$ is the

 B\subset\Omega,
 \qquad

\end{equation}

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{cm:lem:membership}
The spaces $V_N$ are contained in $PW_B\subset PW_\Omega$.
They are nested, and
\[
 \dim V_N=\dim\mathcal P_{2N}=\binom{2N+n}{n}.
\]
The function $\kappa/Q_N$ is nonzero at every point of
$\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{j\leq N}S_j$, and every member of $V_N$
vanishes on $S_j$ for $j>N$.
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
For a positive zero $a$ of $J_{\nu+1}$ define
\[
 \phi_a(x)=\frac1{v_n a^2}
       \left(\frac{|x|^{-\nu}J_\nu(a|x|)}{J_\nu(a)}-1\right)\mathbf 1_B(x).
\]
The value at the origin is defined by continuity; the interior
expression is smooth there by its power series in $|x|^2$.
Both its value and its normal derivative vanish on $\partial B$, since
\[
 \frac{d}{dr}\bigl(r^{-\nu}J_\nu(ar)\bigr)
       =-a r^{-\nu}J_{\nu+1}(ar).
\]
The Bessel equation therefore gives, including the zero extension,
\[
 (-\Delta-a^2)\phi_a=\mathbf 1_B/v_n.
\]
There are no boundary distributions in this equation. Fourier
transformation shows that
$\widehat{\phi_a}=\kappa/(q-a^2)$, with removable singularities.
Partial fractions in $q$ now show that $\kappa/Q_N$ has a
compactly supported inverse transform in $\overline B$.

The real-axis Bessel asymptotic gives
\begin{equation}\label{cm:eq:decay}
 \kappa(\xi)/Q_N(\xi)=O(|\xi|^{-2N-\beta})
                  \quad(|\xi|\longrightarrow\infty).
\end{equation}
Thus $P\kappa/Q_N\in L^2(\mathbb R^n)$ for $\deg P\leq2N$:
the radial majorant for its squared norm at infinity is
$r^{n-1-2\beta}=r^{-2}$.
Its inverse distribution is obtained by differentiating a
distribution supported in $\overline B$, hence has the same support
inclusion. Plancherel makes it an $L^2$ function supported there.
Since $\partial B$ has Lebesgue measure zero, this proves membership
in $PW_B$, including $N=0$ directly.

Nesting follows by multiplying the numerator by $q-a_{N+1}^2$.
Multiplication by $\kappa/Q_N$, nonzero on an open set, is injective
on polynomials. The dimension and the remaining assertions follow
from the simple zeros and \eqref{cm:eq:product}.

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{cm:lem:quadric}
Let $r>0$. If an entire function $F$ on $\mathbb C^n$ vanishes on
$\{\xi\in\mathbb R^n:|\xi|=r\}$, then it vanishes on the complex quadric
\[
 \mathcal Q_r=\left\{z\in\mathbb C^n:\sum_{k=1}^n z_k^2=r^2\right\}.
\]
It is locally divisible by $\sum_{k=1}^n z_k^2-r^2$ at every point
of this quadric.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
For $z=x+iy\in\mathcal Q_r$, with $x,y\in\mathbb R^n$, one has
$x\cdot y=0$ and $|x|^2-|y|^2=r^2$. In particular $x\ne0$.
There is a real two-dimensional linear subspace $E$ containing
$x$ and $y$; if $y=0$, choose any such subspace containing $x$.
In orthonormal coordinates on its complexification, the quadric
section is parametrized by
\[
 z_1=\frac r2(w+w^{-1}),\qquad
 z_2=\frac r{2i}(w-w^{-1}),\qquad w\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}.
\]
This parametrization is onto: its inverse is $w=(z_1+iz_2)/r$,
which is nonzero because $(z_1+iz_2)(z_1-iz_2)=r^2$.
The unit circle in the $w$ plane maps onto the real circle
$E\cap rS^{n-1}$. The one-variable identity theorem therefore
shows that $F$ vanishes on the whole section, in particular at $z$.
Finally, the gradient of the defining polynomial is $2z\ne0$ on
$\mathcal Q_r$. Local holomorphic coordinates make that polynomial
one coordinate; vanishing when this coordinate is zero gives the
asserted local divisibility.

<!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{cm:lem:type}
Suppose $A,D$ are entire on $\mathbb C^n$,
$|A(z)|+|D(z)|\leq C e^{R|z|}$, and $D(0)\ne0$.
If $A/D$ extends to an entire function, that quotient has finite
exponential type.
<!-- end lemma-12 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
Divide both $A$ and $D$ by $D(0)$, adjusting constants, so $D(0)=1$.
For a complex unit vector $u$, restrict the functions to the line
$w\mapsto wu$. Jensen's formula gives
\[
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log|D(re^{it}u)|\,dt\geq0.
\]
Hence the mean of $\log^-|D|$ on that circle is at most the mean
of $\log^+|D|$. Writing $G=A/D$, we obtain, uniformly in $u$,
\[
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log^+|G(re^{it}u)|\,dt
       \leq 2\log^+C+2Rr.
\]
The Poisson inequality for the subharmonic function
$\log|G(wu)|$, using a circle of radius $2|w|$ for $|w|\geq1$,
bounds its value by three times the last mean. This gives
$|G(z)|\leq C' e^{R'|z|}$ for $|z|\geq1$, uniformly in the
direction $u$; the compact remainder is harmless. Radii passing
through zeros are handled by limits.

<!-- lemma-13: body_md -->
\label{cm:lem:division}
If $F\in PW_\Omega$ vanishes on every $S_j$, $j>N$, then
\begin{equation}\label{cm:eq:quotient}
 G=Q_NF/\kappa
\end{equation}
extends to an entire function which is the Fourier transform of a
compactly supported distribution.
<!-- end lemma-13 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
By Lemma~\ref{cm:lem:quadric}, the numerator $Q_NF$ vanishes on all
the complex quadrics $\mathcal Q_r$ for
$r=a_j/(2\pi)$: for $j\leq N$ the corresponding factor is in
$Q_N$, and for $j>N$ use the hypothesis.
They are exactly the zero hypersurfaces of $\kappa$ in
\eqref{cm:eq:product}, each of multiplicity one, and they do not
intersect each other. They form a locally finite collection because
$a_j\longrightarrow\infty$. Local divisibility therefore removes
all singularities of \eqref{cm:eq:quotient}.

The numerator $Q_NF$ and the denominator $\kappa$ have finite
exponential type. Since $\kappa(0)=1$, Lemma~\ref{cm:lem:type}
gives the same property for $G$. We next prove that $G$ is
polynomially bounded on $\mathbb R^n$.

The standard Bessel asymptotics and their differentiated versions
give, for sufficiently large $s$,
\begin{equation}\label{cm:eq:bessellower}
 |k(s)|\geq c(1+s)^{-\beta}
           \min\{1,\operatorname{dist}(s,\{a_j:j\geq1\})\}.
\end{equation}
For completeness, with $c_n>0$ and $\gamma_n=\pi(n+1)/4$,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 k(s)&=c_n s^{-\beta}\bigl(\cos(s-\gamma_n)+O(s^{-1})\bigr),\\
 k'(s)&=c_n s^{-\beta}\bigl(-\sin(s-\gamma_n)+O(s^{-1})\bigr).
 \end{aligned}
\]
The zeros satisfy $a_j=(j+(n-1)/4)\pi+O(j^{-1})$.
Off fixed small neighborhoods of these zeros the cosine is bounded
away from zero. Inside each neighborhood the derivative has constant
sign and magnitude at least $c s^{-\beta}$; integration from the
zero proves \eqref{cm:eq:bessellower}.

For $\theta\in S^{n-1}$, set
\[
 H_\theta(s)=\prod_{j\leq N}(s^2-a_j^2)\,
                       F(s\theta/(2\pi)).
\]
Uniform boundedness of $F$ and its first real derivatives gives
\[
 |H_\theta(s)|+|H_\theta'(s)|\leq C(1+s)^{2N}
                      \qquad(s\geq0),
\]
uniformly in $\theta$. Also $H_\theta(a_j)=0$ for every $j$.
Near a zero, integrate this derivative estimate from that zero;
away from the zeros use the bound on $H_\theta$ itself. Combining
with \eqref{cm:eq:bessellower} proves
\[
 |G(\xi)|\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{2N+\beta}\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb R^n).
\]
The bounded real-frequency region is covered by continuity.
The stated Paley--Wiener--Schwartz theorem now gives a compactly
supported distribution with Fourier transform $G$.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{cm:prop:tail}
For a domain satisfying \eqref{cm:eq:rigidity},
\begin{equation}\label{cm:eq:tail}
 \{F\in PW_\Omega:F|_{S_j}=0\text{ for all }j>N\}=V_N.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Let $F=\widehat f$ belong to the left side, and suppose $F\ne0$.
By Lemma~\ref{cm:lem:division}, $G=Q_NF/\kappa=\widehat g$ for
a nonzero compactly supported distribution $g$. Put $u=\mathbf 1_B/v_n$.
The product identity $\kappa G=Q_NF$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{cm:eq:convolution}
 u*g=\prod_{j\leq N}(-\Delta-a_j^2)f.
\end{equation}
The right side is supported in $\overline\Omega$.
The convolution support theorem \eqref{cm:eq:titchmarsh} implies
\[
 \overline B+\operatorname{conv}\operatorname{supp} g
      =\operatorname{conv}\operatorname{supp}(u*g)\subset\overline\Omega.
\]
Condition \eqref{cm:eq:rigidity} forces $\operatorname{supp} g\subset\{0\}$.
A distribution supported at one point is a finite linear combination
of derivatives of its Dirac measure. Hence $G=P$ is a polynomial,
and
\[
 F=P\kappa/Q_N.
\]

We check the degree rather than assuming it from support. If
$m=\deg P$, its leading homogeneous part has absolute value bounded
below on some open patch of $S^{n-1}$. Uniformly on a smaller patch,
$|P(r\theta)|\geq c r^m$ for large $r$.
On real radial intervals of fixed positive length repeated at
bounded spacing, the Bessel asymptotic gives
$|\kappa(r\theta)|\geq c r^{-\beta}$.
Also $|Q_N(r\theta)|\leq C r^{2N}$ for large $r$.
Thus the polar integral of $|F|^2$ on this patch and these radial
intervals is bounded below by a constant times the integral of
\[
 r^{2m-4N-2\beta+n-1}=r^{2m-4N-2}.
\]
If $m\geq2N+1$, this diverges. Since $F\in L^2(\mathbb R^n)$, it
follows that $m\leq2N$. In particular, the degree bound is the
same in every dimension.
Therefore $F\in V_N$. The zero function is immediate, and the
reverse inclusion is Lemma~\ref{cm:lem:membership}.

<!-- lemma-15: body_md -->
\label{cm:lem:extension}
Let $A$ be an interpolation set at level $m$, and let $C$ be a finite
set of distinct points on spheres with index greater than $m$.
There are $N>m$ and an interpolation set $A'$ at level $N$ such that
\[
 A\cup C\subset A',\qquad
 A'\setminus A\subset\bigcup_{m<j\leq N}S_j.
\]
<!-- end lemma-15 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
Choose $N>m$ containing all sphere indices occurring in $C$ and
satisfying $2N\geq|A\cup C|-1$. Evaluations at these distinct
points are independent on $\mathcal P_{2N}$: for each $\lambda\in A\cup C$,
the polynomial
\[
 \prod_{\substack{\mu\in A\cup C\\\mu\ne\lambda}}
 \frac{(\xi-\mu)\cdot(\lambda-\mu)}{|\lambda-\mu|^2}
\]
has the required Kronecker values and degree $|A\cup C|-1$.

Evaluations on $A\cup\bigcup_{m<j\leq N}S_j$ span the full dual
of $\mathcal P_{2N}$. Indeed, if $P$ vanishes there, successive division
by the sphere equations gives
\[
 P=\prod_{j=m+1}^N(q-a_j^2)R,\qquad\deg R\leq2m.
\]
To justify each division, write $\xi=(\xi',\xi_n)$ and divide in
$\xi_n$ by $|\xi|^2-r^2$. The remainder is
$R_0(\xi')+\xi_nR_1(\xi')$. Its values at
$\xi_n=\pm\sqrt{r^2-|\xi'|^2}$, for $|\xi'|<r$, force both
coefficient polynomials to vanish on an open subset of $\mathbb R^{n-1}$,
hence identically. Distinct sphere factors do not vanish on each other, so division
can be repeated. The displayed product is nonzero on $A$, where
$R$ must therefore vanish. Interpolation at level $m$ gives $R=0$.

Extend the independent evaluations on $A\cup C$ to a basis of this
finite-dimensional dual using points of the indicated spheres.
The resulting points constitute $A'$.
<!-- end proof-20 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
Work first in the normalization of Lemma~\ref{cm:lem:ellipse}.
Choose a countable dense set $\{f_1,f_2,\ldots\}$ in $L^2(\Omega)$.
Construct strictly increasing integers $N_s$ and nested finite sets
$A_s$, starting from $N_0=0$, $A_0=\{0\}$, so that $A_s$
interpolates at level $N_s$, all new points have sphere index greater
than $N_{s-1}$, and
\begin{equation}\label{cm:eq:approx}
 \operatorname{dist}\bigl(f_\ell,\operatorname{span}\{e_\lambda:\lambda\in A_s\}\bigr)
                   <2^{-s}\qquad(1\leq\ell\leq s).
\end{equation}
Given the previous stage, Lemma~\ref{cm:lem:available} supplies a
finite set $C$ of frequencies on later spheres which, together with
$A_{s-1}$, already achieves these finitely many approximations.
Lemma~\ref{cm:lem:extension} then supplies $A_s$ and $N_s$.
Enlargement of the finite span preserves the approximations.

Set $\Lambda=\bigcup_s A_s$. Condition \eqref{cm:eq:approx} proves
completeness. The set is locally finite: the sphere radii tend to
infinity, and spheres with indices $N_{s-1}<j\leq N_s$ are used
only at stage $s$, when finitely many points are selected.

For each $\lambda\in\Lambda$, use the first stage $s$ with
$\lambda\in A_s$. Interpolation by $V_{N_s}$ gives
$F_\lambda\in V_{N_s}$ with
\[
 F_\lambda(\mu)=\delta_{\lambda\mu}\qquad(\mu\in A_s).
\]
Every later frequency lies on a sphere of index greater than $N_s$,
where $F_\lambda$ vanishes identically. Consequently these Kronecker
values hold on all of $\Lambda$. Lemma~\ref{cm:lem:membership}
identifies $F_\lambda$ with the Fourier transform of an $L^2$
function $g_\lambda$ supported in $\overline B$. Extending by zero
inside $\Omega$ gives
\[
 \langle g_\lambda,e_\mu\rangle_{L^2(\Omega)}
                        =\delta_{\lambda\mu},
\]
proving minimality and the asserted support of the biorthogonals.

Finally, if the original domain is $b+A\Omega$ with $A$ invertible,
transport the frequencies to $A^{-\mathsf T}\Lambda$.
The unitary map $f\mapsto |\det A|^{1/2}f(b+A\,\cdot)$ sends
each transported exponential to a nonzero scalar multiple of the
original one. This preserves completeness and minimality, carries
the supports of the biorthogonals into the John ellipsoid of the original domain,
and preserves local finiteness.

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
\label{cm:rem:scope}
The same selected system is complete and minimal on every measurable
set between the inscribed open ellipsoid and $\Omega$, up to null
sets: completeness passes to restrictions by zero extension, and
the biorthogonals supported in the ellipsoid still work. If the ellipsoid
is a proper subset of the convex domain, these biorthogonals cannot
be complete in $L^2(\Omega)$. Completeness of the dual family is not
part of ordinary minimality.
