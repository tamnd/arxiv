<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:www}
Let $F$ be standardised, let $R \in P_d$ have rank $k$, and let $A$ be a rank
decomposition of $R$. Then $R \in S_d^F$ if and only if there is a random vector $\mathbf{V}$
in $\mathbb{R}^k$ with $\mathbb{E} \mathbf{V} = 0$, $\operatorname{Cov} \mathbf{V} = I_k$ and $\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle\sim F$ for every
$j = 1,\dots,d$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Suppose first that such a $\mathbf{V}$ exists, and let $\mathbf{X} = A\mathbf{V}$, the random vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$
with coordinates $X_j = \langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle$. By assumption, each $X_j$ has law $F$, so $\mathbf{X}$ has
all its margins equal to $F$; in particular, each $X_j$ has mean $0$ and variance $1$,
so the correlation matrix of $\mathbf{X}$ is simply $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}]$. Since $\mathbb{E} \mathbf{V} = 0$, we have
$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\!\top}] = \operatorname{Cov} \mathbf{V} = I_k$ (the $k \times k$ identity matrix), and therefore
$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}] = A \, \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\!\top}]A^{\!\top} = AA^{\!\top} = R$. Thus $R \in S_d^F$.

Conversely, suppose $R \in S_d^F$, and let $\mathbf{Y}$ be a random vector with margins $F$ and
correlation matrix $R$. Since $F$ is standardised, $\mathbb{E} \mathbf{Y} = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\!\top}] = R$.
The matrix $A$ has full column rank, so $A^{\!\top}A$ is invertible, and
$A^{+} = (A^{\!\top}A)^{-1}A^{\!\top}$ (the Moore--Penrose inverse of $A$) satisfies
$A^{+}A = I_k$. The matrix $P = AA^{+}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbb{R}^d$ onto the
column space of $A$: it is symmetric, $P^2 = P$, and $PA = A$. Set $\mathbf{V} = A^{+}\mathbf{Y}$. Then
$\mathbb{E} \mathbf{V} = 0$ and
\[
  \operatorname{Cov} \mathbf{V}\; = \; A^{+}R \, (A^{+})^{\!\top} \; = \; (A^{+}A)(A^{+}A)^{\!\top} \; = \; I_k .
\]
Moreover $\mathbf{Y}-A\mathbf{V} = (I-P)\mathbf{Y}$, and $(I-P)^{\!\top}(I-P) = I-P$, so
\[
  \mathbb{E}\bigl|\mathbf{Y}-A\mathbf{V}\bigr|^2 \; = \; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{Y}^{\!\top}(I-P)\mathbf{Y}\bigr] \; = \; \operatorname{tr}\bigl((I-P)R\bigr)
   \; = \; \operatorname{tr}\bigl(((I-P)A)A^{\!\top}\bigr) \; = \; 0 ,
\]
because $(I-P)A = 0$. Hence $\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{V}$ a.s., and $\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle = Y_j$ has law $F$ for
every $j$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:pad}
Let $F$ be standardised. If $M \in P_m\setminus S_m^F$ and $d\ge m$, then
$M\oplus I_{d-m} \in P_d\setminus S_d^F$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The matrix $M\oplus I_{d-m}$ lies in $P_d$. If it were the correlation matrix of some
$\mathbf{X}$ with margins $F$, then $(X_1,\dots,X_m)$ would have margins $F$ and correlation
matrix $M$, which would contradict $M\notin S_m^F$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:11}
$S_d\neq P_d$ for every $d\ge11$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Write $\mathbf{b}_1,\dots,\mathbf{b}_{11}$ for the coefficient vectors of the eleven linear forms in
\eqref{eq:reznick}, with multiplicities $c_1 = 6$ and $c_2 = \dots = c_{11} = 1$. Put
$\mathbf{a}_j = \mathbf{b}_j/|\mathbf{b}_j|$. The first vector has $|\mathbf{b}_1|^2 = 4$ and the rest have
$|\mathbf{b}_j|^2 = 12$, so \eqref{eq:reznick} reads
$192|\mathbf{x}|^4 = \sum_jc_j|\mathbf{b}_j|^4\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4$, with
\[
  \sum_{j = 1}^{11}c_j|\mathbf{b}_j|^4 \; = \; 6\cdot4^2+10\cdot12^2 \; = \; 1536 .
\]
Dividing by $1536$ gives us
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cub11}
  \sum_{j = 1}^{11}w_j \, \langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4 \; = \; \tfrac18|\mathbf{x}|^4\qquad(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^4),
  \qquad w_1 = \tfrac1{16},\quad w_2 = \dots = w_{11} = \tfrac3{32},
\end{equation}
so that $\sum_jw_j = \tfrac1{16}+10\cdot\tfrac3{32} = 1$.

Let $A$ be the $11\times4$ matrix with rows $\mathbf{a}_1^{\!\top},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{11}^{\!\top}$, and
let $M = AA^{\!\top}$, the matrix with entries $M_{ij} = \langle \mathbf{a}_i,\mathbf{a}_j\rangle$. Then $M$ is
symmetric and positive semidefinite with unit diagonal, so $M \in P_{11}$. The $\mathbf{a}_j$
must span $\mathbb{R}^4$ (otherwise they would all be orthogonal to some $\mathbf{b}\neq 0$, and
\eqref{eq:reznick} at $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ would give $0 = 192|\mathbf{b}|^4$). Hence $A$ has rank $4$, so does
$M = AA^{\!\top}$, and $A$ is a rank decomposition of $M$.

Suppose $M \in S_{11}$, and let $\mathbf{V}$ be as in Lemma~\ref{lem:www}, so that
$\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle\sim u$ for every $j$. Substituting $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{V}$ in \eqref{eq:cub11} and taking
expectations, using $\mathbb{E}[\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle^4] = \mathbb{E}[u^4] = 9/5$,
\[
  \tfrac18 \, \mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^4 \; = \; \sum_{j = 1}^{11}w_j \, \mathbb{E}\bigl[\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle^4\bigr]
   \; = \; \tfrac95\sum_{j = 1}^{11}w_j \; = \; \tfrac95 ,
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad \mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^4 = \tfrac{72}{5} = 14.4 .
\]
But $\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^2 = \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov} \mathbf{V} = 4$, so Jensen's inequality gives
$\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^4\ge(\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^2)^2 = 16$, a contradiction. Hence
$M \in P_{11}\setminus S_{11}$. We extend this to all $d \ge 11$ using Lemma~\ref{lem:pad}.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:sharp}
Eleven is the least number of terms for which an identity like
\eqref{eq:cub11} can hold (with nonnegative weights). Such an identity with $n$ terms is exactly a representation of $|\mathbf{x}|^4$ as a
sum of $n$ fourth powers of real linear forms on $\mathbb{R}^4$. By
\cite[Prop.~9.26]{Reznick1992}, the least such $n$ is $\binom52+1 = 11$; the borderline
case $n = \binom52 = 10$ is excluded, per \cite[Prop.~9.2(ii)]{Reznick1992}. Such a representation would force the ten lines $\mathbb{R} \mathbf{a}_j$ to be equiangular, that is, to meet pairwise at one common angle, but $\mathbb{R}^4$ contains at most six equiangular lines \cite{LS1973}. Therefore, a new argument is needed for dimension 10, like the one Wang and Zhang \cite{WZ2026} provided.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:arcsine}
For the standardised arcsine law $F$,
\[
  S_d^F = P_d\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad d\le5 .
\]
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
($\Leftarrow$) This is a result of Devroye and Letac \cite[\S4]{DL2015}: for every
$k\ge\tfrac12$ and every $d\le5$, each $d\times d$ correlation matrix is the correlation
matrix of a random vector whose margins are all $\mathrm{Beta}(k,k)$. The arcsine law is
the case $k = \tfrac12$. We can standardise using the map map $t\mapsto\sqrt2 \, (2t-1)$ and change no correlation (since it is affine). Then $P_d\subseteq S_d^F$ for $d\le5$. Since the opposite
inclusion $S_d^F\subseteq P_d$ holds for any $F$,  $S_d^F = P_d$.

($\Rightarrow$) We show that $S_6^F\neq P_6$; by Lemma~\ref{lem:pad} it follows that
$S_d^F\neq P_d$ for every $d\ge6$. Let
$ \varphi = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$, so that $ \varphi^2 = \varphi+1$. Consider the six
vectors
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{u}_1 & = (0,1, \varphi), & \mathbf{u}_3 & = (\varphi,0,1), & \mathbf{u}_5 & = (1, \varphi,0),\\
  \mathbf{u}_2 & = (0,-1, \varphi), & \mathbf{u}_4 & = (\varphi,0,-1), & \mathbf{u}_6 & = (-1, \varphi,0),
\end{aligned}
\]
that is, one vertex from each pair of opposite vertices of a regular icosahedron. Using
$(p+q)^4+(p-q)^4 = 2 \, (p^4+6p^2q^2+q^4)$ on the pairs $\{\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2\}$,
$\{\mathbf{u}_3,\mathbf{u}_4\}$ and $\{\mathbf{u}_5,\mathbf{u}_6\}$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \langle \mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4+\langle \mathbf{u}_2,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4& = 2 \, (\varphi^4x_3^4+6 \varphi^2 x_2^2 x_3^2+x_2^4),\\
  \langle \mathbf{u}_3,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4+\langle \mathbf{u}_4,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4& = 2 \, (\varphi^4x_1^4+6 \varphi^2 x_1^2 x_3^2 +x_3^4),\\
  \langle \mathbf{u}_5,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4+\langle \mathbf{u}_6,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4& = 2 \, (\varphi^4x_2^4+6 \varphi^2 x_1^2 x_2^2 +x_1^4).
\end{aligned}
\]
Summing up, the coefficient of each $x_i^4$ is $2(1 +  \varphi^4)$, and that of each
$x_i^2 x_j^2 $ is $12 \varphi^2 $. Since $\varphi^4 = (\varphi + 1)^2 = 3 \varphi+2$, we find the sum to be $6 \varphi^2 \bigl(\sum_ix_i^4 + 2\sum_{i<j}
x_i^2 x_j^2 \bigr) = 6 \varphi^2 |\mathbf{x}|^4$. Each $\mathbf{u}_j$ has $|\mathbf{u}_j|^2 = 1 + \varphi^2 = \varphi+2$, and
$(\varphi+2)^2 = 5 \varphi^2 $. Therefore, the unit vectors $\mathbf{a}_j = \mathbf{u}_j/|\mathbf{u}_j|$
satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:icos}
  \sum_{j = 1}^{6}\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{x}\rangle^4 \; = \; \frac{6 \varphi^2 }{(\varphi + 2)^2 } \, |\mathbf{x}|^4
   \; = \; \tfrac65 \, |\mathbf{x}|^4\qquad(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3).
\end{equation}

Let $A$ be the $6\times3$ matrix with rows $\mathbf{a}_1^{\!\top},\dots,\mathbf{a}_6^{\!\top}$, and let
$M = AA^{\!\top}$, the matrix with entries $M_{ij} = \langle \mathbf{a}_i,\mathbf{a}_j\rangle$. Then $M$ is symmetric, positive semidefinite, and has unit diagonal, so $M \in P_6$. The $\mathbf{a}_j$ must span $\mathbb{R}^3$ (otherwise they would all be orthogonal to some $\mathbf{b}\neq 0$, and \eqref{eq:icos} at $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$
would give $0 = \tfrac65|\mathbf{b}|^4$). So $A$ has rank $3$, as does $M = AA^{\!\top}$, meaning that $A$
is a rank decomposition of $M$.

Suppose now that $M \in S_6^F$. Let $\mathbf{V}$ be as in Lemma~\ref{lem:www}, so that
$\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle\sim F$ for every $j$. Substituting $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{V}$ in \eqref{eq:icos} and taking
expectations, using $\mathbb{E}[\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle^4] = \mathbb{E}[F^4] = \tfrac32$,
\[
  \tfrac65 \, \mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^4 \; = \; \sum_{j = 1}^{6}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\langle \mathbf{a}_j,\mathbf{V}\rangle^4\bigr] \; = \; 6\cdot\tfrac32 \; = \; 9,
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad \mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^4 = \tfrac{15}{2} = 7.5 .
\]
But $\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^2 = \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov} \mathbf{V} = 3$, so Jensen's inequality gives $\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^4\ge(\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{V}|^2)^2 = 9$, a
contradiction. Hence $M \in P_6 \setminus S_6^F$.
