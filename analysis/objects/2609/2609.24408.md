{\it If               
$p$ is not an integer and if $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}$ are the non-vanishing zeros of the Bessel functions $J_{\pm p}(x)$ such that
$J_p(\alpha) = J_{-p}(\beta)=0$, then one  has the identity}
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- head-2: body_md -->
Since $p\not\in\mathbb{Z}$, the zeros $\alpha\neq\beta$ are always distinct and are taken to be non-vanishing. The integral (\ref{gl.A1}) always exists at its lower limit. 
We follow \cite{Boas06}, and let $u(x):=J_p(\alpha x)$ and $v(x):=J_{-p}(\beta x)$. 
One has from Bessel's differential equation $x\bigl(x u'\bigr)'+\bigl(\alpha^2 x^2 - p^2\bigr)u=0$ and similarly for $v$ with $\alpha$ replaced by $\beta$. 
Multiplying the first equation by $v$, the second one with $u$, subtracting these two equations and then integrating over the interval $[0,1]$, one obtains \cite{Boas06} 
\begin{displaymath}
\biggl. \left[ v(x) x u'(x) -u(x) x v'(x) \right] \biggr|_0^1  +\bigl(\alpha^2 - \beta^2\bigr) \int_0^1 \!{\rm d} x\; x\, u(x) v(x) = 0 \tag{*} 
\end{displaymath}
One must now evaluate the two boundary terms $T_{1,2}$ in (*). The first one simply is
\begin{displaymath}
T_1 = v(1)\, 1 u'(1) - u(1)\, 1 v'(1) = J_{-p}(\beta) {J_p}'(\alpha) - J_p(\alpha) {J_{-p}}'(\beta) = 0
\end{displaymath}
because of the definition  of $\alpha,\beta$ as zeros of $J_{\pm p}(x)$, respectively. 
For the second term, we recall the small-argument behaviour of the $J_{\pm p}(x)$ \cite{Abra65} and consider 
\begin{eqnarray} 
\lefteqn{T_2 = \lim_{x\to 0} \left[ J_{-p}(\beta x) x {J_p}'(\alpha x) - J_p(\alpha x) x {J_{-p}}'(\beta x) \right]} 
\nonumber \\
&=& \lim_{x\to 0} \left[ \left( \frac{\beta x}{2}\right)^{-p} \!\frac{x}{\Gamma(1-p)} \left( \left(\frac{\alpha x}{2}\right)^p \frac{1}{\Gamma(1+p)} \right)' 
- \left( \frac{\alpha x}{2}\right)^{p} \frac{x}{\Gamma(1+p)} \left( \left(\frac{\beta x}{2}\right)^{-p} \frac{1}{\Gamma(1-p)} \right)' \,\right]
\nonumber \\
&=& \lim_{x\to 0} \left[ \frac{1}{\Gamma(1-p)\Gamma(1+p)} \left( \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^p \left( x^{-p+1+p-1} p - x^{p+1-p-1} \bigl(-p\bigr) \right) \right] 
\:=\: \frac{2p}{\Gamma(1-p)\Gamma(1+p)} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^p
\nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
Inserting this into the basic identity (*) and recalling that $\Gamma(1-p)\Gamma(1+p)=p \pi /\sin p\pi$ \cite[(6.1.17)]{Abra65} gives the assertion. \hfill q.e.d.

The evaluation of the first boundary term is immediate

\end{displaymath}
and can then write by taking into account the small-argument-behaviour of the $J_{\pm p}(x)$ \cite{Abra65}, where the second-order contribution must be included for the $J_{-p}(x)$
\begin{eqnarray}
\lefteqn{T_2 = \lim_{x\to 0} \biggl[ x \biggl( Y_p(\beta x) {Y_p}'(\alpha x) - Y_p(\alpha x) {Y_p}'(\beta x) \biggr) \biggr]}

\nonumber \\

Evaluating this to the leading non-trivial order for $x\ll 1$ gives further

T_2 &=& \frac{1}{\sin^2 \pi p} \lim_{x\to 0} \left\{ \frac{\cos^2 \pi p}{\Gamma^2(1+p)} \left( \frac{\alpha\beta}{4}\right)^p 
\left[ p x^{1+p+p-1} - p x^{1+p+p-1}\right] \left( 1+ {\rm O}(x) \right) \right. 
\nonumber \\
& & \left. + \frac{\cos \pi p}{\Gamma(1+p)\Gamma(1-p)} 
\left\{ \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^p 
\left[ x^{1+p} \left( -p x^{-p-1} - \frac{\beta^2}{4}\frac{2-p}{1-p}x^{1-p}\right) - x^{1-p}\left( 1 - \frac{\beta^2/4}{1-p} x^2\right) p x^{p-1} \right] \right. \right.
\nonumber \\
& & \left. \left. ~~+\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^p \left[ x^{1-p} \left( 1 - \frac{\alpha^2/4}{1-p} x^2\right) p x^{p-1}

\nonumber \\
& & + \left. \frac{1}{\Gamma^2(1-p)}\left(\frac{4}{\alpha\beta}\right)^p 
\left[ x^{1-p}\left( 1 - \frac{\beta^2/4}{1-p} x^2\right)\left( -p x^{-p-1}-\frac{\alpha^2}{4}\frac{2-p}{1-p}x^{1-p}\right)
\right. \right. 
\nonumber \\
& & \left. \left. ~~-x^{1-p}\left( 1 - \frac{\alpha^2/4}{1-p} x^2\right)\left( -p x^{-p-1}-\frac{\beta^2}{4}\frac{2-p}{1-p} x^{1-p}\right)\right] \right\}
\nonumber \\
&=& \frac{1}{\sin^2 \pi p} \lim_{x\to 0} \left\{ {\rm O}(x^{2p}, x^{2p+1}) \right.
\nonumber \\
& & \left.

\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^p\left[ -2p x^0 + {\rm O}(x^2)\right] + \left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^p\left[ -2p x^0 + {\rm O}(x^2)\right] \right\} 
\right.
\nonumber \\
& & \left. 
+\frac{1}{\Gamma^2(1-p)} \left( \frac{4}{\alpha\beta}\right)^p \left[ -p x^{-2p} +\frac{\beta^2}{4} \frac{p}{1-p} x^{2-2p} + p x^{-2p} - \frac{\alpha^2}{4} \frac{p}{1-p} x^{2-2p}

\nonumber \\

\left( 1 + {\rm O}(x^2)\right) \right. 
\nonumber \\
& & \left. + \frac{1}{\Gamma^2(1-p)}\left(\frac{4}{\alpha\beta}\right)^p \frac{1}{2} \frac{\beta^2-\alpha^2}{1-p} x^{2-2p} \left( 1 + {\rm O}(x)\right) \right\}

\lim_{x\to 0} x\bigl( Y_p(\beta x){Y_p}'(\alpha x)-Y_p(\alpha x){Y_p}'(\beta x) \bigr) = \frac{2p \cos \pi p}{\sin^2 \pi p}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-p)\Gamma(1+p)}

\end{displaymath}

{\bf Lemma A.3:} {\it If $0<p<1$ and if $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}$ are the non-vanishing zeros of the Bessel functions $J_{p}(x)$ and $Y_p(x)$ such that
$J_p(\alpha) = Y_{p}(\beta)=0$, then one  has the identity}
\begin{equation}
\int_0^1 \!{\rm d} x\;  x\, J_p(\alpha x) Y_{p}(\beta x) = -\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^p \frac{1}{\alpha^2 - \beta^2} 
\end{equation}

\noindent
<!-- end head-5 -->
<!-- head-6: body_md -->
since $J_p(x)$ and $Y_p(x)$ are independent functions, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are always distinct. 
Letting $u(x) = J_p(\alpha x)$ and $v(x) =Y_p(\beta x)$, one derives as
before the relation (*) \cite{Boas06}. The first boundary term is
\begin{displaymath}
T_1 = v(1)\, 1 u'(1) - u(1)\, 1 v'(1) = Y_{p}(\beta) {J_p}'(\alpha) - J_p(\alpha) {Y_{p}}'(\beta) = 0
\end{displaymath}
and the second boundary term $T_2$ becomes with the definition \cite[(9.1.2)]{Abra65} of $Y_p(x)$ 
\begin{eqnarray}
\lefteqn{T_2 = \lim_{x\to 0} \biggl[ x \biggl( Y_p(\beta x) {J_p}'(\alpha x) - J_p(\alpha x) {Y_p}'(\beta x) \biggr) \biggr]}
\nonumber \\
&=& \frac{1}{\sin\pi p} \lim_{x\to 0} x \biggl[ 
\bigl(J_p(\beta x)\cos(\pi p)-J_{-p}(\beta x)\bigr){J_p}'(\alpha x)- J_p(\alpha x)\bigl({J_p}'(\beta x)\cos(\pi p)-{J_{-p}}'(\beta x)\bigr)  
\biggr]
\nonumber \\
&=& \frac{1}{\sin\pi p} \lim_{x\to 0} x \left\{ 
\left[ \frac{\bigl(\beta x/2\bigr)^p}{\Gamma(1+p)} \left(\frac{\bigl(\alpha x/2\bigr)^p}{\Gamma(1+p)}\right)' 
- \frac{\bigl(\beta x/2\bigr)^p}{\Gamma(1+p)} \left(\frac{\bigl(\alpha x/2\bigr)^p}{\Gamma(1+p)}\right)'\, \right] \cos\pi p  \right. 
\nonumber \\
& & \left. ~~+\left[ \frac{\bigl(\alpha x/2\bigr)^p}{\Gamma(1+p)} \left( \frac{\bigl(\beta x/2\bigr)^{-p}}{\Gamma(1-p)}\left( 1 -\frac{\bigl(\beta x/2\bigr)^2}{1-p}\right) \right)' 
-  \frac{\bigl(\beta x/2\bigr)^{-p}}{\Gamma(1-p)}\left( 1 -\frac{\bigl(\beta x/2\bigr)^2}{1-p}\right) \left( \frac{\bigl(\alpha x/2\bigr)^p}{\Gamma(1+p)} \right)' \, \right] \right\}
\nonumber \\
&=& \frac{1}{\sin\pi p} \lim_{x\to 0} \left\{ {\rm O}(x^{2p+1}) - \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^p \frac{2p}{\Gamma(1-p)\Gamma(1+p)} x^0 + {\rm O}(x) \right\} 
\nonumber \\
&=& - \frac{2}{\pi} \left( \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^p
\nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
where in the last line the Gamma function identity \cite[(6.1.17)]{Abra65} was used again. Insertion into (*) gives the assertion. \hfill q.e.d.

We follow closely \cite[pp. 218-220]{Pap99}. Consider the contour integral
\begin{displaymath}
J := \oint_{\mathscr{C}} \!{\rm d} z\: \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1} = 
\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon'} +\int_{K_+} + \int_{1+\varepsilon'}^R +  \int_{C_R} + \int_{R}^{1+\varepsilon'} + \int_{K_-} + \int_{1-\varepsilon'}^{\varepsilon} + \int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\right) 
\!{\rm d} z\: \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1}     
\end{displaymath}
where the integration contour $\mathscr{C}$ is sketched in figure~\ref{figC1}. 
Since the two singularities (red points in figure~\ref{figC1}) are not in the interior of
$\mathscr{C}$, the contour integral $J=0$. Here, the segments $C_R$, $C_{\varepsilon}$ 
are circles around zero, of radius $R,\varepsilon$ respectively, and $K_{\pm}$ are half-circles around $1$ of radius
$\varepsilon'$. The limits $\varepsilon\to 0$, $\varepsilon'\to 0$ and $R\to\infty$ are to taken at an appropriate moment. 
Since the integral converges in the $\varepsilon\to 0$ and $R\to\infty$ limits, the main focus
must be on how to take $\varepsilon'\to 0$ correctly. 

First, since $0<u<1$, one can estimate
\begin{displaymath}
\left| \int_{C_R}\!{\rm d} z\: \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1} \right| \leq \int_{C_R}\!\bigl|{\rm d} z\bigr|\: \left| \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1} \right| \sim R \frac{R^{u-1}}{R} \to 0
\;\; ,  \;\;
\left| \int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\!{\rm d} z\: \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1} \right| \leq \int_{C_{\varepsilon}}\!\bigl|{\rm d} z\bigr|\: \left| \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1} \right| \sim \varepsilon^u \to 0
\end{displaymath}
when $R\to\infty$ and $\varepsilon\to 0$, respectively. Second, with the choice $z=1+\varepsilon' e^{{\rm i}\theta}$, we have
\begin{displaymath}
\int_{K_+}\!\!{\rm d} z\: \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1} = \int_{\pi}^{0} \!{\rm d}\theta\: {\rm i}\varepsilon'\,e^{{\rm i}\theta} \frac{\bigl(1+\varepsilon' e^{{\rm i}\theta}\bigr)^{u-1}}{\varepsilon' e^{{\rm i}\theta}} 
                                         = -{\rm i} \int_0^{\pi} \!{\rm d}\theta\: \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \binom{u-1}{k} {\varepsilon'}^k e^{{\rm i} k \theta} = -{\rm i} \pi + {\rm O}(\varepsilon')
\end{displaymath}
and in the other case, we let $z=\bigl(1-\varepsilon' e^{{\rm i}\theta}\bigr)e^{2\pi {\rm i}}$ and have
\begin{eqnarray}
\int_{K_-}\!\!{\rm d} z\: \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1} &=& -\int_{0}^{\pi} \!{\rm d}\theta\: \bigl(-{\rm i}\varepsilon'\bigr)\,e^{{\rm i}\theta} 
                                             \frac{\bigl(1+\varepsilon' e^{{\rm i}\theta}\bigr)^{u-1}e^{2\pi{\rm i}(u-1)}}{-\varepsilon' e^{{\rm i}\theta}} 
\nonumber \\
&=& -{\rm i}\, e^{2\pi{\rm i} (u-1)} \int_0^{\pi} \!{\rm d}\theta\: \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \binom{u-1}{k} \bigl(-{\varepsilon'}\bigr)^k e^{{\rm i} k \theta} \:=\: -{\rm i} \pi e^{2\pi{\rm i} (u-1)} + {\rm O}(\varepsilon')
\nonumber 
\end{eqnarray}
where the extra sign arises from the opposite orientation of the contour. Third, the contributions from the straight lines can now be written as
\begin{displaymath}
I_R := \lim_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon'\to 0} \left(  \int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon'} + \int_{1+\varepsilon'}^R \right) {\rm d} z\: \frac{z^{u-1}}{z-1}
\end{displaymath}
and 
\begin{displaymath} 
\lim_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon'\to 0}  \left( \int_{R}^{1+\varepsilon'} + \int_{1-\varepsilon'}^{\varepsilon} \right) {\rm d} z\: \frac{\bigl(e^{2\pi {\rm i}}z\bigr)^{u-1}}{z-1} = - e^{2\pi {\rm i}\bigl(u-1\bigr)} I_R
\end{displaymath}
where the extra sign comes from the opposite orientation for the integration contour 
and the change $z\mapsto z e^{2\pi {\rm i}}$ is necessary in order to remain on the same sheet. Forth, taking all this
together, we have (anticipating that $R\to \infty$ will be taken shortly) 
\begin{displaymath} 
J = 0 = \left( 1 - e^{2\pi {\rm i}\bigl(u-1\bigr)} \right) I_R-{\rm i} \pi \left( 1 + e^{2\pi {\rm i} \bigl(u-1\bigr)} \right)
\end{displaymath}
which finally allows to conclude
\begin{displaymath} 
I = \lim_{R\to\infty} I_R = {\rm i} \pi\, \frac{1+e^{2\pi {\rm i} u}}{1-e^{2\pi {\rm i} u}} ={\rm i} \pi\, \frac{e^{-\pi {\rm i} u} + e^{\pi {\rm i} u}}{e^{-\pi {\rm i} u} - e^{\pi {\rm i} u}} 
= -\pi\, \frac{\cos \pi u}{\sin \pi u} = -\pi \cot\pi u
\end{displaymath}
as claimed. \hfill q.e.d. 

Once established in the strip $0<u<1$, one may use (\ref{gl.C1}) to define the integral $I=I(u)$ for other values of $u$ via analytic continuation. 

On the half-line $\mathbb{R}_+$, the {\em Hilbert transform} $f(x)$ of a function $g(y)$ is defined as
\begin{equation} \label{gl.C2}
f(x) = \mathscr{H}\bigl(g(y)\bigr)(x) := \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} y\: \frac{g(y)}{y-x} \;\; , \;\;
g(y) = -\frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x\: \sqrt{ \frac{x}{y}\, } \,\frac{f(x)}{x-y}
\end{equation}
where the integrals are Cauchy principal values. We implicitly admit that $g(y)$ satisfies sufficient conditions such that its Hilbert transform $f(x)$ exists. 
The inverse Hilbert transformation also included in (\ref{gl.C2}) was derived in \cite{Pave94} 
(the square root is distinct with respect to an inverse Hilbert transform on the entire real line $\mathbb{R}$). 

In addition, we need the {\em Mellin transform} of a function $f(x)$, defined as \cite{Flaj95} 
\begin{equation} \label{gl.C3}
\breve{f}(u) = \mathscr{M}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)(u) := \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x\: x^{u-1} f(x) \;\; , \;\;
f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi {\rm i}} \int_{c-{\rm i}\infty}^{c+{\rm i}\infty} \!{\rm d} u \: x^{-u} \breve{f}(u)
\end{equation}
For the existence of a Mellin transform of a function $f(x)$, a consideration of its {\it fundamental strip} $\langle a, b\rangle$ with $a<b$ is paramount. Herein
\begin{eqnarray}
a  &:=& \mbox{\rm inf}\;\left( A \in\mathbb{R} \left| \mbox{\rm $f(x) x^{A-1}$ is integrable over $(0,1]$}       \right. \right) \nonumber \\
b  &:=& \mbox{\rm sup}  \left( B \in\mathbb{R} \left| \mbox{\rm $f(x) x^{B-1}$ is integrable over $[1,\infty)$}  \right. \right) \nonumber 
\end{eqnarray}
In practise, an useful shortcut goes as follows, which appeals to the asymptotic behaviour of $f(x)$. 
If $f(x)\stackrel{x\to 0}{\sim} x^{-\alpha}$ and $f(x)\stackrel{x\to\infty}{\sim} 
x^{-\beta}$ with $\alpha<\beta$, then the fundamental strip $\langle a, b\rangle$ is a superset of the 
strip $\left\{ u = \sigma +{\rm i} \tau \in\mathbb{C} \bigl|  \tau\in\mathbb{R} 
\mbox{\rm  ~and~} \alpha<\sigma<\beta\bigr.\right\}$. On the fundamental strip, 
$\breve{f}(u)$ exists and is holomorphic \cite{Flaj95}. A monomial $f(x)=x^a$ does not admit a Mellin transform. The constant $c$ in (\ref{gl.C3}) must be in the fundamental strip.

{\it If both the Hilbert and Mellin transforms of a function $f(x)$ exist, as well as the Mellin transform of $\mathscr{H}(f)(x)$, and $0<u<1$, then formally}
<!-- end head-11 -->
<!-- head-12: body_md -->
Under the hypotheses made on the existence of the objects in question, this is merely a formal calculation
\begin{eqnarray}
\lefteqn{ \mathscr{M}\left(\mathscr{H}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\right)(u) = \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x\: x^{u-1} \,\frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} y\: \frac{f(y)}{x-y} 
\:=\: \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} y\: f(y) \int_{0}^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x\: \frac{x^{u-1}}{x-y} }
\nonumber \\
&=& \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} y\: f(y) \int_{0}^{\infty} \!{\rm d} t\: y\, \frac{\bigl(yt\bigr)^{u-1}}{yt -y}
\:=\: \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} y\: y^{u-1}\, f(y) \int_{0}^{\infty} \!{\rm d} t\: \frac{t^{u-1}}{t-1}
\nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
and the assertion follows from the identity (\ref{gl.C1}). \hfill q.e.d. 

As an application, one may treat integral equations of the type $\mathscr{H}(f)(x)=g(x)$ \cite{Blas23} 
where $g(x)$ is given and $f(x)$ is sought. A Mellin transformation of this gives
\begin{displaymath} 
\mathscr{M}\bigl(\mathscr{H}(f)\bigr)(u)= -\cot\pi u\, \mathscr{M}(f)(u) = \mathscr{M}(g)(u)
\end{displaymath} 
hence formally $\mathscr{M}(f)(u)=-\tan\pi u\,  \mathscr{M}(g)(u)$. See \cite{Blas23} for more details. 
It remains to carry out efficiently the inverse Mellin transformation, in order to really find $f(x)$.

This follows from a formal calculation, as follows:
\begin{eqnarray}
\lefteqn{ \mathscr{M}\left( \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x \left( \frac{x}{y} \right)^p \frac{f(x)}{x-y} \right)(u) 
\hspace{2.35cm}= \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} y\: y^{u-1} \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x \left( \frac{x}{y} \right)^p \frac{f(x)}{x-y} }\nonumber \\
&=& \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x\: f(x) \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} y\: y^{u-1} \left( \frac{x}{y} \right)^p \frac{1}{x-y} 
\:=\: \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x\: f(x) \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} t\: x \bigl(t x\bigr)^{u-1} \left( \frac{x}{t x} \right)^p \frac{1}{x-tx} \nonumber \\
&=& \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} x\: x^{u-1}\, f(x) \int_0^{\infty} \!{\rm d} t\:  \frac{t^{u-p-1}}{1-t} 
\hspace{1.05cm}\:=\: \pi \mathscr{M}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)(u) \cdot \cot\bigl( \pi(u-p)\bigr) \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
as claimed, with the help of Lemma C.1. \hfill q.e.d. 

Of course, once eqs.~(\ref{gl.C4},\ref{gl.C5}) are obtained in some strip, one may extend these via analytic continuation. They are required in the main text. 

A few simple rules, besides the obvious properties of linearity and scaling, include the following \cite{Flaj95}
\begin{subequations} \label{gl:Melprops}
\begin{align}
\mathscr{M}\left( f\bigl(1/x) \right)(u) &= - \mathscr{M}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)(-u) \:=\: -\breve{f}(-u) \\
\mathscr{M}\left( f\bigl(x\bigr) \ln x\right)(u) &= \frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d} u}\mathscr{M}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)(u) ~ \:=\: \frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d} u}\breve{f}(u) \\
\mathscr{M}\left( x \frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d} x} f(x)\right)(u) &= - u\mathscr{M}\bigl(f(x)\bigr)(u) \:=\: -u\breve{f}(u) \label{gl:Melprops3}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where in (\ref{gl:Melprops3}) $f(x)$ is assumed continuous and piecewise differentiable. 

The asymptotic form of $f(x)$ as $x\to 0$ ($x\to \infty$) constrains the behaviour of $\breve{f}(u)$ at the
left (right) boundary of its fundamental strip $\langle a, b\rangle$ and inversely. 
For a meromorphic function $\phi(u)$ with a p\^ole of order $r>0$, 
its {\em singular element} at $u_0$ is the sum of the singular terms in its Laurent expansion at $u_0$ and its {\em singular expansion} is the formal sum of its
singular elements at all points $u_0$. If $E$ is such a singular expansion, one writes $\phi(u)\asymp E$. 
For example $\Gamma(u)\asymp\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell !} \frac{1}{u+\ell}$ \cite{Abra65}. 

The relationship with p\^oles of the Mellin transform is expressed in the following two theorems. 
For simplicity, it is admitted throughout that $f(x)$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$, 
and has a non-empty fundamental strip $\langle a, b\rangle$ where the Mellin transform  $\breve{f}(u)$ exists.

This result allows to identify the asymptotic behaviour of a function $f(x)$ if the singular expansion (\ref{gl:C-melsing}) of its Mellin transform $\breve{f}(u)$ is known.

Consider the following function (with $\nu>0$), analytic in its fundamental strip $\langle 0,\nu\rangle$, together with its singular expansion to the left of ${\rm Re\ } u=0$ \cite{Flaj95}
\begin{displaymath}
\phi(u) = \frac{\Gamma(u)\Gamma(\nu-u)}{\Gamma(\nu)} \asymp \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell !} \frac{\Gamma(\nu+\ell)}{\Gamma(\nu)} \frac{1}{u+\ell} 
\;\; ; \;\; {\rm Re\ } u <0 
\end{displaymath}
which only contains simple p\^oles, all on the non-positive real axis ($k=1$ in (\ref{gl:C-melsing})). 
The properties of the Gamma function guarantee a sufficiently fast decrease on the strip such that Theorem C.2 is applicable. Since the singular expansion is to the left of the 
fundamental strip, this gives the small-$x$ behaviour of the original function. 
Eq.~(\ref{gl:C-melx0}) with $k=1$ implies
\begin{displaymath}
f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}} \int_{\nu/2-{\rm i}\infty}^{\nu/2+{\rm i}\infty} \!\!{\rm d} u\: x^{-u}\,\phi(u) 
= \sum_{\ell=0}^L \frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell !} \frac{\Gamma(\ell+\nu)}{\Gamma(\nu)}  x^{\ell} + {\rm O}\bigl( x^{L+1/2}\bigr)
= \bigl(1+x\bigr)^{-\nu} \tag{*}
\end{displaymath}
if one lets $L\to\infty$, up to terms vanishing more fast than any power (see \cite{Flaj95} for details).
