<!-- head-1: body_md -->
In math expressions, \emph{round} brackets are used
exclusively for function arguments, e.g.~$\sin(t-1)$. We use
\emph{square} brackets for matrices and to group expressions. For
instance, $x[t-1]$ means the product of $x$ and $t-1$. The function of
a matrix is defined as the matrix of the functions, e.g.\
$\cos(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} a\\ b 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr])=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} \cos(a)\\ \cos(b) 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ and
$\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} a\\ b 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]^2=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} a^2\\ b^2 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$.  The $\odot$ denotes
\underline{\smash{elementwise product}} of two column vectors,
e.g.~$\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} a\\b 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\odot\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} p\\q 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} ap\\bq 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$.  The function $\operatorname{diag}$
(for ``diagonal'') turns vectors into diagonal matrices,
e.g.~$\operatorname{diag}(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} a\\ b 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr])=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} a&0\\0&b 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$.  We sometimes use $\mathbb{1}$
to mean the column vector $\mathbb{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^n$.

<!-- example-1: body_md -->
\label{SP} Consider the
  single-arm pendulum in $\mathbb{C}$ as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:SDP}. The
  pendulum (its one arm) can rotate freely around the pivot at
  $0\in\mathbb{C}$.  Let $\ell$ be the length of the arm, and let $z\in\mathbb{C}$
  be the position of the tip of the pendulum. Hence,
  \[
    z=\ell\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi},
  \]
  where ``$\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}$'' is the imaginary unit, and $\phi$ is the argument of the
  complex number $z$. The speed and acceleration,
  $\dot{z},\ddot{z}\in\mathbb{C}$, thus are
  \begin{align*}
    \dot{z} 
    = \ell\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\dot{\phi},\qquad
    \ddot{z}
    = \ell\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}[\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\ddot{\phi}-\dot{\phi}^2].
  \end{align*}
  The force $F\in\mathbb{C}$ acting on the tip of the arm (at $z$) consists
  of gravity $gm$ (downwards, so in the ``$-\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}$ direction''), and a
  compression force $\lambda$ in the direction of the pendulum, see
  Fig.~\ref{fig:SDP}. Hence the total force acting on the tip is
  $-\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i} g m +\lambda \hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}\in\mathbb{C}$ for some as yet unknown
  compression force $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$. Newton's 2nd law, $m\ddot{z}=F$,
  therefore becomes
  \[
    m\ell\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}[\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\ddot{\phi}-\dot{\phi}^2]
    = -\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i} g m +\lambda\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}. 
  \]
  Dividing by $m\ell\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}$ gives
  \[
    \hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\ddot{\phi}-\dot{\phi}^2 = -\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}
    \frac{g}{\ell}\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{-\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}+\frac{1}{m\ell}\lambda.
  \]
  This equation is linear in the real pair $(\ddot{\phi},\lambda)$,
  and its imaginary and real part respectively determine $\ddot{\phi}$
  and $\lambda$:
  \begin{align*}
    \ddot{\phi} &=-\frac{g}{\ell}\cos(\phi),\\
    \lambda & = -m\ell \dot{\phi}^2+gm \sin(\phi). 
  \end{align*}
  The first of these is the equation of motion. Once we have
  $\phi,\dot{\phi}$, the second equation determines the compression
  force $\lambda$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:dual}
  For every pendulum tree with $n$ arms we have that~\eqref{eq:cm}
  and~\eqref{fizzz} hold for some $S\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}$ and
  $B\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n\times n}$. In fact, $B=S^{-\textsc{t}}$, and all
  eigenvalues of $S$ and $B$ are equal to 1.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
We prove it by induction in the number of arms $n$. For $n=1$ arms
  (the single-arm pendulum) we have $S=B=1$ (see Example~\ref{SP}) so
  then the result holds.

  Next let $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and suppose that $B=S^{-\textsc{t}}$ for every
  pendulum tree of $n$ arms, and that all eigenvalues of $S$ and $B$
  are $1$, and $S\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}$ and
  $B\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n\times n}$ (the induction hypothesis).  Now
  consider an arbitrary pendulum tree with $n+1$ arms. By removing one
  arm it reduces to an $n$-arm pendulum. Without loss of generality we
  denote the tip of the removed arm by $z_{n+1}$, and the tip to which
  it was connected by $z_n$, see Fig.~\ref{fig:fant}. Thus,
  $z_{n+1}=z_n+\ell_{n+1}\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi_{n+1}}$. The connectivity matrix
  $S_{n+1}$ of the $n+1$-arm pendulum in terms of the connectivity
  matrix $S$ of the $n$-arm pendulum follows from
  \[
    \begin{bmatrix} z\\ z_{n+1} \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix} z\\ z_n + \ell_{n+1}\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi_{n+1}} \end{bmatrix}
    = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} S & 0\\ S_{n*} &
        1 \end{bmatrix}}_{S_{n+1}}\begin{bmatrix} \ell\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}\\ \ell_{n+1}\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi_{n+1}} \end{bmatrix},
  \]
  where $S_{n*}$ means the final row of $S$. By adding arm number
  $n+1$ to the pendulum, the total force $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} F\\ F_{n+1} 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ (acting
  on the $n+1$ joints/tips) becomes
  \[
    \begin{bmatrix} F\\ F_{n+1} \end{bmatrix}
    = -\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i} g \begin{bmatrix} m\\ m_{n+1} \end{bmatrix}
    +\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{c|c}
                         B & \begin{smallmatrix} 0\\[-1ex] \vdots\\ 0\\-1 \end{smallmatrix}\\ \hline
                         0 & 1
                       \end{array}\right]}_{B_{n+1}}
                   \begin{bmatrix} \lambda\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}\\
                     \lambda_{n+1}\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi_{n+1}} \end{bmatrix},
  \]
  (this defines $B_{n+1}$).  This equation expresses that there is one
  additional compression force $\lambda_{n+1}\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi_{n+1}}$ and
  that it acts on $z_{n+1}$, and also on $z_n$ but then in opposite direction
  (see the red and blue forces in Fig.~\ref{fig:fant}).  By the
  induction hypothesis we have $B=S^{-\textsc{t}}$, so
  \[
    S_{n+1}^{\textsc{t}}B_{n+1}=
    \left[\begin{array}{c|c} S^{\textsc{t}} & S_{n*}^{\textsc{t}} \\ \hline 0 & 1\end{array}\right]
    \left[\begin{array}{c|c} S^{-\textsc{t}} &
      \begin{smallmatrix} 0\\[-1ex] \vdots\\ 0\\-1 \end{smallmatrix}\\ \hline 0 & 1\end{array}\right]
      =\left[\begin{array}{c|c} I & 0 \\
           \hline 0 & 1\end{array}\right]=I_{n+1}.
  \]
  Hence, $B_{n+1}=S_{n+1}^{-\textsc{t}}$, and the eigenvalues of $S_{n+1}$ are
  those of $S$ with one more eigenvalue equal to 1. So, by the
  induction hypothesis, they are all equal to $1$. Also, we see that
  $S\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}$ and $B\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n\times n}$.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{CNFP}
  Suppose we have a pendulum tree with $n$ arms and connectivity
  matrix $S$, mass vector $m\in\mathbb{R}_+^n$, vector of angles
  $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^n$, and vector of lengths
  $\ell\in\mathbb{R}^n_+$. 
  Then
  \begin{align}\label{aaa}
    \ddot{\phi}
    & = \mathcal{M}_{\rm re}^{-1}(\phi)\big[\mathcal{M}_{\rm im}(\phi)\dot{\phi}^2
      -g\cos(\phi)\odot\ell\odot[S^{\textsc{t}}m]\bigr],\\
    \lambda
    & =g\sin(\phi)\odot[S^{\textsc{t}}m]-\ell^{-1}\odot\bigl[\mathcal{M}_{\rm 
      im}(\phi)\ddot{\phi}+\mathcal{M}_{\rm re}(\phi)[\dot{\phi}^2]\bigr].
      \label{bbb}
  \end{align}
  Equation~\eqref{aaa} is the \underline{\smash{real equation of motion}},
  and~\eqref{bbb} determines the compression forces $\lambda$.
  Here, $\mathcal{M}_{\rm re}(\phi)$ and $\mathcal{M}_{\rm im}(\phi)$ are the real and
  imaginary parts of the $n\times n$ \underline{\smash{complex mass matrix}}
  $\mathcal{M}(\phi)$ defined as
  \begin{equation}\label{massmat}
    \mathcal{M}(\phi)=\operatorname{diag}(\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{-\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}\odot\ell)
    S^{\textsc{t}}\operatorname{diag}(m) S\operatorname{diag}(\ell\odot\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}).
  \end{equation}
  This complex mass matrix is Hermitian and positive definite, and
  its real part is symmetric and positive definite and invertible.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
First, realize that $\mathcal{M}(\phi)$ defined in~\eqref{massmat} is
  Hermitian and positive definite.  Thus its real part is symmetric
  and positive definite. Positive definite matrices are invertible.
  
  The elementwise product $a\odot b$ of two column vectors $a,b$ is the
  same as the usual matrix-vector product $\operatorname{diag}(a)b$.
  So~\eqref{xyzzz} is the same as
  \[
    \operatorname{diag}(m)S\operatorname{diag}(\ell\odot\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi})[\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\ddot{\phi}-\dot{\phi}^2]=
      -\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i} g m + S^{-\textsc{t}}[\lambda\odot\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}]. 
  \]
  Premultiplying by the invertible $\operatorname{diag}(\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{-\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}\odot\ell)S^{\textsc{t}}$
  turns this into
  \begin{equation}\label{ooo}
    \mathcal{M}(\phi) [\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\ddot{\phi}-\dot{\phi}^2]
    =
    -\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i} g\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{-\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\phi}\odot\ell\odot[S^{\textsc{t}}m]+ \ell\odot\lambda. 
  \end{equation}
  We call this the \underline{\smash{complex equation of motion}}. The imaginary
  part of this expression is
  \begin{equation}\label{ODEn}
    \mathcal{M}_{\rm re}(\phi)\ddot{\phi}-\mathcal{M}_{\rm 
      im}(\phi)\dot{\phi}^2=-g\cos(\phi)\odot\ell\odot[S^{\textsc{t}}m]
  \end{equation}
  which determines the real equation of motion~\eqref{aaa}. Once
  $\ddot{\phi}$ is known, the real part of~\eqref{ooo} uniquely
  determines the compression forces $\lambda$ as given in~\eqref{bbb}.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
For every $n\times n$ connectivity matrix $S$, and every
  $m,\ell\in\mathbb{R}_+^n,g>0$, the matrix $A$ defined in~\eqref{eq:lin} has
  $n$ positive real eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\in\mathbb{R}_+$,
  and $n$ linearly independent real eigenvectors
  $v_1,\ldots,v_n\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Consequently, the
  linearization~\eqref{eq:lin} has $n$ complex eigenmodes
  $\theta_k(t)\operatorname{\,\raisebox{.08ex}{\ensuremath{:}}\!\!=} v_k\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\omega_k t}, k=1,\ldots,n$,
  where $\omega_k\operatorname{\,\raisebox{.08ex}{\ensuremath{:}}\!\!=}\sqrt{\lambda}_k$, and every solution
  $\theta(t)$ of~\eqref{eq:lin} is (the real part of) a linear
  combination of these eigenmodes.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $D=\operatorname{diag}(\ell\odot[S^{\textsc{t}}m])$ and realize that
  $A=g\mathcal{M}_{\rm re}^{-1}(0)D$.  All entries of $S^{\textsc{t}}m$ are positive
  because all entries of $m$ are positive, and $S$ is invertible and
  has nonnegative entries only. Since also all entries of $\ell$ are
  positive we see that $D$ has a positive definite square root,
  $\sqrt{D}$.  Now, $A$ is isomorphic to
  $g\sqrt{D}\mathcal{M}_{\rm re}^{-1}(0)\sqrt{D}$. The latter is symmetric
  positive definite, hence, $\mathbb{R}^n$ has a basis
  $ v_1,\ldots,v_n$ of eigenvectors of $A$, and the
  eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ of $A$ are real and
  positive. Clearly,
  $\theta(t)\operatorname{\,\raisebox{.08ex}{\ensuremath{:}}\!\!=} v_k\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{e}^{\hspace{.4pt}\mathrm{i}\omega_kt}$
  satisfies~\eqref{eq:lin} iff $\omega_k=\pm\sqrt{\lambda_k}$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
For every $n\times n$ connectivity matrix $S$, and every
  $\ell\in\mathbb{R}_+^n,m\in\mathbb{R}^n_+,g>0$, the diagonal entries of $A^{-1}$
  equal $(A^{-1})_{kk}=\ell_k/g$. In particular, the trace of $A^{-1}$
  equals $\ell_{\rm tot}/g$ where
  $\ell_{\rm tot}\operatorname{\,\raisebox{.08ex}{\ensuremath{:}}\!\!=}\sum_{k=1}^n\ell_k$. Hence, the
  eigenfrequencies $\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n$ of the
  linearization~\eqref{eq:lin} satisfy
  \[
    \sum_{k=1}^n \frac1{\omega_k^2}=\frac{\ell_{\rm tot}}{g}.
  \]

<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
We have $A^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(S^{\textsc{t}}m)^{-1}S^\textsc{t}\operatorname{diag}(m)S\operatorname{diag}(\ell/g)$. From
  that it follows that $(A^{-1})_{kk}$ equals $\ell_k/g$ for all
  $k=1,\ldots,n$ iff
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:gek}
    (S^{\textsc{t}}m)_k = (S^{\textsc{t}}\operatorname{diag}(m)S)_{kk}, \qquad k=1,\ldots,n.
  \end{equation}
  The latter we prove by induction. For $n=1$ (the single-arm
  pendulum) the result holds. Now let $n\in\mathbb{N}$, and
  assume~\eqref{eq:gek} holds for pendulums with $n$ arms. The
  connectivity matrix for an $n+1$-arm pendulum is
  $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} S&0\\ S_{n*}&1 
                            \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ (see the proof of Theorem~\ref{CNFP}). So
  for an $n+1$-arm pendulum the expression ``$S^{\textsc{t}}m$'' becomes
  \[
    \begin{bmatrix} S^{\textsc{t}} & S_{n*}^{\textsc{t}}\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} m\\ m_{n+1} \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix} S^{\textsc{t}}m + S_{n*}^{\textsc{t}}m_{n+1}\\ m_{n+1} \end{bmatrix}. 
  \]
  We need to show that its entries equal the diagonal entries of
  \begin{align*}
    \begin{bmatrix} S^{\textsc{t}} & S_{n*}^{\textsc{t}}\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    &
    \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{diag}(m)&0\\0&m_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} S & 0 \\ S_{n*} & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix} S^{\textsc{t}}\operatorname{diag}(m)S+S_{n*}m_{n+1}S_{n*}^{\textsc{t}}& S_{n*}^{\textsc{t}}m_{n+1}\\
        m_{n+1}S_{n*} & m_{n+1} \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{align*}
  That is the case because of the induction hypothesis and the fact
  that all entries of $S_{n*}$ are either zero or one.

  Consequently, $\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1})=\ell_{\rm tot}/g$. It is a classic result
  that the trace of a matrix equals the sum of its eigenvalues, and
  the eigenvalues of $A^{-1}$ are $1/\omega_k^2$.
