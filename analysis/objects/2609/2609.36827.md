<!-- theoremalpha-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:intro-recognition}
Given only a rational nonnegative weight matrix, promised to define an exact instance, recognition of equality between the optimal face and its cut hull has the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The unique optimal dual slack can be recovered in deterministic polynomial time. Under strict complementarity, recognition and recovery of all optimal cut matrices on equality instances take deterministic polynomial time.
\item Recognition is polynomial-time Turing equivalent to exact rational semidefinite feasibility, already for simple unweighted graphs. With a supplied unique optimal cut, the equivalence is many-one, even for connected simple graphs. It also holds for complete graphs with polynomially bounded positive integer weights and a supplied nonnegative integer Gram factor of the slack.
\item Recognition belongs to $\mathsf{NP}$ if and only if it belongs to $\mathsf{coNP}$, and either membership is equivalent to the corresponding membership of exact SDP feasibility. In particular, NP-completeness would imply $\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}$.
\end{enumerate}

\label{thm:intro-realization}
Let $Z$ be doubly nonnegative with positive diagonal and rational kernel. For every integer $k\ge\kappa(Z)$, the matrix $Z\otimes J_{2k}$ is the optimal dual slack of a nonnegatively weighted exact Max-Cut instance whose optimal cut vectors span its kernel. Replication preserves rank, complete positivity, and cp-rank when finite.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:certificate}
For a sign vector $x$, let $Z_x=\operatorname{Diag}(W(x)/2)-L_W/4$. Then $Z_xx=0$, and $Z_x\succeq0$ if and only if the relaxation is exact and $x$ is a maximum cut. In an exact instance the optimal dual solution is unique and satisfies $s_i^*=W_i(x)/2$ for every maximum cut $x$. In particular, all maximum cuts have the same local cut profile, and $W_i(x)>\delta_i/2$ at every vertex.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The identity $(L_Wx)_i=2x_iW_i(x)$ gives $Z_xx=0$. If $Z_x\succeq0$, it is a feasible slack complementary to $xx^\top$, proving exactness and optimality. Conversely, complementary slackness for any optimal slack and cut gives $Z^*x=0$. Its $i$th coordinate forces $s_i^*=W_i(x)/2$, proving uniqueness and the common profile. Finally, a zero diagonal entry of a positive semidefinite matrix forces a zero row. Since $Z^*_{ij}=W_{ij}/4$ off the diagonal and there are no isolated vertices, $Z^*_{ii}>0$. Thus $W_i(x)/2-\delta_i/4>0$.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:slackrecovery}
Let $W$ be rational and nonnegative, let $D\ge1$ be an integer such that $DW$ is integral, and put $M=\sum_{i<j}W_{ij}$. Suppose the relaxation is exact. For every dual feasible $s$ satisfying $\mathbf1^\top s\le\operatorname{SDP}(W)+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon\ge0$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slackerror}
(s_i-s_i^*)^2\le Z(s)_{ii}\varepsilon
\le (M/2+\varepsilon)\varepsilon\qquad(1\le i\le n).
\end{equation}
If $0<\varepsilon\le[32D^2(M+2)]^{-1}$, rounding each $s_i$ to the nearest element of $(2D)^{-1}\mathbb Z$ recovers $s_i^*$. Consequently, the optimal dual slack can be computed from $W$ in deterministic polynomial time under the promise of exactness. Neither an optimal cut nor strict complementarity is required as input.
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Fix an optimal cut $x$ for the analysis. Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate} gives $Z(s^*)x=0$ and $s_i^*=W_i(x)/2$. Therefore
\[
Z(s)x=\operatorname{Diag}(s-s^*)x,\qquad
x^\top Z(s)x=\mathbf1^\top(s-s^*)\le\varepsilon.
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz for the positive semidefinite form defined by $Z(s)$ gives
\[
(s_i-s_i^*)^2=(e_i^\top Z(s)x)^2
\le Z(s)_{ii}\,x^\top Z(s)x\le Z(s)_{ii}\varepsilon.
\]
Also $Z(s)_{ii}\le\operatorname{tr}Z(s)=\mathbf1^\top s-M/2\le M/2+\varepsilon$, since $\operatorname{MC}(W)\le M$. This proves \eqref{eq:slackerror}. Taking $\varepsilon=0$ also recovers uniqueness in the exact case.

Every $s_i^*$ belongs to $(2D)^{-1}\mathbb Z$. For the stated positive accuracy, $\varepsilon<1$ and
\[
(M/2+\varepsilon)\varepsilon
<\frac{M/2+1}{32D^2(M+2)}=\frac1{64D^2}.
\]
Thus $|s_i-s_i^*|<1/(8D)$, less than half the grid spacing, and rounding is unambiguous.

It remains to obtain a sufficiently accurate \emph{feasible} dual point in the bit model. Put $\Delta=\max_i\delta_i$, $c=\Delta/2+1$, and $U_0=\Delta+2$. Consider
\[
K=\{s:Z(s)\succeq0,\ s_i\le U_0\ (1\le i\le n)\}.
\]
It contains $s^*$ because $0\le s_i^*=W_i(x)/2\le\Delta/2$. Moreover, $\lambda_{\max}(L_W)\le2\Delta$, so $Z(c\mathbf1)\succeq I$. The Euclidean ball of radius $1/2$ about $c\mathbf1$ lies in $K$, and $K\subseteq[0,U_0]^n$ because $Z(s)_{ii}\ge0$ implies $s_i\ge\delta_i/4$.

To apply a standard bit-complexity theorem directly, represent $K$ by the affine PSD slice
\[
T(s)=Z(s)\oplus\operatorname{Diag}(U_0\mathbf1-s)\succeq0.
\]
The off-diagonal and off-block entries are fixed, and corresponding diagonal entries in the two blocks sum to $U_0-\delta_i/4$. These rational affine equations describe exactly the matrices $T(s)$. In this affine hull,
$\|T(s)-T(t)\|_F=\sqrt2\|s-t\|_2$.
Consequently a relative Frobenius ball of radius $1/2$ about the rational matrix $T(c\mathbf1)$ is feasible, and the whole slice lies in the ball of radius $2nU_0$ about that matrix. The objective $\mathbf1^\top s$ is the trace of the first block plus the constant $M/2$.

The bounded-SDP approximation theorem, in the precise rational form of \cite[Theorem~1.1]{deKlerkVallentin16}, now returns a rational feasible $s$ of value at most $\operatorname{SDP}(W)+\varepsilon$ in time polynomial in the input length and $\log(1/\varepsilon)$. The theorem is the classical ellipsoid guarantee, for which that reference also supplies an interior-point proof. All the required radii and the strictly feasible point have been exhibited above. Taking $D$ to be the product of the input denominators gives polynomial bit length, as do $M$, $\Delta$, and the chosen $\varepsilon$. Rounding and forming $Z(s^*)$ complete the algorithm. The cut $x$ was used only in the proof of the error bound.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:dnn}
Let $W\ge0$ have no isolated vertices, and let $x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(k)}\in\{-1,1\}^n$, where $k\ge1$. The relaxation is exact with each $x^{(j)}$ optimal if and only if there is a doubly nonnegative matrix $Z$ such that
\[
W_{ij}=4Z_{ij}\quad(i\ne j),
\qquad
Zx^{(j)}=0\quad(1\le j\le k).
\]
In that case $Z=Z^*$. If $Z=Y^\top Y$ and $y_i$ denotes the $i$th column of $Y$, then
\[
W_{ij}=4\langle y_i,y_j\rangle\quad(i\ne j),
\qquad
\langle y_i,y_j\rangle\ge0,
\qquad
Yx^{(j)}=\sum_i x_i^{(j)}y_i=0.
\]
The factor $Y$ may be chosen with $d(W)$ independent rows, and every column is nonzero.
<!-- end proposition-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For an exact instance, the optimal slack is positive semidefinite, its off-diagonal entries are $W_{ij}/4\ge0$, and its diagonal entries are nonnegative. It is therefore doubly nonnegative, and complementary slackness gives $Z^*x^{(j)}=0$ for every optimal cut.

Conversely, suppose that $Z$ satisfies the stated conditions. Set $s_i=Z_{ii}+\delta_i/4$. Then
\[
Z=\operatorname{Diag}(s)-\tfrac14L_W
\]
is dual feasible and complementary to each primal feasible matrix $x^{(j)}(x^{(j)})^\top$. Thus the relaxation is exact and all the prescribed cuts are optimal. Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate} identifies $Z$ with the unique optimal slack.

A Gram factorization with $\operatorname{rank} Z=d(W)$ independent rows gives the remaining identities, using $\ker(Y^\top Y)=\ker Y$. A zero column would make every edge weight incident to the corresponding vertex zero, contrary to the assumption that there are no isolated vertices.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:multiplicity}
Let $Y\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$. Let $b_1,\ldots,b_r$ be its distinct columns, with multiplicities $m_1,\ldots,m_r$, and put $m=(m_1,\ldots,m_r)$. Define
\begin{align*}
T&=\sum_{t=1}^r m_tb_t,\\
S&=\left\{s\in\mathbb Z^r:0\le s_t\le m_t,\ \sum_{t=1}^r s_tb_t=T/2\right\},\\
\mathcal A&=\{t:\text{there exists }s\in S\text{ with }0<s_t<m_t\},\\
Q&=\operatorname{span}\{m-2s:s\in S\}.
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dimensionformula}
\sigma(Y)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal A}(m_t-1)+\dim Q.
\end{equation}
In particular, the sign vectors span $\ker Y$ if and only if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item every $t$ with $m_t\ge2$ belongs to $\mathcal A$; and \label{cond:1}
\item $Q=\{z\in\mathbb R^r:\sum_tz_tb_t=0\}$. \label{cond:2}
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $V_t$ index the columns equal to $b_t$, and let $\pi:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^r$ record the sums over these blocks:
\[
\pi(w)_t=\sum_{i\in V_t}w_i.
\]
For a sign vector $x$, let $s_t(x)$ count its negative entries in $V_t$. Then
\[
\pi(x)=m-2s(x),
\qquad
Yx=T-2\sum_t s_t(x)b_t.
\]
Thus $x\in\ker Y$ precisely when $s(x)\in S$, and every count vector in $S$ is realized by a sign vector. In particular, if
\[
U=\operatorname{span}(\ker Y\cap\{-1,1\}^n),
\]
then $\pi(U)=Q$.

For each block $V_t$, let $K_t$ be the subspace of vectors supported on $V_t$ whose coordinates sum to zero. Suppose first that $t\in\mathcal A$. Some kernel sign vector has both signs in this block. By permuting its entries within the block, we may place opposite signs at any prescribed pair of distinct positions $i,j\in V_t$. Interchanging those signs gives another kernel sign vector, and their difference is $\pm2(e_i-e_j)$. These differences span $K_t$, so $K_t\subseteq U$. If $t\notin\mathcal A$, every kernel sign vector is constant on $V_t$, and hence every vector in $U$ is constant there.

To separate these within-block directions from the block sums, average over all permutations within each block. This gives a linear projection $P$ onto the block-constant vectors, with
\[
(Pw)_i=\frac{\pi(w)_t}{m_t}\qquad(i\in V_t).
\]
The set of kernel sign vectors is invariant under these permutations, so $P(U)\subseteq U$. For every kernel sign vector $x$, the difference $x-Px$ belongs to $\bigoplus_{t\in\mathcal A}K_t$. Consequently,
\[
U=\left(\bigoplus_{t\in\mathcal A}K_t\right)\oplus P(U).
\]
The sum is direct because a block-constant vector with zero sum on every block is zero. Moreover, $\pi$ is injective on the block-constant subspace and
\[
\pi(P(U))=\pi(U)=Q.
\]
Taking dimensions proves \eqref{eq:dimensionformula}.

Finally, the distinct columns span the column space of $Y$, so the space in condition~\ref{cond:2} has dimension $r-\operatorname{rank} Y$. The two contributions in \eqref{eq:dimensionformula} satisfy
\[
\sum_{t\in\mathcal A}(m_t-1)\le n-r,
\qquad
\dim Q\le r-\operatorname{rank} Y.
\]
The first bound is attained precisely when every block of size at least two is active, and the second precisely when condition~\ref{cond:2} holds. Their sum equals $\dim\ker Y=n-\operatorname{rank} Y$ exactly under the two stated conditions. Neither nonzero columns nor full row rank is required.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:replication}
Let $B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}$ and $k\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^r$. Set $Y=B^{(2k)}$ and $n=2\sum_{t=1}^r k_t$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:replication}
\sigma(Y)=\sum_{t=1}^r(2k_t-1)+\dim L_k(B).
\end{equation}
In particular, the sign vectors in $\ker Y$ span $\ker Y$ if and only if $L_k(B)=\ker B$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Let
\[
V=\operatorname{span}(\ker Y\cap\{-1,1\}^n),
\]
and let $C:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^r$ record the sums over the $r$ replication blocks. Then $Y=BC$, and
\[
\ker C
=
\bigoplus_{t=1}^r
\{u\in\mathbb R^{2k_t}:\mathbf1^\top u=0\},
\qquad
\dim\ker C=n-r.
\]

Every sign vector balanced within each block belongs to $\ker Y$. For any two positions in the same block, choose such a vector with opposite signs at those positions and interchange them. The difference of the resulting kernel sign vectors is twice the corresponding coordinate difference. These differences span $\ker C$, so $\ker C\subseteq V$.

The block sums of a sign vector are precisely the vectors $2z$ with $z\in\mathbb Z^r$ and $|z_t|\le k_t$ for every $t$. The additional condition $Yx=0$ is equivalent to $Bz=0$. Hence
\[
C(\ker Y\cap\{-1,1\}^n)
=
\{2z:z\in\ker B\cap\mathbb Z^r,\ |z_t|\le k_t\text{ for every }t\}.
\]
Taking spans removes the common factor two, giving $C(V)=L_k(B)$. Since $\ker C\subseteq V$, the kernel of the restriction $C|_V$ is exactly $\ker C$. Rank--nullity applied to $C|_V$ now yields
\[
\sigma(Y)=\dim V
=
\dim\ker C+\dim L_k(B)
=
n-r+\dim L_k(B),
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:replication}.

Finally, $\operatorname{rank} Y=\operatorname{rank} B$, so $\dim\ker Y=n-\operatorname{rank} B$. Since $L_k(B)\subseteq\ker B$, the equality $\sigma(Y)=\dim\ker Y$ holds precisely when $L_k(B)=\ker B$.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:rational}
For $B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Some positive replication $B^{(m)}$, with $m\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^r$ of arbitrary parity, has a sign-spanned kernel.
\item The subspace $\ker B$ is rational.
\item There is $k^0\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^r$ such that every $B^{(2k)}$ with $k\ge k^0$ coordinatewise has a sign-spanned kernel.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, sufficiently large uniform even replication suffices whenever any positive replication can suffice.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
For a positive replication, let $C$ be the block-sum map, so that $B^{(m)}=BC$. Every block is nonempty, hence $C$ is onto and
\[
C(\ker B^{(m)})=\ker B.
\]
Indeed, any $z\in\ker B$ can be lifted to a vector whose block sums are $z_t$, and every such lift belongs to $\ker(BC)$. If $\ker B^{(m)}$ is spanned by sign vectors, their images under $C$ are integer vectors spanning $\ker B$. Thus (a) implies (b), without a parity assumption.

Suppose now that $\ker B$ is rational. Choose a real vector-space basis consisting of integer vectors $h^{(1)},\ldots,h^{(\ell)}$, where $\ell=\dim\ker B$, and set
\[
k_t^0
=
\max\bigl(\{1\}\cup\{|h_t^{(j)}|:1\le j\le\ell\}\bigr).
\]
For $k\ge k^0$, every basis vector belongs to the defining set of $L_k(B)$, so $L_k(B)=\ker B$. Theorem~\ref{thm:replication} proves (c). The same argument includes the zero kernel by taking an empty basis. Finally, (c) implies (a).

\label{thm:realization}
Let $Z\in\mathbb R^{r\times r}$ be doubly nonnegative with positive diagonal, rank $d$, and rational kernel. For $k\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$, let $n=2kr$ and define
\[
W_{ij}=4(Z\otimes J_{2k})_{ij}\quad(i\ne j),
\qquad
W_{ii}=0.
\]
For every $k\ge1$, the support graph has no isolated vertices, the relaxation is exact, and
\[
Z^*=Z\otimes J_{2k},
\qquad
d(W)=d.
\]
For every $k\ge\kappa(Z)$, moreover,
\[
c(W)=n-d,
\]
so the optimal cut sign vectors span the entire dual kernel. The threshold $\kappa(Z)$ depends only on $\ker Z$. If the off-diagonal support of $Z$ is connected, then the support graph of $W$ is connected for every $k\ge1$.

The matrix $Z\otimes J_{2k}$ is completely positive if and only if $Z$ is. When these matrices are completely positive, their cp-ranks are equal.

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Choose a Gram factor $Z=B^\top B$ with $B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}$, and set $Y=B^{(2k\mathbf1)}$. Then
\[
Y^\top Y=Z\otimes J_{2k},
\qquad
\ker B=\ker Z.
\]
The columns of $B$ are nonzero because $Z$ has positive diagonal, and their pairwise inner products are nonnegative because $Z$ is entrywise nonnegative. Within the replication block corresponding to $b_t$, every edge has weight $4Z_{tt}>0$. Each block has at least two vertices, so the support graph has no isolated vertices. Each nonzero off-diagonal entry of $Z$ joins all vertices in the corresponding pair of blocks; hence connected support of $Z$ gives connected support of $W$.

For every $k\ge1$, choosing equally many positive and negative signs within each block gives a sign vector in $\ker Y$. Corollary~\ref{cor:construction} therefore proves exactness and gives
\[
Z^*=Y^\top Y,
\qquad
c(W)=\sigma(Y).
\]
Also,
\[
d(W)=\operatorname{rank}(Z\otimes J_{2k})=\operatorname{rank} Z=d
\]
for every $k\ge1$. Since $\ker B=\ker Z$ is rational, $\kappa(B)=\kappa(Z)$ is finite. For every $k\ge\kappa(Z)$, the definition of this threshold and Theorem~\ref{thm:replication} give
\[
c(W)=\sigma(Y)=n-\operatorname{rank} B=n-d.
\]

If $Z=N^\top N$ with $N\ge0$, repeating each column of $N$ exactly $2k$ times gives a nonnegative factorization of $Z\otimes J_{2k}$ with the same number of rows. Conversely, selecting one index from each replication block recovers $Z$ as a principal submatrix. Restricting any nonnegative factor to those indices gives a nonnegative factor of $Z$ with no additional rows. These two operations prove both the equivalence of complete positivity and equality of cp-ranks.

\label{thm:hull}

P=\operatorname{span}\bigl(\{\mathbf1\}\cup\{s_j\circ s_k:j<k\}\bigr).

Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:facedim}
\dim F^*(W)=\binom{q+1}{2}-\dim P\ge q-1.
\end{equation}
The following are equivalent: the basis is Schur independent; the face has dimension $q-1$; and $F^*(W)=P^*(W)$. In this case the face is the simplex with vertices $s_js_j^\top$, and the optimal signs are exactly $\pm s_j$. In particular, $1+\binom q2\le n$.

<!-- example-4: body_md -->
\label{ex:supportdistinction}
For product weights $W_{ij}=4v_iv_j$ with a positive $v$ admitting a kernel sign vector, the slack is $vv^\top$ and the face is $F_v=\{X\in\mathcal E_n:Xv=0\}$. When $v=(1,1,2,2)^\top$, the signs satisfy $x_1=-x_2$ and $x_3=-x_4$, so $c(W)=2$. Nevertheless,

\]
is optimal and has eigenvalues $0,1,5/4,7/4$. It has full rank on the three-dimensional dual kernel. Thus strict complementarity does not imply sign-spanning. Hence the face is the single cut matrix associated with $(1,1,1,-1)^\top$, while the dual kernel still has dimension three. Here the face equals its cut hull but strict complementarity fails. This example explains why an algorithm cannot always test the whole dual kernel.

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm:recognition}
Given a rational basis matrix for a subspace $K\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, one can decide in deterministic polynomial time whether $K$ has a Schur-independent sign basis, and recover that basis when it exists. No promise that $K$ contains a sign vector is required.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:structure}
Let $U\in\mathbb Q^{n\times q}$ have full column rank, with $q\ge1$, and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
\mathcal M_U=\{R\in\mathbb S^q:\operatorname{diag}(URU^\top)\in\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf1\}\}.
\end{equation}
If $S=UT$ is a Schur-independent sign basis matrix, then $T\in\mathbb Q^{q\times q}$ is invertible and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diagonalspace}
\mathcal M_U=\{T\operatorname{Diag}(\lambda)T^\top:\lambda\in\mathbb R^q\}.
\end{equation}
In particular, $\dim\mathcal M_U=q$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
The change-of-basis matrix $T$ is invertible, and it is rational because $U$ is rational and $S$ has integer entries. Write $R=TDT^\top$. If $s_j$ is the $j$th column of $S$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diagidentity}
\operatorname{diag}(URU^\top)=(\operatorname{tr} D)\mathbf1+2\sum_{j<k}D_{jk}(s_j\circ s_k).
\end{equation}
Schur independence says that this vector is constant precisely when $D_{jk}=0$ for every $j<k$. This is exactly~\eqref{eq:diagonalspace}.
<!-- end proof-14 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
We assume $n\ge1$. For $K=\{0\}$, we accept the empty basis; its Schur-independence test consists of the single nonzero vector $\mathbf1$. Otherwise choose a rational full-column-rank basis matrix $U\in\mathbb Q^{n\times q}$, where $q\ge1$. The algorithm consists of the following four steps.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step 1: Compute the constant-diagonal matrix space.}
Writing $u_i^\top$ for the $i$th row of $U$, the space in~\eqref{eq:M} is defined by the rational linear equations
\[
u_i^\top Ru_i-u_1^\top Ru_1=0,\qquad 2\le i\le n.
\]
Compute its dimension by Gaussian elimination. Reject if it is not $q$, as justified by Lemma~\ref{lem:structure}. Otherwise choose a rational basis $M_1,\ldots,M_q$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pencil}
M(t)=\sum_{i=1}^q t^{i-1}M_i.
\end{equation}

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step 2: Find an invertible member.}
Test the integers $a=0,1,\ldots,q(q-1)$ and retain the first one for which $A=M(a)$ is invertible. Reject if none exists. To see that rejection cannot occur on a yes-instance, use~\eqref{eq:diagonalspace} to write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polydiagonal}
M(t)=T\operatorname{Diag}(p_1(t),\ldots,p_q(t))T^\top.
\end{equation}
The $p_j\in\mathbb Q[t]$ have degree at most $q-1$ and are linearly independent: the coefficient matrix is invertible because the $M_i$ form a basis of the diagonal-congruence space. In particular, every $p_j$ is nonzero, and
\[
\det M(t)=(\det T)^2\prod_{j=1}^q p_j(t)
\]
is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $q(q-1)$. It cannot vanish at all the tested integers.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step 3: Separate the eigenlines.}
For integers
\[
b=0,1,\ldots,(q-1)\binom q2,
\]
form $C_b=M(b)A^{-1}$ and test whether its characteristic polynomial is square-free. This can be checked by a polynomial gcd with its derivative. Retain the first successful $C_b$, denoted $C$, and reject if none exists.

On a yes-instance,~\eqref{eq:polydiagonal} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eigenlines}
C_b=T\operatorname{Diag}\left(\frac{p_1(b)}{p_1(a)},\ldots,\frac{p_q(b)}{p_q(a)}\right)T^{-1}.
\end{equation}
All denominators are nonzero by the choice of $a$. For $j<k$, a collision of the corresponding eigenvalues occurs only at a root of
\[
p_j(t)p_k(a)-p_k(t)p_j(a).
\]
This polynomial is nonzero, since otherwise $p_j$ and $p_k$ would be proportional. It has degree at most $q-1$. Taking the union over all pairs excludes at most $(q-1)\binom q2$ integers, so one of the tested matrices has distinct eigenvalues. When $q=1$, both searches consist of the single integer zero, and the same argument applies.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step 4: Recover and verify the sign basis.}
Factor the characteristic polynomial of $C$ over $\mathbb Q$. Reject unless every factor is linear. Compute a nonzero rational eigenvector $v_j$ for each of its $q$ distinct eigenvalues. For each $j$, put $y_j=Uv_j$. Reject if its first coordinate is zero; otherwise normalize $x_j=y_j/(y_j)_1$. Accept precisely when every $x_j$ is a sign vector, the matrix $[x_1\ \cdots\ x_q]$ has rank $q$, and the vectors $\mathbf1,x_j\circ x_k$ for $j<k$ have rank $1+\binom q2$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Correctness.} On a yes-instance,~\eqref{eq:eigenlines} has rational eigenvalues and its eigenlines are exactly the column lines of $T$. Thus Step~4 recovers the sign basis up to column permutation and sign changes. This proves completeness. On an arbitrary input, acceptance produces a directly checked Schur-independent sign basis of $K$, proving soundness. In particular, it is harmless that Steps~2 and~3 may reject other types of matrix spaces: only yes-instances are required to pass them.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Bit complexity.} All linear systems have polynomial size in $n$ and $q$. Rational elimination yields basis matrices of polynomial bit length. The tested integers have polynomial magnitude, and their powers in~\eqref{eq:pencil} have polynomial bit length. Determinants, inverses, characteristic polynomials, polynomial gcds, and rational eigenspaces can therefore be computed with polynomial bit complexity. Factoring a univariate polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ has a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm \cite{LLL82}. There are $O(q^2)$ invertibility tests and $O(q^3)$ square-free tests, completing the complexity proof.

<!-- corollary-6: body_md -->
\label{cor:rankone-recognition}
Let $W$ be an exact nonnegatively weighted instance without isolated vertices and $Z^*=vv^\top$ with $v>0$. Then
\[
F^*(W)=P^*(W)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
2\max_i v_i=\sum_i v_i
\quad\text{or}\quad
n=4\text{ and }v_1=v_2=v_3=v_4.
\]
For rational weights, recognition and recovery of all optimal cut matrices on equality instances take deterministic polynomial time, without a strict-complementarity promise.
<!-- end corollary-6 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
A kernel sign vector gives $2\max_i v_i\le\sum_i v_i$. If equality holds at $i$, any optimal Gram representation by unit vectors satisfies
$ v_i u_i=-\sum_{j\ne i}v_j u_j$.
Equality in the triangle inequality forces all $u_j$, $j\ne i$, to coincide and $u_i$ to be their negative. The face is therefore a single cut matrix. This includes $n=2$.

Otherwise $v$ is strictly balanced. The Laurent--Poljak criterion gives an optimal matrix of rank $n-1$ \cite[Proposition~22]{deCarliSilvaTuncel19}: apply it to $b=\operatorname{Diag}(x)v\perp\mathbf1$ for a kernel sign vector $x$, then switch back. If $F^*=P^*$, a convex combination of optimal cut matrices of rank $n-1$ forces their sign vectors to span $v^\perp$. Hence $c(W)=n-1$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:hull} gives $1+\binom{n-1}{2}\le n$, so $n\le4$. At $n=3$, every kernel sign would give a balance equality. At $n=4$, strict balancedness excludes one-versus-three partitions. Three independent kernel signs therefore require all three two-versus-two partitions; their equations force $v_1=v_2=v_3=v_4$. Conversely, the uniform $K_4$ has the triangular optimal face described in Section~\ref{sec:faces}.

For rational input, recover $Z^*$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:slackrecovery} and test the displayed conditions on its positive rational column $Z^*e_1$, which is proportional to $v$. No square roots are needed. The equality cases yield either the single cut separating $i$ from the other vertices or the three balanced cuts of $K_4$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:embedding}
From $B$ one can construct, in polynomial time, an exact rational Max-Cut instance with strictly positive edge weights, an explicit unique optimal cut, and an explicit rational nonnegative Gram factor of its optimal dual slack, such that
\[
F^*(W)=P^*(W)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal T(B)=\varnothing.
\]
The construction uses $n=4(p+2)$ vertices and can have positive integer weights. Moreover, these weights are bounded by $O(n^3)$, with an integer nonnegative Gram factor, if $B$ has the following properties for some integer $1\le q\le p$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item $qB$ is integral, every entry of $B$ has magnitude at most one, every row has at most three nonzero entries, and the first row is a coordinate row;
\item an explicit left inverse $L_BB=I$ has each row either a single coefficient $1$ or two coefficients in $\{-1/2,1/2\}$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
The construction has three parts: remove rank-one solutions from the fitting problem, realize the enlarged system inside a dual kernel, and describe the resulting optimal face.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Excluding additional cuts.} First exclude rank-one solutions of the auxiliary fitting problem without changing feasibility. Let $A\in\mathbb Q^{(p+2)\times(d+1)}$ have rows
\[
(b_i^\top,0)\ (1\le i\le p),\qquad (0,\ldots,0,1),\qquad (\tfrac35 b_1^\top,\tfrac45).
\]
The matrix $A$ has full column rank. If $Q\in\mathcal T(B)$, then $Q\oplus[1]\in\mathcal T(A)$. Conversely, the leading principal block of any member of $\mathcal T(A)$ belongs to $\mathcal T(B)$. Thus the two sets are nonempty simultaneously.

The set $\mathcal T(A)$ contains no rank-one matrix. Indeed, if $vv^\top$ were feasible, the measurements at $(b_1,0)$ and at the last coordinate would give numbers $\varepsilon,\delta\in\{-1,1\}$. The last measurement would require $|(3\varepsilon+4\delta)/5|=1$, whereas its possible absolute values are $7/5$ and $1/5$.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Constructing the certificate.} The paired rows below will separate the known cut coordinate from the fitting coordinates. Their global negatives make the column space orthogonal to $\mathbf1_n$, allowing an all-ones term to make the certificate entrywise positive without changing its kernel. Put $M=p+2$, $n=4M$, and form
\[
C=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf1_M&A\\ \mathbf1_M&-A\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
U=\begin{pmatrix}C\\-C\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
x=Ue_1=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf1_{2M}\\-\mathbf1_{2M}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The matrix $U$ has full column rank and $U^\top\mathbf1_n=0$. Choose a rational left inverse $L_UU=I$, and an integer $a>0$ clearing the denominators of $I-UL_U$. Define
\[
R_0=a(I-UL_U),\qquad h=1+\max_{i,j}|(R_0)_{ij}|,\qquad
N=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf1_n^\top\\R_0+hJ_n\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Then $R_0$ is integral with $\ker R_0=\operatorname{range} U$, and $N$ is entrywise positive. Its first row forces zero coordinate sum, after which the remaining rows impose $R_0v=0$. Hence $\ker N=\operatorname{range} U$. Set $Z=N^\top N$, $W_{ij}=4Z_{ij}$ off the diagonal, and $W_{ii}=0$. This gives positive integer weights on a complete graph. Taking $s_i=Z_{ii}+\tfrac14\sum_jW_{ij}$ makes $Z$ dual feasible; $Zx=0$ proves exactness and identifies the optimal slack.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Controlling the integer weights.} Under (a)--(b), a left inverse $L_A$ uses $L_B$ on the first $p$ rows and the new unit row for the final coordinate. To form $L_U$, recover the cut coordinate by the half-sum of a paired row $(1,a_i^\top)$ and $(1,-a_i^\top)$; recover the other coordinates by applying $L_A$ to their half-differences. Thus $L_UU=I$, its entries are multiples of $1/4$ of magnitude at most $1/2$, and each row has at most four nonzero entries. Every row of $U$ has at most four entries of magnitude at most one, and $5qU$ is integral. Therefore the choice $a=20q$ makes $R_0$ integral and gives
\[
|(UL_U)_{ij}|\le2,\qquad |(R_0)_{ij}|\le60q,\qquad
N_{ij}\le120q+1.
\]
Since $q\le p<n/4$ and $N$ has $n+1$ rows, $W_{ij}=O(nq^2)=O(n^3)$. The direct left inverse avoids determinant denominators, whose magnitudes need not be polynomial even when their bit lengths are.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Describing the face.} Each $X\inF^*(W)$ has a unique representation $X=URU^\top$ with $R\succeq0$. Write
\[
R=\begin{pmatrix}t&h^\top\\h&H\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The rows $(1,a_i^\top)$ and $(1,-a_i^\top)$ of $C$ give
\[
t+2a_i^\top h+a_i^\top Ha_i=1,
\qquad
t-2a_i^\top h+a_i^\top Ha_i=1.
\]
Since $A$ has full column rank, these equations imply $h=0$. Consequently
\[
t\ge0,\qquad H\succeq0,\qquad a_i^\top Ha_i=1-t\quad\text{for every }i.
\]
In particular $0\le t\le1$. If $t=1$, then $AHA^\top$ is PSD with zero diagonal, hence zero; full column rank gives $H=0$. If $t<1$, then $H/(1-t)\in\mathcal T(A)$. We therefore have the exact description
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pyramid}
F^*(W)=\{xx^\top\}\ \cup\ \left\{U\bigl([1-\tau]\oplus(\tau H)\bigr)U^\top:0<\tau\le1,\ H\in\mathcal T(A)\right\}.
\end{equation}
Because $U$ has full column rank, congruence by $U$ preserves the rank of the coefficient matrix. For $0<\tau<1$ that rank is $1+\operatorname{rank} H$, and for $\tau=1$ it is $\operatorname{rank} H$. Every $H\in\mathcal T(A)$ has rank at least two, so every matrix in the second set does as well. Thus $xx^\top$ is always the only rank-one optimum, whether or not $\mathcal T(A)$ is empty. The optimal cut sign vectors are exactly $\pm x$, and~\eqref{eq:pyramid} proves the equivalence.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Encoding size.} For general rational $B$, a left inverse is computable by rational elimination. The entries of $L_U$ and $I-UL_U$ have polynomial bit length; so do a common denominator $a$, $R_0,h,N,Z$, and $W$. Under (a)--(b), the explicit formulas above give the stronger magnitude bound. No algebraic numbers or feasible fitting matrices are computed.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:bounded}
There is a deterministic polynomial-time feasibility-preserving transformation from \eqref{eq:sdf} to a system of order $D$ of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bounded}
Q\succeq0,\qquad \operatorname{tr} Q\le1,\qquad
\langle C_i,Q\rangle=d_i\quad(1\le i\le l).
\end{equation}
The entries of $C_i$ and the numbers $d_i$ can be integers of magnitude $O(D)$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
\emph{Small coefficients.} Clear denominators in each source equality, and express its integer linear form by binary addition and subtraction circuits on the matrix entries and an anchor equal to one. A coefficient $a$ takes $O(1+\log(1+|a|))$ gates. A doubling can use a separately named equal copy, so each gate has distinct inputs and coefficients $0,\pm1$. Represent every unrestricted auxiliary scalar $z$ by nonnegative scalar blocks $z^+,z^-$ with $z=z^+-z^-$. Combine these with the original PSD variable in a block-diagonal matrix. Gate equations, off-block zeros, and the anchor equation preserve feasibility in both directions: evaluate the circuits to extend a source solution, and substitute to recover it. Converting entry equations to trace inner products and multiplying by two gives integer coefficients and constants of bounded absolute magnitude. Relabel this equivalent system as~\eqref{eq:sdf}.

\emph{A positive scale.} Obtain a sufficiently large bound on \emph{one} feasible point. Write $X=VV^\top$ with $V\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$. Clear denominators in each equation $\langle A_i,VV^\top\rangle=b_i$ and sum the squares of the resulting integer polynomials. This gives an integer polynomial $f$ of degree at most four in $n^2$ variables and with polynomial coefficient bit length. Its real zero set is nonempty exactly when \eqref{eq:sdf} is feasible. It can be expanded in polynomial time: a fixed-degree polynomial in $n^2$ variables has only polynomially many monomials, and clearing denominators increases coefficient bit lengths by at most the sum of the input denominator lengths. In the identically zero case output the scalar bounded system $Q=0$; in the nonzero constant case output $Q=-1$, $Q\succeq0$, $\operatorname{tr} Q\le1$. We may assume $n\ge1$, since a zero-order source system is decided by its scalar equalities.

The explicit algebraic sampling-radius bound of Basu and Roy \cite[Theorem~2]{BasuRoy10} gives, from the degree, number of variables and coefficient bits of $f$, a polynomially computable integer $h$ of polynomial magnitude such that, if the source is feasible, it has a solution with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hugebound}
\operatorname{tr} X\le 2^{2^h}-1.
\end{equation}
To compute $h$, write $\operatorname{bit}(a)$ for binary length, with $\operatorname{bit}(0)=1$. The squared-radius bound is $C2^E$, where $C,E$ have polynomial bit length at degree at most four. Choose $h=\operatorname{bit}(E+\operatorname{bit}(C)+2)$; then $C2^E<2^{2^h}-1$, and a zero $V$ in that ball has $\operatorname{tr}(VV^\top)=\|V\|_F^2\le C2^E$. Only $h$ is output. Remark~\ref{rem:radius} gives the explicit formulas.

Introduce a symmetric matrix $H$ and scalars $t,z_0,\ldots,z_h$. Impose
\begin{align}
&H\succeq0,\qquad \langle A_i,H\rangle=t b_i\quad(1\le i\le m),\label{eq:hom1}\\
&\operatorname{tr} H+t\le1,\qquad t\ge z_h,\qquad z_0=\tfrac12,\qquad 0\le z_j\le1,\label{eq:hom2}\\
&\begin{pmatrix}z_{j+1}&z_j\\z_j&1\end{pmatrix}\succeq0
\quad(0\le j<h).\label{eq:hom3}
\end{align}
These are affine semidefinite constraints. They imply $z_{j+1}\ge z_j^2$, hence
\[
t\ge z_h\ge2^{-2^h}>0.
\]
Consequently any feasible solution gives a source solution $X=H/t$.

Conversely, choose a source solution satisfying \eqref{eq:hugebound}. Set
\[
t=\frac1{1+\operatorname{tr} X},\qquad H=tX,\qquad z_j=2^{-2^j}.
\]
Then $t\ge z_h$ and all displayed constraints hold. These real numbers establish existence; the transformation does not output them.

The reverse implication uses only $t>0$, so infeasible systems, including weakly infeasible ones, cannot acquire a feasible solution through homogenization. The sampling bound is needed only for the forward implication.

To obtain a single PSD matrix variable, use the block diagonal matrix comprising $H$, the $2\times2$ matrices in \eqref{eq:hom3}, and scalar blocks $t$, $1-\operatorname{tr} H-t$, $t-z_h$, $z_j$, and $1-z_j$. Its order is $D=n+4h+5$. Impose the affine equations matching the repeated entries and all off-block zeros. The trace inequality bounds $t$ and each diagonal entry of $H$ by one. The chain variables lie in $[0,1]$, and each remaining scalar slack lies there as well. Thus every diagonal entry of this block matrix is between zero and one, so its trace is at most $D$. All affine matching equations have coefficients and constants of bounded absolute magnitude and a common denominator dividing two. Substitute the block matrix $DQ$ and clear this denominator. This gives~\eqref{eq:bounded} with integer coefficients of magnitude $O(D)$. The trace bound was already implied before normalization. All dimensions are polynomial in the source input length.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:gadgets}
The following operations can be implemented by a constant number of unit-norm measurements using fresh Gram coordinates.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item Enforce $\|u\|^2\le1$ by a fresh padding coordinate $p$ and the two constraints $\|u+p\|^2=\|u-p\|^2=1$.
\item Copy a squared norm from a rational linear form $v$ into a fresh scalar coordinate $a$ using a fresh padding coordinate $p$ and
\[
\|v\pm p\|^2=1,\qquad \|a\pm p\|^2=1.
\]
These force $\|a\|^2=\|v\|^2\le1$.
\item For distinct scalar coordinates $a,b$, introduce fresh $c,p$ and impose
\[
\|\varepsilon a+\delta b+p\|^2=1\quad(\varepsilon,\delta\in\{-1,1\}),
\qquad \|c\pm p\|^2=1.
\]
These force $\|c\|^2=\|a\|^2+\|b\|^2\le1$.
\item Equal squared norms of scalar coordinates $a,c$, each at most one, can be imposed by $\|a\pm p\|^2=\|c\pm p\|^2=1$ with a fresh $p$.
\end{enumerate}
In a forward construction all auxiliary scalar coordinates can be chosen mutually orthogonal and orthogonal to the original Gram configuration. In (c), orthogonality of $a$ and $b$ is an additional enforced condition: the gadget is used on freely chosen auxiliary copies, not on arbitrary prescribed original vectors.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
Adding the two equations in (a) gives $\|u\|^2+\|p\|^2=1$, and subtracting gives $\langle u,p\rangle=0$. This also proves (b) and (d). Averaging the four equations in (c) gives
\[
\|a\|^2+\|b\|^2+\|p\|^2=1;
\]
weighting them by $\varepsilon$, $\delta$ and $\varepsilon\delta$ shows that the three cross inner products vanish. The last pair gives $\|c\|^2+\|p\|^2=1$. Conversely choose each fresh scalar coordinate in a new orthogonal direction, with its required nonnegative squared norm. For (c), the padding squared norm is $1-\|a\|^2-\|b\|^2$. Thus the forward construction works whenever the stated sums are at most one.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:fitting}
From the integer-coefficient system~\eqref{eq:bounded} supplied by Lemma~\ref{lem:bounded}, one can construct in deterministic polynomial time a full-column-rank rational matrix $B$ satisfying (a)--(b) of Lemma~\ref{lem:embedding}, such that
\[
\mathcal T(B)\ne\varnothing
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\eqref{eq:bounded}\text{ is feasible}.
\]
Restriction to the original Gram coordinates maps $\mathcal T(B)$ onto the source feasible set. A rational affine lift of every source feasible matrix is available.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
We first enforce the trace bound, then encode each affine equation by copying and adding squared norms.

\emph{Enforcing the trace bound.} Use original Gram coordinates $u_1,\ldots,u_D$ for $Q$, and an anchor $u_0$ with $\|u_0\|^2=1$. Add the bound gadget for each $u_i$. Copy each $\|u_i\|^2$ into a fresh scalar coordinate and add them successively with the addition gadget. All intermediate sums are at most one exactly as required by $\operatorname{tr} Q\le1$; add the bound gadget to the final sum coordinate. For $D=1$ this last bound is already sufficient. If $s_j$ denotes the auxiliary coordinate representing the first $j$ squared norms, the gadget identities give $\|s_j\|^2=\sum_{i\le j}\|u_i\|^2$. Bounding the last sum therefore enforces exactly the trace inequality. The copied coordinates may be orthogonal even when the original $u_i$ are not, because a copy constrains only a squared norm. No orthogonality is imposed between the original coordinates themselves.

\emph{Expressing an affine equation through squared norms.} Consider $\langle C,Q\rangle=d_0$. The identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polar}
\langle C,Q\rangle
=\sum_i\left(C_{ii}-\sum_{j\ne i}C_{ij}\right)\|u_i\|^2
 +\sum_{i<j}C_{ij}\|u_i+u_j\|^2
\end{equation}
expresses its left side using rational multiples of squared norms. Move $d_0\|u_0\|^2$ to the left and separate the positive and negative coefficients.

All coefficients are integers. Expand a coefficient of absolute value $c$ into $c$ occurrences, separating positive and negative sides. Every occurrence is $u_i$, $u_i+u_j$, or $u_0$. If the source order is $D$, its coefficient bound is $H_0=O(D)$, and it has $l$ equations, the total number $M$ of occurrences is at most $l(2D^2+1)H_0$. Thus this expansion is polynomial. Each equation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sum-norms}
\sum_{v\in\mathcal V_+}\|v\|^2=\sum_{v\in\mathcal V_-}\|v\|^2.
\end{equation}

\emph{One small denominator.} Let $T$ be the sum of squared Euclidean norms of the coefficient rows of all occurrences, across all equations. Then $M\le T\le2M$. Put $q=1+T$ and replace every occurrence $v$ by $v/q$. Since $Q\preceq I$, the combined squared norms of all these scaled forms are at most $T/(1+T)^2\le1/4$. This estimate does not require orthogonality between the anchor and original coordinates: no occurrence mixes the two. Every addition therefore remains bounded by one, and every coefficient is in $\{0,\pm1,\pm1/q\}$. For $M=0$, take $q=1$.

\emph{Implementing the equation.} Copy every scaled squared norm to a distinct fresh scalar coordinate. Add each list by successive addition gadgets, and impose equality of the resulting two squared norms using (d). For an empty list introduce a zero coordinate $z$ by $\|u_0\pm z\|^2=1$, which forces $\|z\|^2=0$. A list of length one needs no addition. In an addition chain, the accumulated sum coordinate and the next copied coordinate are distinct auxiliary coordinates; their forced orthogonality therefore imposes no extra condition on the original Gram matrix. A completely zero equation can instead be omitted. Repeat for every source equation.

\emph{Soundness.} Given a feasible Gram matrix for the constructed measurements, its original principal block $Q$ is PSD. The trace gadgets give $\operatorname{tr} Q\le1$. Copies and addition gadgets give exactly \eqref{eq:sum-norms}, hence all source equations by \eqref{eq:polar}.

\emph{Completeness and affine lift.} Given a source solution $Q$, start with the Gram matrix $[1]\oplus Q$. Give every auxiliary coordinate its prescribed squared norm and put it in a new orthogonal direction, orthogonal to all original coordinates and all other auxiliary coordinates. Every padding value is nonnegative by the trace bound and the scaling above. Thus every measurement is one. Equivalently, the lifted Gram matrix is the block diagonal sum of $[1]$, $Q$, and scalar blocks which are affine rational functions of $Q$. This proves the asserted affine lift and rationality preservation.

\emph{Size and explicit left inverse.} Let $p$ be the number of measurement rows. Each occurrence uses four copy measurements, so $p\ge1+4M\ge q$. Each row has at most three nonzero entries, and the anchor is the first row. Every coordinate has a direct expression in the rows: the original bound pairs isolate their coordinate and padding by half-sums and half-differences; the pairs $a\pm p$ do the same for a copied coordinate and its padding; the pairs $c\pm p$ isolate a sum coordinate and its padding. Equality pairs isolate their padding, and $u_0\pm z$ isolates a zero coordinate. These identities give $L_BB=I$ with each row as in Lemma~\ref{lem:embedding}(b), including on infeasible inputs. All dimensions are $O(D+M+l)$ and all coefficients have polynomial bit length. The corresponding affine-lift construction for arbitrary rational coefficients is recorded in Remark~\ref{rem:rational-fitting}.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:unweight}
Let $W$ be an exact instance with nonnegative integer weights and a known optimal cut $x$. Put
\[
\Delta=\max_i\sum_jW_{ij},\qquad r=1+\max_{i,j}W_{ij},\qquad \ell=2\Delta+1.
\]
One can construct a simple graph $G$ on $n(r+\ell)$ vertices whose optimal face is affinely isomorphic to $F^*(W)$. The map preserves rank and all optimal cut matrices, and supplies an optimal cut of $G$. Connected support of $W$ gives connected $G$. Construction time is polynomial in $n$ and the numerical weights.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-23: body_md -->
\emph{The graph.} For each vertex $i$, create $r$ copies $C_i$ and $\ell$ auxiliary vertices $D_i$, and join $C_i$ completely to $D_i$. Between $C_i$ and $C_j$ put a $W_{ij}$-regular simple bipartite graph, using the first $W_{ij}$ cyclic perfect matchings on the two sets of size $r$. There are no other edges. Set $\widehat x=x_i$ on $C_i$ and $\widehat x=-x_i$ on $D_i$.

\emph{The exact kernel.} For signs $z$ and weights $V$, write the switched certificate as
\[
S(V,z)=\operatorname{Diag}(-z\circ(Vz))+\operatorname{Diag}(z)V\operatorname{Diag}(z)=4\operatorname{Diag}(z)Z_z\operatorname{Diag}(z).
\]
The old certificate $S=S(W,x)$ is PSD. Decompose a vector $y$ on the new vertices into means $a_i$ on $C_i$, $b_i$ on $D_i$, and zero-sum deviations $\eta_i,\zeta_i$ on those sets. The internal edges cross $\widehat x$, so their contribution is
\[
r\ell\|a-b\|^2+\ell\|\eta\|^2+r\|\zeta\|^2.
\]
For the external edges, every copy of $i$ has diagonal entry $-x_i\sum_jW_{ij}x_j$ and $W_{ij}$ neighbors in $C_j$. Thus their switched certificate $E$ acts as $S$ on block means, contributing $r a^\top Sa$. Regularity and symmetry separate this invariant subspace from the zero-sum deviations. Moreover, each absolute diagonal entry and off-diagonal absolute row sum of $E$ is at most $\Delta$, so $\|E\|_2\le2\Delta$. Consequently the new switched certificate satisfies
\[
y^\top\widehat Sy\ge r a^\top Sa+r\ell\|a-b\|^2
 +(\ell-2\Delta)\|\eta\|^2+r\|\zeta\|^2\ge0.
\]
Because $\ell-2\Delta=1$, equality holds exactly when $\eta=\zeta=0$, $a=b$, and $a\in\ker S$; substituting these conditions also proves the reverse inclusion. Hence $\widehat x$ is optimal and the new instance is exact.

\emph{All optima correspond.} Define $T$ by placing $u_i$ on $C_i$ and $-u_i$ on $D_i$. Unswitching gives $\ker Z_G^*=T\ker Z_W^*$. Thus $X\mapsto TXT^\top$ lifts every old optimum to a new one. Conversely, $T^\top T=(r+\ell)I$, so $L=(r+\ell)^{-1}T^\top$ is a left inverse. For a new optimum $\widehat X$, the matrix $X=L\widehat XL^\top$ is PSD, supported on $\ker Z_W^*$, and satisfies $TXT^\top=\widehat X$. Its diagonal is one, as seen on any copy of each vertex. This proves surjectivity. Full column rank of $T$ proves injectivity and rank preservation; rank-one unit-diagonal PSD matrices are exactly cut matrices. Each $C_i\cup D_i$ is connected, and old support edges join these sets, giving the connectedness assertion.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:oracle}
There is a deterministic polynomial-time $\mathsf{SDF}$-oracle algorithm for $\mathsf{CH}$. It uses $O(n^2)$ feasibility queries. On a yes-instance, it returns all optimal cuts up to global sign.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-26: body_md -->
First compute $Z^*$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:slackrecovery}, so that $X\inF^*(W)$ is given by the rational constraints $X\succeq0$, $\operatorname{diag} X=\mathbf1$, and $Z^*X=0$. Start with an empty list $S$. Throughout the algorithm, its columns are independent optimal signs. During a prefix search, feasibility of the current prefix together with the outside-support condition is the invariant. Repeat the following steps.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item Test Schur independence of the current list by rational rank computation. If it fails, return no. The empty list passes.
\item Ask whether $X\inF^*(W)$ and \eqref{eq:outside} are feasible, using \eqref{eq:strict}. If not, return yes with the list $S$.
\item Otherwise initialize an empty first-row prefix. For $j=2,\ldots,n$, test feasibility with $X_{1j}=-1$ added to the accumulated prefix and the outside-support condition. If feasible, retain $-1$. If infeasible, test $+1$ instead and retain it if feasible. If neither endpoint is feasible, return no.
\item After all $n-1$ choices, put $x_1=1$ and $x_j=X_{1j}$ as chosen. Append $x$ to $S$ and repeat.
\end{enumerate}

All successful endpoint choices preserve feasibility. A PSD unit-diagonal matrix whose first-row entries are all signs must be $xx^\top$: in a unit-vector Gram representation, equality in Cauchy--Schwarz gives $v_j=x_jv_1$. Hence the vector appended in step~4 is an optimal cut, and \eqref{eq:outside} makes it independent of the previous columns. There are at most $n$ append operations. Notice that the algorithm needs only a yes/no answer to each query: after a successful prefix search, the signs themselves specify the cut, so no real feasible matrix needs to be extracted from the oracle.

\emph{A no answer is sound.} If the list fails Schur independence, the classical cut-face characterization rules out $F^*(W)=P^*(W)$. If an endpoint extension fails on both signs, Lemma~\ref{lem:endpoints} rules out that equality. These are the only ways to return no.

\emph{A yes answer is sound and returns all cuts.} When step~2 returns no, every $X\inF^*(W)$ is supported on $\operatorname{range} S$. The list cannot be empty, since $\operatorname{tr} X=n$ on the nonempty face. Thus write $X=SRS^\top$ with $R\succeq0$. Schur independence and
\[
\operatorname{diag}(SRS^\top)=(\operatorname{tr} R)\mathbf1+2\sum_{i<j}R_{ij}(s_i\circ s_j)=\mathbf1
\]
force $R$ to be diagonal with trace one. Consequently $F^*(W)$ is exactly the convex hull of the listed optimal cut matrices. Since $S$ has independent columns, the rank of $S\operatorname{Diag}(\lambda)S^\top$ is the number of positive coefficients. Its only rank-one members are the listed vertices, proving complete recovery.

\emph{Termination and encoding.} Each successful iteration adds an independent sign, and when $S$ spans $\mathbb R^n$ step~2 is infeasible. Each iteration uses one support query and at most $2(n-1)$ endpoint queries. The Gram matrix $S^\top S$ has integer entries of magnitude at most $n$, so its inverse and $Q_S$ have polynomial bit length. Every query is a polynomial-size rational SDP; all remaining work is polynomial-time rational linear algebra. No bound on the bit length of a feasible answer to an oracle query is used.

For the supplied unique optimal cut $x$, Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate} gives the optimal slack $Z^*=Z_x=\operatorname{Diag}(W(x)/2)-L_W/4$. Put $Q_x=I-xx^\top/n$. The condition $F^*(W)=\{xx^\top\}$ holds exactly when no $X\inF^*(W)$ has $\langle Q_x,X\rangle>0$. Indeed, a feasible PSD unit-diagonal matrix supported on $\operatorname{span}\{x\}$ is exactly $xx^\top$. Encode positivity by \eqref{eq:strict}. Thus $\mathsf{CH}_1\le_m^p\overline{\mathsf{SDF}}$, and the exact-alternative transformation gives $\mathsf{CH}_1\le_m^p\mathsf{SDF}$.

Part (ii) is Theorem~\ref{thm:oracle}. The many-one lower bound and the oracle upper bound give the equivalence of polynomial-time solvability.

If $\mathsf{CH}\in\mathsf{NP}$, then $\mathsf{SDF}\in\mathsf{NP}$ by the many-one reduction; by \eqref{eq:ramana}, $\mathsf{SDF}\in\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$. For every language $L\in\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$,

\]
For clarity, an accepting computation of a deterministic polynomial-time oracle machine can be certified by listing its polynomially many queries, their answers, and an appropriate NP certificate for each yes or no answer. \label{thm:hadamard}
For an exact instance with nonnegative weights and no isolated vertices, $o(W)\le n-1$. Equality holds if and only if a Hadamard matrix of order $n$ exists and $W=\lambda A(K_n)$ for some $\lambda>0$.

<!-- proposition-7: body_md -->
\label{prop:cofactor}
Suppose that $n\ge2$, $v\in\mathbb R_{>0}^n$, and $v^\perp$ is spanned by sign vectors. Then the positive normal direction has a unique primitive integer representative $p\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^n$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:detbound}
\max_i p_i
\le D_{01}(n-2)
\le
\frac{(n-1)^{(n-1)/2}}{2^{n-2}}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for each fixed $n$, only finitely many such positive directions exist, even before identifying vertex permutations.
<!-- end proposition-7 -->
<!-- proof-32: body_md -->
For $n=2$, a sign vector orthogonal to $v>0$ forces $v_1=v_2$, so the primitive representative is $(1,1)$ and the bound holds. Suppose $n\ge3$, and form a matrix $A\in\{-1,1\}^{(n-1)\times n}$ from independent signs spanning $v^\perp$. Its signed maximal minors $\eta_i=(-1)^i\det A_{\widehat i}$ form a nonzero integer vector in $\ker A=\operatorname{span}\{v\}$. Thus all coordinates are nonzero and have a common sign. Choose that sign positive and divide by $g=\gcd(\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_n)$ to obtain the unique positive primitive representative $p$.

For any sign matrix $M$ of order $n-1$, row and column sign changes make its first row and column all ones without changing its absolute determinant. Subtracting the first row from each other row and expanding along the first column then gives $|\det M|=2^{n-2}|\det N|$ for a binary matrix $N$ of order $n-2$. Consequently every $\eta_i$ is divisible by $2^{n-2}$, and
\[
g\ge2^{n-2},\qquad |\eta_i|\le2^{n-2}D_{01}(n-2),\qquad p_i\le D_{01}(n-2).
\]

Conversely, every binary matrix $N$ of order $n-2$ can be bordered to give
\[
M_N=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf1^\top\\\mathbf1&J_{n-2}-2N\end{pmatrix},
\qquad |\det M_N|=2^{n-2}|\det N|.
\]
Each row of $M_N$ has norm $\sqrt{n-1}$, so Hadamard's inequality bounds this determinant by $(n-1)^{(n-1)/2}$. Maximizing over $N$ proves the second inequality. The coordinate bound leaves finitely many primitive representatives.

<!-- proposition-8: body_md -->
\label{prop:binarydimension}
Let $F\subseteq\{0,1\}^n$ be nonempty and invariant under independent permutations within blocks $V_1,\ldots,V_r$ of sizes $m_1,\ldots,m_r$. Let $\pi$ record block sums, put $S_F=\pi(F)$, and call block $t$ active if $0<s_t<m_t$ for some $s\in S_F$. Then
\[
\dim\operatorname{conv} F=\sum_{t\text{ active}}(m_t-1)+\dim\operatorname{conv} S_F.
\]
<!-- end proposition-8 -->
<!-- proof-33: body_md -->
Put $L=\operatorname{span}(F-F)$, and let $K_t$ be the zero-sum space supported on block $t$. In an active block, exchanging a zero and a one generates its coordinate differences, so $K_t\subseteq L$. Every vector of $L$ is constant on each inactive block. Therefore
\[
L\cap\ker\pi=\bigoplus_{t\text{ active}}K_t,
\qquad \pi(L)=\operatorname{span}(S_F-S_F).
\]
Rank--nullity gives the formula.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:knapsack}
Proposition~\ref{prop:binarydimension} recovers the following result of Lee \cite[Theorem~2.1(a)]{Lee97}. Let $b\ge2$ be an integer, let the sets $R_1,\ldots,R_b$ partition $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, allowing empty sets, and suppose that $|R_1|\ge b+1$. Then
\[
P_b=\operatorname{conv}\left\{x\in\{0,1\}^n:
\sum_{i=1}^b i\sum_{j\in R_i}x_j=b\right\}
\]
has dimension $n-1$.

To verify this specialization, write $m_i=|R_i|$, let $I=\{i:m_i>0\}$, and set $r=|I|$. The count patterns
\[
be_1,\qquad (b-i)e_1+e_i\quad(i\in I\setminus\{1\})
\]
are feasible and affinely independent. All count patterns satisfy $\sum_{i\in I}is_i=b$, so their convex hull has dimension $r-1$. The first pattern makes block $1$ active, since $0<b<m_1$. For every other block of size at least two, the corresponding pattern has the interior count $s_i=1$. Thus all blocks of size at least two are active. Proposition~\ref{prop:binarydimension} gives
\[
\dim P_b=\sum_{i\in I}(m_i-1)+(r-1)=(n-r)+(r-1)=n-1.
\]
This calculation uses precisely the hypotheses stated above; it does not assert full dimensionality for general equality-knapsack polytopes.

Suppose $0<s<r$, and choose a nonsingular $s$-column submatrix $A_I$. For each $j\notin I$, define $h^{(j)}\in\mathbb Z^r$ by
\[
h_j^{(j)}=\det A_I,
\qquad
h_I^{(j)}=-\operatorname{adj}(A_I)A_j,
\]
with all remaining coordinates zero. Then $Ah^{(j)}=0$. Each coordinate is, up to sign, an $s\times s$ minor of $A$, so
\[
\|h^{(j)}\|_\infty\le\Delta(A).
\]
The vectors $h^{(j)}$, $j\notin I$, are independent because their coordinates outside $I$ have disjoint nonzero supports. They form a real basis of $\ker A$, proving the bound.

The zero subspace needs no generators, while $\mathbb R^r$ is spanned by its coordinate vectors, so both extreme cases have threshold one. If $B$ is totally unimodular, select independent rows to obtain a full-row-rank matrix $A$ with the same kernel. In the nontrivial case, $\Delta(A)=1$, and the bound applies.

\label{thm:arithmetic}
Given positive binary integers $a_1,\ldots,a_m,b$, one can construct in deterministic polynomial time an exact rational positively weighted complete graph on $n=16m+20$ vertices, its unique optimal cut $xx^\top$, and a rational nonnegative matrix $N$ whose Gram matrix is the optimal slack $Z^*$. <!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:roots}
For positive integers $a_1,\ldots,a_m,b$, one can construct a full-column-rank rational matrix $B$ with $4m+3$ rows and $2m+1$ columns such that
\[
\mathcal T(B)\ne\varnothing\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{i=1}^m\sqrt{a_i}\ge b.
\]
Furthermore, $\mathcal T(B)$ contains a positive definite matrix if and only if the inequality is strict.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-37: body_md -->
Index the coordinates by $0$ and $(i,\varepsilon)$, where $1\le i\le m$ and $\varepsilon\in\{+,-\}$. Let $e_0$ be the first coordinate vector, let $f_{i,\varepsilon}$ denote the remaining coordinate vectors, and put $z=\sum_{i,\varepsilon}f_{i,\varepsilon}$. For a positive rational $c$, set
\[
h_c=\frac{c-1}{c+1},\qquad g_c=\frac{2}{c+1},\qquad h_c^2+c g_c^2=1.
\]
The rows of $B$ are
\[
e_0^\top;\qquad
(h_{a_i}e_0\pm g_{a_i}f_{i,\varepsilon})^\top\quad(i,\varepsilon);
\qquad
(h_{4b^2}e_0\pm g_{4b^2}z)^\top.
\]
There are $1+4m+2$ rows. Their differences, together with $e_0$, span every coordinate direction, so $B$ has full column rank. The entries have polynomial bit length in the original integers.

For $R\in\mathcal T(B)$, the first measurement gives $R_{00}=1$. Adding the two measurements in each pair shows that the principal block $Q$ on the other $2m$ coordinates satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gramcondition}
Q\succeq0,\qquad Q_{(i,\varepsilon),(i,\varepsilon)}=a_i,
\qquad \mathbf1^\top Q\mathbf1=4b^2.
\end{equation}
Conversely, any $Q$ satisfying~\eqref{eq:gramcondition} gives $[1]\oplus Q\in\mathcal T(B)$. The possible additional equations on the first row cause no difficulty, since this block-diagonal choice satisfies all of them.

Represent $Q$ as a Gram matrix of vectors $v_{i,+},v_{i,-}$ of norm $\sqrt{a_i}$. The last equality in~\eqref{eq:gramcondition} says
\[
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^m(v_{i,+}+v_{i,-})\right\|=2b.
\]
The triangle inequality gives $b\le S:=\sum_i\sqrt{a_i}$. Conversely, if $0<b\le S$, take orthonormal vectors $u,w$, put $\theta=b/S$, and choose
\[
v_{i,\pm}=\sqrt{a_i}\bigl(\theta u\pm\sqrt{1-\theta^2}\,w\bigr).
\]
Their norms and sum give~\eqref{eq:gramcondition}. These algebraic vectors are used only to establish existence; they are not computed or included in the reduction.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Positive definite feasibility.} To distinguish the strict comparison from its boundary, let $D=\operatorname{Diag}(a_1,a_1,\ldots,a_m,a_m)$ and let $Q_-$ be block diagonal with blocks $a_i\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. Finally let $Q_+=ww^\top$, where here $w=(\sqrt{a_1},\sqrt{a_1},\ldots,\sqrt{a_m},\sqrt{a_m})^\top$. All three matrices have the required diagonal, and
\[
\mathbf1^\top Q_-\mathbf1=0,\qquad \mathbf1^\top D\mathbf1=2\sum_i a_i,
\qquad \mathbf1^\top Q_+\mathbf1=4S^2.
\]
Every number strictly between $0$ and $4S^2$ is attained by $D$ itself or by a convex combination of $D$ with $Q_-$ or $Q_+$ in which $D$ has positive coefficient. That combination is positive definite. Thus $b<S$ gives a positive definite $Q$ in~\eqref{eq:gramcondition} and a positive definite member of $\mathcal T(B)$. If $b=S$, equality in the triangle inequality forces all $2m$ Gram vectors to have the same direction. Every feasible $Q$ then has rank one, so no feasible $R$ is positive definite. If $b>S$, the set is empty.

Apply the projector normalization above to the matrix from Lemma~\ref{lem:roots}. Its order is $n=4((4m+3)+2)=16m+20$. The equality criterion and uniqueness follow from Lemma~\ref{lem:embedding}; the bit, weight, and objective bounds follow from the projector normalization.

<!-- corollary-10: body_md -->
\label{cor:srs}
There is a polynomial-time many-one reduction $\mathrm{SRS}_{\le}\le_p\mathsf{CH}$, even for the restricted inputs in Theorem~\ref{thm:arithmetic}. Consequently, $\mathsf{CH}\in\mathsf P$ would imply $\mathrm{SRS}_{\le}\in\mathsf P$, and $\mathsf{CH}\in\mathsf{NP}$ would imply $\mathrm{SRS}_{\le}\in\mathsf{NP}$.

If every $a_i$ is a perfect square, integer square roots decide the comparison, and we output a fixed yes- or no-instance. <!-- proof-40: body_md -->
We encode a certified computation of the oracle algorithm in Theorem~\ref{thm:oracle}. There are two issues: certifying either answer to each SDP query, and ensuring that the answers describe one consistent adaptive computation.

For a rational SDP $D$, feasibility has a polynomial-size system of real polynomial equations: write each PSD matrix as $VV^\top$ and substitute into the affine constraints. Rational denominators can be cleared. Apply the same construction to Ramana's exact alternative for $D$ to obtain a polynomial-size system certifying infeasibility of $D$. Thus there are uniformly constructible systems
\[
f_D(u)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\text{ is feasible},
\qquad
g_D(v)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\text{ is infeasible},
\]
where the equivalences mean existence of the displayed real witnesses. The second equivalence includes weak infeasibility.

Fix the input and guess the oracle-answer bits $a_1,\ldots,a_T$, padding the computation to a polynomial bound on its number of queries. After termination, use a fixed feasible dummy query. A Boolean circuit simulates the deterministic algorithm with these answers and produces each query $D_j$ from the preceding answers. We may impose a polynomial clock and reject malformed intermediate encodings; these conventions do not change the actual computation. For each query impose, componentwise,
\[
a_j(a_j-1)=0,\qquad
 a_j f_{D_j}(u_j)=0,\qquad
 (1-a_j)g_{D_j}(v_j)=0.
\]
Thus only the certificate selected by the answer bit is required. The circuit also performs the polynomial-time construction of each alternative system.

All circuit wires are constrained to be Boolean. For example, an AND gate is encoded by $z=xy$ and a NOT gate by $z=1-x$, together with Boolean equations for the wires. The output bits specify signed integer numerators and positive integer denominators of the SDP coefficients. Reconstruct those integers by their binary expansions and introduce a real coefficient $c$ with $dc=p$ for a numerator $p$ and denominator $d\ge1$. The selected Gram equations therefore use exactly the encoded rational coefficients. Query dimensions, numbers of equations, and witness matrices can all be padded to polynomial bounds. Keeping one variable per gate or intermediate expression avoids expanding compositions, so the total number of variables and the encoding length of the polynomial equations remain polynomial in the input length.

Finally require the simulated output to be yes. Any solution certifies the first oracle answer correctly; induction then certifies each subsequent query and answer, since its query is determined by the preceding transcript. Hence the simulation is the actual oracle computation and its yes answer is correct. Conversely, the actual yes computation has witnesses for every selected feasibility or infeasibility certificate and satisfies the whole system. This proves membership in $\exists\mathbb R$. Requiring the final output to be no gives the same argument for the complement on the promise domain. The real witnesses need not have short rational encodings.
