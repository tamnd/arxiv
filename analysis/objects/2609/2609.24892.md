<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $\psi$ be a complete Bernstein function and $D$ a nonempty bounded Lipschitz domain in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Denote by $\mu_n^{\mathrm{N}}$ and $\mu_n^{\mathrm{D}}$ the Neumann and Dirichlet eigenvalues of $-\Delta$ in $D$, arranged in a nondecreasing order, and let $\lambda_n$ denote the Dirichlet eigenvalues of $\psi(-\Delta)$ in $D$. Then
\formula[eq:main]{
 \psi(\mu_n^{\mathrm{N}}) & \leqslant \lambda_n \leqslant \psi(\mu_n^{\mathrm{D}})
}
for $n = 1, 2, \ldots\,$ The inequalities are strict unless $\psi$ extends to a meromorphic function in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:interval}
Let $\psi$ be a complete Bernstein function and $D = (a, b)$ a finite interval in $\mathbb{R}$. Let $\lambda_n$ denote the Dirichlet eigenvalues of $\psi(-\Delta)$ in $D$, arranged in a nondecreasing order. Then
\formula[eq:interval]{
 \psi\bigl((\tfrac{(n - 1) \pi}{b - a})^2\bigr) & \leqslant \lambda_n \leqslant \psi\bigl((\tfrac{n \pi}{b - a})^2\bigr)
}
for $n = 1, 2, \ldots\,$ The inequalities are strict, and so $\lambda_n$ are simple, unless $\psi$ extends to a meromorphic function in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$.
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:ball}
Let $D = B(0, 1)$ be the unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^d$, $d \geqslant 2$, and $\psi$ be a complete Bernstein function. Fix a nonzero solid harmonic polynomial $P$ of degree $\ell$. Denote by $\mu_{\ell, n}^{\mathrm{N}}$ and $\mu_{\ell, n}^{\mathrm{D}}$ the Neumann and Dirichlet eigenvalues of $-\Delta$ in $D$ that correspond to the symmetry class of $P$ (that is, with eigenfunctions of the form $P(x) \varphi(\lvert x \rvert)$), arranged in a nondecreasing order. Similarly, let $\lambda_{\ell,n}$ denote the Dirichlet eigenvalues of $\psi(-\Delta)$ in $D$ that correspond to the symmetry class of $P$, arranged in a nondecreasing order. Then
\formula[eq:ball]{
 \psi(\mu_{\ell, n}^{\mathrm{N}}) & \leqslant \lambda_{\ell, n} \leqslant \psi(\mu_{\ell, n}^{\mathrm{D}})
}
for $n = 1, 2, \ldots\,$ The inequalities are strict unless $\psi$ extends to a meromorphic function in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:shape}
Under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:ball}, we have
\formula[eq:shape]{
 \lambda_{1, 1} \leqslant \psi(\mu_{1, 1}^{\mathrm{D}}) = \psi(\mu_{0, 2}^{\mathrm{N}}) \leqslant \lambda_{0, 2} .
}
Hence, the second smallest eigenvalue of $\psi(-\Delta)$ in the unit ball (counting multiplicities) corresponds to an antisymmetric eigenfunction. Unless $\psi$ extends to a meromorphic function in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$, the inequalities in~\eqref{eq:shape} are strict, and every eigenfunction corresponding to the second smallest eigenvalue is antisymmetric.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
For the upper bound in~\eqref{eq:main}, the Laplace operator $-\Delta$ in the above result can be replaced by an arbitrary self-adjoint operator $L$ associated to a regular Dirichlet form $\mathscr{E}$, and $D$ can be an arbitrary nonempty open set; see~\cite{cs:2} for further discussion. In this case, the operators $L$ and $\psi(L)$ with Dirichlet boundary/exterior conditions correspond to the closures of restrictions of the quadratic forms of $L$ and $\psi(L)$ to the class of continuous functions with a compact support contained in $D$.

The lower bound in~\eqref{eq:main} is also quite general. It extends to a wider class of operators $L$, which are associated to regular Dirichlet forms $\mathscr{E}$. The quadratic form of the corresponding operator $L^{\mathrm{N}}$ on $D$ with Neumann boundary conditions is then defined by restricting the domain of integration in the Beurling--Deny representation of $\mathscr{E}$ to $D$. Our methods apply if the quadratic form $\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{N}}$ of $L^{\mathrm{N}}$ defined above is closed, $\mathscr{E}^{\mathrm{N}} \leqslant \mathscr{E}$, $L^2(D)$ is an invariant space for $L^{\mathrm{N}}$, and the resolvent operators of $L^{\mathrm{N}}$ are compact on $L^2(D)$.

Strict inequalities in~\eqref{eq:main} depend on the following property of $\Delta$: there is no nontrivial closed subspace of $L^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ which is invariant for the resolvent of $\Delta$ and which consists of functions vanishing in the complement of $D$. Our proof applies to more general operators $L$ with this property.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
In~\cite{cs:1}, Caffarelli and Silvestre proved that the fractional Laplace operator $(-\Delta)^{\alpha / 2}$ is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for an appropriate second-order differential operator $\mathscr L$ in the half-space $\mathbb{R}^d \times [0, \infty)$. In the same vein, the fractional Dirichlet and Neumann Laplace operators $(-\Delta^{\mathrm{D}})^{\alpha / 2}$ and $(-\Delta^{\mathrm{N}})^{\alpha / 2}$ are the Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps for the same differential operator $\mathscr L$ in $D \times [0, \infty)$, with a Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition imposed on $(\partial D) \times (0, \infty)$. The Dirichlet--Neumann bracketing~\eqref{eq:main} follows in this case from a simple comparison of the quadratic forms of $\mathscr L$ in these three settings: on $\mathbb{R}^d \times [0, \infty)$, on $D \times [0, \infty)$ with the Dirichlet boundary condition, and on $D \times [0, \infty)$ with the Neumann boundary condition. Furthermore, strict inequalities can be obtained using the unique continuation property. This approach was taken in~\cite{mn:1,mn:2,nazarov}.

When $\psi$ is an arbitrary complete Bernstein function, a similar description of the operator $\psi(-\Delta)$ as the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for a (possibly singular, generalised) second-order differential operator $\mathscr L$ in the half-space $\mathbb{R}^d \times [0, \infty)$ was found in~\cite{km}. It is quite clear that $\psi(-\Delta^{\mathrm{D}})$ and $\psi(-\Delta^{\mathrm{N}})$ admit analogous representations (see~\cite{atw,ah,gms,lee,st,tan}). Thus, an argument sketched in the previous paragraph likely leads to an alternative proof of weak inequalities in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. However, as we expect various technical difficulties related to highly singular differential operators $\mathscr L$ involved, we decided to take a different approach.
<!-- end remark-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
Probabilistically, $\Delta$ is the generator of the Brownian motion in $\mathbb{R}^d$ (with covariance matrix $2 t I$), while $\Delta^{\mathrm{D}}$ and $\Delta^{\mathrm{N}}$ are the generators of the killed and reflected Brownian motions in $D$, respectively. The operators $-\psi(-\Delta)$, $-\psi(-\Delta^{\mathrm{D}})$ and $-\psi(-\Delta^{\mathrm{N}})$ are the generators of subordinate processes, where $\psi$ is the Laplace exponent of the corresponding (complete) subordinator (killed at the uniform rate $\psi(0)$ if $\psi(0) > 0$). Finally, $-(\psi(-\Delta))^{\mathrm{D}}$ is the generator of the killed subordinate process.
