<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:diff_op_orb_int_generic}
Let $\mathcal{D}\in \mathbb{C}\{V\}^G$ be an invariant differential operator. Then, for every $Φ\in \mathcal{S}(V(\mathbb{R}))$,
$$\mathrm{Orb}(-, \mathcal{D}(Φ)) = (\mathcal{D}\vert_\mathcal{A})(\mathrm{Orb}(-, Φ)).$$
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The restriction $\mathcal{D}\vert_\mathcal{A}$ is defined by Lemma \ref{lem:restrict_diff_op}. By our assumption on stabilizers, the morphism $π^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_\mathrm{rs}(\mathbb{R}))\to \mathcal{Q}_\mathrm{rs}(\mathbb{R})$ is a $G(\mathbb{R})$-bundle over its image. Working locally on open subsets $U$ of the image, we can assume that there exists a section $U\to V(\mathbb{R})_\mathrm{rs}$ and hence a trivialization
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trivialize_pi}
G(\mathbb{R})\times U \overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow} π^{-1}(U).
\end{equation}
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_r \in \mathfrak{g}$ be a basis for the Lie algebra of $G(\mathbb{R})$ viewed as left-invariant vector fields on $G(\mathbb{R})$. Working locally on $U$, we may further assume that there exist smooth coordinate functions $x_1,\ldots,x_m$ on $U$. Let $\partial_1,\ldots, \partial_m$ denote the corresponding partial derivatives. Then $\mathcal{D}$ being $G(\mathbb{R})$-invariant implies that there exist smooth functions $f_{α,β}$ on $U$, where $α\in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^r$ and $β\in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^m$ are multi-indices, such that, with respect to \eqref{eq:trivialize_pi},
$$\mathcal{D}\vert_{π^{-1}(U)} = \sum_{α, β} π^*(f_{α,β}) X^α\partial^β.$$
We obtain that
$$\mathcal{D}\vert_{\mathcal{C}^\infty(U)} = \sum_{β} f_{(0,\ldots,0),\, β} \partial^β.$$
This restriction agrees with $\mathcal{D}\vert_{\mathcal{A}}$ because every element of $\mathbb{C}[V]$ is uniquely determined by the function it defines on $π^{-1}(U)$.

Let $X\in \mathfrak{g}$ be an element viewed as left-invariant vector field on $G(\mathbb{R})$, let $Φ\in \mathcal{S}(V(\mathbb{R}))$ be a Schwartz function, and let $x\in V(\mathbb{R})_\mathrm{rs}$ be a regular semi-simple element. Let $Ψ(g) := η(g)Φ(g^{-1}\cdot x)$ be the Schwartz function obtained from $η$ and by restricting $Φ$ to the orbit $G(\mathbb{R})\cdot x$. Since $η$ is locally constant as function on $G(\mathbb{R})$, we find
\begin{equation}\label{eq:orb_int_vanishing_derivative}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Orb}(x, X(Φ)) &\ =\ \widetilde{η}(x)\int_{G(\mathbb{R})} η(g) (X(Φ))(g^{-1}\cdot x)\, dg\\[1mm]
&\ =\ \widetilde{η}(x)\int_{G(\mathbb{R})} (X(Ψ))(g)\,dg\\
&\ =\ 0
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
because the integral of the derivative of a Schwartz function is zero. Hence, as functions in $u\in U$ and using the coordinates from \eqref{eq:trivialize_pi}, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:orb_int_restriction_diffop}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Orb}(u, \mathcal{D}(Φ)) &\ =\ \widetilde{η}(1,u) \int_{G(\mathbb{R})} η(g) \mathcal{D}(Φ)(g,u)\,dg\\[1mm]
&\ =\ \widetilde{η}(1,u) \sum_{β} f_{(0,\ldots,0),\, β}(u) \Big(\partial^β \int_{G(\mathbb{R})} η(g)Φ(g,-)\,dg\Big)(u).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The transfer factor is locally constant by assumption, hence commutes with the $\partial^β$, so \eqref{eq:orb_int_restriction_diffop} agrees with $\mathcal{D}\vert_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{Orb}(-, Φ))$ as claimed.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:inv_diff_op_isomorphic}
There exists a $\mathbb{C}$-linear isomorphism $\mathfrak{s}_n(\mathbb{C})\overset{\sim}{\to} \mathfrak{t}_V(\mathbb{C})$ which is equivariant for an isomorphism of algebraic groups $\operatorname{GL}_{n,\mathbb{C}}\overset{\sim}{\to} U(V)_\mathbb{C}$. The resulting isomorphism
$$ρ:\mathbb{C}\{\mathfrak{s}_n\}^{\operatorname{GL}_n} \overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow} \mathbb{C}\{\mathfrak{t}_V\}^{U(V)}$$
extends the identification $ρ:\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{s}_n]^{\operatorname{GL}_n} \overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow} \mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{t}_V]^{U(V)}$ given by \eqref{eq:invariant_functions_SL} and \eqref{eq:invariant_functions_SU}. In particular, for every invariant differential operator $\mathcal{D}\in \mathbb{C}\{\mathfrak{s}_n\}^{\operatorname{GL}_n}$,
$$ρ(\mathcal{D})\vert_{\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{t}_V]^{U(V)}} = ρ\big(\mathcal{D}\vert_{\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{s}_n]^{\operatorname{GL}_n}}\big)$$
and for any pair of transferring data $Φ \in \mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{s}_n(\mathbb{R}))$ and $(Ψ_{p,q})_{p+q = n} \in \bigoplus_{p+q = n} \mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{t}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R}))$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transfer_inv_diff_op}
\mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal{D}(Φ)) = \sum_{p + q = n} \mathrm{Orb}(-,ρ(\mathcal{D})(Ψ_{p,q})).
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Let $(\ ,\ )$ be the hermitian form on $V$ and let $\langle\ ,\ \rangle = \mathrm{tr}_{\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}}\circ (\ ,\ )$ be its trace. Then
$$\langle λv, w\rangle = \langle v, σ(λ)w\rangle$$
where $λ\in \mathbb{C}$ and where $σ$ denotes complex conjugation. We decompose $\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{C}\overset{\sim}{\to} \mathbb{C}\times \mathbb{C}$ via $a\otimes b \mapsto (ab, a\overline b)$. The $\mathbb{C}$-linear extension $σ = 1\otimes σ$ of $σ$ is then given by $σ(a, b) = (b, a)$. We obtain a corresponding decomposition $\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R} V \overset{\sim}{\to} V_0\oplus V_1$ and both $V_0$ and $V_1$ are Lagrangian. Choose $\mathbb{C}$-bases for $V_0$ and $V_1$ such that the symmetric bilinear form $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ is given by
$$\langle\ \,,\ \rangle \longleftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
&1_n \\ 1_n &
\end{pmatrix}.$$
Recall that $\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}(V)$ is the space of those trace $0$ complex linear endomorphisms $x$ of $V$ such that $\langle x v, w\rangle = -\langle v, x w\rangle$ for all $v,w\in V$. For the above choice of basis of $\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R} V$, this gives
$$\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}(V)(\mathbb{C}) = \big\{(x^t, -x)\ \mid\ x \in \mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}_n(\mathbb{C})\big\}.$$
Similarly, $U(V)_\mathbb{C}$ is identified with $\{(g^{-1,t}, g)\ \mid\ g\in \operatorname{GL}_{n,\mathbb{C}}\}$. In these coordinates, we define $ρ:\mathfrak{s}_n(\mathbb{C})\to \mathfrak{t}_V(\mathbb{C})$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:def_ρ_linear_identification}
ρ(y, v, w)\,:=\,\big((iy^t, -iy),\ (w^t, v)\big)
\end{equation}
and this map is equivariant for the identification $\operatorname{GL}_{n,\mathbb{C}}\overset{\sim}{\to} U(V)_\mathbb{C}$, $g\mapsto (g^{-1,t}, g)$. The normalizing factors $i^k$ in \eqref{eq:invariant_functions_SU} are given by $1\otimes i^k \mapsto (i^k, (-i)^k)$ in $\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{C}$. So it is immediate that
$$\mathrm{char}(y;T) = \mathrm{char}((i,-i)^{-1}\cdot (iy^t, -iy); T)$$
under the diagonal embedding $\mathbb{C}\to \mathbb{C}\times \mathbb{C}$. In the same way, we have
$$\begin{aligned}
wy^kv & = \big((w^t,v), (i, -i)^{-k} ((iy^t)^kw^t, (-iy)^kv)\big)\\
& = \big\langle (w^t,v),\ ((y^t)^kw^t, y^kv)\big\rangle/2\\
& = (wy^kv + v^t(y^t)^kw^t)/2.
\end{aligned}$$
This shows that the isomorphism
$$ρ:\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{s}_n]^{\operatorname{GL}_n}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow} \mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{t}_V]^{U(V)}$$
induced from \eqref{eq:def_ρ_linear_identification} agrees with the isomorphism given by identifying the invariant polynomials in \eqref{eq:invariant_functions_SL} and \eqref{eq:invariant_functions_SU}. It follows formally from definitions that for all invariant differential operators $\mathcal{D}\in \mathbb{C}\{\mathfrak{s}_n\}^{\operatorname{GL}_n}$,
$$ρ(\mathcal{D})\vert_{\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{t}_V]^{U(V)}} = ρ\big(\mathcal{D}\vert_{\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{s}_n]^{\operatorname{GL}_n}}\big).$$
Finally, if $Φ\in \mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{s}_n(\mathbb{R}))$ and $(Ψ_{p,q})\in \bigoplus \mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{t}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R}))$ are Schwartz functions which are mutual transfers, then
$$\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal{D}(Φ)) & \ \overset{\text{Prop. \ref{prop:diff_op_orb_int_generic}}}{=}\ (\mathcal{D}\vert_{\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{s}_n]^{\operatorname{GL}_n}})\mathrm{Orb}(-,Φ)\\[1mm]
& \ \mkern 5mu \overset{\text{transfer}}{=}\ \sum_{p + q = n} ρ(\mathcal{D}\vert_{\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{s}_n]^{\operatorname{GL}_n}})\mathrm{Orb}(-,Ψ_{p,q})\\[1mm]
& \ \overset{\text{Prop. \ref{prop:diff_op_orb_int_generic}}}{=}\ \sum_{p+q=n} \mathrm{Orb}(-,ρ(\mathcal{D})(Ψ_{p,q})).
\end{aligned}$$
All claims of the proposition are now proved.

\iota(X)

\end{align}

\[
\omega(0,L ) = 2 \pi \frac{\partial^2}{\partial z_1^2} - \frac{1}{8 \pi} z_2^2, \qquad \omega(0,R) = - \frac{1}{8 \pi} z_1^2 + 2 \pi \frac{\partial^2}{\partial z_2^2}.
\]

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:struct_of_S1}
As an $ \mathfrak{A}$-module, the quotient $\overline{\mathcal{S}_1}$ is generated by the classes of the two functions
\[
\Phi_{1,0} := x_1 \varphi, \qquad \Phi_{0,1} := x_2 \varphi,
\]
where $\varphi $ is the Siegel Gaussian on $ \mathfrak{s}_1$. Consequently, the orbital integrals $\mathrm{Orb}(-,\Phi_{1,0})$ and $\mathrm{Orb}(-,\Phi_{0,1})$ generate $\mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal{S}_1)$ as $\mathfrak{A}$-module.

\begin{proof}
We translate the problem to the Fock model. For $n \geq 0$, let
\[ \mathcal F^n\subset \mathcal F = \mathcal F[ \mathfrak{s}_1] \]
denote the subspace of polynomials of degree $\leq n$. Using the intertwiner $\iota \colon \mathcal S_1 \overset{\sim}{\to} \mathcal F$, we consider $\operatorname{GL}_1(\mathbb{R})$- and $(\{\pm 1\}, η)$-coinvariants on $\mathcal{F}$. Set
\[
R :=  \mathfrak{A} \cdot z_1 +   \mathfrak{A} \cdot z_2 + \langle L(Φ) \mid L\in \mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}_1(\mathbb{R}),\ Φ\in \mathcal{F}\rangle + \mathcal{F}^{O(1)}.
\]
Here, we note that $-1\in O(1) = \{\pm 1\}$ acts as $-1$ on $\mathfrak{s}_1$. The monomial-wise even degree polynomials $\mathcal{F}^{O(1)}$ provide the kernel for the $(O(1), η)$-coinvariants in \eqref{eq:def_bar_S_n}.
We also define
\[
R^n =  \begin{cases}  \mathfrak{A} \cdot \mathcal F^{n-1} + R, & n > 0 \\ R, & n=0 \end{cases}
\]
Noting that  $4\pi \iota(\Phi_{1,0}) =  z_1$ and $ 4\pi \iota(\Phi_{0,1}) =  z_2$,  the proposition is equivalent to the equality $\mathcal F = R$; for this, it will suffice to show that $\mathcal F^n  \subset  R^n$ for all $n$, and we proceed by induction on $n$.

The claim is clear for $n = 0$ because $φ\leftrightarrow 1 \in \mathcal{F}$ lies in $\mathcal{F}^{O(1)}$. It is also clear for $n = 1$ because $z_1,z_2\in R^1$ by construction. For the induction step,  suppose $ f= z_1^a z_2^b \in \mathcal F^{n}$ is a monomial with $a+b=n \geq 2$. If $ab > 0$, then \eqref{eq:n1_Lie_alg_operator} allows  us to write
\[
f =  - 4 \pi \iota(X) ( z_1^{a-1} z_2^{b-1} ) +   (\text{lower degree terms}) \ \in R^n.
\]
Hence, we may assume that $f = z_1^a$ or $f= z_2^b$. If, for example, $a \geq 2$, we have
\[
z_1^{a} = -8 \pi \omega(0,R)(z_1^{a-2}) + (\text{lower degree terms}) \in R^n
\]
and similarly, if $b \geq 2$, then
\[
z_2^b = -8 \pi \omega(0,L)(z_2^{b-2}) +(\text{lower degree terms}) \in R^n.
\]
This concludes the induction.
\end{proof}
\end{proposition}

We can immediately draw our final conclusion for $n = 1$:

\begin{theorem} \Cref{conj:polynomial_Schwartz_SL} holds for $n=1$, i.e. we have an $ \mathfrak{A}$-module equality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transfer_n_1}
\mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal S_1) =  \mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal T_{1,0}) \oplus \mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal T_{0,1}).
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}
 After an appropriate normalization of Haar measures, \cite[Example 4.16]{MSY} (which is the specialization of \Cref{thm:pos_def} for $n=1$) states that the functions $\Phi_{1,0}$ and $\Phi_{0,1}$ defined above are transfers of $\psi_{1,0}$ and $\psi_{0,1}$ respectively.
By Proposition \ref{prop:struct_of_S1}, $\mathrm{Orb}(-,Φ_{1,0})$ and $\mathrm{Orb}(-,Φ_{0,1})$ generate $\mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal{S}_1)$ as $\mathfrak{A}$-module. By Example \ref{ex:cylic_T_definite}, $\mathrm{Orb}(-,ψ_{1,0})$ generates $\mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal{T}_{1,0})$ and $\mathrm{Orb}(-,ψ_{0,1})$ generates $\mathrm{Orb}(-,\mathcal{T}_{0,1})$. Since transfer is compatible with $\mathfrak{A}$-actions, we obtain \eqref{eq:transfer_n_1}.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

Noting that the biweights of $Φ_{1,0}$ and $Φ_{0,1}$ equal those of $ψ_{1,0}$ and $ψ_{0,1}$, Proposition \ref{prop:struct_of_S1} also proves Conjecture \ref{conj:cyclic_coinvariants} for $n = 1$.

\label{lem:Q11_connected}
An invariant $(α, β, γ) \in \mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{R})$ is regular semi-simple if and only if $Δ(α, β, γ) \neq 0$. It lies in $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{rs}, (1,1)}$ if and only if $Δ(α, β, γ) > 0$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Consider an invariant $(α, β, γ) \in \mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{R})$. It has representative
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rep_for_abc}
\begin{pmatrix}
y & v\\ w &
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
& 1 & β\\
-α & & γ\\
1 & &
\end{pmatrix} \in \mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}_3(\mathbb{R}).
\end{equation}
The triple $(y, v, w)$ is regular semi-simple if and only if $\{w, wy\}$ spans $\mathbb{R}_2$ and $\{v, yv\}$ spans $\mathbb{R}^2$. The first always holds, the second if and only if $Δ = αβ^2 + γ^2\neq 0$.

We next claim that $Δ(\mathrm{inv}(y, v, w)) < 0$ if and only if $(y,v,w)$ is regular semi-simple and matches to signature $(2,0)$ or $(0,2)$. For the 'if' direction, we may proceed as around \eqref{eq:block_diag_general} and assume that $(y,v,w)$ is of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda_mu_form}
\begin{pmatrix}
λ & & μ_1\\
& -λ & μ_2\\
1 & 1 &
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
with $μ_1 > 0$ and $μ_2 > 0$ (signature $(2,0)$ case) or $μ_1 < 0$ and $μ_2 < 0$ (signature $(0,2)$ case). We find that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ineq_lambda_mu}
Δ(\mathrm{inv}(y,v,w)) = -λ^2(μ_1 + μ_2)^2 + λ^2(μ_1-μ_2)^2 = -4λ^2μ_1μ_2 < 0.
\end{equation}
For the converse direction, assume that $Δ(\mathrm{inv}(y,v,w)) < 0$. This is only possible if $α = \det(y) < 0$. Hence $y$ has two real eigenvalues, so we may again assume that $(y,v,w)$ is of the form shown in \eqref{eq:lambda_mu_form}. Then \eqref{eq:ineq_lambda_mu} shows that $μ_1$ and $μ_2$ have the same sign, meaning that \eqref{eq:lambda_mu_form} comes from signature $(2,0)$ or $(0,2)$. This proves the claim and it follows that $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{rs}, (1,1)} = \{Δ > 0\}$.

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:unique_extension}
Let $f_0(α, 0, γ)$ be a smooth function on $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{rs},(1,1)}\cap \{β = 0\}$. There exists at most one function $f \in \mathcal{C}^\infty(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{rs},(1,1)})$ with the following properties:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] $f(α,0,γ) = f_0(α, 0, γ)$ for all $α$ and $γ$,
\item[(b)] $ω(0,H)\vert_{\mathcal{Q}}(f) = 0$,
\item[(c)] for all $k\geq 1$, the iterated partial derivative $\partial_β^k(f)$ have rapid decay in $γ$, and
\item[(d)] $f(α, -, γ)$ is analytic for all fixed $α$ and $γ$ with $αγ\neq 0$.
\end{itemize}
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
The difference $δ = f - f'$ of two solutions $f, f'$ to the given differential equation is a smooth function that satisfies (b), (c), (d) and $δ(α, 0, γ) \equiv 0$. So in the following, we assume that $f$ satisfies (b), (c) and (d) with $f_0(α, 0, γ) \equiv 0$ and show that $f = 0$.

As $f$ is continuous by assumption, it suffices to show $f(α,-,γ) \equiv 0$ whenever $αγ \neq 0$. So we assume $αγ \neq 0$ in the following. The intersection
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interval_11}
(\{α\}\times \mathbb{R}\times \{γ\}) \cap \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{rs}, (1,1)}
\end{equation}
equals $\mathbb{R}$ if $α>0$ and $(-|γ|/\sqrt{|α|},\,\, |γ|/\sqrt{|α|})$ if $α < 0$. In particular, it is always an open interval that contains the point $(α, 0, γ)$. By assumption, the restriction of $f$ to \eqref{eq:interval_11} is analytic. In order to prove its vanishing, it hence suffices to show that its Taylor series in $β = 0$ vanishes. In other words, our task is to prove that all directional derivatives $f^{(k)}(α,γ) := \partial_β^k(f)(α,0,γ)$ vanish.

We fix $k \geq 1$ and assume by induction that we already know $f^{(i)} = 0$ when $i < k$, the statement $f^{(0)} = 0$ corresponding to our assumption $f(α, 0, γ) = 0$. Note that $\partial^m_β (βf)\vert_{\{β = 0\}} = m f^{(m-1)}$ for all $m\geq 0$, our convention being that $0\cdot f^{(-1)} = 0$. Going through \eqref{eq:H2_restriction} term by term, this gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
0 & = 4π[\partial_β^{k-1} ω(0, H)\vert_{\mathcal{Q}}](f)\vert_{\{β = 0\}}\\[1mm]
& = 16π^2(k-1)f^{(k-2)} - 2 f^{(k)} - (k-1)f^{(k)} + (k-1) α\partial_γ^2 f^{(k-2)} - 2γ\partial_γ f^{(k)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Since $(k-1)f^{(k-2)} = 0$ by induction hypothesis (if $k = 1$ our convention applies instead), this leads to the Euler type differential equation
$$γ\partial_γf^{(k)} = - \frac{k+1}{2}f^{(k)}$$
which implies
$$f^{(k)}(α, λγ) = λ^{-(k+1)/2} f^{(k)}(α,γ)$$
for all $λ \geq 1$. Since $\partial^k_β(f)$ has rapid decay in $γ$ by assumption (c), the only possibility is $f^{(k)} = 0$ as was to be shown.

In particular, each $\mathcal{D}_i$ annihilates the image of $Φ_{1,1}'$ in the space of $\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}_2(\mathbb{C})$-coinvariants, and hence annihilates $\mathrm{Orb}(-,Φ_{1,1}')$. Proposition \ref{prop:diff_op_orb_int_generic} applies and shows that
$$(\mathcal{D}_i\vert_\mathcal{Q})\mathrm{Orb}(-,Φ_{1,1}') = 0$$
for all $i = 1,\ldots,4$. The restrictions $\mathcal{D}_i\vert_\mathcal{Q}$ as elements of $\mathbb C\{ \mathcal Q\} = \mathbb{C}\{α, β, γ\}$ are calculated by the provided Sage code as well. For example,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:restriction_abc_D_12}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{D}_1\vert_\mathcal{Q} & = β^2 \partial_γ^2 - 4 γ \partial_α\partial_γ - 4 α \partial_α^2 - 6\partial_α + 4α - 2,\\[1mm]
\mathcal{D}_2\vert_\mathcal{Q} & = αβ\partial_γ^2 - 2γ\partial_β\partial_γ - β\partial_β^2 - 2\partial_β + β.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Only the principal symbols of the $\mathcal{D}_i\vert_\mathcal{Q}$ matter for the desired application of Theorem \ref{thm:reg_thm_diff_eqn}. We write $A$, $B$ and $C$ for the polynomial variables corresponding to $\partial_α$, $\partial_β$ and $\partial_γ$ and recall $Δ = αβ^2 + γ^2$ denotes the discriminant. Then the principal symbols are given as follows:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:princ_symbols}
\begin{aligned}
σ_1 & = β^2C^2 - 4γAC - 4αA^2\\[1mm]
σ_2 & = αβC^2 - 2γBC - βB^2\\[1mm]
σ_3 & = 2αγ^2C^3 + 6αβγBC^2 + 4αβ^2B^2C - 2γ^2B^2C - 2βγB^3\\[1mm]
                                                                                       
σ_4 & = -2αΔBC^2 - 2ΔB^3 + βΔAC^2 + 2ΔA^2B.
                                                                                                                  
                                                                                                                                            
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
It remains to check that for all $(α,β,γ)\in \mathbb{R}^3$ with $Δ >0$ and $αγ \neq 0$, $A = B = C = 0$ is the only solution to $σ_1 = \ldots = σ_4 = 0$.
We first assume that $β = 0$. Then \eqref{eq:princ_symbols} specializes to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:princ_symbols_b_zero}
\begin{aligned}
σ'_1 & = - 4γAC - 4αA^2\\[1mm]
σ'_2 & = - 2γBC\\[1mm]
σ'_3 & = 2αγ^2C^3 - 2γ^2B^2C\\[1mm]
σ'_4 & = -2αΔBC^2 - 2ΔB^3 + 2ΔA^2B.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
If $C = 0$, then $σ_1' = 0$ implies $A = 0$ and then $σ_4' = 0$ implies $B = 0$. So any non-trivial solution will have $C \neq 0$. But then $σ_2' = 0$ implies $B = 0$ which leads to $σ'_3 \neq 0$. This shows our claim in points of the form $(α, 0, γ)$, so we can henceforth assume $β \neq 0$.

Then $σ_2 = 0$ gives rise to the relation $βB = C( \pm \sqrt{Δ}-γ)$. Substituting this into $β^2σ_3$ gives,  after a short calculation, the equation
\begin{equation}
β^2σ_3 = 4Δ^{3/2}(Δ^{1/2}  \mp γ)C^3.
\end{equation}
The coefficient is always nonzero because $αβγ \neq 0$ implies $Δ^{1/2} \neq |γ|$. So $σ_3 = 0$ implies $C = 0$. Then $σ_1 = 0$ implies $A = 0$, and finally $σ_4 = 0$ gives $B = 0$. That is, $A = B = C = 0$ is the only solution to $σ_1 = \ldots = σ_4 = 0$ for all parameters $(α, β, γ)$ in question, as was to be shown.
