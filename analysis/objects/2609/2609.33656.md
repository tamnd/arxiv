<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{main thm}
    If $X$ is a compact complex surface with a nonflat Ricci-flat K\"ahler metric, then the holomorphic tangent bundle $T^{1,0}X$ admits an RC-positive Hermitian metric.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor}

However, the exterior power $\bigwedge^2 T^{1,0}X$, the tensor power $\bigotimes^k T^{1,0}X$, and the symmetric power $S^kT^{1,0}X$, for $k\oldgeq 2$, do not admit any RC-positive Hermitian metric.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->

\label{cor 2}

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\
\begin{enumerate}
\item A Hermitian metric $h$ is RC-positive at $x\in X$ if, for every nonzero $v\in E_x$, there is a nonzero $u\in T_x^{1,0}X$ such that $\langle R^hv,v\rangle_h(u,\bar u)>0$.

\item A Hermitian metric $h$ is uniformly RC-positive at $x\in X$ if there is a nonzero
$u\in T_x^{1,0}X$ such that $\langle R^hv,v\rangle_h(u,\bar u)>0$ for every nonzero $v\in E_x$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{line bundle}
    Let $E$ be a holomorphic vector bundle of rank $r$ over  a compact complex manifold $X$ of dimension $n$. If $E$ is weakly RC-positive, then $H^0(X, S^kE^*)=0$ for any $k\oldgeq 1$. In particular, trivial bundles over a compact complex manifold cannot be RC-positive.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
By \cite[Theorem 4.5]{YangCamb}, $H^0(X, S^lE^*)=0$ for large $l$. Now, fix $k \oldgeq 1$ and let $s\in H^0(X, S^kE^*)$. Denote by $\mu_{l,k}$ the canonical map $S^l(S^kE^*)\to S^{lk}E^*$. For $f_1,\ldots,f_l\in S^kE^*$,  $\mu_{l,k}(f_1\odot \cdots \odot f_l)=f_1\cdots f_l$ where $\odot$ is the symmetric product in $S^l(S^kE^*)$ and $f_1\cdots f_l$ is the ordinary polynomial multiplication. We have $\mu_{l,k}(s^{\odot l})=s^l\in H^0(X, S^{lk}E^*)$. For large $l$, $H^0(X, S^{lk}E^*)=0$ by \cite[Theorem 4.5]{YangCamb}, so $s^l=0$. For $x\in X$, $s^l(x)=s(x)^l$ and $s(x)$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$ on $E_x$, so $s^l(x)=0$ implies $s(x)=0$. As a result, any $s\in H^0(X, S^kE^*)$ is zero, and hence   $H^0(X, S^kE^*)=0$ for any $k\oldgeq 1$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{RC criterion'}
     Let $(E,h)$ be a Hermitian holomorphic vector bundle over a complex manifold $X$ of dimension $n$ and let $\omega$ be a Hermitian metric on $X$. Assume the mean curvature $\sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_\omega R^h$ is nonnegative. The metric $h$ is RC-positive at $x\in X$ if and only if the $(1,1)$-form $\langle R^h v,v\rangle_h$ is nonzero for any nonzero $v\in  E_x$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
The statement is pointwise, so $X$ does not have to be compact. The only if part is direct by the definition of RC-positivity. So, let us assume the $(1,1)$-form $\langle R^h v,v\rangle_h$ is nonzero for any nonzero $v\in  E_x$.
    Fix a nonzero $v\in  E_x$. If all the eigenvalues of the $(1,1)$-form $\langle R^h v,v\rangle_h$ are nonpositive, then all the eigenvalues have to be zero because    $\sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_\omega\langle R^h v,v\rangle_h   =\langle  \sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_\omega R^h v,v\rangle_h \oldgeq 0$ by the assumption in the lemma. But this implies the $(1,1)$-form $\langle R^h v,v\rangle_h=0$, a contradiction. Therefore, the $(1,1)$-form $\langle R^h v,v\rangle_h$ has at least one positive eigenvalue, and hence $h$ is RC-positive at $x$.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem conformal}
   Let $(E,h)$ be a Hermitian holomorphic vector bundle over a compact complex manifold $X$ of dimension $n$ and let $\omega$ be a Hermitian metric on $X$. Assume the mean curvature $\sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_\omega R^h$ is nonnegative. If  $h$ is RC-positive on a nonempty open set $U\subset X$, then there exists $f\in C^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R})$  such that the  conformal change $he^{-f}$ is RC-positive on $X$
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $\hat{\omega}$ be the Gauduchon metric conformal to $\omega$. So, $\hat{\omega}=e^u\omega$ for some $u\in C^\infty(X,\mathbb{R})$, and $\sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_{\hat{\omega}} R^h=e^{-u}\sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_\omega R^h$ is still nonnegative. Choose an open set $B$ whose  closure is contained in $U$. For $v\in E$, we have the $(1,1)$-form $\langle  R^h v, v\rangle_h$, and we  denote by $$\lambda_{\max, \hat{\omega}}(\langle  R^h v, v\rangle_h)$$ the largest eigenvalue of $\langle  R^h v, v\rangle_h$ with respect to $\hat{\omega}$. If $v \in E|_U$, then $\lambda_{\max, \hat{\omega}}(\langle  R^h v, v\rangle_h)>0$ because $h$ is RC-positive on $U$.
 
 
 Consider the continuous map $ \{v\in E|_{\overline{B}}:|v|_h=1 \}\ni v\mapsto \lambda_{\max, \hat{\omega}}(\langle  R^h v, v\rangle_h)$. As $\{v\in E|_{\overline{B}}:|v|_h=1 \}$ is compact, $\lambda_{\max, \hat{\omega}}(\langle  R^h v, v\rangle_h)$ achieves its minimum somewhere. The minimum, say $\delta$, is positive because $h$ is RC-positive on $\overline{B}\subset U$.



Let $\psi$ be a real-valued smooth function on $X$ supported in $B$, $\psi\oldgeq 0$, and $\int_X \psi dV_{\hat{\omega}} >0$. Define
\begin{equation}
    \rho := 1- \frac{ \int_X dV_{\hat{\omega}} }{ \int_X \psi dV_{\hat{\omega}} } \psi.
\end{equation}
Then, $\rho = 1$ on $X \smallsetminus B$. Moreover, $\int_X \rho dV_{\hat{\omega}}=0$, so there is  $f\in C^{\infty} (X,\mathbb{R})$ such that $\oldDelta_{\hat{\omega}} f=\oldLambda_{\hat{\omega}}\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}f = \rho$ (see \cite{Gauduchon} and \cite[Theorem 2.2]{CTW}).

Define $h_\epsilon=e^{-\epsilon f}h$ for $\epsilon>0$. Because $\sqrt{-1}R^{h_\epsilon}=\sqrt{-1}R^h+\epsilon\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}f\otimes \operatorname{Id}_E$, we have, for $v\in E$,
\begin{equation}\label{11form}
    \langle \sqrt{-1}R^{h_\epsilon} v, v\rangle_{h_\epsilon}=e^{-\epsilon f}\big( \langle \sqrt{-1}R^{h} v, v\rangle_{h}+|v|^2_h \epsilon\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}f \big).
\end{equation}
We discuss the case $X\smallsetminus B$ first. Taking $\oldLambda_{\hat{\omega}}$ on (\ref{11form}) yields
\begin{equation}
    \langle \sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_{\hat{\omega}} R^{h_\epsilon} v, v\rangle_{h_\epsilon}=e^{-\epsilon f}\big( \langle \sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_{\hat{\omega}} R^{h} v, v\rangle_{h}+|v|^2_h \epsilon\sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_{\hat{\omega}}\partial\bar{\partial}f \big)\oldgeq e^{-\epsilon f} |v|^2_h \epsilon \rho=e^{-\epsilon f} |v|^2_h \epsilon, 
\end{equation}
where the inequality is due to the fact $\sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_{\hat{\omega}} R^h \oldgeq 0$ and $\oldDelta_{\hat{\omega}} f=\rho$, and the last equality is due to $\rho=1$ on $X\smallsetminus B$. Therefore, for any $v\neq 0$, $\langle \sqrt{-1}\oldLambda_{\hat{\omega}} R^{h_\epsilon} v, v\rangle_{h_\epsilon}>0$, so $h_\epsilon$ is RC-positive over $X\smallsetminus B$ for any $\epsilon >0$.

It remains to prove RC-positivity on $\overline{B}$. Let $C=\sup_X|\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}f|_{\hat{\omega}}$. For $v\in E|_{\overline{B}}$ and $|v|_h=1$, we obtain from (\ref{11form}) the following estimate
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{\max,{\hat{\omega}}}(\langle  R^{h_\epsilon} v, v\rangle_{h_\epsilon})\oldgeq e^{-\epsilon f}\big[  \lambda_{\max,{\hat{\omega}}}(\langle  R^{h} v, v\rangle_{h})-\epsilon C    \big]\oldgeq e^{-\epsilon f }(\delta-\epsilon C). 
\end{equation}
Because $\delta$ and $C$ are independent of $\epsilon$, we can make $\epsilon$ small so that $(\delta-\epsilon C)$ becomes positive. Therefore, for any nonzero $v\in E|_{\overline{B}}$,  the $(1,1)$-form $\langle  R^{h_\epsilon} v, v\rangle_{h_\epsilon}$ has at least one positive eigenvalue, so $h_\epsilon$ is RC-positive on $\overline{B}$. Together with the previous case, we have proved that $h_\epsilon$ is RC-positive on $X$.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{surjection}
   Let $M$ be a complex surface with a Hermitian metric $\omega$. For $x\in M$, let  $(\wedge^{1,1}_0)_x$ be the space of the real $(1,1)$-forms at $x$ that are orthogonal to $\omega$.  The map $\mathcal{H}:T_x^*M\to (\wedge^{1,1}_0)_x$ defined by
   \begin{equation}\label{the map}
       \xi \mapsto (\xi\wedge J\xi-\frac{|\xi|^2}{2}\omega  )
\end{equation} is surjective. Moreover, if $\xi$ is nonzero, then $\xi\wedge J\xi -|\xi|^2\omega/2$ is nonzero.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
First, the real 2-form $\xi\wedge J\xi$ is $J$-invariant, so it is a real $(1,1)$-form. Thus, $(\xi\wedge J\xi-|\xi|^2\omega/2  )$ is a real $(1,1)$-form. To see that $(\xi\wedge J\xi-|\xi|^2\omega/2  )$ is orthogonal to $\omega$, one notice that $\langle\omega,\omega\rangle=n=2$ and $\langle\xi\wedge J\xi, \omega\rangle=\omega(\xi^\#,J\xi^\#)=g(J\xi^\#, J\xi^\#)=|\xi^\#|^2=|\xi|^2 $, where $\#:T^*M\to TM$ is the canonical map induced by the metric $g$. Hence, $(\xi\wedge J\xi-|\xi|^2\omega/2  )$ is indeed in $(\wedge^{1,1}_0)_x$.  


One way to prove that $\mathcal{H}$ is surjective is through local coordinates, and we will see that the map $\mathcal{H}$ is basically the Hopf map $S^3\to S^2$. 

Let the local coordinates around $x$ be $\{z_1=x_1+\sqrt{-1}y_1, z_2=x_2+\sqrt{-1}y_2\}$ such that $Jdx_j=dy_j$, $Jdy_j=-dx_j$. At $x$, we assume $\omega=2(dx_1\wedge dy_1+dx_2\wedge dy_2)$, so $\{dx_1,dy_1,dx_2,dy_2\}$ is an orthogonal basis of $T_x^*M$ and each vector has length $1/\sqrt{2}$. Assume $\xi=adx_1+bdy_1+cdx_2+ddy_2$ with $a,b,c,d\in \mathbb{R}$. A direct computation gives 
\begin{equation}\label{local}
\begin{aligned}
    \xi\wedge J\xi-\frac{|\xi|^2}{2}\omega=&\frac{a^2+b^2-c^2-d^2}{2}(dx_1\wedge dy_1-dx_2\wedge dy_2)\\+&(ac+bd)(dx_1\wedge dy_2-dy_1\wedge dx_2)\\+&(bc-ad)(dx_1\wedge dx_2+dy_1\wedge dy_2).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Meanwhile, by \cite[(2.3)]{Itoh}, 
$$
\begin{cases}
    e_1=:(dx_1\wedge dy_1-dx_2\wedge dy_2),\\ e_2=:(dx_1\wedge dy_2-dy_1\wedge dx_2),\\
    e_3=:(dx_1\wedge dx_2+dy_1\wedge dy_2)
\end{cases}
$$
is an orthogonal basis for the space $(\wedge^{1,1}_0)_x$ and each vector has length $1/\sqrt{2}$. If we write $\zeta_1=a+\sqrt{-1}b$ and $\zeta_2=c+\sqrt{-1}d$, then (\ref{local}) becomes 
\begin{equation}
    \xi\wedge J\xi-\frac{|\xi|^2}{2}\omega=\frac{(|\zeta_1|^2-|\zeta_2|^2)}{2}e_1+\operatorname{Re}{(\zeta_1\bar{\zeta}_2)}e_2+\operatorname{Im}{(\zeta_1\bar{\zeta}_2)}e_3.
\end{equation}
Therefore, if we  identify $T^*_xM$ with $\mathbb{R}^4$ using the basis $\{dx_1,dy_1,dx_2,dy_2\}$ and $(\wedge^{1,1}_0)_x$ with $\mathbb{R}^3$ using the basis $\{e_1/2, e_2/2, e_3/2\}$, then the map $\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{R}^4\to \mathbb{R}^3$
is \begin{equation}
  (a,b,c,d)\mapsto  \big(|\zeta_1|^2-|\zeta_2|^2, 2\operatorname{Re}{(\zeta_1\bar{\zeta}_2)}, 2\operatorname{Im}{(\zeta_1\bar{\zeta}_2)} \big),
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_1=a+\sqrt{-1}b$ and $\zeta_2=c+\sqrt{-1}d$. When $\mathcal{H}$ is restricted to $S^3\subset \mathbb{R}^4$, we obtain the Hopf map from $S^3$ to $S^2$. In particular, $\mathcal{H}$ maps the sphere of radius $1/\sqrt{2}$ in $T^*_x M$ onto the sphere of radius $1/\sqrt{8}$ in $(\wedge^{1,1}_0)_x$. Because $\mathcal{H}$ satisfies $\mathcal{H}(\lambda\xi)=\lambda^2\mathcal{H}(\xi)$ for any $\lambda>0$ and $\xi \in T^*_x M$, we get that $\mathcal{H}:T^*_x M\to (\wedge^{1,1}_0)_x$ is surjective.

Finally, for $\xi\neq 0$, $\mathcal{H}(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{2}|\xi|})$ is on the sphere of radius $1/\sqrt{8}$, so it is nonzero. But $$\mathcal{H}(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{2}|\xi|})=\frac{\mathcal{H}(\xi)}{2|\xi|^2},$$ so $\mathcal{H}(\xi)=\xi\wedge J\xi -|\xi|^2\omega/2$ is nonzero.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{Itoh}
For a complex surface $M$ with a Hermitian metric $\omega$, the following hold for each $x\in M$
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A real 2-form $\alpha$ is self-dual if and only if $\alpha=\beta+\bar{\beta}+b\omega$ for a $(2,0)$-form $\beta$ and a real number $b$.
    \item A real 2-form $\alpha$ is anti-self-dual if and only if $\alpha$ is a real $(1,1)$-form orthogonal to $\omega$. That is, $(\wedge^- T^*M)_x=(\wedge^{1,1}_0)_x$ for any $x\in M$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem long} \
\begin{enumerate}
\item    Let $V$ be a complex vector space of dimension $2$ and let $k\oldgeq 2$. Any $u\in S^kV$ can be written as $v_1\odot\cdots \odot v_k$ for $v_1, \ldots, v_k\in V$, where $\odot$ denotes the symmetric product in $S^kV$. For $u\neq 0$, the factorization is unique up to permutation and rescaling.  For $\sigma\in \wedge^2 V^*$,  the map $\oldDelta^k_\sigma: S^kV \to \mathbb{C}$ given by 
    \begin{equation}\label{Delta}
     \oldDelta^k_\sigma(u)=\prod_{1\oldleq i<j \oldleq k}\sigma(v_i,v_j)^2   
    \end{equation}
  is well-defined. Moreover, $\oldDelta^k_\sigma$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2(k-1)$ on $S^kV$; that is,  $\oldDelta^k_\sigma\in S^{2(k-1) }(S^kV)^*$.  Finally, if $\sigma\neq0$, then $\oldDelta_\sigma^k\neq 0$.


  \item Let $X$ be a complex surface and $\sigma$ be a local holomorphic section of the bundle  $\wedge^2 (T^{1,0}X)^*$. Let $k\oldgeq 2$. The section defined by
    \begin{equation}\label{sigma section}
       x\mapsto  \oldDelta^k_{\sigma(x)}\in S^{2(k-1)}(S^kT^{1,0}_xX)^*   
    \end{equation}
is holomorphic.
 \end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
First, we show that any $u\in S^kV$ can be written as $v_1\odot\cdots \odot v_k$ for $v_1, \ldots, v_k\in V$. Because $\dim_\mathbb{C} V=2$, we can write $u\in S^kV$ as a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$ with two variables $x$ and $y$,  
\begin{equation}\label{homogeneous}
    u=\sum_{j=0}^k a_j x^{k-j} y^j 
\end{equation}
for complex numbers $\{a_j\}_{j=0}^k$. By setting $x=t$ and $y=1$ in (\ref{homogeneous}), we obtain the polynomial $\sum_{j=0}^k a_j t^{k-j} $, which is of degree $m\oldleq k$, and we can factorize
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=0}^k a_j t^{k-j}=c(t-r_1)\cdots (t-r_m), \text{ where } c, r_1, \ldots, r_m\in \mathbb{C}.
\end{equation}
Therefore, 
\begin{equation}
 u=y^k\sum_{j=0}^k a_j \big(\frac{x}{y}\big)^{k-j}=y^kc(\frac{x}{y}-r_1)\cdots (\frac{x}{y}-r_m)=  y^{k-m}c(x-r_1 y)\cdots (x-r_my),  
\end{equation}
which is a product of homogeneous polynomials of degree $1$. Therefore, $u$ can be written as $v_1\odot\cdots \odot v_k$ for $v_1, \ldots, v_k\in V$. 

Suppose $u\neq 0$ has two factorizations $v_1\odot\cdots \odot v_k$ and $w_1\odot\cdots \odot w_k$. Using the view of homogeneous polynomials, we can write $v_j=a_jx+b_jy$ and $w_j=c_jx+d_jy$ with $a_j,b_j,c_j, d_j\in \mathbb{C}$ for each $j$. Because $\mathbb{C}[x,y]$ is a UFD, there exist a permutation $\pi$ of $\{1,\ldots, k\}$ and $\lambda_j\in \mathbb{C}$ for $j=1\sim k$ with $\oldPi\lambda_j=1$ such that $v_j=\lambda_j w_{\pi(j)}$ for each $j$. So, the factorization of $u$ is unique up to permutation and rescaling. Moreover, we observe that
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    &\prod_{1\oldleq i<j \oldleq k}\sigma(v_i,v_j)^2=
    \prod_{1\oldleq i<j \oldleq k}\sigma(\lambda_i w_{\pi(i)},\lambda_j w_{\pi(j)})^2=
    \prod_{1\oldleq i<j \oldleq k}\lambda_i^2\lambda_j^2\sigma( w_{\pi(i)}, w_{\pi(j)})^2\\
    = &(\prod_{j=1}^k\lambda_j)^{2(k-1)} \prod_{1\oldleq i<j \oldleq k}\sigma( w_{\pi(i)}, w_{\pi(j)})^2   =\prod_{1\oldleq i<j \oldleq k}\sigma(w_i,w_j)^2  
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
showing that the map $\oldDelta^k_\sigma: S^kV \to \mathbb{C}$ in (\ref{Delta}) is well-defined.

Next, we are going to show that $\oldDelta^k_\sigma$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2(k-1)$ on $S^kV$. Let $\{e_0,e_1\}$ be a basis for $V$ and denote $\sigma (e_0,e_1)=s$. For $u\in S^kV $, we write $u=v_1\odot \cdots \odot v_k$ for $v_1,\ldots, v_k\in V$. Let $u=\sum_{j=0}^k a_j e_0^{k-j}e_1^j$ with $a_0,\ldots, a_k\in \mathbb{C}$, and  $v_j=b_je_0+c_je_1$ with $b_j,c_j\in\mathbb{C}$ for $j=1\sim k$. Hence,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=0}^k a_j e_0^{k-j}e_1^j=u=v_1\odot \cdots \odot v_k=\bigodot_{j=1}^k (b_je_0+c_je_1).
\end{equation}
Note that $\sigma(b_ie_0+c_ie_1, b_je_0+c_je_1)=s(b_ic_j-c_ib_j)$, so 
\begin{equation}\label{product}
 \oldDelta_\sigma^k(u)=\prod_{1\oldleq i<j\oldleq k}s^2(b_ic_j-c_ib_j)^2=(s^2)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{1\oldleq i<j\oldleq k}(b_ic_j-c_ib_j)^2.   
\end{equation}
On the other hand, if we use the view of homogeneous polynomials, then
 $u$ corresponds to 
\begin{equation}
 F(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^k a_j y^{k-j}x^j=\prod_{j=1}^k (b_jy+c_jx).  
\end{equation} 
The discriminant of $F$ is
\begin{equation}\label{disc'}
 D(F(x,y))=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{1\oldleq i<j\oldleq k}(b_ic_j-c_ib_j)^2.   
\end{equation}
Combing (\ref{product}) and (\ref{disc'}) yields 
\begin{equation}\label{delta and D}
     \oldDelta_\sigma^k(u)=(-s^2)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}D(F).
\end{equation}



Let us assume $c_j\neq 0$ for any $j$ for the moment. If we denote $b_j/c_j$ by $r_j$ and 
denote $F(x,1)$ by $f(x)$, then $f(x)=\prod(b_j+c_jx)=\prod (r_j+x)\prod c_j$ and the discriminant of $f$ is
\begin{equation}\label{disc}
\begin{aligned}
 D(f(x))=&(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}(\prod_{j=1}^k c_j)^{2(k-1)}\prod_{1\oldleq i<j\oldleq k}(r_i-r_j)^2\\
 =&(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{1\oldleq i<j\oldleq k} (c_ic_j)^{2}\prod_{1\oldleq i<j\oldleq k}(r_i-r_j)^2\\
 =& (-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\prod_{1\oldleq i<j\oldleq k} (b_ic_j-c_ib_j)^2=D(F(x,y)).   
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
By \cite[Page 405, (1.30)]{GKZ}, because $f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^k a_j x^j$, $D(f(x))$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2(k-1)$ in $a_0,\ldots, a_k$. So, $D(F(x,y))$ is also a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2(k-1)$ in $a_0,\ldots, a_k$, and the statement holds without assuming $c_j\neq 0$ for any $j$ because both sides are polynomials in $b_i$ and $c_i$. Therefore, from (\ref{delta and D}), we obtain that $\oldDelta^k_\sigma$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2(k-1)$ on $S^kV$. 

Assuming $\sigma \neq 0$, we see that $\sigma(e_0,e_1)=s$ must be nonzero. If we define $u=\bigodot_{j=1}^k (b_je_0+e_1)$ with distinct $b_j$, then $D(F)\neq 0$ by (\ref{disc'}), and so $\oldDelta_\sigma^k(u)\neq 0$ by (\ref{delta and D}). We obtain $\oldDelta_\sigma^k\neq 0$.



For the second part of the lemma, it suffices to show that $x\mapsto \oldDelta^k_{\sigma(x)}(u(x))$ is holomorphic for any local holomorphic section $u$ of $S^kT^{1,0}X$. Consider a local holomorphic frame $\{e_0,e_1\}$ of $T^{1,0}X$. Because $\sigma$ is holomorphic, $\sigma(e_0,e_1):=s$ is holomorphic. For a local holomorphic section $u$ of $S^kT^{1,0}X$, if we write $u=\sum_{j=0}^k a_j e_0^{k-j}e_1^j$, then all the $a_j$ are holomorphic.  From (\ref{delta and D}), we see $$x\mapsto \oldDelta^k_{\sigma(x)}(u(x))=(-s^2)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}D(F)$$ is holomorphic because $D(F)$ is a homogeneous polynomial in $a_0,\ldots, a_k$ with constant coefficients (see \cite[Page 405, (1.30)]{GKZ}).
