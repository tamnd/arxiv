<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
The ANOVA decomposition of a $d$-variate function $x$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{AltSumANOVA}
x = \sum_{S \subseteq [1:d]} \Pi_S(x),
\end{equation}
where the linear operators $\Pi_S$ are mutually orthogonal projectors given by
\begin{equation}
\label{AltANOVA}
\Pi_S = \prod_{j \in S} (\mathrm{Id} - P_{-j}) \; P_S
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} (-1)^{|S|-|R|} P_R.
\end{equation}
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The arguments only use the fact that the operators $P_{-j}
                         
$,
which average out the $j$th variable,
are orthogonal projectors which commute with one another.
It starts with the following expansion:
$$
\mathrm{Id} = \prod_{i=1}^d (\mathrm{Id}-P_{- i} + P_{- i})
= \sum_{S \subseteq [1:d]} \prod_{j \in S} (\mathrm{Id} - P_{- j})
\prod_{\ell \not \in S} P_{- \ell}
= \sum_{S \subseteq [1:d]} \Pi_S.
$$
This is the decomposition \eqref{AltSumANOVA}
in which we have defined 
$$
\Pi_S
:=
\prod_{j \in S} (\mathrm{Id} - P_{- j})
\prod_{\ell \not \in S} P_{- \ell}
= \prod_{j \in S} (\mathrm{Id} - P_{-j}) \; P_S.
$$
The commutativity of the $P_{-i}$ readily implies that $\Pi_S^* = \Pi_S$ and $\Pi_S \Pi_S = \Pi_S$,
i.e., that the $\Pi_S$ are orthogonal projectors.
Moreover,
they are mutually orthogonal, 
i.e., $\Pi_S \Pi_{S'} = 0$ for $S \not= S'$---indeed, 
picking e.g. $i \in S' \setminus S$, say,
one sees that $\Pi_S \Pi_{S'}$ contains the product
$P_{- i} (\mathrm{Id} - P_{- i}) = 0$.
Notice that the expansion of 
$\prod_{j \in S} (\mathrm{Id} - P_{- j})$
in the expression for $\Pi_S$ yields
$$
\Pi_S = \prod_{j \in S} (\mathrm{Id} - P_{- j}) \; P_S
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} \prod_{i \in S \setminus R}(-P_{- i}) \; P_S
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} (-1)^{|S|-|R|} P_R,
$$ 
which is the second identity in \eqref{AltANOVA}.
Finally,
the recursive definition \eqref{TradANOVA} is retrieved by way of yet another expansion, namely  
$$
P_S = \prod_{i \in S} (\mathrm{Id}- P_{- i} + P_{- i}) \; P_S
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} \prod_{j \in R} (\mathrm{Id}- P_{-j}) \prod_{\ell \in S \setminus R} P_{- \ell} \; P_S
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} \prod_{j \in R} (\mathrm{Id}- P_{- j}) \; P_R
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} \Pi_R,
$$
which yield $\displaystyle{\Pi_S = P_S - \sum_{\substack{R \subseteq S \\ R \not= S}} \Pi_R}$ after a rearrangement.

\label{LemEstimK}
The dimension of the space $V_s$ defined in~\eqref{DefSpaceVs} is
\begin{equation}
\label{dimVs_exact}
k_s := \dim(V_s) = \sum_{|S| \le s} \prod_{j \in S} (n_j-1).
\end{equation}
In the particular case $n_1 = \cdots = n_d =: n$, it can be lower- and upper-estimated as
$$
\left( \frac{d \, (n-1)}{s} \right)^s
\le 
           
k_s \le
\left( \frac{e \, d \, (n-1)}{s} \right)^s.
$$
<!-- end lem-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Since rank and trace coincide for (not necessarily  orthogonal) projectors
and in view  of the second identity of \eqref{AltANOVA},
namely $\Pi_S = \sum_{R \subseteq S} (-1)^{|S|-|R|} P_R$,
we observe that, for a fixed $S \subseteq [1:d]$,
\begin{align*}
{\rm rk}(\Pi_S) 
& = \mathrm{tr}(\Pi_S)
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} (-1)^{|S|-|R|} \mathrm{tr}(P_R)
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} (-1)^{|S|-|R|} {\rm rk}(P_R)
= \sum_{R \subseteq S} (-1)^{|S|-|R|} \prod_{i\in R} n_i\\
& = \prod_{j \in S} (n_j-1).
\end{align*}
Now, since the space $V_s$ is the orthogonal sum  of the spaces $\operatorname{range}(\Pi_S)$ for $|S| \le s$,
its dimension is equal to $\sum_{|S| \le s} {\rm rk}(\Pi_S)$, which justifies \eqref{dimVs_exact}.
When $n_1=\cdots=n_d=n$,
this yields
$$
\dim(V_s) = \sum_{r=0}^s \binom{d}{r} (n-1)^r.
$$
For the lower bound, we only consider the summand corresponding to $r=s$ and use $\binom{d}{r} \ge \big(\frac{d}{s} \big)^s$.
For the upper~bound, we use $(n-1)^r \le (n-1)^s$ and the inequality $\sum_{r=0}^s \binom{d}{r} \le \big( \frac{ed}{s} \big)^s$, see e.g. \citep[Lemma~2.6]{foucart2022mathematical}.

\label{ThmCheapANOVAS}
The ANOVA component of $X \in \mathbb{C}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_d}$ indexed by $S \subseteq [1:d]$ can be expressed as
$$
\Pi_S(X)
= \mathcal{F}^{-1} \big( M_S \odot \mathcal{F}(X) \big),
$$
where $\mathcal{F}$ is the $d$-dimensional Fourier transform on $\mathbb{C}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_d }$ producing discrete Fourier coefficients 
and where the mask $M_S \in \{0,1\}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_d}$
is given for $\mathbf{i} = (i_1,\ldots,i_d) \in [1:n_1] \times \cdots \times [1,n_d]$ by 
$$
(M_S)_{\mathbf{i}}
= \begin{cases}
1 & \mbox{ if }
i_j \not= 1 \mbox{ for } j \in S  \mbox{ and } i_\ell = 1 \mbox{ for } \ell \not\in S,\\
0 & \mbox{ otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
For each $j \in [1:d]$,
let $(\varphi^{(j)}_1, \varphi^{(j)}_2, \ldots, \varphi^{(j)}_{n_j})$ denote an unnormalized Fourier basis of $\mathbb{C}^{n_j}$
with $\varphi^{(j)}_{1}$ being equal to the all-one vector.
Then,
for $\mathbf{i} = (i_1,\ldots,i_d) \in [1:n_1] \times \cdots \times [1,n_d]$,
let $\phi_{\mathbf{i}} := \varphi^{(1)}_{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \varphi^{(d)}_{i_d}$.
It is easily seen---recalling that $P_{-\ell}$ averages out $\ell$-th variable---that
$$
P_{-\ell}(\phi_{\mathbf{i}})
= \begin{cases}
\phi_{\mathbf{i}} & \mbox{ if } i_\ell = 1,\\
0 & \mbox{ if } i_\ell \not= 1.
\end{cases}
$$
Therefore,
in view of the leftmost identity of \eqref{AltANOVA} affirming that $\Pi_S = \prod_{j \in S} (\mathrm{Id} - P_{-j}) \; \prod_{\ell \not\in S} P_{- \ell}$,
we obtain
$$
\Pi_S(\phi_{\mathbf{i}})
= \left.
\begin{cases}
\phi_{\mathbf{i}} & \mbox{ if } i_j \not= 1 \mbox{ for all }
j \in S \mbox{ and } i_\ell = 1 \mbox{ for all } \ell \not\in S,  \\
0 & \mbox{ otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\right\} = (M_S)_{\mathbf{i}} \, \phi_{\mathbf{i}}.
$$
Finally, for any $X \in \mathbb{C}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_d}$ written as $X = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\big( \mathcal{F}(X) \big)
= \sum_{\mathbf{i}} \mathcal{F}(X)_{\mathbf{i}} \, \phi_{\mathbf{i}}$,
we conclude that
$$
\Pi_S(X) = 
\sum_{\mathbf{i}} \mathcal{F}(X)_{\mathbf{i}} \, (M_S)_{\mathbf{i}} \, \phi_{\mathbf{i}}
= \sum_{\mathbf{i}}  (M_S \odot \mathcal{F}(X))_{\mathbf{i}} \, \phi_{\mathbf{i}}
= \mathcal{F}^{-1} \big( M_S \odot \mathcal{F}(X) \big),
$$
as announced.

<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{CorCompPi<=s}
The order-$s$ ANOVA projection of $X \in \mathbb{C}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_d}$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\label{CheapAction}
\Pi_{\le s}(X)
= \mathcal{F}^{-1} \big( M_{\le s} \odot \mathcal{F}(X) \big),
\end{equation}
where the binary mask $M_{\le s} \in \{0,1\}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_d}$ is precomputed offline
through its entries given for $\mathbf{i} = (i_1,\ldots,i_d) \in [1:n_1] \times \cdots \times [1:n_d]$ by
$$
(M_{\le s})_{\mathbf{i}}
= \begin{cases}
1 & \mbox{ if }
|\{ j \in [1:d]: i_j \not= 1 \}| \le s,\\
0 & \mbox{ otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
<!-- end cor-1 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
In view of $\Pi_{\le s}(X)= \sum_{|S| \le s} \Pi_S(X)$,
the identity~\eqref{CheapAction} follows with $M_{\le s} := \sum_{|S| \le s} M_S$.
Note that $M_{\le s}$ takes values in $\{0,1\}$
because the summands $M_S \in \{0,1\}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_d}$ are disjointly supported,
as Theorem~\ref{ThmCheapANOVAS}
says that $(M_{S})_{\mathbf{i}} = 1$ if and only if
$S = \{ j \in [1:d]: i_j \not= 1 \}$.
Moreover,
the entry $(M_{\le s})_{\mathbf{i}}$ equals $1$
if and only if there exists $S \subseteq [1:d]$ with $|S| \le s$ such that $(M_S)_{\mathbf{i}} = 1$,
i.e., such that $S = \{ j \in [1:d]: i_j \not= 1 \}$.
In other words, one has $(M_{\le s})_{\mathbf{i}} = 1$
if and only if $|\{ j \in [1:d]: i_j \not= 1 \}| \le s$,
as desired.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Showing that  $V_s \cap \ker(\Lambda) = \{ 0 \}$
consists in proving that,
if $X \in {\rm range}(\Pi_{\le s})$ satisfies $X[\mathbf{i}] = 0$ for all $\mathbf{i} \in \mathcal{O}$,
then $X = 0$.
In view of~\eqref{CheapAction},
such an $X$ can be written as $X = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(Z)$,
where $Z := M_{\le s} \odot \mathcal{F}(X)$ is supported on $\mathcal{I}_{d,s}$.
Taking $\mathcal{O} \supseteq \mathcal{I}_{d,s}$ into account,
it is therefore enough to prove that
\begin{equation}
\label{IndHyp}
\mbox{if } Z \mbox{ is supported on } \mathcal{I}_{d,s}
\mbox{ and satisfies }
\mathcal{F}(Z)[\mathbf{i}] = 0
\mbox{ for all } i \in \mathcal{I}_{d,s},
\mbox{ then }
Z = 0.
\end{equation}
We shall prove by induction on $d \ge 1$ that \eqref{IndHyp} holds for all $s=1,\ldots,d$.
The base case $d=1$ is nothing but the invertibility of the one-dimensional discrete Fourier transform.
Now, assuming that \eqref{IndHyp} holds up to $d-1$ for all relevant~$s$,
our goal is to prove that it holds for $d$ and  all relevant~$s$.
As the case $s=d$ is again nothing but the invertibility of the $d$-dimensional discrete Fourier transform, we suppose that $s \le d-1$.
At this point, it is useful to remark that
$$
\mathcal{I}_{d,s} = (\mathcal{I}_{d-1,s} \times \{n_d\}) 
\sqcup (\mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1} \times [1:n_d-1])
\qquad \mbox{and} \qquad
\mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1} \subseteq \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s}.
$$
For instance,
the former is used to explicit the identities $0 = \mathcal{F}(Z)[\mathbf{i}]$ for any $\mathbf{i} \in \mathcal{I}_{d,s}$ as
\begin{align*}
0 & = 
\sum_{\mathbf{k} \in \mathcal{I}_{d,s}} 
Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},k_d] 
\; 
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \left( \frac{i_1 k_1}{n_1} + \cdots + \frac{i_{d-1}k_{d-1}}{n_{d-1}} + \frac{i_{d} k_{d}}{n_{d}} \right) } \\
& = \sum_{ (k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s}} 
Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},n_d] 
\; 
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \left( \frac{i_1 k_1}{n_1} + \cdots + \frac{i_{d-1}k_{d-1}}{n_{d-1}} \right) }\\
& + 
\sum_{(k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1}}
\bigg(
\sum_{k_d = 1}^{n_d - 1} Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},k_d] 
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \frac{i_d k_d}{n_d}}
\bigg)
\;
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \left( \frac{i_1 k_1}{n_1} + \cdots + \frac{i_{d-1}k_{d-1}}{n_{d-1}} \right) } .
\end{align*}
At this point, there are two cases to separate,
namely $\mathbf{i} \in  \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s} \times \{n_d\}$ and $\mathbf{i} \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1} \times [1:n_d-1]$.
In the fist case, one obtains that,
for all $(i_1,\ldots,i_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s}$,
\begin{align*}
0 & = 
\sum_{ (k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s} \setminus \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1}} 
Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},n_d] 
\; 
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \left( \frac{i_1 k_1}{n_1} + \cdots + \frac{i_{d-1}k_{d-1}}{n_{d-1}} \right) }\\
& + 
\sum_{(k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1}}
\bigg(
\sum_{k_d = 1}^{n_d} Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},k_d] 
\bigg)
\;
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \left( \frac{i_1 k_1}{n_1} + \cdots + \frac{i_{d-1}k_{d-1}}{n_{d-1}} \right) } .
\end{align*}
Invoking the induction hypothesis                 
for $(d-1,s)$,
one deduces that
\begin{align}
\label{PfNecCond1}
& \mbox{for all } (k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s} \setminus \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1},
& Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},n_d] & = 0,\\
\label{PfNecCond2}
& \mbox{for all } (k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1},
&  \sum_{k_d = 1}^{n_d} Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},k_d] 
& = 0.
\end{align}
In the second case, 
making use of \eqref{PfNecCond1},
one obtains that,
for all $(i_1,\ldots,i_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1}$ and all $i_d \in [1:n_d - 1]$,
$$
0 = 
\sum_{(k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1}}
\bigg(
\sum_{k_d = 1}^{n_d } Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},k_d] 
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \frac{i_d k_d}{n_d}}
\bigg)
\;
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \left( \frac{i_1 k_1}{n_1} + \cdots + \frac{i_{d-1}k_{d-1}}{n_{d-1}} \right) } .
$$
Invoking the induction hypothesis                 
for $(d-1,s-1)$,
one deduces that, for all $(k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in~\mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1}$,
$$
\sum_{k_d = 1}^{n_d } Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},k_d] 
e^{\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \frac{i_d k_d}{n_d}}
= 0
\qquad \mbox{for all } i_d \in [1:n_d - 1],
$$
which also holds for $i_d = n_d$ by virtue of~\eqref{PfNecCond2}.
Collectively interpreted as the one-dimensional discrete Fourier transform of $Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},\cdot]$,
these equalities imply that 
$Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},k_d] = 0$
for all $(k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1}$ and all $k_d \in [1:n_d]$.
In particular,
the latter yields $Z[\mathbf{k}] = 0$ for all $\mathbf{k} \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1} \times [1:n_d-1]$.
It also yields $Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},n_d] = 0$ for all $(k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s-1}$,
which,
combined with~\eqref{PfNecCond1}, implies that $Z[k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1},n_d] = 0$ for all $(k_1,\ldots,k_{d-1}) \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s}$,
or equivalently that $Z[\mathbf{k}] = 0$ for all $\mathbf{k} \in \mathcal{I}_{d-1,s} \times \{n_d\}$.
Altogether, these two cases ensures that $Z[\mathbf{k}] = 0$ for all $\mathbf{k} \in \mathcal{I}_{d,s}$.
Since $Z$ was supported on $\mathcal{I}_{d,s}$,
one concludes that $Z = 0$,
hence establishing \eqref{IndHyp} for $(d,s)$.
This concludes the inductive proof.

<!-- thm-2: body_md -->
\label{ThmNov1}
Given the model and uncertainty sets $\{ f \in F: \|P_{V^\perp} f \| \le \varepsilon \}$ and $\{ e \in \ell_2^m: \|e\| \le \eta \}$,
if $\Lambda \Lambda^* = \mathrm{Id}$,
then, 
when ${\rm range}(\Gamma^*) \subseteq \ker(\Lambda)$ 
or when $\Gamma = \mathrm{Id}$,
one has
$$
{\rm gwce}(\Gamma \circ \Delta^\sigma) 
= \inf_{\Delta} {\rm gwce}(\Delta)
\qquad \mbox{ for all } \sigma \in (0,1),
$$
i.e.,
all regularization maps \eqref{RegProg},
when composed with $\Gamma$, produce globally optimal recovery maps.
<!-- end thm-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
For now, let $\Gamma$ be an arbitrary (linear) quantity of interest.  
According to \eqref{GWCE-lin} and 
\eqref{MinGWCEasSDP},
with the change $c = a \varepsilon^{-2}$ and $d = b \eta^{-2}$,
both ${\rm gwce}(\Gamma \circ \Delta^\sigma)^2$ 
and $\inf_{\Delta} {\rm gwce}(\Delta)^2$
are mininizers of $c \varepsilon^2 + d \eta^2$ subject to, respectively,
\begin{align}
\label{Const1}
c \|P_{V^\perp}f\|^2 + d  \| e\|^2 \phantom{\Lambda}
& \ge \|\Gamma(f - \Delta^\sigma(\Lambda f +e)) \|^2
& & \forall f\in F, e \in \mathbb{R}^m,\\
\label{Const2}
c \|P_{V^\perp}f\|^2 + d \|\Lambda f\|^2 
& \ge \| \Gamma f\|^2
& & \forall f \in F.
\end{align}
Thus,
it is enough to show that the constraints \eqref{Const1} and \eqref{Const2} are equivalent.
Since it is clear that \eqref{Const1} implies \eqref{Const2} by taking $e = - \Lambda f$,
it remains to show that \eqref{Const2} implies \eqref{Const1}.
So let us assume that \eqref{Const2} holds in the form
\begin{equation}
\label{Const2bis}
c \|P_{V^\perp}f\|^2 + d \|P_{W^\perp} f\|^2 
 \ge \| \Gamma f\|^2
\qquad \forall f \in F,
\end{equation}
where $P_{W^\perp} = \Lambda^* \Lambda$ is the orthogonal projector onto $\operatorname{range}(\Lambda^*) = W^\perp$,
$W := \ker(\Lambda)$.
Let us now consider $f \in F$ and $e \in \mathbb{R}^m$.
Introducing $g:= f - \Delta^\sigma(\Lambda f +e)$, 
we need to prove that 
\begin{equation}
\label{Const1bis}
c\|P_{V^\perp} f \|^2 + d \|e\|^2 \overset{?}{\ge} \|\Gamma g \|^2.
\end{equation}
Note that, in view of $\|e\|^2 = \| \Lambda^* e\|^2$, the left-hand side is 
$$
c\|P_{V^\perp} f \|^2 + d \|e\|^2 
= \alpha + \beta,
\qquad \mbox{where }
\begin{cases}
\alpha = c \|P_{\phantom{(}V+W \phantom{)^\perp}} P_{V^\perp} f\|^2 + d \|P_{\phantom{(}V+W \phantom{)^\perp}}\Lambda^* e\|^2,\\
\beta = c \|P_{(V+W)^\perp} P_{V^\perp} f\|^2 + d \|P_{(V+W)^\perp}\Lambda^* e\|^2.
\end{cases}
$$
Because we can express the regularization map as\footnote{The existence of the inverse owes to the running assumption \eqref{NecCond} that $V\cap W = \{0\}$, which, in infinite dimensions, must be strengthened to the existence of some $\delta>0$ such that $\max \{ \|P_{V^\perp} f\|, \| P_{W^\perp} f\| \} \ge \delta \|f\|$ for all $f \in F$.}
$\Delta^\sigma = [(1-\sigma) P_{V^\perp} + \sigma P_{W^\perp}]^{-1} \sigma \Lambda^*$,
we have
\begin{align*}
g & = [(1-\sigma) P_{V^\perp} + \sigma P_{W^\perp}]^{-1} 
((1-\sigma) P_{V^\perp}f + \sigma P_{W^\perp}f - \sigma \Lambda^* (\Lambda f + e))\\
& = [(1-\sigma) P_{V^\perp} + \sigma P_{W^\perp}]^{-1} 
((1-\sigma) P_{V^\perp} f - \sigma \Lambda^* e). 
\end{align*}
This implies that $(1-\sigma) P_{V^\perp} g + \sigma P_{W^\perp}g = (1-\sigma) P_{V^\perp} f - \sigma \Lambda^* e$,
and hence
\begin{equation}
\label{in(V+W)perp} 
(1-\sigma) (P_{V^\perp} g - P_{V^\perp} f) 
= - \sigma (\Lambda^* e + P_{W^\perp} g)
\quad \mbox{ belongs to }
 V^\perp \cap W^\perp = (V+W)^\perp.
\end{equation}
Applying $P_{V+W}$ to \eqref{in(V+W)perp} leads to
$P_{V+W} (P_{V^\perp} g - P_{V^\perp} f) = 0 $
and $P_{V+W}(\Lambda^* e + P_{W^\perp} g) = 0$, 
so that 
$$
P_{V+W}  P_{V^\perp} f = P_{V+W} P_{V^\perp} g 
\qquad \mbox{and} \qquad
P_{V+W}\Lambda^* e = - P_{V+W} P_{W^\perp} g.
$$
As a result, we obtain
$$
\alpha = c \|P_{V+W } P_{V^\perp} g\|^2 + d \|P_{V+W } P_{W^\perp} g\|^2
= c \| P_{V^\perp} P_{V+W }  g\|^2 + d \|P_{W^\perp} P_{V+W } g\|^2,
$$
where the last step used the fact that orthogonal projectors $P_{Z'}$ and $P_{Z^\perp}$ commute for subspaces $Z \subseteq Z'$
(by virtue of $P_{Z^\perp} = \mathrm{Id} - P_{Z}$
and of $P_{Z'} P_Z = P_Z P_{Z'} = P_Z$).
Therefore, as a consequence of the constraint \eqref{Const2} expressed as in \eqref{Const2bis}, we arrive at 
$$
\alpha \ge \| \Gamma P_{V+W}  g\|^2.
$$
This is enough to brush aside the case ${\rm range}(\Gamma^*) \subseteq \ker(\Lambda)$,
as then ${\rm range}(\Gamma^*) \subseteq V + W$ yields $P_{V+W} \Gamma^* = \Gamma^*$,
or equivalently $\Gamma P_{V+W}= \Gamma$,
so that the previous inequality reads $\alpha \ge \|\Gamma g \|^2$
and the inequality $\alpha + \beta \ge \|\Gamma g \|^2$ 
requested in \eqref{Const1bis} immediately follows.

For the quantity of interest $\Gamma = \mathrm{Id}$,
in addition to $\alpha \ge \|P_{V+W}g\|^2$,
we shall also establish that $\beta \ge \|P_{(V+W)^\perp}g\|^2 $,
yielding $\alpha + \beta \ge \|g\|^2$ to validate \eqref{Const1bis}.
To this end, we apply $P_{(V + W)^\perp}$ to \eqref{in(V+W)perp},
which leads to, in view of $(V + W)^\perp \subseteq V^\perp$ and $(V + W)^\perp \subseteq W^\perp$ and after some rearrangement,
$$
P_{(V+W)^\perp} g
= 
(1-\sigma) P_{(V+W)^\perp} f - \sigma P_{(V+W)^\perp}\Lambda^* e.
$$
By convexity, we deduce that 
$$
\|P_{(V+W)^\perp} g \|^2
\le (1-\sigma) \|P_{(V+W)^\perp} f\|^2 + \sigma \| P_{(V+W)^\perp}\Lambda^* e\|^2.
$$
Notice that the constraint \eqref{Const2bis} with $\Gamma = \mathrm{Id}$ forces $c,d \ge 1$ by taking some nonzero $f \in W$ and $f \in V$.
Consequently,
we arrive at 
$$
\|P_{(V+W)^\perp} g \|^2
\le c \|P_{(V+W)^\perp} f\|^2 + d \| P_{(V+W)^\perp}\Lambda^* e\|^2
= c \|P_{(V+W)^\perp} P_{V^\perp} f\|^2 + d \| P_{(V+W)^\perp}\Lambda^* e\|^2 = \beta.
$$
As this was the last inequality to establish,
the proof is now complete.

\label{ThmNov2}
Given the model and uncertainty sets $\{ f \in F: \|P_{V^\perp} f \| \le \varepsilon \}$ and $\{ e \in \ell_2^m: \|e\| \le \eta \}$,
if $\Lambda \Lambda^* = \mathrm{Id}$,
then the locally optimal recovery map for $\Gamma = \mathrm{Id}$ is given by
$$
y \in \mathbb{R}^m
\mapsto
\Big[ 
\underset{f \in F}{{\rm argmin}\, \;} 
(1-\tau^\sharp) \|P_{V^\perp}f\|^2 + \tau^\sharp \|y-\Lambda f\|^2
\Big] \in F,
$$
where the regularization parameter $\tau^\sharp$,
depending on $y$, 
is the unique solution between $1/2$ and $\varepsilon/(\varepsilon+\eta)$ to the sextic equation 
\begin{equation}
\label{Sextic}
c \, \big( (1-\tau)\varepsilon^2 - \tau \eta^2 + (1-\tau)\tau(1-2\tau)\delta^2 \big)^2
- (\varepsilon^2 - \eta^2 + (1-2\tau) \delta^2) ((1-\tau)^2 \varepsilon^2 - \tau^2 \eta^2) = 0,
\end{equation}
with $c := {\rm eig}_{\min}(P_{V^\perp} + \Lambda^* \Lambda)(2-{\rm eig}_{\min}(P_{V^\perp} + \Lambda^* \Lambda)$ and $\delta := \min_{\Lambda f = y} \|P_{V^\perp} f\|  = \min_{f \in V }  \|\Lambda f - y\|$.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
As recalled in the previous subsection,
the locally optimal recovery map is 
$y \in \mathbb{R}^m \mapsto \Delta^{\sigma^\sharp} y$,
where the regularization parameter $\sigma^\sharp$
corresponds via \eqref{CorrespSigTau}
to the unique solution~$\tau^\sharp$ between $1/2$ and $\varepsilon/(\varepsilon+\eta)$ 
to
$$
                   
e(\tau) = r(\tau),
\qquad \mbox{where} \quad
r(\tau) := \frac{(1-\tau)^2 \varepsilon^2 - \tau^2 \eta^2}{(1-\tau) \varepsilon^2 - \tau \eta^2 + (1-\tau) \tau (1-2\tau) \delta^2}.
$$
Here $e(\tau) := {\rm eig}_{\min}((1-\tau) P_{V^\perp} + \tau \Lambda^* \Lambda)$ satisfies the differential equation
\begin{equation}
\label{DiffEq}
\frac{d e(\tau)}{d\tau}
= \frac{1-2\tau}{\tau (1-\tau)} \frac{e(\tau)(1-e(\tau))}{1-2 e(\tau)}.
\end{equation}
The original article \citep{foucart2023optimal} failed to notice, however,
that this differential equation could be solved somewhat explicitly.
To this end,
consider the function of $\tau \in [0,1]$ defined by
$$
C(\tau) = \frac{e(\tau)(1-e(\tau))}{\tau(1-\tau)}
= \frac{e(\tau)-e(\tau)^2}{\tau - \tau^2},
$$
whose derivative, thanks to \eqref{DiffEq}, becomes 
$$
\frac{dC(\tau)}{d\tau}
= 
\frac{1-2e(\tau)}{\tau(1-\tau)} \frac{de(\tau)}{d\tau} 
- \frac{e(\tau)(1-e(\tau))}{(\tau(1-\tau))^2} (1-2\tau)
= 0.
$$
This implies that the function $C$ is constant,
equal to $c:=C(1/2)$, say, and we deduce that
\label{quadratic_tau}
$$
e(\tau)(1-e(\tau)) = c \, \tau(1-\tau)
\qquad \mbox{for all } \tau \in [0,1].
$$
On the one hand, since the optimal parameter $\tau^\sharp$,
lying between $1/2$ and $\varepsilon / (\varepsilon + \eta)$,
must satisfy $e(\tau) = r(\tau)$ and in turn 
$r(\tau)(1-r(\tau)) = c \, \tau(1-\tau)$,
we derive,
after expressing $1-r(\tau)$ as a ratio,
that $\tau^\sharp$ must be a solution to 
$$
\frac{\tau (1-\tau) (\varepsilon^2 - \eta^2 + (1-2\tau) \delta^2) ((1-\tau)^2 \varepsilon^2 - \tau^2 \eta^2)}{\big( (1-\tau)\varepsilon^2 - \tau \eta^2 + (1-\tau)\tau(1-2\tau)\delta^2 \big)^2} = c \, \tau (1-\tau),
$$
which is readily equivalent to the announced sextic equation. 

On the other hand, let $\tau$ be a solution to this sextic equation lying between $1/2$ and $\varepsilon / (\varepsilon + \eta)$.
Reverting the above,
we obtain that $r(\tau)(1-r(\tau)) = c \, \tau(1-\tau)$,
and since $e(\tau)(1-e(\tau)) = c \, \tau(1-\tau)$,
we deduce that $e(\tau)(1-e(\tau)) =  r(\tau)(1-r(\tau))$.
This implies that
$e(\tau)=r(\tau)$---which is what we need for $\tau = \tau^\sharp$ to follow---or $e(\tau)=1-r(\tau)$---which we are aiming to invalidate.
To this end, it~is useful to recall from \citep[Proof of Theorem~8]{foucart2023optimal}
that the denominator $d(\tau)$ of $1-r(\tau)$ does not vanish between $1/2$ and $\varepsilon/(\varepsilon+\eta)$,
that $\delta \le \varepsilon + \eta$,
and that $e(\tau) \in [0,1/2]$.
By contradiction,
assume now that $e(\tau) = 1-r(\tau) \not= r(\tau)$, so that $1-r(\tau) \in [0,1/2)$,
and consider e.g. the case $\varepsilon \ge \eta$.
Since then $1/2 \le \tau \le \varepsilon/(\varepsilon+\eta)$,
the numerator $n(\tau)$ of $1-r(\tau)$ satisfies
$$
\frac{n(\tau)}{\tau(1-\tau)} 
= \varepsilon^2 - \eta^2 + (1-2\tau) \delta^2
\ge \varepsilon^2 - \eta^2 + (1-2\tau) (\varepsilon + \eta)^2
\ge \varepsilon^2 - \eta^2 + \Big(1-\frac{2 \varepsilon}{\varepsilon + \eta} \Big) (\varepsilon + \eta)^2 = 0,
$$
i.e., its is nonnegative, 
hence the denominator $d(\tau)$ must be positive.
Therefore, the inequality $1 - r(\tau) < 1/2$ yields $2 n(\tau) - d(\tau) < 0$.
However, after a simplification step, we have
\begin{align*}
2 n(\tau) - d(\tau)
& = (2\tau-1) [(1-\tau) \varepsilon^2 + \tau \eta^2 - \tau(1-\tau) \delta^2]\\
& \ge (2\tau-1) [(1-\tau) \varepsilon^2 + \tau \eta^2 - \tau(1-\tau) (\varepsilon^2 + \eta^2 + 2 \varepsilon \eta)]\\
& = (2\tau-1) [(1-\tau)^2 \varepsilon^2 + \tau^2 \eta^2 - 2 \tau(1-\tau)  \varepsilon \eta]
= (2\tau - 1) [(1-\tau) \varepsilon - \tau \eta]^2\\
& \ge 0.
\end{align*}
This provides the contradiction desired to finish the proof.

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
\label{PropPower}
Let $B \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ be positive semidefinite matrix with largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$.
Given integers $k,t \ge 0$,
consider independent standard gaussian vectors $g^{(1)},\ldots,g^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^N$
and form the computable randomized estimator
$$
\widehat{\lambda}_{k,t} :=
\bigg[
\frac{5}{k}
\sum_{j=1}^k \big\langle B^t g^{(j)}, g^{(j)} \big\rangle
\bigg]^{1/t}.
$$
Given two small parameters $\iota,\theta>0$,
as soon as $\displaystyle{k \ge 25 \ln \Big(  \dfrac{2}{\theta} \Big)}$
and
$\displaystyle{t \ge \dfrac{\ln(9N)}{\ln(1+\iota)}}$,
it holds with probability at least $1-\theta$ that
$$
\lambda_{\max}
\le
\widehat{\lambda}_{k,t}
\le
(1+\iota)\lambda_{\max}.
$$
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Write the eigendecomposition of $B$ as 
$$
B = \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i v_i v_i^*,
\quad \mbox{with } \lambda_{\max} = \lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_N \ge 0
\; \mbox{ and } \;
(v_1, \ldots,v_N) \mbox{ an orthonormal basis of } \mathbb{R}^N. 
$$
For each standard gaussian vector $g^{(j)}$,
we have $g^{(j)} = \sum_{i=1}^N g^{(j)}_i v_i$,
where all the $g^{(j)}_i = \langle g^{(j)}, v_i \rangle$ are independent standard gaussian variables.
The random variable $\xi_{j,t}:=\langle B^t g^{(j)}, g^{(j)} \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i^t (g^{(j)}_i)^2$
therefore has expectation 
$$
\mathbb{E}_t: = \mathbb{E} \big[ \langle B^t g^{(j)}, g^{(j)} \rangle \big]
= \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i^t \;
\begin{cases}
\le & N \lambda_{\max}^t,\\
\ge & \lambda_{\max}^t. \phantom{N}
\end{cases}
$$
Based on the moment generating function of the square of a standard gaussian variable (see e.g. \citep[(B.5)]{foucart2022mathematical}),
the moment generating function of $\xi_{j,t}$ at $u>0$ is
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}[\exp(u \xi_{j,t})]
& = \prod_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[\exp(u \lambda_i^t (g^{(j)}_i)^2)]
= \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2u\lambda_i^t}}\\
& \le \prod_{i=1}^N \exp( u \lambda_i^t + 4 u^2 \lambda_i^{2t} )
= \exp(u \mathbb{E}_t + 4 u^2 \mathbb{E}_{2t})\\
& \le \exp(u \mathbb{E}_t + 4 u^2 \mathbb{E}_{t}^2).
\end{align*}
As a result, for any $\omega \in (0,1)$
and making the choice $u = \omega/(8 \mathbb{E}_t)$,
an application of Markov inequality yields
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{P} \bigg[ \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=1}^k \big\langle B^t g^{(j)}, g^{(j)} \big\rangle & > (1+\omega) \mathbb{E}_t \bigg]
= \mathbb{P} \bigg[ \exp\Big( u \sum_{j=1}^k \xi_{j,t} \Big) 
> \exp(u k (1+\omega) \mathbb{E}_t)
\bigg]\\
& \le \frac{\mathbb{E} \big[ \exp\big( u \sum_{j=1}^k \xi_{j,t} \big) \big]}{\exp(u k (1+\omega) \mathbb{E}_t)}
= \prod_{j=1}^k  \frac{\mathbb{E}[\exp(u \xi_{j,t})]}{\exp(u(1+\omega) \mathbb{E}_t)}
\le \prod_{j=1}^k  \frac{\exp(u \mathbb{E}_t + 4 u^2 \mathbb{E}_{t}^2)}{\exp(u(1+\omega) \mathbb{E}_t)}\\
& = \prod_{j=1}^k \exp(-u \omega \mathbb{E}_t + 4 u^2 \mathbb{E}_t^2)
= \prod_{j=1}^k \exp \Big( - \frac{\omega^2}{16} \Big)
= \exp \Big( - \frac{\omega^2 k}{16} \Big).
 \end{align*}
In a similar fashion and with details left to the reader,
we also derive
$$
\mathbb{P} \bigg[ \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=1}^k  \big\langle B^t g^{(j)}, g^{(j)} \big\rangle < (1-\omega) \mathbb{E}_t \bigg]
\le \exp \Big( - \frac{\omega^2 k}{16} \Big).
$$
This means that, with failure probability at most $2 \exp(-\omega^2 k/16)$,
we have
$$
\frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=1}^k  \big\langle B^t g^{(j)}, g^{(j)} \big\rangle \;
\begin{cases}
\le & (1+\omega) \mathbb{E}_t \le (1+\omega)N \lambda_{\max}^t,\\
\ge & (1-\omega) \mathbb{E}_t \ge (1-\omega) \lambda_{\max}^t, \phantom{N}
\end{cases}
$$
which implies that
$$
\lambda_{\max}
\le \bigg[ \frac{1}{(1-\omega)k} \sum_{j=1}^k  \big\langle B^t g^{(j)}, g^{(j)} \big\rangle \bigg]^{1/t}
\le \bigg( \frac{1+\omega}{1-\omega} N \bigg)^{1/t} \lambda_{\max}.
$$
By choosing $\omega = 4/5$,
we conclude that $\lambda_{\max} \le \widehat{\lambda}_{k,t} \le (9N)^{1/t} \lambda_{\max}$ with failure probability at most $2 \exp(-k/25)$. 
We arrive at $\lambda_{\max} \le \widehat{\lambda}_{k,t} \le (1+\iota) \lambda_{\max}$ with failure probability at most $\theta$ when $k \ge 25 \ln(2/\theta)$ and $t \ge \ln(9N)/\ln(1+\iota)$,
as announced in Proposition~\ref{PropPower}.
