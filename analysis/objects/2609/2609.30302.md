\label{teo-pspace1}
The games $\operatorname{CHG}_{\ell_k}$, $\operatorname{CHG}_{\operatorname{m}}$ and $\operatorname{CHG}_{\operatorname{g}}$ are PSPACE-complete even in graphs with diameter at most three for every $k\ge2$.

First consider the monophonic $\operatorname{m}$-convexity or some $\ell_k$-convexity with $k\ge3$. The result of the $\ell_2$-convexity will be shown later.
Let $H$ be an instance of the \textsc{Clique Forming} game. We may assume that $H$ is not complete.

Notice that the only induced paths of length greater than two are inside $H$ or are of type $wu_1''vu_2''$, where $v\in V(H)$, $u_1''\in\{u_1,u_1'\}$ and $u_2''\in\{u_2,u_2'\}$.

If $Y$ plays in $w$, then $X$ plays in $u_2$ and wins immediately.
If $Y$ plays in $u_2$ or $u_2'$, then $X$ plays in $w$ and wins immediately.
If $Y$ plays in $u_1$ (resp. $u_1'$), then $X$ plays in $u_1'$ (resp. $u_1$).

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm-sprague}
The 1st player wins an impartial finite game in the normal variant if and only if the nimber is positive.
Moreover, the nimber of the sum of $k$ disjoint positions with nimbers $n_1,\ldots,n_k$ is equal to $n_1\oplus\ldots\oplus n_k$, where $\oplus$ is the bitwise xor (exclusive-or) operation.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm-nimber-monof}
Let $n\ge1$.
Then the nimber $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P'_n)=n-1$.
Moreover, the nimber $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P_n)=1$, if $n$ is odd, and $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P_n)=0$, otherwise. Finally, $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(C_3)=1$ and $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(C_n)=0$ if $n\ge4$.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Notice that $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P'_1)=0$, since there is no move. So, let $v_1,\ldots v_n$ be the vertices of the path $P'_n$, assuming that $v_n$ is already selected in the start of the game. Considering all possible moves for the 1st player, in the vertices $v_1,\ldots,v_{n-1}$, we have
\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P'_n)\ =\ \operatorname{mex}\{\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P'_1),\ldots,\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P'_{n-1})\}.
\]
Then, by induction, $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P'_n)=\operatorname{mex}\{0,\ldots,n-2\}=n-1$.
Also note that, by playing in the vertex $v_{i+1}$ with $i=0,\ldots,n-1$,
\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P_n)\ =\ \operatorname{mex}\{\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P'_{i+1})\oplus\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P'_{j+1}):\ i+j=n-1,\ \ i,j\ge0\}.
\]
Then
\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P_n)\ =\ \operatorname{mex}\{i\oplus j:\ i+j=n-1,\ \ i,j\ge0\}.
\]
If $n$ is even, then $i+j$ is odd and, consequently, $i\ne j$ and $i\oplus j\ne 0$, implying that $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P_n)=0$.
If $n$ is odd, then $i+j$ is even (have the same parity) and consequently $i\oplus j=0$ when $i=j=(n+1)/2$ and $i\oplus j$ is never equal to $1$, implying that $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(P_n)=1$.
 
Finally, it is easy to check that $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(C_3)=1$ and that $\operatorname{nim}_{\operatorname{m}}(C_n)=0$ with $n\ge4$, since the first player always loses (the opponent just select a non-adjacent vertex of the 1st selected vertex).

<!-- theorem-5: body_md -->
\label{thm-nimber-Pn}
Let $k\ge2$.
The nimbers $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})$, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n})$ and $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})$ in the game $\operatorname{CHG}_{\ell_k}$ can be determined in quadratic time by the following recurrences: $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{1})=1$, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{2})=0$, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{1})=0$, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{2})=1$ and $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})=0$ for $n=1,\ldots,k+1$. Moreover,
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})\ =\ &\operatorname{mex}\Big\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{i}) \oplus\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n-i+1})\ :\ i=1\ldots\lceil n/2\rceil\Big\},\ \mbox{for $n\ge3$};\\
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n})\ =\ &\operatorname{mex}\Big\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{i}) \oplus\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n-i+1})\ :\ i=2\ldots n\Big\},\ \ \ \ \ \ \mbox{for $n\ge3$};\\
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})\ =\ &\operatorname{mex}\Big\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{i}) \oplus\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n-i+1})\ :\ i=2\ldots\lceil n/2\rceil\Big\},\ \mbox{for $n\ge k+2$}.
\end{align*}
<!-- end theorem-5 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Clearly, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{1})=0$ and $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})=0$ if $n\in\{2,\ldots,k+1\}$, since there is no move. Moreover, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{1})=\operatorname{mex}\{0\}=1$, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{2})=\operatorname{mex}\{1\}=0$ and $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{2})=\operatorname{mex}\{0\}=1$.
So, let $v_1,\ldots v_n$ be the vertices of the path.
 
First consider the game in $P_n$ with $n\ge3$.
By symmetry, we may consider that the first move is in a vertex $v_i$ for $i\in\{1,\ldots,\lceil n/2\rceil\}$.
This move produces two independent games, $P'_{i}$ and $P'_{n-i+1}$, and we are done by the Sprague-Grundy Theorem~\ref{thm-sprague}.
 
Now consider the game in $P'_n$ with $n\ge3$ and $v_1$ already selected. Then the possible moves are in a vertex $v_i$ among $v_2,\ldots,v_n$. This move produces two independent games, $P''_i$ and $P'_{n-i+1}$, and we are done from Sprague-Grundy Theorem~\ref{thm-sprague}.
 
Finally consider the game in $P''_n$ with $n\ge k+2$ and $v_1$ and $v_n$ already selected. Then the possible moves are in the vertices $v_2,\ldots,v_{n-1}$. By symmetry, we may assume that the move is in a vertex $v_i$ with $i\in\{2,\ldots,\lceil n/2\rceil\}$. This move produces two independent games, $P''_i$ and $P''_{n-i+1}$, and we are done from Sprague-Grundy Theorem~\ref{thm-sprague}.
 
With a dynamic programming algorithm, it is possible to determine $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})$, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n})$ and $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})$ in time $O(n^2)$.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor-nimber-Cn}
Let $k\ge2$.
The nimber $\operatorname{nim}(C_n)$ of the cycle $C_n$ with $n\ge3$ vertices in the normal variant of the game $\operatorname{CHG}_{\ell_k}$ is $1$ for $n=3$, is $0$ for $4\le n\le2k$, and is $\operatorname{mex}\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n+1})\}$, otherwise. Therefore, its value is either $0$ or $1$, and can be determined in quadratic time.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Clearly $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(C_3)=1$ for any $k\ge2$, since all the three vertices will be selected during the game.
Moreover, if $4\le n\le2k$, then $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(C_n)=0$, since, after Alice's first move, Bob can select the farthest vertex, winning the game immediately.
 
So let $n>2k$. Notice that the first move of Alice generates the game in $P''_{n+1}$, by duplicating the chosen vertex and considering them as the endpoints of $P''_{n+1}$ (recall that $n>2k$ and then all internal vertices of $P''_{n+1}$ are playable, just as in $C_n$ after Alice's first move).
From the Sprague--Grundy Theorem~\ref{thm-sprague}, $\operatorname{nim}(C_n)=\operatorname{mex}\{\operatorname{nim}(P''_{n+1})\}$.
From Theorem~\ref{thm-nimber-Pn}, it is possible to determine $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(C_n)$ in time $O(n^2)$.

\label{lem:phaseA}
Let $k\ge2$ and $1\le n\le 3k+2$. Then

\begin{cases}

\end{cases}

From Theorem~\ref{thm-nimber-Pn}, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})=0$ for $n\leq k+1$. So, let $n>k+1$ and let $r=n\%(k+1)$.
The $\operatorname{mex}$ function of the recurrence of $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})$ in Theorem~\ref{thm-nimber-Pn} includes all terms
$\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{i})\oplus\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{j})$ such that $i+j=n+1$ and $i\ge j\ge2$.
 
First assume that $n\in\{k+2,\ldots,2k+1\}$.
Since $i+j=n+1\leq 2k+2$ and $i\ge j$, it follows that $2\le j\le k+1$ and $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{j})=0$. Then the $\operatorname{mex}$ function exactly contains the $k$ values $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n-1}),\ldots,\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n-k})$ and, by induction, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})=\operatorname{mex}(\{0,\ldots,r-1\})=r$.

\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})\ =\ \operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{2k+2})\ =\ \operatorname{mex}\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{k+2}),\ldots,\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{2k+1})\}\ =\ \operatorname{mex}\{1,\ldots,k\}=0.
\]

As before, the $\operatorname{mex}$ function for $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})$ contains the $k$ values $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n-1}),\ldots,\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n-k})$, which are by induction $\{0,\ldots,k\}\setminus\{r\}$.

It also contains the values $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{i})\oplus\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{j})$ with $i+j=n+1$ and $i\ge j\ge k+2$, which are by induction $r_i\oplus r_j$, where $r_i=i\%(k+1)$ and $r_j=j\%(k+1)$.
Since $j\ge k+2$: $i\le 2k+1$.
Therefore $r_i\oplus r_j$ has the same parity of $i\oplus j$, which is the same parity of $n+1$ and also of $(n+1)\%(k+1)$, because $n\in\{2k+3,\ldots,3k+2\}$. Consequently $r_i\oplus r_j$ cannot be $r=n\%(k+1)$.

<!-- theorem-6: body_md -->
\label{thm:shift}
For every $k\ge2$ and every $n\ge1$,
\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n}) = \operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n+k}).
\]
<!-- end theorem-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We argue by induction on $n$. First notice that $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{1})=\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{k+1})=0$ and $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{2})=\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{k+2})=1$ from Theorem~\ref{thm-nimber-Pn} and Lemma~\ref{lem:phaseA}. First assume that $3\le n\le k+1$.
Since $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{i})=0$ for every $i=2,\ldots,k+1$, we have by induction and Theorem~\ref{thm-nimber-Pn},
\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n})\ =\ \operatorname{mex}\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n-1})\}\ =\ \operatorname{mex}\{0,\ldots,n-2\}\ =\ n-1,
\]
and consequently $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n})=(n-1)\%(k+1)=(n+k)\%(k+1)=\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n+k})$, since $n\leq k+1$.
 
Finally assume that $n\ge k+2$.
From Theorem~\ref{thm-nimber-Pn}, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n})=\operatorname{mex}_{i=2}^{n}\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{i})\ \oplus\ \operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n-i+1})\}$. Since $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n-i+1})=\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n-i+k+1})$ for $i=2,\ldots,n$ by induction:
\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{n})\ =\ \operatorname{mex}_{i=2}^{n}\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{i})\oplus\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n-i+k+1})\}.
\]
Since $n>\lceil(n+k)/2\rceil$, we may write the recurrence of Theorem~\ref{thm-nimber-Pn} for $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n+k})$ as
\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n+k})\ =\ \operatorname{mex}_{i=2}^{n}\{\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{i})\oplus\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n-i+k+1})\},
\]
and we are done.

<!-- theorem-9: body_md -->
\label{thm:family-closed-form}
Let $k=2^h-4$ with $h\ge3$. For every $n\ge 3k+4$,
\[
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n}) =
\begin{cases}
0, & \mbox{if}\ n = 5k+4,\\
k+1, & \mbox{if}\ n\%(k+1)=0,\\
n\%(k+1), & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\]
that is, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})=\sigma(n)$ for every $n\ge3k+4$ with $n\neq5k+4$, and $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{5k+4})=0$.
<!-- end theorem-9 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Let $p''(n)=\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P''_{n})$ and $E=\{2k+2,\,5k+4\}$ be the exceptional set. Recall that $k\ge4$ is even, so $k+1\ge5$ is odd.
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:phaseA} and~\ref{lem:phaseBwrap}, $p''(n)=0$ for $1\le n\le k+1$, $p''(2k+2)=0$, and $p''(n)=\sigma(n)$ for every other $n\in\{k+2,\ldots,3k+3\}$.
We prove the theorem by induction on $n\ge3k+4$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm-nimber-Pn}, $p''(n)=\operatorname{mex} S(n)$, where
\[
S(n)=\bigl\{p''(i)\oplus p''(j)\ :\ i+j=n+1,\ \ \ 2\le i\le j\le n-1\bigr\}.
\]
We split the pairs $\{i,j\}$ (with $i\le j$) into three types:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item[($T_1$)] $i\le k+1$. Then $p''(i)=0$ and the term is $p''(j)$, where $j$ ranges over the $k$ consecutive integers $n-k,\ldots,n-1$, all at least $2k+4$.
\item[($T_2$)] $i,j\ge k+2$ and $i,j\notin E$. The term is $x\oplus y$ with $x=\sigma(i)$, $y=\sigma(j)\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}$, where $x+y\equiv i+j=n+1\pmod{k+1}$ and $2\le x+y\le2k+2$.
\item[($T_3$)] $i,j\ge k+2$ and $\{i,j\}\cap E\neq\emptyset$.
\end{itemize}
The hypothesis $k=2^h-4$ is used only in the following claim, applied to type $T_2$.
 
\medskip\noindent\textbf{Claim.} Let $x,y\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}$ and $\tau\ge1$ with $x+y=k+2+\tau$. Then $x\oplus y\ne\tau$.
 
\noindent\emph{Proof of the claim.} Suppose $x\oplus y=\tau$. By $(\ast)$,
$x\mathbin{\&}y=\bigl((k+2+\tau)-\tau\bigr)/2=\rho=2^{h-1}-1$, so bits
$0,\ldots,h-2$ are set in both $x$ and $y$. Since $x,y\le k+1<2^h$, the only
other bit available is bit $h-1$, hence $x,y\in\{2^{h-1}-1,\,2^h-1\}$; as
$2^h-1=k+3>k+1$, we get $x=y=\rho$. Then $x\oplus y=0\ne\tau$, a contradiction.
\hfill$\diamond$
 
\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: the case $n=5k+4$.} Here $n+1=5(k+1)\equiv0\pmod{k+1}$, and $n+1$ is odd, so there is no self-pair $i=j$. We show $0\notin S(n)$.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Type $T_1$: $j\in\{4k+4,\ldots,5k+3\}$, which is disjoint from $E$, so $p''(j)=\sigma(j)\ge1$.
\item Type $T_2$: $x\oplus y=0$ implies $x=y$, and $x+y\equiv0\pmod{k+1}$ with $2\le x+y\le2k+2$ implies $2x\in\{k+1,2k+2\}$. The first case is impossible because $k+1$ is odd. The second gives $x=y=k+1$, so $i,j$ are multiples of $k+1$ outside $E$ and at least $k+2$, hence $i,j\ge3k+3$ and $i+j\ge6k+6>n+1$, a contradiction.
\item Type $T_3$: only $2k+2\in E$ is smaller than $n$. Its partner is $3k+3$, and the term is $0\oplus p''(3k+3)=k+1\ne0$.
\end{itemize}
Hence $p''(5k+4)=\operatorname{mex} S(5k+4)=0$.
 
\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: the case $n\neq5k+4$.} Let $\tau:=\sigma(n)\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}$. Then $n+1\equiv\tau+1\pmod{k+1}$. We show that $\tau\notin S(n)$ and $\{0,\ldots,\tau-1\}\subseteq S(n)$, so that $p''(n)=\tau$.
 
\smallskip\noindent\emph{(i) $\tau\notin S(n)$.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Type $T_1$: the $k$ consecutive integers $n-k,\ldots,n-1$ cover every residue modulo $k+1$ except that of $n$. So $\sigma(j)\ne\tau$, and $p''(j)\in\{0,\sigma(j)\}$ differs from $\tau\ge1$.
\item Type $T_2$: $x+y\equiv\tau+1\pmod{k+1}$ and $2\le x+y\le2k+2$, with $2\le\tau+1\le k+2$. Hence $x+y=\tau+1$ or $x+y=\tau+k+2$. In the first case, by $(\ast)$, $x\oplus y$ has the parity of $\tau+1$, so $x\oplus y\ne\tau$. In the second case, $x\oplus y\ne\tau$ by the claim.
\item Type $T_3$ with $2k+2$: the partner is $j=n-2k-1\ge k+3$, with $j\equiv n+1\pmod{k+1}$. If $j\in E$, the term is $0\ne\tau$. Otherwise the term is $\sigma(j)=\sigma(n+1)\in\{\tau+1,\,1\}$, where the value $1$ occurs only if $\tau=k+1$. In both cases the term differs from $\tau$.
\item Type $T_3$ with $5k+4$ (only when $n\ge5k+5$): the partner is $j=n-5k-3$. If $j\le k+1$, the pair is of type $T_1$. If $j\in E$, the term is $0\ne\tau$. Otherwise, since $5k+3\equiv-2\pmod{k+1}$, the term is $\sigma(j)\equiv j=n-(5k+3)\equiv n+2\equiv\tau+2\pmod{k+1}$, which cannot be
$\tau$, since $k+1\ge5$.
\end{itemize}
 
\smallskip\noindent\emph{(ii) $\{1,\ldots,\tau-1\}\subseteq S(n)$.} By (i), the integers $j\in\{n-k,\ldots,n-1\}$ of type $T_1$ satisfy $\{\sigma(j)\}=\{1,\ldots,k+1\}\setminus\{\tau\}$. None of these $j$ equals $2k+2$ (as $j\ge2k+4$), so $p''(j)=\sigma(j)$ for all of them, except when $5k+4\in\{n-k,\ldots,n-1\}$. In that case $p''(5k+4)=0$ replaces the value $\sigma(5k+4)=k$. This loses a needed value only if $k\le\tau-1$, that is, $\tau=k+1$, which together with $5k+5\le n\le6k+4$ means $n=5k+5$. In that case, the pair $\{k+2,\,4k+4\}$ (sum $5k+6=n+1$, both indices outside $E$) contributes $p''(k+2)\oplus p''(4k+4)=1\oplus(k+1)=k$, because $k$ is even.
 
\smallskip\noindent\emph{(iii) $0\in S(n)$.} If $n$ is odd, the self-pair $i=j=(n+1)/2$ gives $0$. Let $n$ be even. 
Let $s=\sigma(n+1)/2$ if $\sigma(n+1)$ is even, or $s=(\sigma(n+1)+k+1)/2$, otherwise.
In both cases, $s\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}$ and $2s\equiv \sigma(n+1)\equiv n+1\pmod{k+1}$.
Write $n+1-2s=N\cdot(k+1)$. Here $N(k+1)$ is odd because $n+1$ is odd, so $N$ is odd. Also $N(k+1)\ge(3k+5)-(2k+2)=k+3>k+1$, so $N\ge3$.
Take $i=2(k+1)+s$ and $j=(N-2)(k+1)+s$.
Then $i+j=n+1$ and $i,j\ge k+2$. Moreover $i\notin E$: $i=2k+2$ would need $s=0$, and $i=5k+4$ would need $s=3k+2$. Also $j\notin E$: $j=2k+2$ would need $N=3$ and $s=k+1$, that is, $n=5k+4$; $j=5k+4$ would need $N=6$, which is even. By induction, $p''(i)=p''(j)=\sigma(i)=\sigma(j)=s$, so $p''(i)\oplus p''(j)=0\in S(n)$.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:xor-realize}
Let $T,v\ge0$. There exists $x\in\{0,\ldots,T\}$ with $x\oplus(T-x)=v$ if and only if
\[
T\ge v,\qquad T\equiv v\pmod2,\qquad \text{and}\qquad v\mathbin{\&}w=0,\ \text{where } w=\tfrac{T-v}{2}.
\]
Moreover, whenever these three conditions hold, $x=w$ is such a witness.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
($\Leftarrow$) Suppose the three conditions hold, and set $x=w$, $y=v+w$. Since $v\mathbin{\&}w=0$, the numbers $v$ and $w$ have disjoint sets of set bits, so the sum $y=v+w$ has no carries and its set bits are exactly those of $v$ together with those of $w$. Hence $x\mathbin{\&}y=w\mathbin{\&}y=w$. By $(\ast)$, $x\oplus y=(x+y)-2(x\mathbin{\&}y)=(v+2w)-2w=v$, and $x+y=v+2w=v+(T-v)=T$, so $y=T-x$. Also $0\le x=w\le T$ since $0\le v\le T$. Thus $x=w\in\{0,\ldots,T\}$ is a witness.
 
($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $v=x\oplus(T-x)$ for some $x\in\{0,\ldots,T\}$; set $y=T-x$. By $(\ast)$, $T=x+y=v+2(x\mathbin{\&}y)$, so $T\ge v$, $T\equiv v\pmod 2$, and $x\mathbin{\&}y=(T-v)/2=w$. Since $x\oplus y=v$, every set bit of $v$ is a bit where exactly one of $x,y$ is set, hence not a bit of $x\mathbin{\&}y=w$. Thus $v\mathbin{\&}w=0$.
<!-- end proof-17 -->

For $i=1,\ldots,k-2$ (partner $j$ in the 3rd range, $x=i-1=0,\ldots,k-3$) and for $i=k+2,\ldots,2k$ (partner in the 2nd range, $x=i-(k+2)=0,\ldots,k-2$), both give the term $x\oplus((k-2)-x)$; together they realize the full family of Lemma~\ref{lem:xor-realize} with $T=k-2\equiv2\pmod8$, so it meets $\{0,1,2,3\}$ in $\Phi(T)=\{0,2\}$. The remaining indices $i=k-1,k,k+1$ give the terms $(k-1)\oplus k$ and $(k+1)\oplus(k-2)$, both equal to $(111)_2=7$, since $(010)_2\oplus(101)_2=(011)_2\oplus(100)_2=(111)_2$. Then $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{3k+1})=\operatorname{mex}(\{0,2,7\}\cap\{0,1,2,3\})=1$.
 
For $n=3k+2$: for $i=1,\ldots,k-1$ (partner $j$ in the 3rd range, $x=i-1=0,\ldots,k-2$) and $i=k+2,\ldots,2k+1$ (partner $j$ in the 2nd range, $x=i-(k+2)=0,\ldots,k-1$), both give the term $x\oplus(k-1-x)$, together realizing the full family with $T=k-1\equiv3\pmod8$, so $\Phi(T)=\{3\}$. The remaining $i=k,k+1$ give $(k-1)\oplus(k+1)=(011)_2\oplus(101)_2=6$ and $k\oplus k=0$. Then $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{3k+2})=\operatorname{mex}(\{3,6,0\}\cap\{0,1,2,3\})=1$.
 
For $n=3k+3$: for $i=1,\ldots,k$ (partner $j$ in the 3rd range, $x=i-1=0,\ldots,k-1$) and $i=k+2,\ldots,2k+2$ (partner $j$ in the 2nd range, $x=i-(k+2)=0,\ldots,k$), both give the term $x\oplus(k-x)$, together realizing the full family with $T=k\equiv4\pmod8$, so $\Phi(T)=\{0,2\}$. The remaining index $i=k+1$ gives $k\oplus(k+1)=1$. Then $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{3k+3})=\operatorname{mex}\{0,2,1\}=3$.
 
For $n=3k+4$: every index lies in one of $i=1,\ldots,k+1$ (partner $j$ in the 3rd range), $i=k+2,\ldots,2k+3$ (partner $j$ in the 2nd range), or its mirror $i=2k+4,\ldots,3k+4$. Writing $x=i-1=0,\ldots,k$ on the 1st range and $x=i-(k+2)=0,\ldots,k+1$ on the 2nd, both give the term $x\oplus((k+1)-x)$: the full family with $T=k+1\equiv5\pmod8$, so $\Phi(T)=\{1\}$. Then $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{3k+4})=\operatorname{mex}\{1\}=0$.
 
For $n=3k+5$: the pair $(i=1,j=3k+5)$ contributes $g(1)\oplus g(3k+5)=0\oplus1=1$; the pair $(i=k+2,j=2k+4)$ gives $g(k+2)\oplus g(2k+4)=0\oplus1=1$ (the 1st point of the 2nd range against the 1st point of the 3rd). For $i=2,\ldots,k+1$ (partner $j$ in the 3rd range, $x=i-1=1,\ldots,k$) and $i=k+3,\ldots,2k+3$ (partner in the 2nd range, $x=i-(k+2)=1,\ldots,k+1$), both give the term $x\oplus((k+2)-x)$: the family of Lemma~\ref{lem:xor-realize} with $T=k+2\equiv6\pmod8$, restricted to $x\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}$ instead of the full $\{0,\ldots,k+2\}$. By Table~\ref{tab:Phi}, the full family meets $\{0,1,2,3\}$ only in $\Phi(T)=\{0\}$, so the restricted family meets it in a subset of $\{0\}$; and $0$ does occur, since Lemma~\ref{lem:xor-realize}'s witness $x=w=(T-0)/2=k/2+1$ lies in $\{1,\ldots,k+1\}$ (as $k\ge4$). Then $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{3k+5})=\operatorname{mex}\{0,1\}=2$.

<!-- lemma-12: body_md -->
\label{lem:tail}
Given $p\ge0$ and $0\le t\le k$, let $n=3k+6+p(k+1)+t$, and let $\alpha(t)$ be the symbol at position $t$ (counting from $0$) of the word $B^bC$, that is, $\alpha(t)=B[t\%8]$ if $0\le t\le8b-1$, and $\alpha(t)=C[t-8b]$ if $8b\le t\le k$, where $B=(1,1,3,3,1,1,2,2)$ and $C=(1,1,3,2,3)$. Then $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})=\alpha(t)$.

<!-- proof-24: body_md -->
Let $g(m):=\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P'_{m})$ and $X:=\{1,\,k+2,\,4k+4\}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:nimpb-small}, $g(m)=0$ for $m\in X$ and $g(m)=\sigma(m-1)$ otherwise. We have $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})=\operatorname{mex} S$, where $S=\{g(i)\oplus g(j):i+j=n+1,\ i,j\geq 1\}$.
Since $n-1=(p+3)(k+1)+t+2$, we have $n-1\equiv t+2\pmod{k+1}$. Let
\[
T_1:=t+2,\qquad T_2:=t+k+3,\qquad R(T):=\bigl\{x\oplus(T-x)\ :\ x,\,T-x\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}\bigr\}.
\]
Since $R(T)$ is contained in the family $\{x\oplus(T-x):0\le x\le T\}$, we have $R(T)\cap\{0,1,2,3\}\subseteq\Phi(T)$. Since $k\equiv4\pmod8$, $T_1\equiv t+2$ and $T_2\equiv t+7\pmod8$.
 
\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 1: $S\subseteq R(T_1)\cup R(T_2)\cup\{0,\ \sigma(t+2),\ \sigma(t+3)\}$.} Consider a pair $i+j=n+1$.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item If $i,j\notin X$: the term is $x\oplus y$ with $x=\sigma(i-1)$, $y=\sigma(j-1)\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}$, where $x+y\equiv i+j-2=n-1\equiv T_1\pmod{k+1}$. Since $2\le x+y\le2k+2$ and $2\le T_1\le k+2$, we get $x+y\in\{T_1,T_2\}$, so the term lies in $R(T_1)\cup R(T_2)$.
\item If $i,j\in X$: the term is $0$.
\item If $i\in X$ and $j\notin X$: if $i\in\{1,k+2\}$, then $j-1\in\{n-1,\,n-k-2\}$, both congruent to $n-1\equiv t+2$, so the term is $\sigma(t+2)$. If $i=4k+4$, then $j-1=n-1-(4k+3)\equiv t+3$, because $4k+3\equiv-1\pmod{k+1}$, so the term is $\sigma(t+3)$.
\end{itemize}
 
\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 2: $R(T_1)\cup R(T_2)\subseteq S$.} Let $x,y\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}$ with $x+y\in\{T_1,T_2\}$. Take $i=k+2+x\in\{k+3,\ldots,2k+3\}$ and $j=n+1-i\ge n-2k-2\ge k+4$. Then $i\notin X$ and $\sigma(i-1)=x$. Also $j\notin\{1,k+2\}$ and $j-1\equiv n-1-x\equiv T_1-x\equiv y\pmod{k+1}$, so $\sigma(j-1)=y$. If $j\ne4k+4$, the pair $\{i,j\}$ gives $x\oplus y$. If $j=4k+4$, use instead $i'=i+(k+1)\in\{2k+4,\ldots,3k+4\}$ and $j'=j-(k+1)=3k+3$. Neither is in $X$, they have the same residues as $i$ and $j$, and $i'+j'=n+1$, so this pair also gives $x\oplus y$.
 
\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 3: witnesses.} Let $v\in\Phi(T)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:xor-realize}, $x=(T-v)/2$ is a witness, with partner $T-x=(T+v)/2\ge x$. Hence $v\in R(T)$ as soon as $(T-v)/2\ge1$ and $(T+v)/2\le k+1$. For $T=T_1$ and $v\le3$, the second condition always holds (as $t+v\le k+3\le2k$), and the first reads $v\le t$. For $T=T_2$, the first condition always holds, and the second reads $t\le k-1-v$.
 
\medskip
By Steps 1 and 2, $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})=\alpha(t)$ follows once we show that (a) $\alpha(t)\notin R(T_1)\cup R(T_2)\cup\{0,\sigma(t+2),\sigma(t+3)\}$, and (b) every $v<\alpha(t)$ lies in $S$.
 
\medskip\noindent\textbf{Case $0\le t\le k-2$.} Since $8b=k-4\equiv0\pmod8$ and $C[0],C[1],C[2]=1,1,3=B[0],B[1],B[2]$, we have $\alpha(t)=B[t\%8]$ throughout this case. Here $\sigma(t+2)=t+2$ and $\sigma(t+3)=t+3$; both exceed $3$ when $t\ge2$, and when $t\le1$ we have $\alpha(t)=B[t]=1\notin\{0,t+2,t+3\}$. Since $T_1\equiv t+2$ and $T_2\equiv t+7\pmod8$ are the residues of Table~\ref{tab:PhiT1T2} in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:block3}, we get $\alpha(t)\notin\Phi(T_1)\cup\Phi(T_2)\supseteq(R(T_1)\cup R(T_2))\cap\{0,1,2,3\}$, which proves (a), and every $v<\alpha(t)$ (so $v\le2$) lies in $\Phi(T_1)\cup\Phi(T_2)$. For (b), we apply Step~3. If $v\in\Phi(T_1)$, we need $v\le t$: this holds for $t\ge2$ because $v\le2$, and for $t\le1$ because then $\alpha(t)=1$ and only $v=0$ is needed. Otherwise $v\in\Phi(T_2)$, and we need $t\le k-1-v$: this holds for $t\le k-3$ because $v\le2$, and for $t=k-2$ (where $t\%8=2$ and $\Phi(T_2)=\{1\}$) because $v=1$.
 
\medskip\noindent\textbf{Case $t=k-1$} (so $\alpha(t)=C[3]=2$). Here $T_1=k+1\equiv5\pmod8$, so $\Phi(T_1)=\{1\}$, and $T_2=2k+2$; since $x+y=2k+2$ with $x,y\le k+1$ forces $x=y=k+1$, we get $R(T_2)=\{0\}$. The extra values are $\sigma(k+1)=k+1$ and $\sigma(k+2)=1$. Hence $2\notin S$, while $0\in R(T_2)$ and $1\in R(T_1)$ (as $1\le t$), so $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})=2$.
 
\medskip\noindent\textbf{Case $t=k$} (so $\alpha(t)=C[4]=3$). Here $T_1=k+2\equiv6\pmod8$, so $\Phi(T_1)=\{0\}$, and $T_2=2k+3>2k+2$, so $R(T_2)=\emptyset$. The extra values are $\sigma(k+2)=1$ and $\sigma(k+3)=2$. Hence $3\notin S$. Moreover, $0\in R(T_1)$ (as $0\le t$); the pair $\{1,n\}$ gives $g(n)=\sigma(n-1)=\sigma(k+2)=1$, since $n=4k+6+p(k+1)\notin X$; and the pair $\{4k+4,\,j\}$ with $j=n-4k-3=p(k+1)+3$ gives $\sigma(j-1)=2$, since $j\equiv3\pmod{k+1}$ implies $j\notin X$ (whose elements are $\equiv1,1,0$). Hence $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})=3$.

<!-- theorem-10: body_md -->
\label{thm:Pn}
Let $k=2^h-4$ with $h\ge3$. Then the nimber sequence $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})$ is periodic with period $k+1$, the transient has length $3k+5$, the only value of $n$ for which $\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n})=0$ with $n>k$ is $n=3k+4$, and the sequence has the following expression:
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{nim}_{\ell_k}(P_{n}):&\ \ \ \ \ \ [1,0]^{k/2}, 1, 2, 2, B^b, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, B^b, 1, 1, 3, 0, 2, [B^b, C]^*,
\end{align*}
where $b=(k-4)/8$, $B=11331122$ and $C=11323$.
