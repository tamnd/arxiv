Let $A$ be a finite skew brace. Then $A$ is left-simple if and only if exactly one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $A \simeq \mathrm{Triv}(C_p)$ for some prime $p$;
\item $A \simeq \mathrm{aTriv}(S)$ for a finite non-abelian simple group $S$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:TsangSetup}
Let $(A,\cdot,\circ)$ be a left-simple skew brace and suppose that $ (A,\cdot) = S^n,$
where $S$ is non-abelian simple and $n\ge2$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=1$;
\item $\rho$ is injective, and hence $|\operatorname{Im}(\rho)|=|A|=|S|^n=|\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|$;
\item $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)$;
\item If for
$\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(S^n)$ we denote by $\sigma_\alpha\in S_n$ the permutation
defined by $\alpha(S_i)=S_{\sigma_\alpha(i)}$ for every $i$,
then $\{\sigma_h:h\in\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\}
=
\{\sigma_k:k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\},$
and this common subgroup of $S_n$ acts transitively on
$\{1,\ldots,n\}$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
The argument is essentially contained in \cite[Section~3.2]{Tsang}. We recall it in some detail in order to make the proof of Theorem~A self-contained. We first prove that $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=1.$ Write $(A,\cdot)=S_1\times\cdots\times S_n$, where $S_i = S$ for every $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$. Suppose first that $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\le\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)$. Then every element of $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)$ is an inner automorphism of $(A,\cdot)$ and therefore preserves each direct factor $S_i$. Hence $\lambda_a(S_i)=S_i$ for every $a\in A$ and every $i$, so each $S_i$ is a left ideal of $A$. Since $n\ge2$, each $S_i$ is non-trivial and proper in $(A,\cdot)$, contradicting the left-simplicity of $A$. Therefore $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\not\le\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)$.
Now let $J_1=\operatorname{conj}^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)).$
Since \(J_1\) is a left ideal and \(A\) is left-simple, we have $J_1=1$ or $J_1=A$.
Assume that \(J_1=A\). Then $\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=\operatorname{conj}(A)\le\operatorname{Im}(\lambda).$
Moreover, since $(A,\cdot)=S^n$ is centreless, the conjugation map identifies $(A,\cdot)$ with $\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)$, and hence we have
$|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|\le |(A,\circ)|=|A|=|\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|,$
and therefore \(\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)\le\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\) forces \(\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)=\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)\), a contradiction.
Consequently $J_1=1.$ Thus $\operatorname{conj}^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot))
=
\operatorname{conj}^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}(\lambda))
=
J_1=1.$ Since \(\operatorname{conj}\colon A\to\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)\) is an isomorphism, we have $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=1.$ We next prove that \(\rho\) is injective. Recall that $J_2=\ker(\rho).$
Again \(J_2\) is a left ideal of \(A\), so left-simplicity gives $J_2=1$ or $J_2=A.$
If \(J_2=A\), then \(\rho\) is trivial and hence
$\operatorname{Im}(\rho)=1.$
Using the relation between the two canonical actions we would obtain, for every $a \in A$
$\lambda_a=\operatorname{conj}(a^{-1})$.
Thus $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)=\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot),$ a contradiction. Therefore $J_2=1,$
and hence $\rho$ is injective. In particular we have $|\operatorname{Im}(\rho)|=|A|=|\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|.$
For every $\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(S^n),$
there exists a unique permutation $\sigma_\alpha\in S_n$ such that $\alpha(S_i)=S_{\sigma_\alpha(i)}$ for every $i$. Consider the map
\[
\Phi:\operatorname{Aut}(S^n)\longrightarrow S_n,
\qquad
\alpha\longmapsto \sigma_\alpha.
\]
This is a group homomorphism and $\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)\le\ker\Phi.$
Applying $\Phi$ to the equality $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)$ and using $\Phi(\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot))=1$, we obtain $\Phi(\operatorname{Im}(\lambda))=\Phi(\operatorname{Im}(\rho)).$
Thus
\[
\{\sigma_h:h\in\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\}
=
\{\sigma_k:k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\}
\le S_n.
\]
We claim that this common subgroup acts transitively on $\{1,\ldots,n\}.$
Suppose otherwise. Then it has a non-empty proper orbit $\varnothing\neq\Omega\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}.$
Set $S_\Omega:=\prod_{i\in\Omega}S_i.$
Let $h\in\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)$, and let $\sigma_h$ be the permutation induced by $h$. We have
\[
h(S_\Omega)
=
h\left(\prod_{i\in\Omega}S_i\right)
=
\prod_{i\in\Omega}S_{\sigma_h(i)}
=
S_\Omega.
\]
This means that $\lambda_a(S_\Omega)=S_\Omega$ for every $a \in A$.
Thus $S_\Omega$ is a left ideal of $A$. Since $\Omega$ is non-empty and
proper we have $1<S_\Omega<(A,\cdot),$
contradicting the left-simplicity of $A$. Hence the common subgroup acts transitively on
$\{1,\ldots,n\}$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:out-bounds}
Let $S$ be a finite non-abelian simple group. Then $ q(S)<m(S)$ and $ q(S)^3<|S|.$
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For a non-abelian simple group, the least degree $P(S)$ of a faithful permutation representation equals $m(S)$. Holt and Tracey prove that $2P(S)>3|\operatorname{Out}(S)|$ for every finite non-abelian simple group \cite[Lemma~8]{HoltTracey}, and hence $q(S)<m(S)$. The estimate $q(S)^3<|S|$ is \cite[Lemma~4.8]{Fawcett}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:Scott}
Let $ N=S_1\times\cdots\times S_n,$
where the $S_i$ are isomorphic non-abelian simple groups. If $L\le N$ is subdirect, then there is a partition $ \{1,\ldots,n\}=\Omega_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_r$
such that $ L=D_1\times\cdots\times D_r,$
where each $D_j\cong S$ is a full diagonal subgroup of $\prod_{i\in\Omega_j}S_i$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
See \cite[Theorem~4.16]{PraegerSchneider}.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:vp-bound}
Let $p$ be a prime and let $n\ge1$. Then
\[
v_p(n!)<\frac{n}{p-1}.
\]
In particular, $v_p(n!)<n$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
By Legendre's formula \cite[Theorem~6.5.1]{AndreescuAndrica},
\[
v_p(n!)=\sum_{j\ge1}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^j}\right\rfloor.
\]
Choose $J$ such that $p^J>n$. Then
$\lfloor n/p^j\rfloor=0$ for every $j\ge J$, and hence
\[
v_p(n!)
=
\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^j}\right\rfloor
\le
\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\frac{n}{p^j}
<
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{p^j}
=
\frac{n}{p-1}.
\]
Thus $v_p(n!)<n/(p-1)$. In particular, $v_p(n!)<n$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:no-power}
Let $S$ be a finite non-abelian simple group and let $n\ge2$. There is no finite left-simple skew brace $(A,\cdot,\circ)$ such that $ (A,\cdot) = S^n.$
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Assume, for a contradiction, that such a skew brace $(A,\cdot,\circ)$ exists. Write $(A,\cdot)=S_1\times\cdots\times S_n$, where $S_i=S$ for every $i$. We will work with a particular subgroup inside $(A,\cdot)$. Let $\operatorname{conj}:(A,\cdot)\to\operatorname{Aut}(A,\cdot)$ be the conjugation map and set
\[
M:=\operatorname{conj}^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}(\rho)).
\]
Since $\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(A,\cdot)$ and $\operatorname{conj}$ is a homomorphism, $M$ is a subgroup of $(A,\cdot)$.
\medskip
\noindent\emph{Step 1: $k(M)=M$ for every $k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$}
\medskip 

 We claim that $M$ is invariant under every element of $\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$. Let $k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$ and $x\in M$. Since $\operatorname{conj}(x)\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$, we have $k\operatorname{conj}(x)k^{-1}\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$. Moreover $k\operatorname{conj}(x)k^{-1}=\operatorname{conj}(k(x))$, and hence $k(x)\in M$. Therefore $k(M)=M$ for every $k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$. For $1\le i\le n$, let $\pi_i:(A,\cdot)\to S_i$ be the $i$-th coordinate projection and put $M_i:=\pi_i(M)$.

\medskip 
\noindent\emph{Step 2: $|M_1|=\cdots=|M_n|$}
\medskip 

 By Lemma~\ref{lem:TsangSetup}, the subgroup $\{\sigma_k:k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\}\le S_n$ acts transitively on $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, where $\sigma_k$ is defined by $k(S_i)=S_{\sigma_k(i)}$. Let $i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$. By transitivity, there exists $k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$ such that $\sigma_k(i)=j$. Let $k_i:S_i\to S_j$ be the restriction of $k$ to $S_i$; this is an isomorphism. Since $k$ permutes the direct factors according to $\sigma_k$, we have $\pi_j(k(m))=k_i(\pi_i(m))$ for every $m\in(A,\cdot)$. Hence, since $k(M)=M$, if $x\in M_i=\pi_i(M)$ and $m\in M$ satisfies $\pi_i(m)=x$, then $k(m)\in M$ and $\pi_j(k(m))=k_i(x)$. Hence $k_i(M_i)\le M_j$. Applying the same argument to $k^{-1}$ gives $M_j\le k_i(M_i)$, and therefore $k_i(M_i)=M_j$. Thus $M_i\cong M_j$. Since $i$ and $j$ are arbitrary, it follows that $|M_1|=\cdots=|M_n|$.

\medskip 


\noindent\emph{Step 3: An arithmetic restriction on $[(A,\cdot):M]$.}

\medskip 


By Lemma~\ref{lem:TsangSetup},
\begin{align*}
|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)||\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|
&=|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|
&&\bigl(\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=1\bigr)\\
&=|\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|
&&\bigl(\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)\bigr)\\
&=\frac{|\operatorname{Im}(\rho)||\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|}{|\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|}
&&\bigl(\text{product formula}\bigr)\\
&=\frac{|\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|^2}{|\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|}
&&\bigl(|\operatorname{Im}(\rho)|=|\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|\bigr).
\end{align*}
Therefore
$|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|=[\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot):\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)]$.
Since $(A,\cdot)$ is centreless, the conjugation map
$\operatorname{conj}:(A,\cdot)\to\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)$ is an isomorphism. Thus we have $\operatorname{conj}(M)=\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot).$
In particular we get $[(A,\cdot):M]
=
[\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot):\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)].$
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:index-M}
[(A,\cdot):M]=|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|.
\end{equation}

Consider the natural quotient homomorphism $\operatorname{Aut}(A,\cdot)\to\operatorname{Aut}(A,\cdot)/\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)$. Its restriction to $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)$ has kernel $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\cap\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)=1$, so $\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)$ embeds into $\operatorname{Aut}(A,\cdot)/\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)$. Writing $q:=|\operatorname{Out}(S)|$, by \cite[Theorem~3.3.20]{Robinson} we have
\[
\operatorname{Aut}(A,\cdot)/\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)
\cong
\operatorname{Out}(S)^n\rtimes S_n.
\]
Consequently
\[
|\operatorname{Aut}(A,\cdot)/\operatorname{Inn}(A,\cdot)|=q^n n!.
\] Hence, by Lagrange's theorem and \eqref{eq:index-M},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:divisibility}
[(A,\cdot):M]=|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|\mid q^n n!.
\end{equation}
Since $|M_1|=\cdots=|M_n|$ and $|S_1|=\cdots=|S_n|=|S|$, either $M_i<S_i$ for every $i$ or $M_i=S_i$ for every $i$.


\medskip
\noindent\emph{Step 4: The case of proper coordinate projections.}
\medskip 


Suppose that $M_i<S_i$ for every $i$. Since all the $M_i$ have the same order, the indices $[S_i:M_i]$ are equal; put $d:=[S_i:M_i]$. Since $M\le M_1\times\cdots\times M_n$, we have
$d^n=[(A,\cdot):M_1\times\cdots\times M_n]\mid[(A,\cdot):M].$
Together with \eqref{eq:divisibility}, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:d-div}
d^n\mid q^n n!.
\end{equation}
We claim that $d\mid q$. Let $p$ be any prime and put $e:=v_p(d)$ and $b:=v_p(q)$. From \eqref{eq:d-div} we have $v_p(d^n)\le v_p(q^n n!).$ Since $v_p(d^n)=ne$ and $v_p(q^n n!)=nb+v_p(n!)$, we obtain $ne\le nb+v_p(n!)$, hence $n(e-b)\le v_p(n!)$. If $e>b$, then $e-b\ge1$, so $n\le n(e-b)\le v_p(n!)$, contradicting $v_p(n!)<n$. Thus $e\le b$ for every prime $p$, and therefore $d\mid q$. On the other hand, since $M_i<S_i\cong S$, we have $d\ge m(S)>q$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:out-bounds}, a contradiction.

\medskip 
\noindent\emph{Step 5: The case of surjective coordinate projections.}
\medskip 


Suppose that $M_i=S_i$ for every $i$. Since $M_i=\pi_i(M)$, every coordinate projection $\pi_i:M\to S_i$ is surjective. Thus $M$ is a subdirect subgroup of $(A,\cdot)=S_1\times\cdots\times S_n$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:Scott}, there exists a partition
$\{1,\ldots,n\}=\Omega_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_r$
and, for every $j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}$, a full diagonal subgroup
$D_j\le\prod_{i\in\Omega_j}S_i$ such that
$M=D_1\times\cdots\times D_r$. Since each $D_j$ projects isomorphically onto every factor $S_i$ with $i\in\Omega_j$, we have $D_j\cong S$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M-order}
|M|=|D_1|\cdots|D_r|=|S|^r.
\end{equation}
For each $j$, the set $\Omega_j$ is precisely the set of coordinates on which $D_j$ has non-trivial projection; we call it the support of $D_j$. Since $M=D_1\times\cdots\times D_r$ and every $D_j$ is non-abelian simple, the subgroups $D_1,\ldots,D_r$ are exactly the minimal normal subgroups of $M$. Let $k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)$. Since $k(M)=M$, the restriction $k|_M$ is an automorphism of $M$, and therefore permutes the minimal normal subgroups $D_1,\ldots,D_r$. Thus, for every $j$, there exists some $\ell$ such that $k(D_j)=D_\ell$. Let $\sigma_k$ be the permutation induced by $k$ on the factors $S_1,\ldots,S_n$, so that $k(S_i)=S_{\sigma_k(i)}$. If $i\in\Omega_j$, then $D_j$ has non-trivial projection onto $S_i$, and therefore $k(D_j)$ has non-trivial projection onto $S_{\sigma_k(i)}$. Hence the support of $k(D_j)$ is $\sigma_k(\Omega_j)$. Since $k(D_j)=D_\ell$, whose support is $\Omega_\ell$, we obtain
$\sigma_k(\Omega_j)=\Omega_\ell.$
Therefore $\{\sigma_k:k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\}$ permutes the sets $\Omega_1,\ldots,\Omega_r$.
We now show that all the $\Omega_j$ have the same cardinality. Let $\Omega_a$ and $\Omega_b$ be two of these sets, and choose $i\in\Omega_a$ and $j\in\Omega_b$. Since $\{\sigma_k:k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\}$ acts transitively on $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, there exists $\sigma\in\{\sigma_k:k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\}$ such that $\sigma(i)=j$. Since this group permutes the sets $\Omega_1,\ldots,\Omega_r$, the set $\sigma(\Omega_a)$ is one of the $\Omega_\ell$. Moreover, it contains $\sigma(i)=j$, and the sets $\Omega_1,\ldots,\Omega_r$ are pairwise disjoint. Hence $\sigma(\Omega_a)=\Omega_b$. Therefore $|\Omega_a|=|\Omega_b|$. Since $a$ and $b$ are arbitrary, all the supports have the same cardinality; write $|\Omega_j|=c$. Since they form a partition of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, we have $n=rc$.

Suppose that $c=1$. Then every $\Omega_j$ consists of a single index, so every full diagonal subgroup $D_j$ is simply one of the factors $S_i$. Hence $M=S_1\times\cdots\times S_n=(A,\cdot)$, and therefore $[(A,\cdot):M]=1$. By \eqref{eq:index-M}, $|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|=1$. Thus $\{\sigma_h:h\in\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)\}=1$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:TsangSetup} also $\{\sigma_k:k\in\operatorname{Im}(\rho)\}=1$, contradicting its transitivity on $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ because $n\ge2$. Therefore $c\ge2$, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r-bound}
r\le\frac n2.
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:M-order},
$[(A,\cdot):M]=|S|^{n-r}$.
Consequently \eqref{eq:divisibility} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S-div}
|S|^{n-r}\mid q^n n!.
\end{equation}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:out-bounds}, $q^3<|S|$. Hence there exists a prime $p\mid |S|$ such that, setting $a:=v_p(|S|)$ and $b:=v_p(q)$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a>3b}
a>3b.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $v_p(|S|)\le3v_p(q)$ for every prime $p\mid |S|$, then $v_p(|S|)\le v_p(q^3)$ for every prime $p$, so $|S|\mid q^3$, contradicting $q^3<|S|$. Fix such a prime $p$. From \eqref{eq:S-div} we have $v_p(|S|^{n-r})\le v_p(q^n n!)$. Since $v_p(|S|^{n-r})=(n-r)a$ and $v_p(q^n n!)=nb+v_p(n!)$, we obtain $(n-r)a\le nb+v_p(n!)$. By \eqref{eq:r-bound}, $n-r\ge n/2$, and therefore
\[
\frac{na}{2}\le(n-r)a\le nb+v_p(n!).
\]
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:valuation-final}
n\left(\frac a2-b\right)\le v_p(n!).
\end{equation}
Suppose first that $b\ge1$. Since $a>3b$ and $a,b$ are integers, $a\ge3b+1$, so
\[
\frac a2-b\ge\frac{3b+1}{2}-b=\frac{b+1}{2}\ge1.
\]
By \eqref{eq:valuation-final}, $n\le v_p(n!)$, contradicting $v_p(n!)<n$. Suppose now that $b=0$ and $p\ge3$. Since $p\mid|S|$, we have $a\ge1$, and \eqref{eq:valuation-final} gives $n/2\le v_p(n!)$. On the other hand,
\[
v_p(n!)<\frac{n}{p-1}\le\frac n2,
\]
a contradiction. Finally, suppose that $b=0$ and $p=2$. Since the prime $p$ was chosen to divide $|S|$, we have $2\mid|S|$. In fact, $4\mid|S|$. Indeed, if $v_2(|S|)=1$ and $P$ is a Sylow $2$-subgroup of $S$, then $|P|=2$. Since $\operatorname{Aut}(P)=1$, we have $N_S(P)=C_S(P)$. Burnside's normal $p$-complement theorem \cite{Robinson} then gives a normal $2$-complement in $S$, contradicting simplicity. Hence $a=v_2(|S|)\ge2$. Thus $a/2-b=a/2\ge1$, and \eqref{eq:valuation-final} gives
$n\le v_2(n!)<n,$
again a contradiction. Thus every case leads to a contradiction, proving the proposition.

<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Suppose first that $A$ is finite and left-simple. Every characteristic subgroup of $(A,\cdot)$ is a left ideal, so $(A,\cdot)$ is characteristically simple. Hence we can assume that $ (A,\cdot) = T^n$
for a finite simple group $T$ and an integer $n\ge1$. If $T$ is abelian, then $T=C_p$ for some prime $p$, and \cite[Corollary~1.3]{Tsang} gives $A\simeq\mathrm{Triv}(C_p)$ directly. Assume that $T$ is non-abelian. Proposition~\ref{prop:no-power} excludes $n\ge2$. Thus $n=1$, and \cite[Theorem~1.4(a)]{Tsang} shows that $A$ is almost trivial. Conversely, the trivial skew brace on $C_p$ is left-simple because $C_p$ has no non-trivial proper subgroup. If $S$ is non-abelian simple and $A$ is almost trivial, then
$ \lambda_a(x)=a^{-1}xa,$
so the left ideals are exactly the normal subgroups of $S$. Hence $A$ is left-simple.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:subbracefree}
Let $A$ be a non-trivial finite skew brace with no non-trivial proper sub-skew braces. Then $A$ is the trivial skew brace of prime order.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Every left ideal is a sub-skew brace, so $A$ is left-simple. By Theorem~A, either $A$ is trivial of prime order or it is almost trivial on a finite non-abelian simple group $S$. The second possibility is impossible because every finite non-abelian simple group has non-trivial proper subgroups, and in an almost trivial skew brace every subgroup is a sub-skew brace.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:HG}
Let $L/K$ be a finite Galois extension and let $\mathcal H$ be a Hopf--Galois structure on $L/K$. Then $\mathcal H$ is minimal if and only if one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\cong C_p$ for some prime $p$ and $\mathcal H$ is the classical structure;
\item $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is finite non-abelian simple and $\mathcal H$ is the canonical non-classical structure.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
By \cite[Theorem~4.3]{Tsang}, $\mathcal H$ is minimal if and only if its associated skew brace is left-simple. Theorem~A gives exactly the two possibilities in the statement, and the identifications with the classical and canonical non-classical structures follow from the correspondence recalled in \cite[Theorem~4.2]{Tsang}.
