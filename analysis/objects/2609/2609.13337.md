\label{thm:main-intro}
    The stringy mixed Hodge polynomial of $T^r/W$ is given by
    \[
        \mu^{\textup{str}}(T^r/W)(t,u,v)=\sum_{[w] \in \textup{Conj}(W)}
        \frac{(t^{2}uv)^{\frac{r}{2}\operatorname{rk} \operatorname{Im}(I_\Lambda -w)}}{|C(w)|}\left(\sum_{g \in C(w)} \, n_{w}(g)^r \det\left(I_{\Phi_w} + tuv\,\phi_w(g)\right)^r \right).
    \]
<!-- end theintro-1 -->
<!-- theintro-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:algorithm_Higgs-intro}
    The stringy mixed Hodge polynomial of the normalization $\hat{\mathcal{M}}_A(G) = \mathcal{M}_A(T) / W$ is given by
\[
        \mu^{\textup{str}} (\mathcal{M}_A(T) / W)(t,u,v) 
        =\sum_{[w] \in \textup{Conj}(W)}
        \frac{(t^{2}uv)^{d\operatorname{rk} \operatorname{Im}(I_\Lambda -w)}}{|C(w)|}\left(\sum_{g \in C(w)} \, n_{w}(g)^{2d}\, p_{g,w}(tu)^d\, p_{g,w}(tv)^d \right),
\]
with $p_{g,w}(x):=\det (I_{\Phi_w} + x\,\phi_w(g))$.

<!-- rem-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:mu-and-muc} When $X$ is irreducible of complex dimension $d$,
and its mixed Hodge structure satisfies Poincar\'e duality (eg.\ when $X$ is smooth
or $X=Y/F$, the quotient of a smooth irreducible variety $Y$ by a finite
group $F$) the compactly supported mixed Hodge polynomial can be obtained from the usual one as (see \cite[Remark 3.10]{FS})
\[
\mu_c(X)(t,u,v)=(t^{2}uv)^{d}\,\mu(X)(t^{-1},u^{-1},v^{-1})\; .
\]
<!-- end rem-1 -->
<!-- rem-2: body_md -->
(1) Note that the Euler characteristic $\chi(X)$ and the compactly supported 
one $\chi_c(X)$ agree on complex varieties. Indeed, they agree on smooth varieties, 
by Poincar\'e duality and the fact that the real dimension is even. Moreover, if the singular locus $X^{\textup{sing}}\subseteq X$ of $X$ is smooth (and again $X^{\textup{sing}}$ has even real dimension),
then the additivity of $\chi$ and of $\chi^c$ implies 
\[
\chi(X)=\chi(X^{\textup{sing}})+\chi(X\setminus X^{\textup{sing}})=
\chi^c(X^{\textup{sing}})+\chi^c(X\setminus X^{\textup{sing}})=\chi^c(X)\; .
\]
Finally, the same holds in general, by using the finite singular stratification.

(2) The $E$-polynomial can also be seen as a specialization of a more general
invariant, known as the virtual class. Let ${\rm KVar}$ be the Grothendieck
ring of algebraic varieties, generated by isomorphism classes $[X]$ of algebraic varieties
modulo cut-and-paste relations $[X]=[Y]+[X-Y]$, $Y\subseteq X$ being
a closed subvariety, and where the ring multiplication comes from
the cartesian product $[X\times Z]=[X]\cdot[Z]$. The elements in
this ring are called virtual classes of the varieties and the $E$-polynomial
factors through the Grothendieck ring as a ring homomorphism $E:{\rm KVar}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}[u,v]$.
<!-- end rem-2 -->
<!-- defn-1: body_md -->
\label{def:CR-cohomology}
The Chen--Ruan cohomology, also known as the orbifold cohomology, of $[X/F]$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CR}
H^\bullet_{\text{CR}}([X/F]; \mathbb{C}) := \bigoplus_{[g] \in \text{Conj}(F)} H^{\bullet}(X^g / C(g); \mathbb{C})[2 \, \operatorname{age}(g)]\; .
\end{equation}
Here, $\text{Conj}(F)$ is the set of conjugacy classes of $F$, $g \in [g]$ is any representative of the class, $X^g$ is the fixed locus of the action of $g\in F$ and $C(g)$ is the centralizer of $g$ in $F$, acting as the residual symmetry group on the fixed locus $X^g$. The shifting  $2\,\operatorname{age}(g)$ in the cohomology complex is the so-called \emph{fermionic shift} (cf.\ \cite[Definition 3.1.1]{CR}), and its definition is recalled in subsection \ref{subsec:age-shift}.
<!-- end defn-1 -->
<!-- rem-3: body_md -->
From a modern stack-theoretic perspective, the Chen--Ruan cohomology is equivalent to the ordinary singular cohomology of the \emph{inertia stack $\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})$} associated to the orbifold $\mathcal{X}$. For a global quotient stack $\mathcal{X} = [X/F]$, the inertia stack parameterizes pairs of points and group elements that fix them, decomposing topologically into the disjoint union of the twisted sectors $X^g / C(g)$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}([X/F]) \cong \coprod_{[g] \in \text{Conj}(F)} [X^g / C(g)]\; 
    \end{equation}
    Consequently, Chen--Ruan cohomology can be interpreted as the cohomology of the inertia stack, further equipped with a fundamentally modified grading defined by the fermionic shift.
<!-- end rem-3 -->
<!-- defn-2: body_md -->
\label{def:str_hodge_pol}
    Let $X$ be a smooth complex variety acted by a finite group $F$. The \emph{stringy mixed Hodge polynomial} of $[X/F]$ is 
$$
    \mu^{\textup{str}}(X/F)(t, u, v) = \sum_{[g] \in \text{Conj}(F)} \mu(X^g / C(g))\, (t^{2}uv)^{\operatorname{age}(g)}.
$$

$$
    \mu^{\textup{str}}(X/F)(t, q) = \sum_{[g] \in \text{Conj}(F)} \mu(X^g / C(g))\, (t^2q)^{\operatorname{age}(g)}.
$$

<!-- thm-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:crepant-resolution}
Suppose a finite group $\Gamma$ acts effectively
by symplectic automorphisms on a smooth symplectic variety $X$. For $g \in \Gamma$, let $X^g \subseteq X$ be the locus of fixed points of $g$, and consider the union
$$
    X_0 = \bigcup_{g \neq 1} X^g \subseteq X\; .
$$
If $X_0 \neq \emptyset$ and the quotient $X/\Gamma$ admits a symplectic resolution, then $X_0$ has codimension 2 in $X$.
<!-- end thm-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $\pi:Z\to X/\Gamma$ be a resolution of singularities of $X/\Gamma$, and consider $E\subseteq Z$ the exceptional locus of the resolution.
By definition $Z\setminus E$ is the largest open set where we have
a local isomorphism
\[
\pi|_{Z\setminus E}:Z\setminus E\to(X/\Gamma)\setminus\pi(E)\; .
\]
Since $X$ is smooth, the quotient $X/\Gamma$ is normal and $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial.
Hence, by van der Waerden's theorem (see \cite[Thm 21.12.12]{EGA}),
$E$ has pure codimension $1$ in $Z$.

Now, if $\pi$ is also symplectic, then it is a semismall morphism
(see \cite[Lemma 2.11]{kaledin2006symplectic}), that is, 
\[
2=2\,\text{codim}\,E\geq\text{codim}\,\pi(E)\; .
\]
Furthermore, since $X/\Gamma$ is normal, its singular locus $\pi(E)$
has codimension $\geq2$. This implies that $\textrm{codim}\,\pi(E)=2$.

Now, consider the projection
$p:X\to X/\Gamma$. Notice that any singular point of $X/\Gamma$
appears at a fixed point, which implies that $\pi(E)\subseteq p(X_{0})$,
and thus $\textrm{codim}\,p(X_{0})\leq\textrm{codim}\,\pi(E)=2$.
As $p$ is finite, we also have $\textrm{codim}\,X_{0}\leq2$. Finally,
since $\Gamma$ acts by symplectic automorphisms, each irreducible
component of $X^{g}$ is a symplectic subvariety, and thus it has
even codimension. This implies that the codimension of $X_0$ is $2$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->

<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:existence-crepant}
For any connected reductive group $G$ with positive semisimple rank, the symplectic variety $T^{2d}/W$ does not admit a symplectic resolution for $d \geq 2$.
<!-- end cor-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Notice that, since $G$ has positive semisimple rank, its Weyl group $W$ is non-trivial. 
Recall that $T = \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Lambda, \mathbb{C}^*)$, where $\Lambda$ is the character lattice of $G$, and the action of $W$ on $T$ is the one induced by $W$ acting on $\Lambda$, so in particular $\dim T = \operatorname{rk} \Lambda$. Furthermore, for any $w \in W$, its fixed locus is given by $T^w = \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\operatorname{coker}(I_\Lambda - w), \mathbb{C}^*)$, where $I_\Lambda$ is the identity map on $\Lambda$ (see Section \ref{section:stringy-CV}). In particular, for $w$ a simple reflection, we have that $I_{\Lambda} - w$ has rank $1$, and thus $\dim T^w = \operatorname{rk} \Lambda - 1$.

Applying this to $T^{2d} = \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Lambda^{\oplus 2d}, \mathbb{C}^*)$, we get that for a reflection $w$ we have $\dim (T^{2d})^w = 2d \operatorname{rk} \Lambda - 2d$, and therefore $\operatorname{codim} (T^{2d})^w = 2d$. Furthermore, since any $w \in W$ with $w \neq 1$ is a non-trivial product of reflections, $\operatorname{rk} \operatorname{Im}(I_\Lambda - w) \geq 1$, so $\dim (T^{2d})^w \leq 2d \operatorname{rk} \Lambda - 2d$ and thus $\operatorname{codim} (T^{2d})^w \geq 2d$. Therefore, we get that
$$
	\operatorname{codim} \bigcup_{w \neq 1} (T^{2d})^w = 2d\; .
$$
Hence, by Theorem \ref{thm:crepant-resolution}, $T^{2d}/W$ can only admit a symplectic resolution if $d=1$.

<!-- thm-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:resolution_ABC}
Let $G$ be a connected reductive group with irreducible root system, maximal torus $T$ and Weyl group $W$. If the symplectic variety $T^2/W$ admits a symplectic resolution, then $G$ has a root system of Dynkin type $A$, $B$ or $C$.
<!-- end thm-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Taking into account that the center component is acted trivially, without loss of generality we can suppose that $G$ is semisimple. By a result of Ginzburg and Kaledin \cite[Theorem 1.1]{GK04}, the quotient $\mathfrak{t}^2 /W$ admits a symplectic resolution only if $G$ has type $A$, $B$ or $C$, where $\mathfrak{t}$ is the Lie algebra of $T$ and $W$ acts on $\mathfrak{t}^2 = \mathfrak{t} \oplus \mathfrak{t}$ diagonally. We will thus prove that if $T^2/W$ admits a symplectic resolution, then $\mathfrak{t}^2/W$ also admits such a resolution, leading to the desired result.

In order to do that suppose that $Z \to T^2/W$ is a symplectic resolution. The exponential map of the abelian group $T^2$, $\exp: \mathfrak{t}^2 \to T^2$, is a $W$-equivariant analytic local isomorphism. In particular, it defines a $W$-equivariant isomorphism $\widehat{\exp}: \widehat{\mathfrak{t}^2_{0}} \to \widehat{T^2_{1}}$ between the formal completions $\widehat{\mathfrak{t}^2_{0}}$ of $\mathfrak{t}^2$ at $0 \in \mathfrak{t}^2$ and $\widehat{T^2_{1}}$ of $T^2$ at $1 \in T^2$. Since $0$ and $1$ are fixed points of the action of $W$, this isomorphism descends to an isomorphism
$$
\widehat{\exp}_W: \widehat{(\mathfrak{t}^2/W)}_{[0]} \to \widehat{(T^2/W)}_{[1]}
$$
between the corresponding formal completions of $\mathfrak{t}^2/W$ and $T^2/W$.

In particular, the restriction of $Z \to T^2/W$ to the formal completion gives a symplectic formal resolution
$$
    Z|_{\widehat{(T^2/W)}_{[1]}} \to \widehat{(T^2/W)}_{[1]}\; ,
$$
and its pullback $\widehat{\exp}_W^*(Z|_{\widehat{(T^2/W)}_{[1]}}) \to \widehat{(\mathfrak{t}^2/W)}_{[0]}$ is a symplectic resolution of $\widehat{(\mathfrak{t}^2/W)}_{[0]}$. By \cite[Theorem 1.4]{kaledin2003crepant}, this local symplectic resolution can be extended to a global symplectic resolution of $\mathfrak{t}^2/W$, as we wanted to prove.
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- rem-8: body_md -->
Under the additional assumption that $G$ is an almost simple group, Shu has recently obtained a complete classification of the connected components of the $G$-character variety of an elliptic curve that admit a symplectic resolution \cite[Theorem C]{shu2024singularities}. In particular, for the identity component $T^2/W$, his results show that the necessary restriction to Dynkin types $A$, $B$, and $C$ established in Theorem \ref{thm:resolution_ABC} can be sharpened considerably: existence of a symplectic resolution depends also on the isogeny type of $G$ (\cite[Propositions 7.9, 7.11, and 7.13]{shu2024singularities}). Shu's analysis of the genus one case uses Borel--Friedman--Morgan theory to describe the connected components and the corresponding Weyl group actions. By contrast, our argument relies on the Ginzburg--Kaledin obstruction and yields a uniform necessary condition for every reductive group, without any almost-simplicity assumption.

<!-- prop-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:Burnside}
Let $X$ be a complex algebraic variety and let $F$ be a finite group acting algebraically on $X$. Then, the mixed Hodge polynomial of $X/F$ is
$$
    \mu(X/F) = \frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{g \in F}\sum_{k,p,q} \chi_{k,p,q}(g) \,t^ku^pv^q\; ,
$$
where $\chi_{k,p,q}(g) =\operatorname{tr}(g: H^{k,p,q}(X) \to H^{k,p,q}(X))$ is the character of the action of $g \in F$ on $H^{k,p,q}(X)$, i.e.\ the trace of the $\mathbb{C}$-linear map induced in cohomology by $g$.
<!-- end prop-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
In every degree, the cohomology of $X/F$ is the $F$-fixed part $H^k(X)^F = H^k(X/F)$.
In particular, since $F$ preserves the bigrading of the mixed Hodge structure, its $(p,q)$-pieces are
$$
    H^{k,p,q}(X/F) = H^{k,p,q}(X)^F.
$$
Recall that if $H$ is any $F$-module, then
$$
    \dim H^F = \frac{1}{|F|} \sum_{g \in F} \chi_H(g)\; ,
$$
where $\chi_H$ is the character associated to $H$. This follows directly from decomposing $H$ into its irreducible components $H = H_0^{\oplus d_0} \oplus \ldots \oplus H_n^{\oplus d_n}$, with $H_0$ being the trivial representation. Now, considering the irreducible characters $\chi_0, \ldots, \chi_n$ of $H_0, \ldots, H_n$, respectively, and using the orthogonality of characters we get
$$
    \dim H^F = d_0 = \left\langle \sum_i d_i \chi_i, \chi_0 \right\rangle = \left\langle \chi_H, \chi_0 \right\rangle = \frac{1}{|F|} \sum_{g\in F} \chi_H(g)\overline{\chi}_0(g) = \frac{1}{|F|} \sum_{g\in F} \chi_H(g)\; .
$$

Therefore, applying this to $H = H^{k,p,q}(X)$, we get
$$
    \mu(X/F) = \sum_{k,p,q} \dim H^{k,p,q}(X)^F\,t^ku^pv^q = \frac{1}{|F|} \sum_{k,p,q} \sum_{g \in F} \chi_{k,p,q}(g)\,\,t^ku^pv^q\; ,
$$
as we wanted to prove.

<!-- lem-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:fermionic-shift}
Let a finite group $F$ act symplectically and diagonally on the $r$-fold cartesian product $X^{r}$ for some $r \geq 1$ (we do not require the action to be symplectic on $X$). Then, the fermionic shift of $g \in F$ on $X^{r}$ satisfies 
$$\operatorname{age}_{X^{r}}(g) = \frac r2\operatorname{codim}_\mathbb{C} X^g.$$
<!-- end lem-2 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
If $F$ acts symplectically on a complex symplectic manifold $Y$, the linearization of the action of $g \in F$ on its fixed point locus is a symplectic matrix. Hence, if $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ is an eigenvalue of the linearization of $g$, then so is $\lambda^{-1}$. In particular, if 
$\lambda = e^{2\pi i q} \neq 1$, then $0 < q < 1$ thus $\lambda^{-1} = e^{-2 \pi i q} = e^{2\pi i (1-q)}$, where now $0 < 1-q < 1$; so this pair of eigenvalues contributes to the fermionic shift by $q + (1-q) = 1$. However, this symmetry does not happen when $\lambda = 1$ since in this case the phase of both $\lambda$ and $\lambda^{-1}$ is $q = 0$, so this pair does not contribute to the shift. The number of eigenvalues $\lambda = 1$ is equal to the dimension of the fixed locus $Y^g$, so we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:age}
    \operatorname{age}_Y(g) = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{codim}_\mathbb{C} Y^g.
\end{equation}

Finally, if $Y=X^r$ and the action is diagonal, we have that the eigenvalues of the action of $g$ on $Y$ are the same as those of the action on $X$, but repeated $r$ times. Therefore, we get $\operatorname{age}_Y(g) = r\, \operatorname{age}_X(g)$, finishing the proof.
<!-- end proof-6 -->

The term $\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, \mathbb{C}^*)$ can actually be described in very simple terms. In fact, if $D_w = \mathbb{Z}/(k_1\mathbb{Z}) \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/(k_l\mathbb{Z})$, with $k_1, \ldots, k_l$ the invariant factors of $D_w$, then $\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, \mathbb{C}^*) = \mu_{k_1} \times \cdots \times \mu_{k_l}$, where $\mu_n \subseteq \mathbb{C}^*$ is the set of $n$-th roots of unity. However, we will not need this description in the following.

<!-- thm-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:algorithm_CV}
    The stringy mixed Hodge polynomial of $T^r/W$ under the diagonal action of $W$ is:
    \[
        \mu^{\textup{str}}(T^r/W)(t,u,v)=\sum_{[w] \in \textup{Conj}(W)}
        \frac{(t^{2}uv)^{r\operatorname{age}(w)}}{|C(w)|}\left(\sum_{g \in C(w)} \, n_{w}(g)^r \det\left(I_{\Phi_w} + tuv\,\phi_w(g)\right)^r \right)
        ,
    \]
    where $\operatorname{age}(w) = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{rk} \operatorname{Im}(I_\Lambda -w)$.
<!-- end thm-3 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
By Definition \ref{def:str_hodge_pol} of the stringy mixed Hodge polynomial, we have
$$
    \mu^{\textup{str}}(T^r/W) = \sum_{[w] \in \text{Conj}(W)} \mu(T^r(\mathbb{C})^w / C(w))\, (t^{2}uv)^{\operatorname{age}_{T^r}(w)},
$$
where $\operatorname{age}_{T^r}(w) = r \operatorname{age}(w)$ is the fermionic shift of $w$ on $T^r$ by Lemma \ref{lem:fermionic-shift}, being $\operatorname{age}(w)$ the shift on $T$. For this shift, recall that the action of $W$ on $T^2$ is symplectic (cf.\ subsection \ref{ssec:symplectic_resolutions}). Therefore, by (\ref{eq:age}) and the previous discussion, for the action of $w \in W$ on $T$ we have $\operatorname{age}(w) = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{codim} T(\mathbb{C})^w = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{rk} A_w$. Thus, for the action of $W$ on $T^r$ we have $\operatorname{age}_{T^r}(w) = r \operatorname{age}(w) = \frac{r}{2}\operatorname{rk} A_w$.

Therefore, using the Burnside formula from Proposition \ref{prop:Burnside}, the fact that $T^r(\mathbb{C})^w = (T(\mathbb{C})^w)^r$ and observation (\ref{item:product}) from Section \ref{sec:mhs-quotients}, it is enough to prove that, for any $w \in W$ and $g \in C(w)$, we have
$$
    \sum_{k,p,q} \chi_{H^{k,p,q}(T(\mathbb{C})^w)}(g) \,t^ku^pv^q = n_{w}(g) \det\left(I_{\Phi_w} + tuv\,\phi_w(g)\right)\; .
$$

For this purpose, recall that $T(\mathbb{C})^w$ is isomorphic to the disjoint union of $|\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, \mathbb{C}^*)|$ copies of the torus $(\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}$, with $s_w= \operatorname{rk} K_w$. Therefore, by observation (\ref{item:disjoint-union}) of Section \ref{sec:mhs-quotients}, we have that
$$
\chi_{H^{k,p,q}(T(\mathbb{C})^w)}(g) = \sum_{\rho \in \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, \mathbb{C}^*)^g} \operatorname{tr}\left(g_{\rho}: H^{k,p,q}(\{\rho\} \times (\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}) \to H^{k,p,q}(\{\rho\} \times (\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w})\right),
$$
where the sum runs over those $\rho \in \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, \mathbb{C}^*)$ such that $g \cdot \rho = \rho$, the torus $\{\rho\} \times (\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}_\rho$ is the copy of $(\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}$ corresponding to $\rho$, and
$g_{\rho}: H^{k,p,q}(\{\rho\} \times (\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w})\to H^{k,p,q}(\{\rho\} \times (\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w})$ is the associated automorphism on the cohomology of the torus induced by $\phi_w(g): \Phi_w \to \Phi_w$ using that $(\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w} = \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Phi_w, \mathbb{C}^*)$.

The number of homomorphisms $\rho \in \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, \mathbb{C}^*)$ with $g \cdot \rho = \rho$ can be easily calculated as above, since it corresponds to those $\rho: D_w \to \mathbb{C}^*$ with $\rho(\operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g))) = 1$. Hence, $\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, \mathbb{C}^*)^g = \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w/ \operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g)), \mathbb{C}^*)$ and thus its cardinality is $|D_w/ \operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g))| = n_w(g)$. Now, observe that the maps $g_\rho: \{\rho\} \times (\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w} \to \{\rho\} \times (\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}$ for different $\rho \in \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, \mathbb{C}^*)^g$ are all homotopic, since they differ by a translation in the torus. Therefore, we get that
\begin{align*}
\sum_{k,p,q} \chi_{H^{k,p,q}(T(\mathbb{C})^w)}(g) \,t^ku^pv^q & = \sum_{k,p,q}  n_w(g) \operatorname{tr}\left(g: H^{k,p,q}((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}) \to H^{k,p,q}((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w})\right) \,t^ku^pv^q \\
& = n_w(g) \sum_{k,p,q} \chi_{H^{k,p,q}((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w})}(g)\,t^ku^pv^q\; .
\end{align*}

Now, observe that the cohomology of $(\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}$ is an exterior algebra generated by the degree one elements, i.e.\ $H^\bullet((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}) = \Lambda^\bullet H^1((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}) = \Lambda^\bullet(\mathbb{C}(-1)^{\oplus s_w})$, with $\mathbb{C}(-1)$ the Tate twist of weight $2$. The mixed Hodge structure on the basic piece is $(\mathbb{C}(-1)^{\oplus s_w})^{1,1} = \mathbb{C}^{s_w}$ and $(\mathbb{C}(-1)^{\oplus s_w})^{p,q} = 0$ otherwise, so by observation (\ref{item:determinant}) we have
$$
\sum_{k,p,q} \chi_{H^{k,p,q}((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w})}(g)\,t^ku^pv^q = \det(I_{\mathbb{C}^{s_w}} + tuv\,g)\; ,
$$
where $g$ is acting on the cohomology $H^{1}((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w})=H^{1,1,1}((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}) = \mathbb{C}^{s_w}$. The proof is thus completed by noticing that $H^{1}((\mathbb{C}^*)^{s_w}) = \Phi_w \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{C}$ and that the action of $\phi_w(g)$ preserves the lattice, so \linebreak $\det(I_{\mathbb{C}^{s_w}} + tuv\,g) = \det(I_{\Phi_w} + tuv\,\phi_w(g))$, with $\phi_w$ acting on $\Phi_w$.

<!-- thm-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:sector-Higgs}
    Let $A$ be an abelian variety of dimension $d$. For every $w\in W$, \linebreak $\operatorname{age}(w)=d \operatorname{rk} \operatorname{Im}(I_\Lambda - w)$, and the mixed Hodge polynomial of its corresponding twisted sector is:
$$
    \mu(\mathcal{M}_A(T)^w / C(w)) = \frac{1}{|C(w)|} \sum_{g \in C(w)}
   n_w(g)^{2d} \det(I_{\Phi_w} + tu\,\phi_w(g))^d\det(I_{\Phi_w} + tv\,\phi_w(g))^d.
$$
<!-- end thm-4 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The action of $W$ on $\mathcal{M}_A(T) = \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Lambda, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A))$ is induced by its natural action on $\Lambda$. This implies that the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:algorithm_CV} can be repeated verbatim in this setting to show that
$$
    \mu(\mathcal{M}_A(T)^w / C(w)) = \frac{1}{|C(w)|} \sum_{g \in C(w)}
    \sum_{k,p,q} \chi_{H^{k,p,q}(\mathcal{M}_A(T)^w)}(g) \,t^ku^pv^q\; .
$$
Arguing as for character varieties in subsection \ref{section:stringy-CV} we have that
\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{M}_A(T)^w = \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(K_w, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A)) = \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, A^\vee) \times \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Phi_w, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A))\; ,
\end{align*}
where we have used that $\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w, H^0(A, \Omega^1_A)) = 0$ since $D_w$ is a finite group and $H^0(A, \Omega^1_A)$ is a complex vector space (and thus is torsion free). 

The fixed components are now parametrized by $\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w/\operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g)), A^\vee)$. If we write $D_w/\operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g)) \cong \prod_i \mathbb{Z}_{n_i}$, then we have $\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w/\operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g)), A^\vee) = \prod_i A^\vee[n_i]$, where $A^\vee[n_i]$ denotes the elements of $n_i$-torsion in $A^\vee$. Using that $|A^\vee[n_i]| = n_i^{2d}$, we get that the number of fixed components is
$$
    |\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(D_w/\operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g)), A^\vee)| = |D_w/\operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g))|^{2d} = \prod_i n_i^{2d} = n_w(g)^{2d},
$$
where recall that, by definition, $n_w(g) = |D_w/\operatorname{Im}(I_{D_w} - \tau_w(g))|$. Furthermore, all these components are isomorphic to $\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Phi_w, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A))$, and the induced maps are isomorphic. Therefore, for any $g \in C(w)$, we have 
$$
 \sum_{k,p,q} \chi_{H^{k,p,q}(\mathcal{M}_A(T)^w)}(g) \,t^ku^pv^q = n_w(g)^{2d} \sum_{k,p,q} \chi_{H^{k,p,q}(\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Phi_w, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A)))}(g)\,t^ku^pv^q\; ,
$$
where $g: H^{k,p,q}(\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Phi_w, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A))) \to H^{k,p,q}(\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Phi_w, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A)))$ is the induced map in cohomology by the free part $\phi_w(g): \Phi_w \to \Phi_w$ of $g$. Since $H^0(A, \Omega^1_A)$ is contractible, we have that $\operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Phi_w, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A))$ has the same homotopy type as the space $Z_w := \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\Phi_w, A^\vee)$. Notice that $Z_w \cong (A^\vee)^{s_w}$, with $s_w = \operatorname{rk} \Phi_w$.  

The cohomology of $A^\vee$ is an exterior algebra generated by the degree one elements, so $H^\bullet(A^\vee) = \bigwedge^\bullet H^1(A^\vee)$. Now, observe that $A^\vee$ is a smooth projective variety, so $H^1(A^\vee)$ is a pure Hodge structure of weight $1$ given by $H^1(A^\vee) = H^{1,0}(A^\vee) \oplus H^{0,1}(A^\vee)$ with $H^{1,0}(A^\vee) = H^{0,1}(A^\vee) = \mathbb{C}^d$. In this way, the cohomology of $Z_w$ is also an exterior algebra of the form
    $$
H^\bullet(Z_w) = \bigwedge^\bullet H^1(Z_w), \textrm{ with } H^1(Z_w) = \Phi_w^\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{C} H^1(A^\vee)\; , 
$$
where $\Phi_w^\mathbb{C} = \Phi_w \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{C}$ is the complexification of the lattice $\Phi_w$. Again, the Hodge structure on $H^1(Z_w)$ is pure of weight $1$, with
$$
    H^{1,0}(Z_w) = \Phi_w^\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{C} H^{1,0}(A^\vee) = \Phi_w^\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{C} \mathbb{C}^d\quad , \quad H^{0,1}(Z_w) = \Phi_w^\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{C} H^{0,1}(A^\vee) = \Phi_w^\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{C} \mathbb{C}^d\; .
$$

From this cohomological calculation, we thus get
$$
    \sum_{k, p, q} \chi_{H^{k,p,q}(Z_w)}(g)\,t^ku^p v^q = \det(I_{H^{1,0}(Z_w)} + tu\,g^{1,0})\det(I_{H^{0,1}(Z_w)} + tv\,g^{0,1})\; ,
$$
where we are considering the maps in each piece of the Hodge structure $g^{1,0}: H^{1,0}(Z_w) \to H^{1,0}(Z_w)$ and $g^{0,1}: H^{0,1}(Z_w) \to H^{0,1}(Z_w)$.

Actually, using that $H^{1,0}(Z_w) = H^{0,1}(Z_w) = \Phi_w^\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{C} \mathbb{C}^d$ and that $g$ only acts on the lattice part, we observe that $g^{1,0} = g^{0,1} = \phi_w(g)_\mathbb{C} \otimes I_{\mathbb{C}^d}$, where $\phi_w(g)_\mathbb{C}: \Phi_w^\mathbb{C} \to \Phi_w^\mathbb{C} $ is the complexification of the free part $\phi_w(g): \Phi_w \to \Phi_w$.
This implies that $\det(I_{H^{1,0}(Z_w)} + tu\,g^{1,0}) = \det(I_{\Phi_w^\mathbb{C}} + tu\,\phi_w(g)_\mathbb{C})^d = \det(I_{\Phi_w} + tu\,\phi_w(g))^d$, and analogously $\det(I_{H^{0,1}(Z_w)} + tv\,g^{0,1}) = \det(I_{\Phi_w} + tv\,\phi_w(g))^d$. Hence, we have
$$
    \mu(\mathcal{M}_A(T)^w / C(w)) = \frac{1}{|C(w)|} \sum_{g \in C(w)}
   n_w(g)^{2d} \det(I_{\Phi_w} + tu\,\phi_w(g))^d\det(I_{\Phi_w} + tv\,\phi_w(g))^d\; .
$$



Finally, with respect to the fermionic shift of $w \in W$ when acting on $\mathcal{M}_A(T)$, recall that
\begin{align*}
    \operatorname{age}(w) &= \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{codim}_\mathbb{C} \mathcal{M}_A(T)^w = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{codim}_\mathbb{C} \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(K_w, A^\vee \times H^0(A, \Omega^1_A)) \\
    &= \frac{2d\operatorname{rk} \Lambda - 2d \operatorname{rk} K_w}{2} = d\operatorname{rk} A_w\; ,
\end{align*}
where recall that $A_w = \operatorname{Im}(I_\Lambda - w)$. This completes the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:sector-Higgs} and thus also of Theorem \ref{thm:algorithm_Higgs-intro}.

\begin{align*}

2m \\ 2i

<!-- prop-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:palindromic}
For any connected reductive group $G$ with maximal torus $T$ and Weyl group $W$, the stringy $E$-polynomial of $T^{2d}/W$ is palindromic in the variable $q=uv$ for all $d \geq 1$.
<!-- end prop-2 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
By Corollary \ref{cor:compact-support}, and writing the stringy $E$-polynomial of $T^{2d}/W$ as a polynomial in the variable $q := uv$, we have
    \[
        E^{\textup{str}}(T^{2d}/W)(q)=\sum_{[w] \in \textup{Conj}(W)}
        \frac{1}{|C(w)|}\left(\sum_{g \in C(w)} \, q^{2d\operatorname{age}(w)}n_{w}(g)^{2d} \det\left(\phi_w(g)-q\,I_{\Phi_w}\right)^{2d} \right),
    \]
where $2\operatorname{age}(w) = \operatorname{rk}(I_\Lambda - w)$.

Now, observe that a polynomial $P(q)$ of degree $n$ is palindromic if and only if $q^nP(q^{-1})= P(q)$. In the case of the stringy $E$-polynomial, its degree is $\dim T^{2d} = 2dr$, with $r = \dim T = \operatorname{rk} G=\operatorname{rk} \Lambda$. Therefore, if we show that such symmetry holds for each term of the form
$$
P_w(q) := \sum_{g \in C(w)} \, q^{2d\operatorname{age}(w)}n_{w}(g)^{2d} \det\left(\phi_w(g)-q\,I_{\Phi_w}\right)^{2d},
$$
for every $w \in W$, the result follows.

Indeed, recalling $s_w = \operatorname{rk} \operatorname{coker}(I_{\Lambda} - w) = \operatorname{rk} \Phi_w$, we have $r = \operatorname{rk} \operatorname{coker}(I_{\Lambda} - w) + \operatorname{rk} \operatorname{Im}(I_{\Lambda} - w) = s_w + 2\operatorname{age}(w)$ or, equivalently, $2(r - \operatorname{age}(w)) = 2(\operatorname{age}(w) + s_w)$. Therefore, we have
\begin{align}
\begin{split}\label{eq:palindromic-term}
q^{2dr}P_w(q^{-1}) & = \sum_{g \in C(w)} \, q^{2dr-2d\operatorname{age}(w)}n_{w}(g)^{2d} \det\left(\phi_w(g)-q^{-1}\,I_{\Phi_w}\right)^{2d} \\
& = \sum_{g \in C(w)} \, q^{2d\operatorname{age}(w)}n_{w}(g)^{2d} \left(q^{s_w}\det\left(\phi_w(g)-q^{-1}\,I_{\Phi_w}\right)\right)^{2d}.
\end{split}
\end{align}
Now, since $s_w$ is exactly the dimension of the space on which the determinant is taken, we have
\begin{align*}
q^{s_w}\det\left(\phi_w(g)-q^{-1}\,I_{\Phi_w}\right) & = \det\left(q\phi_w(g)-I_{\Phi_w}\right) = \det\left(\phi_w(g)(qI_{\Phi_w}-\phi_w(g)^{-1})\right) \\
& = \det(\phi_w(g)) \det\left(qI_{\Phi_w}-\phi_w(g)^{-1}\right) = \pm \det\left(\phi_w(g^{-1}) - qI_{\Phi_w}\right),
\end{align*}
where in the last equality we have used that $\det(\phi_w(g)) = \pm1$ (since $g$ is an automorphism), and that $\phi_w(g)^{-1} = \phi_w(g^{-1})$. Additionally, recall that $n_w(g)$ can be understood as the number of fixed points of $g$ when acting on the torsion part of $\operatorname{coker}(I_\Lambda - w)$, and this number agrees with the number of fixed points of $g^{-1}$ on the same torsion part, so $n_w(g)= n_w(g^{-1})$.

Thus, returning to (\ref{eq:palindromic-term}), we get
\begin{align*}
q^{2dr}P_w(q^{-1}) & = \sum_{g \in C(w)} \, q^{2d\operatorname{age}(w)}n_{w}(g)^{2d} \left(q^{s_w}\det\left(\phi_w(g)-q^{-1}\,I_{\Phi_w}\right)\right)^{2d} \\
& = \sum_{g \in C(w)} \, q^{2d\operatorname{age}(w)}n_{w}(g^{-1})^{2d} \left(\det\left(\phi_w(g^{-1}) - qI_{\Phi_w}\right)\right)^{2d} = P_w(q)\; ,
\end{align*}
as we wanted to show.

<!-- lem-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:pairing-well-defined}
The pairing $\langle-, -\rangle: D \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \hat{D} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ described above is well-defined and perfect.
<!-- end lem-4 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
To prove that the pairing is well-defined, we must show that $\langle -, [\alpha]\rangle = 0$ for any $\alpha \in \operatorname{Im}(\iota^\vee)$, as well as $\langle [\lambda], -\rangle = 0$ for any $\lambda \in \operatorname{Im}(f)$. We will show the former condition, the latter being analogous. Indeed, if $\alpha \in \operatorname{Im}(\iota^\vee) \subseteq \operatorname{Im}(f)^\vee$, then $\alpha$ is defined on the whole $\Lambda$ and thus $\alpha(n\lambda)=n\alpha(\lambda)$, so $\alpha(n\lambda)/n$ is an integer and therefore it vanishes in $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$. Using this description, in a normal basis we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairing-perfect}
    \left\langle [\lambda], [\alpha]\right\rangle = \sum_i \frac{\lambda_i \alpha_i}{n_i} \mod \mathbb{Z}\; ,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_i$ and $\alpha_i$ are the coordinates of $[\lambda]$ and $[\alpha]$ in the normal basis respectively, and $D \cong \hat{D} \cong \prod_i \mathbb{Z}_{n_i}$ is the normal factor decomposition. From (\ref{eq:pairing-perfect}), we directly see that the pairing is perfect.
<!-- end proof-15 -->
<!-- rem-18: body_md -->
In a similar spirit to the proof of Lemma \ref{lem:pairing-well-defined}, it can be proven that $\langle[\lambda],[\alpha]\rangle$ does not depend on the choice of the integer $n \neq 0$ such that $n\lambda \in \operatorname{Im}{f}$. In fact, given $[\lambda] \in D$ of order $n$, let $m \in \mathbb{N}$ be another nonzero integer with $m\lambda \in \operatorname{Im}(f)$. Then $m = ns$ for some integer $s$, and thus $\alpha(m\lambda)/m=\alpha(ns\lambda)/ns = \alpha(n\lambda)/n$.

<!-- prop-3: body_md -->
The morphisms $\tau(h): D \to D$ and $\hat{\tau}(h^\vee): \hat{D} \to \hat{D}$ are adjoint with respect to the pairing $\langle-, -\rangle: D \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \hat{D} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, that is
    $$
        \langle \tau(h)[\lambda], [\alpha]\rangle = \langle [\lambda], \hat{\tau}(h^\vee)[\alpha]\rangle\; ,
    $$
    for all $[\lambda] \in D$ and $[\alpha] \in \hat{D}$.
<!-- end prop-3 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ so that $n\lambda \in \operatorname{Im}(f)$, say $n\lambda = f(\eta)$. Observe that, in that case, we also have $nh(\lambda) = h(n\lambda) = h(f(\eta)) = f(h(\eta))$, so $nh(\lambda)$ also belongs to $\operatorname{Im}(f)$. In this manner, we have
$$
\langle [\lambda], h^\vee[\alpha]\rangle = \frac{h^\vee\alpha(n\lambda)}{n} = \frac{\alpha(h(n\lambda))}{n} = \frac{\alpha(nh(\lambda))}{n} = \langle h[\lambda], [\alpha]\rangle\; ,
$$
as we wanted to prove.

<!-- cor-6: body_md -->
\label{cor:number-points}
    The number of points of $\operatorname{coker}(\tau(h)) = D/\operatorname{Im} \tau(h)$ and $\operatorname{coker}(\hat{\tau}(h^\vee)) = \hat{D}/\operatorname{Im} \hat{\tau}(h^\vee)$ agree.
<!-- end cor-6 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
Since $\tau(h)$ and $\hat{\tau}(h^\vee)$ are adjoint, the pairing between $D$ and $\hat{D}$ induces an isomorphism between $\ker(\tau(h))$ and the Pontryagin dual $ \operatorname{coker}(\hat{\tau}(h^\vee))^P := \operatorname{Hom}_{\textup{Ab}}(\operatorname{coker}(\hat{\tau}(h^\vee)), \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})$. Indeed, if $[\lambda] \in \ker(\tau(h))$, then $\langle [\lambda], -\rangle: \hat{D} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is a functional vanishing on $\operatorname{Im}(\hat{\tau}(h^\vee))$, so it descends to a morphism $\operatorname{coker}(\hat{\tau}(h^\vee))=\hat{D}/\operatorname{Im}{\hat{\tau}(h^\vee)} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$. Reciprocally, since the pairing is perfect, any $\varphi:  \hat{D} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is of the form $\varphi = \langle [\lambda], -\rangle$ for some $[\lambda] \in D$, and if $\varphi$ vanishes on $\operatorname{Im}(\hat{\tau}(h^\vee))$, then $[\lambda] \in \ker(\tau(h))$ since $\langle \tau(h)[\lambda], -\rangle = \langle [\lambda], \hat{\tau}(h^\vee)-\rangle = \varphi \circ \hat{\tau}(h^\vee) = 0$.

    Therefore, using that on any finite abelian group we have $|\operatorname{coker}(\hat{\tau}(h^\vee))^P|=|\operatorname{coker}(\hat{\tau}(h^\vee))|$ and $|\ker(\tau(h))| = |\operatorname{coker}(\tau(h))|$, the result follows.
