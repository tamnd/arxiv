\label{thm:main}
Let $q\ge2$. The attainable eigenvalues in the upper half-plane are
\begin{equation}
 \Sigma_q\cap\{\operatorname{Im}\lambda\ge0\}
 =[-1,1]\cup\left\{\rho e^{2\pi ix}:\frac1{2q}\le x<\frac12,\quad0<\rho\le r_q(x)\right\}.\label{eq:region}
\end{equation}
Thus every real number in $[-1,1]$ is attainable. At each angle $2\pi x$
with $1/(2q)\le x<1/2$, every radius from $0$ to $r_q(x)$ is attainable;
there are no nonreal eigenvalues with angle strictly between $0$ and
$\pi/q$. The eigenvalues in the lower half-plane are the complex
conjugates of those in the upper half-plane.

On every interval between consecutive fractions in $\mathcal F_q$, the function
$r_q$ is real-analytic and is determined by \eqref{eq:scalar}, using the
data in \eqref{eq:base-data} or \eqref{eq:lateral-data}, respectively.
Its limit is $1$ at $1/(2q)$ and at every fraction $f\in\mathcal F_q$ with
$1/(2q)<f<1/2$. As $x\uparrow1/2$, its limit is $1$ for even $q$
and $\rho_q$ for odd $q$.

Define the curved part of the upper boundary by
$C_q^+=\{r_q(x)e^{2\pi ix}:1/(2q)<x<1/2\}$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \partial\Sigma_q\cap\{\operatorname{Im}\lambda\ge0\}
 =[0,1]\cup[0,e^{i\pi/q}]\cup C_q^+\cup E_q,\label{eq:boundary}
\end{equation}
where $[0,e^{i\pi/q}]$ denotes the straight line segment from the origin
to $e^{i\pi/q}$, and $E_q=\{-1\}$ for even $q$, while
$E_q=[-1,-\rho_q]$ for odd $q$. The eigenvalues on the unit circle are
exactly the roots of unity whose orders are at most $2q$:
\begin{equation}
 \Sigma_q\cap\mathbb T=\{\zeta:\zeta^m=1\text{ for some }1\le m\le2q\}.\label{eq:unit}
\end{equation}

Every $\lambda\in\Sigma_q$ is an eigenvalue of a matrix of the form
\[
 w_qU_q+w_MU_M+w_NU_N,\qquad q<M<N\le2q,
\]
with $w_q,w_M,w_N\ge0$ and $w_q+w_M+w_N=1$. Such matrices form the
triangular face $\operatorname{conv}\{U_q,U_M,U_N\}$ of $\mathcal S_q$: at most three vertices
are needed, one of which is $U_q$. Every $\lambda\in\partial\Sigma_q$
is an eigenvalue of a matrix of the form
\[
 (1-t)U_K+tU_L,\qquad q\le K<L\le2q,\quad0\le t\le1.
\]
This is a convex combination of two of the fixed matrices; $t=0$ or
$t=1$ gives an individual matrix. Thus a realizing matrix can be chosen
on an edge of $\mathcal S_q$.

\label{prop:balancing}
The characteristic polynomial of $B_{2q}(a,b,p)$ is
\begin{equation}
 \chi_{a,b,p}(X)=(X^q-a)(X^q-b)-(1-a)(1-b)P_p(X).\label{eq:characteristic}
\end{equation}
To replace $a$ and $b$ by equal probabilities, set $c=(a+b)/2$.
If $c<1$, define
\begin{equation}
 \tau=\frac{(1-a)(1-b)}{(1-c)^2},\qquad \widetilde p=\tau p+(1-\tau)e_0.\label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
Here $0\le\tau\le1$, so $\widetilde p$ is again a probability vector:
it combines the original vector $p$ with $e_0$, which assigns all
probability to $A_0$. The resulting balanced matrix satisfies
\[
 \chi_{a,b,p}(X)=\chi_{c,c,\widetilde p}(X).
\]
It therefore has the same eigenvalues, with the same algebraic
multiplicities, as the original matrix. If $c=1$, then $a=b=1$ and the
matrix is already $U_q$; take $U_q$ as its image.

This construction defines a continuous map from the full matrix family
onto the balanced family $\mathcal S_q$. Every matrix with $a=b$ is left
unchanged by the map.

Let $z$ be a right eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$, and set

 \det(XI-B_{2q}(a,b,p))

 1-aX^{-q}&-(1-a)X^{-q}\\

 \end{pmatrix}.

 \end{aligned}

matrix is therefore left unchanged.

These follow from the definition of $w$ and

Every $\lambda\in\Sigma_q$ is an eigenvalue of a balanced matrix

 \Sigma_q=\bigcup_{q<M<N\le2q}\ \bigcup_{B\in\operatorname{conv}\{U_q,U_M,U_N\}}\operatorname{spec} B.\label{eq:triangles}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:persistence}
Let $q\ge3$ and let $\lambda_0$ be nonreal with $|\lambda_0|<1$.
If, for some fixed $s_0\ge1$,
\[
 \Gamma_{\lambda_0}(s_0)\in\operatorname{int} K_q(\lambda_0),
\]
then $\lambda_0\in\operatorname{int}\Sigma_q$: a sufficiently small open disk
centered at $\lambda_0$ is contained in $\Sigma_q$.

In particular, if $\lambda_0$ is an eigenvalue of a balanced matrix
$B_{2q}(c,c,p)$ with $p_j>0$ for every $j$, then $\lambda_0$ is an
interior point of $\Sigma_q$.

Every attainable nonreal eigenvalue with modulus less than one is a
limit of interior points of $\Sigma_q$. Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 \Sigma_q\cap(\mathbb D^\circ\setminus\mathbb R)\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{int}\Sigma_q}.\label{eq:approximation}
\end{equation}

For $|u|=1$, define
\[
 h_\lambda(u)=\max_{0\le j<q}\operatorname{Re}(u\lambda^j).
\]
This is the largest value of the linear functional $z\mapsto\operatorname{Re}(uz)$
on $K_q(\lambda)$. A point $z$ belongs to the hull precisely when
$\operatorname{Re}(uz)\le h_\lambda(u)$ for every $|u|=1$.
The function $h_\lambda(u)$ is jointly continuous in $\lambda$ and $u$,
since it is the maximum of finitely many continuous functions.

Because $\Gamma_{\lambda_0}(s_0)$ lies strictly inside $K_q(\lambda_0)$,
the difference
$h_{\lambda_0}(u)-\operatorname{Re}(u\Gamma_{\lambda_0}(s_0))$ is positive for every
$|u|=1$. Its minimum on the compact unit circle is therefore a positive
number $\delta$, giving
\[
 \operatorname{Re}\bigl(u\Gamma_{\lambda_0}(s_0)\bigr)
 \le h_{\lambda_0}(u)-\delta\qquad(|u|=1).
\]
Joint continuity and compactness ensure that, for all $\lambda$
sufficiently close to $\lambda_0$, the same $s_0$ satisfies
\[
 \operatorname{Re}\bigl(u\Gamma_\lambda(s_0)\bigr)
 \le h_\lambda(u)-\delta/2\qquad(|u|=1).
\]
Choose this neighborhood inside the open unit disk. The inequalities put $\Gamma_\lambda(s_0)$ in
$K_q(\lambda)$, so the geometric criterion realizes every point in the
neighborhood. The required bound $s_0\le s^*(\lambda)$ follows from
this inclusion, as established above. Hence
$\lambda_0\in\operatorname{int}\Sigma_q$.

\[
 \operatorname{Im}\bigl(\overline{(\lambda-1)}(\lambda^2-1)\bigr)
 =|\lambda-1|^2\operatorname{Im}\lambda\ne0.
\]
Thus $K_q(\lambda)$ has nonempty planar interior when $q\ge3$.
A weighted average with every $p_j>0$ cannot lie on its boundary:
a line through a boundary point with the whole hull on one side would
force every positively weighted power onto that line, contradicting
the three noncollinear points. Therefore $P_p(\lambda)$ lies in the
interior of $K_q(\lambda)$. For the balanced matrix in the lemma,
$c<1$ and $\Gamma_\lambda((1-c)^{-1})=P_p(\lambda)$, so the first part
of the proof applies. This includes $c=0$, where $s_0=1$.

Every entry of $p(\varepsilon)$ is at least $\varepsilon/q>0$, and $p(\varepsilon)\to p$.

just proved places them in $\operatorname{int}\Sigma_q$, establishing the
approximation claim. The argument also applies when $\lambda_0$ is a
multiple eigenvalue.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:elementary}
The region $\Sigma_q$ is compact and contained in the unit disk. It is
symmetric about the real axis: whenever $\lambda$ is attainable, so is
$\overline\lambda$. Its real points are exactly $[-1,1]$. As stated in
\eqref{eq:unit}, its points on the unit circle are precisely the roots of
unity whose orders are at most $2q$.

No nonzero eigenvalue satisfies $0<|\operatorname{Arg}\lambda|<\pi/q$.
The three line segments $[0,1]$, $[0,e^{i\pi/q}]$, and
$[0,e^{-i\pi/q}]$ lie on the boundary of $\Sigma_q$.

<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The parameters $a,b,p$ range over a compact set, and the transition
matrix depends continuously on them. Stochasticity places every
eigenvalue in the unit disk. The equation
$\det(\lambda I-B_{2q}(a,b,p))=0$ therefore defines a closed subset of
the compact product of the parameter set and the unit disk. Its
projection onto the $\lambda$ coordinate is $\Sigma_q$, proving
compactness. Since the matrices have real entries, their nonreal
eigenvalues occur in conjugate pairs, giving symmetry about the real axis.

The matrix $cU_q+(1-c)U_{2q}=B_{2q}(c,c,e_0)$ has characteristic polynomial
\begin{equation}
 \chi_c(X)=(X^q-c)^2-(1-c)^2=(X^q-1)\bigl(X^q-(2c-1)\bigr).\label{eq:first-edge}
\end{equation}
For any $\lambda\in\mathbb D$ with $\lambda^q\in[-1,1]$, choose
$c=(1+\lambda^q)/2\in[0,1]$. Then $\chi_c(\lambda)=0$.
This realizes every real number in $[-1,1]$, and also every point on
the three segments in the proposition: on the positive real segment
$\lambda^q\in[0,1]$, and on the segments at angles $\pm\pi/q$,
$\lambda^q\in[-1,0]$. The unit-disk bound excludes any other real points.

To prove the angular gap, suppose
$\lambda=\rho e^{i\theta}$ with $\rho>0$ and $0<\theta<\pi/q$.
The powers $1,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda^{q-1}$ have angles
$0,\theta,\ldots,(q-1)\theta$. Since $(q-1)\theta<\pi$, every weighted
average of these powers is nonzero and has angle between $0$ and
$(q-1)\theta$. If $\lambda$ were attainable, the balancing proposition
would provide a matrix $B_{2q}(c,c,p)$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$.
This is a \emph{balanced realization} of $\lambda$: a permitted matrix
with equal probabilities $a=b=c$ that has the specified eigenvalue.
For $c<1$, its eigenvalue equation would require
\[
 P_p(\lambda)=\frac{(\lambda^q-c)^2}{(1-c)^2}.
\]
The number $\lambda^q$ has angle $q\theta\in(0,\pi)$. Subtracting
$c\ge0$ moves it horizontally to the left, so
$\phi=\operatorname{Arg}(\lambda^q-c)$ satisfies $q\theta\le\phi<\pi$.
If $\phi\le\pi/2$, its square has angle
$2\phi\ge2q\theta>(q-1)\theta$. If $\phi>\pi/2$, the principal angle
of its square is $2\phi-2\pi<0$. Neither case gives a possible angle
for $P_p(\lambda)$. The remaining case $c=1$ gives $U_q$, whose
eigenvalues satisfy $\lambda^q=1$ and cannot have $0<\theta<\pi/q$.
Symmetry gives the same exclusion below the real axis. Every point of
the three attainable segments can be approached by points in this
excluded sector, so the segments lie on the boundary.

Finally, roots satisfying $\lambda^q=1$ already have order at most $q$.
If $|\lambda|=1$ but $\lambda^q\ne1$, then $s^*(\lambda)=1$.
The geometric criterion gives
\[
 \lambda^{2q}=\sum_jp_j\lambda^j.
\]
A weighted average of points on the unit circle can lie on that circle
only when all positively weighted points coincide. Hence
$\lambda^{2q}=\lambda^j$ for at least one $j$ with $p_j>0$, so
$\lambda^{2q-j}=1$ and the order of $\lambda$ is at most $2q$.
Conversely, let $\lambda$ be a root of unity of order $k\le2q$.
The length $L=\ell_q(k)$ is a multiple of $k$ with $q\le L\le2q$,
so $\lambda^L=1$. If $L=q$, the matrix $U_q$ realizes it. If $L>q$,
the matrix $U_L$ has a cycle of length $L$, which realizes every root
satisfying $\lambda^L=1$. This proves the unit-circle classification.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:chord}
Let $u,v>0$ be real exponents, and let $\alpha,\beta>0$ be angles with
$\alpha+\beta<\pi$. The equation \eqref{eq:scalar}, namely
\[
 \rho^v\sin\alpha+\rho^u\sin\beta=\sin(\alpha+\beta),
\]
has exactly one solution $\rho\in(0,1)$.
For $R=\rho^{-1}$, this equation is equivalent to requiring the complex
number $1$ to lie strictly between the endpoints of the straight line
segment joining $R^ue^{i\alpha}$ and $R^ve^{-i\beta}$:
\begin{equation}
 1\in\bigl(R^ue^{i\alpha},R^ve^{-i\beta}\bigr).\label{eq:chord}
\end{equation}
More explicitly,
\[
 1=(1-t)R^ue^{i\alpha}+tR^ve^{-i\beta},\qquad
 t=\frac{\rho^v\sin\alpha}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}\in(0,1).
\]
Thus $t$ is the weight of the second endpoint and $1-t$ that of the first.
If $u,v,\alpha,\beta$ vary real-analytically with real parameters, then
$\rho$ and $t$ do as well, provided the stated inequalities remain valid.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
The two endpoints have imaginary parts of opposite signs. Solving for the
average with zero imaginary part shows that their segment meets the
positive real axis at
\[
 \frac{R^{u+v}\sin(\alpha+\beta)}{R^u\sin\alpha+R^v\sin\beta}.
\]
Setting this expression equal to $1$ and substituting $R=\rho^{-1}$ gives
\eqref{eq:scalar}.

The left-hand side of that equation is continuous and strictly increasing
for $\rho>0$, with limit $0$ as $\rho\to0$. Its right-hand side is positive.
At $\rho=1$, the left-hand side exceeds the right-hand side by
\[
 \sin\alpha+\sin\beta-\sin(\alpha+\beta)
 =4\sin(\alpha/2)\sin(\beta/2)\sin((\alpha+\beta)/2)>0.
\]
The intermediate value theorem therefore gives exactly one solution in
$(0,1)$.

At this solution, cancellation of the imaginary parts gives the averaging
weights
\[
 t=\frac{\rho^v\sin\alpha}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)},\qquad
 1-t=\frac{\rho^u\sin\beta}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}.
\]
Both are positive, and they sum to one by \eqref{eq:scalar}, so the point
$1$ lies strictly between the two endpoints.
Finally, the derivative of the equation's left-hand side with respect to
$\rho$ is
\[
 v\rho^{v-1}\sin\alpha+u\rho^{u-1}\sin\beta>0.

\label{lem:convex}
Consider the line through the endpoints in \eqref{eq:chord}, with $R>1$.
Let $\psi(\varphi)$ be the logarithm of the radius at which the ray at angle
$\varphi$ meets this line. For any $a,b\in[0,1]$,

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
The line meets the rays at angles $\alpha$ and $-\beta$ at its two
endpoints, whose radii are $R^u$ and $R^v$. Hence
\[
 \psi(\alpha)=u\log R>0,\qquad \psi(-\beta)=v\log R>0.
\]
Set $y=a\alpha-b\beta$. Since $a,b\in[0,1]$, we have
$-\beta\le y\le\alpha$.

\[
 \psi(y)\le\frac y\alpha\psi(\alpha)
 \le a\psi(\alpha)
 \le a\psi(\alpha)+b\psi(-\beta).
\]
For $b>0$, the last inequality is strict. If $b=0$, then $y=a\alpha$,
and strict convexity makes the first inequality strict unless $a=0$ or
$a=1$.

If $y<0$, convexity on $[-\beta,0]$ instead gives
\[
 \psi(y)\le\frac{-y}{\beta}\psi(-\beta)
 \le b\psi(-\beta)
 \le a\psi(\alpha)+b\psi(-\beta).
\]
For $a>0$, the last inequality is strict. If $a=0$, then $y=-b\beta$,
and strict convexity leaves equality only at $b=1$, since $y<0$.
Together, the two cases give precisely the three stated equality pairs.
Reflection across the real axis sends $\varphi$ to $-\varphi$ and preserves
strict convexity, so the same argument applies to the reflected

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:phases}
For $x\in(h/k,r/s)$ and every integer $q\le L\le2q$, the fractional parts
satisfy
\begin{align}
 \{Mx\}&\le\{Lx\}\le\{Nx\},\label{eq:phase-order}\\
 \frac{\{Mx\}}M&\le\frac{\{Lx\}}L,\qquad
 \frac{1-\{Nx\}}N\le\frac{1-\{Lx\}}L.\label{eq:phase-ratios}
\end{align}
In \eqref{eq:phase-order}, equality on the left occurs exactly when $L=M$,
and equality on the right exactly when $L=N$.
Thus, among the powers $z^q,\ldots,z^{2q}$, $z^M$ is closest to the
positive real axis when measured counterclockwise, and $z^N$ when measured
clockwise. The inequalities in \eqref{eq:phase-ratios} compare these
angular distances after division by the corresponding exponents.
The integer multipliers $u$ and $v$ also satisfy
\[
 2u\le s,\qquad 2v\le k.
\]
These two bounds cannot both be equalities. Moreover, $2u=s$ is possible
only when $s=2$, and $2v=k$ only when $k=2$.

Set $A=kx-h$. The Farey identity $kr-hs=1$ gives
$0<A<1/s$. Since $q<(k+s)/2\le ks$, we have
$u=\lceil q/k\rceil\le s$, and hence $0<uA<1$. Thus
$Mx=uh+uA$ and $\{Mx\}=uA$.

 H=hL-kn\ge0,\qquad \{Lx\}=\frac{LA+H}{k}.
\]
If $L\ge M=uk$, this expression is at least $uA$, and is strictly greater
when $L>M$.

\[
 kK-sH=L.
\]
Since $kK\ge L\ge q>(u-1)k$, the integer $K$ satisfies $K\ge u$.
It follows that $sH=kK-L\ge M-L$. Combining this with $A<1/s$ yields
\[

 =\frac{H-(M-L)A}{k}>0.
\]
This proves $\{Mx\}\le\{Lx\}$, with equality exactly when $L=M$.
The corresponding ratio bound follows directly from $n/L\le h/k$:
\[
 \frac{\{Lx\}}L=x-\frac nL
 \ge x-\frac hk=\frac{\{Mx\}}M.
\]

$(s-r)/s<(k-h)/k$. This interchanges $M$ and $N$.

$x$ itself between the Farey neighbors with denominator at most $2q$.

\[
 \{Lx\}\le\{Nx\},\qquad
 \frac{1-\{Nx\}}N\le\frac{1-\{Lx\}}L.
\]
Equality in the upper bound $\{Lx\}\le\{Nx\}$ occurs exactly when $L=N$.

therefore singles out $L=N$.

hold except for the unordered pair $\{k,s\}=\{2,3\}$.

$k+s=7>2q$.
Therefore $v=1$ and $k=2$. Interchanging $k,u$ with $s,v$ gives

\label{cor:exhaustion}

\end{enumerate}

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:sharpness}
For every $q\ge2$, the matrices in $\mathcal S_q^{(1)}$ have the form
\[
 (1-t)U_M+tU_N,\qquad q\le M<N\le2q,\quad 0\le t\le1.
\]
These are convex combinations of two of the fixed matrices
$U_q,\ldots,U_{2q}$; $t=0$ or $t=1$ gives an individual matrix.
Their eigenvalues form a set with empty interior in the complex plane, and
\[
 \bigcup_{A\in\mathcal S_q^{(1)}}\operatorname{spec} A\subsetneq\Sigma_q.
\]
Thus some attainable eigenvalues cannot be realized by a convex
combination of two of the fixed matrices, although convex combinations
of three always suffice by Theorem~\ref{thm:triangles}.

form, but the individual matrices $U_q,\ldots,U_{2q}$ do not realize all
boundary eigenvalues.

<!-- proof-20: body_md -->
Fix two distinct matrices $A_0,A_1$ from $U_q,\ldots,U_{2q}$ and
write their convex combinations as
\[
 A(t)=(1-t)A_0+tA_1,\qquad 0\le t\le1.
\]
Their characteristic polynomial $F(X,t)=\det(XI-A(t))$ has degree
$2q$ in $X$, with leading coefficient $1$, and its coefficients are
polynomials in $t$.
To track its distinct roots even when some remain repeated as $t$ varies,
take its square-free part $G(X,t)$ over $\mathbb C(t)$, normalized to have
leading coefficient $1$.
Here $\mathbb C(t)$ consists of rational functions of $t$, and taking the
square-free part means retaining one copy of each distinct polynomial
factor. Thus $G$ has the same distinct roots as $F$ except at finitely
many exceptional parameter values.

The discriminant of $G$ detects repeated roots: wherever its coefficients
are defined, the discriminant is zero exactly when $G$ has a repeated
root. It is a nonzero rational function of $t$ because $G$ is square-free.
Exclude its zeros and poles and the poles of the coefficients of $G$;
there are only finitely many such parameter values.
At every other value, each root has $\partial G/\partial X\ne0$, so the
implicit-function theorem gives it locally as an analytic function of $t$.

For real $t$, these functions trace curves in the complex plane.
On compact subintervals their derivatives are bounded, so their images
have planar area zero. A countable collection of these local curves
covers all roots at nonexceptional parameters.
The finitely many exceptional values contribute only finitely many
eigenvalues of $A(t)$.
Hence all eigenvalues obtained from this pair of matrices form a set
of planar area zero. There are only finitely many pairs, so their
combined eigenvalue set also has area zero and therefore contains no
open disk.

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} gives $\Sigma_q$ nonempty interior for every
$q\ge2$, so convex combinations of two of the fixed matrices cannot
realize all of $\Sigma_q$. Convex combinations of three do realize it by
Theorem~\ref{thm:triangles}.
Finally, the individual matrices $U_q,\ldots,U_{2q}$ have only finitely
many eigenvalues in total,
whereas $[0,1]\subseteq\partial\Sigma_q$.
Thus these individual matrices cannot realize the whole boundary.
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} gives a convex combination of two of them with
any prescribed boundary point as an eigenvalue.

\end{equation}
where $0\le\alpha\le1$ \cite{ito,johnson}.
If $k>q$, then $d=1$. Cancelling the common factor $X^k$ for $X\ne0$
gives
\[
 X^s-\beta X^{s-k}-\alpha=0.
\]
By \eqref{eq:ito-base}, this is the equation for the nonzero eigenvalues
of $\beta U_k+\alpha U_s$.

If $k=q$, then $d=2$. Also $q<s<2q$: the equality $s=2q$ is excluded
because the denominators of consecutive Farey fractions have no common
divisor greater than one. Cancelling $X^s$ for $X\ne0$ gives
\[
 (X^q-\beta)^2-\alpha^2X^{2q-s}=0.
\]
This is \eqref{eq:ito-lateral} with $L=s$ and $c=\beta$, the
characteristic equation of $\beta U_q+\alpha U_s$.

In both cases, $0\le\alpha\le1$ and $\beta=1-\alpha$ give convex
combinations of our fixed matrices throughout the parameter range in
Ito's equation. Every nonzero point on this boundary curve of
$\Theta_{2q}$ is therefore an eigenvalue of a matrix in our family,
and so belongs to $\Sigma_q$.
Conversely, every matrix in our family is a $2q\times2q$ row-stochastic
matrix, hence $\Sigma_q\subseteq\Theta_{2q}$.
Fix $x\in(f,g)$ and let $R$ be the largest modulus attained in
$\Theta_{2q}$ on the ray with argument $2\pi x$.
The point $Re^{2\pi ix}$ lies on the boundary curve just realized in
$\Sigma_q$, while the inclusion prevents any point of $\Sigma_q$ on
this ray from having modulus greater than $R$.
Thus the largest attainable modulus in $\Sigma_q$ is also $R$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} identifies this modulus as $r_q(x)$, proving the
claimed equality of boundary curves.

Put
\[
 d=\gcd(q,a),\qquad P=dP_*,\qquad
 P_*=(r,p)=(q/d,a/d),\qquad \delta=\det(P_*,Q)>0.
\]
The coordinates of $P_*$ have greatest common divisor $1$, so we can
choose an integer vector $C$ with $\det(P_*,C)=1$.
Consequently, every integer point has a unique expression
$U=xP_*+yC$ with $x,y\in\mathbb Z$.
In these coordinates, write $Q=\kappa P_*+\delta C$, where
$\kappa\in\mathbb Z$. The coordinate $y$ is exactly $\det(P_*,U)$.

For $U=\sigma P+\tau Q$, we have
$x=d\sigma+\kappa\tau$ and $y=\delta\tau$.
Thus $0\le\sigma\le1$ and $1/2\le\tau\le1$ are equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 \lceil\delta/2\rceil\le y\le\delta,\qquad
 0\le\delta x-\kappa y\le d\delta.\label{eq:lattice-membership}
\end{equation}
Fix an integer $y$ satisfying $\lceil\delta/2\rceil\le y\le\delta$.
The smallest integer $x$ satisfying the second inequality in
\eqref{eq:lattice-membership} is
\begin{equation}
 x_y=\lceil\kappa y/\delta\rceil,\qquad
 \nu_y=\delta x_y-\kappa y\in\{0,\ldots,\delta-1\},\qquad
 U_y=x_yP_*+yC.\label{eq:lattice-level}
\end{equation}
Since $U_y=(\nu_y/\delta)P_*+(y/\delta)Q$, its first coordinate
in the original plane, denoted $m_y$, is
\begin{equation}
 m_y=\frac{yL+\nu_yr}{\delta}.\label{eq:lattice-first}
\end{equation}
All integer points in $H$ with this coordinate $y$ are obtained by
adding $kP_*$ to $U_y$. Equation~\eqref{eq:lattice-membership} allows
$0\le k\le d-1$, and also $k=d$ precisely when $\nu_y=0$.
Each addition of $P_*$ increases the first coordinate by $r$.

If $\delta=1$, the only possible integer $y$ is $1$, and the integer
points in $H$ are
\[
 Q,Q+P_*,\ldots,Q+dP_*.
\]
Their first coordinates are $L,L+r,\ldots,L+dr$.
Since $q<L\le2q$, a point other than $Q$ has first coordinate in
$(q,2q]$ if and only if $L+r\le2q$.
Thus $Q$ is the only such point exactly when $L+r>2q$, as claimed
in the second alternative of the lemma.
For the rest of the proof, assume $\delta\ge2$; we will find a point
other than $Q$ with the required first coordinate.

First suppose $d\ge2$ and choose $y_0=\lceil\delta/2\rceil<\delta$.
Using $m_y=(yL+\nu_yr)/\delta$ from \eqref{eq:lattice-first} gives
\[
 m_{y_0}\ge L/2>q/2\ge r,\qquad
 m_{y_0}<\frac{y_0L}{\delta}+r
 \le q+\frac q\delta+\frac qd\le2q.
\]
If $m_{y_0}>q$, the point $U_{y_0}$ works.
Otherwise choose the smallest integer $k\ge0$ such that
$m_{y_0}+kr>q$. Since $q=dr$ and $m_{y_0}>r$, we have $k\le d-1$,
so this translate belongs to $H$. Minimality gives
\[
 q<m_{y_0}+kr\le q+r\le2q.
\]
Its coordinate $y_0<\delta$ distinguishes it from $Q$, whose coordinate
is $\delta$.

It remains to treat $d=1$, so $P=P_*$ and $r=q$.
Set $T=q\delta/L\in[\delta/2,\delta)$.
In \eqref{eq:lattice-first}, the term $yL/\delta$ equals $q$ when
$y=T$; we therefore examine the integer values at or immediately
beside $T$.
If $T$ is an integer, take $y=T$. Then
\[
 m_y=q+\frac{\nu_yq}{\delta}.
\]
If $\nu_y>0$, this lies in $(q,2q)$.
If $\nu_y=0$, the allowed point $U_y+P$ has first coordinate $2q$.
Both points differ from $Q$ because $y<\delta$.

Now suppose $T$ is not an integer, and put
$y_-=\lfloor T\rfloor$, $y_+=\lceil T\rceil$.
If $y_+=\delta$, then
$\lceil\delta/2\rceil\le y_-=\delta-1<\delta$, so $y_-$ satisfies
both bounds on $y$ in \eqref{eq:lattice-membership}.
Since $y_-<T$ and $\nu_{y_-}<\delta$, equation
\eqref{eq:lattice-first} gives $m_{y_-}<2q$. If $m_{y_-}>q$, take $U_{y_-}$.
Otherwise \eqref{eq:lattice-first} gives
\[
 \nu_{y_-}q\le q\delta-(\delta-1)L<q,
\]
so the nonnegative integer $\nu_{y_-}$ must be zero.
The allowed translate $U_{y_-}+P$ then has first coordinate in
$(q,2q]$, and its coordinate $y_-<\delta$ shows that it differs from $Q$.

If $y_-<\lceil\delta/2\rceil$, then $\delta$ is odd and
$y_+=(\delta+1)/2=\lceil\delta/2\rceil<\delta$.
Thus $y_+$ satisfies both bounds on $y$ in
\eqref{eq:lattice-membership}.
Since $y_+>T$, equation \eqref{eq:lattice-first} gives
\[
 q<m_{y_+}\le
 \frac{(\delta+1)q+(\delta-1)q}{\delta}=2q.
\]
Thus $U_{y_+}$ works.

In the remaining case, both integers satisfy
$\lceil\delta/2\rceil\le y_-<y_+<\delta$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:lattice-first}, with $y_-<T<y_+$, gives
$m_{y_-}<2q$ and $m_{y_+}>q$.
If neither point has first coordinate in $(q,2q]$, then
$m_{y_-}\le q$ and $m_{y_+}>2q$.
Put $V=U_{y_+}-U_{y_-}$, let $h$ be its first coordinate, and set
$\sigma=\nu_{y_+}-\nu_{y_-}$. These inequalities give $h>q$.
Because $y_+-y_-=1$, the coordinate expressions yield
\[
 \det(P,V)=1,\qquad Q=-\sigma P+\delta V.
\]
Taking first coordinates gives $L=-\sigma q+\delta h$.
Together with $h>q$ and $L\le2q$, this implies $\delta<\sigma+2$.
But $\sigma\le\delta-1$, so $\sigma=\delta-1$.
Since both $\nu$ values belong to $\{0,\ldots,\delta-1\}$, this forces
$\nu_{y_-}=0$ and $\nu_{y_+}=\delta-1$.

Since $\det(P,V)=1$, every integer point also has integer coefficients
in the basis $(P,V)$. Write
$U_{y_-}=uP+y_-V$ with $u\in\mathbb Z$.
By \eqref{eq:lattice-level},
$\det(U_{y_-},Q)=\nu_{y_-}=0$. Substituting the expressions in this
basis gives
\[
 u\delta+y_-(\delta-1)=0.
\]
Reducing this equality modulo $\delta$ shows that $\delta$ divides
$y_-$, contradicting $0<y_-<\delta$.
Thus this last possibility cannot occur, and a required point exists
whenever $\delta\ge2$.
For negative determinant, negate the second coordinate of every point.
This changes the determinant's sign and preserves the first coordinates,
so the same conclusion applies.
