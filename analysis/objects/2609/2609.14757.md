<!-- defi-1: body_md -->
Let $S$ be a fixed resource set of $n$-qubit Hamiltonians. A Hamiltonian $H\in {\mathbb M}_{2^n}$ is called \emph{magic with respect to $S$} if the Lie algebra generated by $iS$ and $iH$ is $\mathfrak{su}(2^n)$.
<!-- end defi-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{sample} The following table, excerpted from Theorem \ref{thm:controlsets}, provides examples and non-examples of magic Hamiltonians: 
    \[ \begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
 \text{No.}& \text{local set } S & \text{Dim}(L(S)) & \text{Hamiltonian} & \text{magic} \\  \hline
 1)& \mathcal{X}(2)\cup \mathcal{Z}(1)& 2n^2-n& X_1+Z_{1}Z_2 & \text{yes}  \\ 
  5)&\{X_1,Z_1Z_2\}\cup \mathcal{Z}(1) & n+5 & J_{\gamma,\lambda} &  \text{yes}\\
  8)&\{X_1\}\cup \mathcal{Z}(2)  & n+1 & J_{\gamma,\lambda} & \text{yes}^* \\ 
  \end{array} \] 
Here yes$^*$ is under the condition $\lambda\neq 0$, $\gamma\notin\{-1,0,1\}$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
All the magic Hamiltonians in Theorem \ref{sample} and section 3 have exponential approximation length (in the number of qubits).
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- defi-2: body_md -->
\label{def:paulie} Let $\{P_1,...,P_m\}\subset U(2^n)$ be a set of Pauli strings. 
 \begin{enumerate}
 \item[i)] We denote by $ D=D(P_1,...,P_m) \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} \{[P_{i_1},[P_{i_2},\cdots ]]\cdots ]] | 1\le  i_1,...,i_k\le m \}$  
the set of their iterated commutators.  
\item[ii)] We denote by $G(P_1,...,P_m)\subset U(2^n)$ the abstract subgroup they generate. 
\item[iii)] Let $K=\{1,-1\}\subset U(2^n)$. By $G(K,P_1,...,P_m)$ we denote the subgroup generated by $K$ and $P_1...,P_m$. 
  \item[ii)] A map  $\sigma:\{P_1,...,P_m\}\to {\mathbb M}_{2^m}$ is said to be \textbf{PauLie} if $\sigma(P_j)$ is again a Pauli string and
 \[ [P_j,P_k]\hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} 0 \hspace{.3cm} \Leftrightarrow \hspace{.3cm} [\sigma(P_j),\sigma(P_k)] \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} 0 \hspace{.1cm} .\]
 \end{enumerate}
<!-- end defi-2 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:PauLie_homomorphism} Let $P_1,..,P_m$'s be Pauli strings. The group 
$G(K,P_1,...,P_m)$ is uniquely specified by the  matrix of coefficients $\varepsilon_{ij}$ in the relation 
 \[ P_iP_j\hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} \varepsilon_{ij}P_jP_i \hspace{.1cm} .\]  
In particular, a  PauLie map induces a group homomorphism.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Let $G_k=G(K,P_1,...,P_k)$ be the group generated by the first $k$ elements. We note that for a fixed $P_{k+1}$  the map 
 \[ \alpha(Q) \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} P_{k+1}QP_{k+1} \hspace{.1cm} \]
is a group homomorphism. Therefore $G_{k+1}=G_k\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb Z}_2$ can be interpreted 
as  a semi-direct product consisting of pairs $(Q_1,Q_2P_{k+1})$ with $Q_{1,2}\in G_k$ using 
$Q_1P_{k+1}Q_3=Q_1\alpha(Q_3)P_{k+1}$. Given a PauLie-map $\sigma$ we can proceed by induction to show that the canonical extension $\sigma(P_{i_1}\cdots P_{i_l})=\sigma(P_{i_1})\cdots \sigma(P_{i_l})$ is a well-defined group homomorphism. Indeed, since the matrix $\varepsilon_{ij}$ is preserved, we see that 
 \[  \sigma(P_{k+1})\sigma(Q)\sigma(P_{k+1}) \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} \sigma(\alpha(Q))   \]
 induces exactly the same automorphism.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- cor-1: body_md -->
\label{LGG} Let $\sigma$ be a PauLie map, $\sigma^{G}$  be the unique extension of $\sigma^G$  to the group algebra ${\mathbb C}[G(K,P_1,...P_m)]$, and $\sigma^{Lie}$ be the unique Lie algebra extension. Then
 \[ \sigma^{G}|_{D(P_1,...,P_m)} \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} \sigma^{Lie} \hspace{.1cm} .\]
<!-- end cor-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
By linear independence and linearity, the group homomorphism $\sigma^{G}$ extends to ${\mathbb C}[G]$. Then 
\[ \sigma^G([P,Q]) \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} 2\delta_{[P,Q]\neq 0}
\sigma^G(PQ) \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm}  2\delta_{[P,Q]\neq 0} \sigma^G(P)\sigma^G(Q)
\hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} [\sigma^G(P),\sigma^G(Q)] \hspace{.1cm} . \] 
By applying this relation iteratively and observing that iterated commutators are linear multiples of Pauli strings, we deduce that the restriction of the group homomorphism induces an extension on the Lie algebra which preserves commutators. By the uniqueness assumption, this coincides with the Lie group extension of the PauLie map.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{cstar} Let $G\subset U(n)$ be a group and $\pi:G\to U(m)$ be a group homomorphism such that $\tau(\pi(g))=\tau(g)$ holds for the normalized trace $\tau(x)=\frac{tr(x)}{tr(1)}$. Then $\pi$ extends to a $C^*$-homomorphism.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
We note that $A=\{\sum_g \alpha_g g|\alpha_g \in {\mathbb C}\}$ is a $^*$-algebra. Then 
 $\pi(\sum_g\alpha_g g)=\sum_{g}\alpha_g \pi(g)$. Note that for $x=\sum_g a_gg$ we have  
  \[ 
  \tau(\pi(x)^*\pi(x)) \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} \sum_{g,g'}\bar{\alpha}_g\alpha_{g'}\tau(\pi(g^{-1}g') )
  \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} \tau(x^*x) \hspace{.1cm}. 
  \] 
This implies that $\pi$ is injective and well-defined. Since we are in the finite-dimensional case, we obtain a $^*$-homomorphism defined on the $C^*$-algebra $A$.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- cor-2: body_md -->
Let $P_1,...,P_m$ be a set of Pauli strings and $\sigma$ a PauLie map with values in ${\mathbb M}_d$. Then the extension $\sigma^G$ to the group algebra of ${\mathbb C}[G(K,P_1,...,P_m)]$ extends to an injective $C^*$-homomorphism and also induces a group homomorphism on the Lie group  which preserves the Finsler metric given by the operator norm (see section \ref{sub:univeral_sets})  and an isometry for the distance given by the operator norm.
<!-- end cor-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $\sigma^{G}:{\mathbb C}[G(K,P_1,...,P_m)]\to {\mathbb M}_d$ be the induced $^*$-algebra homorphism. Since the map $\sigma$ is PauLie it is also trace preserving and extends to a trace preserving map. By Lemma \ref{cstar}, we deduce that $\sigma^{G}$ is a $^*$-homomorphism on $A={\mathbb C}[G]\subset {\mathbb M}_n$. Note that the identity map is also PauLie, and hence the concrete embedding of ${\mathbb C}[G]$ is indeed injective, hence $\sigma:A\to {\mathbb M}_d$ is an injective $^*$-homomorphism.  Since we are  in finite dimension, $A$ is also closed.  Recall that injective $^*$-homorphisms are indeed isometries, see \cite{TAk1}. Then we observe that for all $j$, the operator $P_j$ is in the image of  the group algebra. The same is true for $e^{itP_j}$ for all $t$ and iterated products. This means the Lie group generated by $G$ is also in $A$. The Finsler metric on $G$ is given by 
 \[ d_{op}(g,h) \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} \inf \int_0^1 \|\gamma'(t)\|_{op} dt, \]
where the infimum is taken over all piecewise differentiable paths $\gamma(t)\in G$. Then $\gamma'(g)=h(t)\gamma(t)$ is given by a vector $h(t)$ in the Lie algebra. In particular 
 \[ d_{op}(\sigma^G(g),\sigma^G(h)) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \int_0^1 \|\sigma^G(h(t))\|_{op} dt \hspace{.1cm}. \]
Taking the infimum shows that $d(\sigma^G(g),\sigma^G(h))\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} d(g,h)$. We have equality by applying this to the inverse of $\sigma$, again a PauLie map.

 \begin{align*}

  &= 
   [[R,S],T]+[S,[R,T]] \hspace{.1cm} .
  \end{align*}
Because commutators of Paulis obey \eqref{comP} we have only to consider the cases where each commutator is resepctively zero or nonzero. If $[R,S]=0=[R,T]$, we find $0$. If $[R,S]\neq 0=[R,T]$ we find
 \[ 2[RS,T] \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} 2[R,T]S+2R[S,T] \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} 4\delta_{[S,T]\neq 0} RST \hspace{.1cm} .\]

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{additive} $l([P,Q])\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} l(P)+l(Q)$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
For a generator $P_{i_j}$ we obviously have
  \[ l([P_{i_j},Q])\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} 1+ l(Q) \hspace{.1cm}. \]
This is the needed induction step in proving $l(P)\le k$ implies $l([P,Q])\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} l(P)+l(Q)$. Thus we may assume $P=[P_{i_k},\hat{P}]$ with $\hat{P}$ of length $k-1$. Then the Jacobi idenity tells us that 
 \[ [P,Q] \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} [[P_{i_m},\hat{P}],Q] 
 \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} [P_{i_m},[\hat{P},Q]]+ [\hat{P},[Q,P_{i_m}] ]\hspace{.1cm}. \] 
By the induction hypothesis, $l([\hat{P},Q])\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} k-1+l(Q)$, and hence we find an upper estimate $k+l(Q)$ for the first term. Of course, $l([P_{i_m},Q])\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} l(Q)+1$, and again the induction hypothesis applies for the second term (because we ignore scalar coefficients). However, $[P,Q]=2PQ$ is a non-trivial multiple of a Pauli string and this also true for the two terms on the right. By the orthogonality of Pauli strings \eqref{comP}, $[P,Q]$ has to coincide with one of them, up to scalar multiples. But both of $\{P,Q\}$ have length $\le k+l(Q)$.

\label{prop:single-double}
    The Lie algebra generated by $\mathcal{X}(2)$ and $\mathcal{Z}(1)$ is spanned by
    \begin{equation}
        \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(1) \rangle = \mathcal{Z}(1) \cup \{\alpha_i Z^{\otimes m} \beta_{i+m+1} ~:~ \alpha, \beta \in \{X,Y\},~ m \in [n-2] \},

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We first show that $\alpha_i Z^{\otimes m} \beta_{i+m+1} \in \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(1)\rangle$, and then we show that this set is closed under the commutator. 

We start the calculations with $X_i X_{i+1}$ and commute with other elements of the generator set to find
\begin{equation}
    X_i X_{i+1} \stackrel{Z_{i+1}}{\longleftrightarrow}X_i Y_{i+1} \stackrel{X_{i+1}X_{i+2}}{\longleftrightarrow} X_i Z_{i+1} X_{i+2} \stackrel{Z_{i+2}}{\longleftrightarrow} \cdots \stackrel{X_{i+m} X_{i+m+1}}{\longleftrightarrow} X_i Z^{\otimes m} X_{i+m+1}.
\end{equation}
The final term we can commute with $Z_i$ and/or $Z_{i+m+1}$ depending on whether $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_{i+m+1}$ are $X$ or $Y$ operators. Thus, we find $\alpha_i Z_{i+1}\cdots Z_{i+m_1-1}\beta_{i+m_1} \in \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(1)\rangle$. 

Next, we show that the vector space spanned by these chains and the $\mathcal{Z}(1)$ is closed under the Lie bracket. First, we notice that the commutator of two chains will result in either a new chain of the same form or an individual $Z_i$. Secondly, the individual chains preserve their form under commutation with $Z_i$. Finally, the commutator of a $Z_i$ with a $Z_j$ will always be 0. Together, this means that the proposed vector space is closed under the commutator and that the generated sub-Lie algebra is thus given by 
\begin{equation}
    \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(1) \rangle = \{Z_j|j \in [n]\} \cup \{\alpha_iZ^{\otimes_m}\beta_{i+m+2}| \alpha, \beta \in \{X,Y\},~ m \in [n-2] \}.
\end{equation}

Using the classification method for Lie algebras generated by Pauli strings given in \cite{Aguilar2024FullClassificationLie}, we find that the Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{so}(2n)$ with dimension $2n^2-n$.

We can also find this dimension by explicitly counting all possible Pauli strings to get
\begin{equation}
    n+\sum_{k=2}^{n}4(n-k+1) = 2n^2-n. \qedhere
\end{equation}

\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These generate the same Lie algebra as the generators

\begin{aligned}

    T_2 &= \{Y_{n/2+1}, X_{n/2+1}X_{n/2+2}, Y_{n/2+2}, \cdots  X_{n-1} X_n, Y_n \}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The map $\sigma$ from $S_1, S_2$ to $T_1, T_2$ is a PauLie maps (Definition \ref{def:paulie}). As discussed in Section 2, because this mapping preserves the commutation relations between the elements, it generates the same Lie algebra and we do not have to construct an explicit map. We nevertheless provide such a map and comment on its properties in App.~\ref{app:explicit_map_S_T}.

We can now calculate the algebra generated by $S_1$ and $S_2$ by investigating the algebra generated by $T_1$ and $T_2$. Since $T_1$ and $T_2$ commute, the Lie algebra generated by both is the direct sum of the individual algebras and thus given by $\oplus_{i=1}^2 \mathfrak{so}(n)$.

For this direct calculation, we first show that $\alpha_i Z^{\otimes m} \beta_{i+m+1} \in \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Y}(2) \rangle$. Assume w.l.o.g. by symmetry that $\alpha_i = X$. Applying the following chain of commutators for $m$ even gives
\begin{equation}
    X_i X_{i+1} \stackrel{Y_{i+1} Y_{i+2}}{\longleftrightarrow} X_i Z_{i+1} Y_{i+2} \stackrel{X_{i+2} X_{i+3}}{\longleftrightarrow} X_i Z_{i+1} Z_{i+2} X_{i+3} \stackrel{Y_{i+3} Y_{i+4}}{\longleftrightarrow} \cdots \stackrel{X_{i+m} X_{i+m+1}}{\longleftrightarrow} X_i Z^{\otimes m} X_{i+m+1}.
\end{equation}
The other case for $m$ odd is exactly the same but ends by commuting with $Y_{i+m} Y_{i+m+1}$ to get $X_i Z^{\otimes m} Y_{i+m+1}$. Thus, these strings are contained in the Lie algebra generated by $\mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Y}(2)$.

\end{equation}

\end{equation}

In this proof we understand Pauli strings as words in the alphabet $\{X,Y,Z,1\}$. In this sense we first show permutation invariance. Indeed, we obtain all transpositions through the chain of commutators
    \begin{equation}
        ZI \stackrel{XX}{\longleftrightarrow} YX \stackrel{YY}{\longleftrightarrow} IZ \stackrel{XX}{\longleftrightarrow} XY.
    \end{equation}
    This shows that we can permute $XY$ into $YX$ and $ZI$ into $IZ$. Because of symmetry in the generator set, we can make a similar chain of commutators for $XZ$ with $YI$ and $YZ$ with $XI$. Thus, we can apply any transposition, and thus any permutation.
    To prove the parity constraint, we see that the commutator of $XX$ with any $\alpha \beta$ is non-zero if either $\alpha \in \{Y, Z\}$ and $\beta \in \{X,I\}$, or the reverse. Taking the commutator of such a string with $XX$ flips the choice for $\alpha$ and $\beta$. This means that $k_x, k_y$, and $k_z$ all increase or decrease by exactly 1. Taking a commutator with $YY$ or $ZZ$ changes the parity in the same way. Since all the generators are themselves Pauli strings with $k_x \equiv k_y \equiv k_z \equiv 0 \mod{2}$, taking a commutator with another generator preserves the $\mathrm{mod}\: 2$ equivalence of $k_x, k_y$, and $k_z$ and can only flip $k_x, k_y,$ and $k_z$ to all be equal to $1 \mod{2}$.

    Furthermore, an easy calculation shows that $X^{\otimes n}, Y^{\otimes n}$, and $Z^{\otimes n}$ all commute with all the generators. This means that \begin{equation}\label{eq:subset2}
        \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Y}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(2) \rangle \subset Q_n \backslash \{I, X^{\otimes n}, Y^{\otimes n}, Z^{\otimes n} \}.
    \end{equation} 
A simple but tedious calculation shows that for any Pauli string 
     \[ P \in Q_n \backslash \{I, X^{\otimes n}, Y^{\otimes n}, Z^{\otimes n} \} ,\] 
one can make a Pauli string from $\mathcal{X}(2), \mathcal{Y}(2)$ and $\mathcal{Z}(2)$ with the same $k_x(P), k_y(P)$ and $k_z(P)$. The permutation invariance of $\langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Y}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(2) \rangle$ then implies that
    \begin{equation}
        \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Y}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(2) \rangle \supset Q_n \backslash \{I, X^{\otimes n}, Y^{\otimes n}, Z^{\otimes n} \}.
    \end{equation}
    Together with Eq.~(\ref{eq:subset2}), this proves the second equality. Using the classification method of \cite{Aguilar2024FullClassificationLie}, one directly finds
    \begin{align*}
        \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Y}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(2) \rangle &= \begin{cases}
            \mathfrak{su}(2^{n-1}) &\text{ if $n$ odd} \\
            \oplus_{i=1}^{4}\mathfrak{su}(2^{n-2}) &\text{ if $n$ even}
        \end{cases}.
    \end{align*}

One can see this by first considering an arbitrary Pauli string up to index $n-2$, of which there are $4^{n-2}$. Then these strings can be extended by $4\cdot 4=16$ combinations for the last 2 indices, of which $4$ will satisfy the parity constraints, while the other 12 will not.             4^{n-1}-4 \quad \text{if } n \text{ even}.  
        \end{cases}\qedhere

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
The proof for the corresponding abstract Lie algebra follows from \cite{Aguilar2024FullClassificationLie} for all three cases. Here we show the calculations to find the concrete representation corresponding to the given generators.
    
    For assertion $i)$ let us consider the PauLie map which keeps every generator the same, except for $X_1$ being sent to $X_0X_1$. With this generator we are again in $\mathcal{X}(2)\cup \mathcal{Z}(1)$, but now acting on one more total qubit. We recall that this Lie algebra is exactly given by the original generators and alternating products. Forgetting the $X_0$ registers is the inverse PauLie map which gives exactly the elements listed above. 
    
    For $ii)$, the extension with $Z_1 Z_2$, we first show by induction that, together with elements of $\mathcal{X}(2)$ and $\mathcal{Z}(1)$, we can use $Z_1 Z_2$ to make any element $Z_k Z_{k+1}$. We can interchange any $Z$ with a neighbouring $I$ using the commutator chain
    \begin{equation}
        ZI \stackrel{XX}{\longleftrightarrow} YX \stackrel{YY}{\longleftrightarrow} IZ.
    \end{equation}
    By applying the appropriate transpositions to $Z_1 Z_2$, we can make any $Z_{k} Z_{k+1}$. Thus, we find that 
    \begin{equation}
         \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(1) \cup \{Z_1 Z_2\} \rangle = \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(1) \rangle \supset \langle \mathcal{X}(2) \cup \mathcal{Y}(2) \cup \mathcal{Z}(2) \rangle.
    \end{equation}
    By using Proposition \ref{prop:X2_Y2_Z2}, we find that the resulting Lie algebra is permutation-invariant. If we track the number of Pauli $X, Y$, and $Z$ operators with $k_x, k_y$, and $k_z$ respectively, we see that the presence of individual $Z$ operators means that $k_x \equiv k_y \mod{2}$ still holds, but that $k_z$ has no such constraints.

    To summarize, for arbitrary $\alpha, \beta \in \{X,Y\}$ and $\alpha', \beta' \stackrel{Z}{\longleftrightarrow} \alpha, \beta$, the following operations on Pauli strings are allowed:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I \alpha &\stackrel{ZZ}{\longleftrightarrow} Z \alpha', &  \alpha I & \stackrel{ZZ}{\longleftrightarrow} \alpha' Z \\
        \alpha \beta &\stackrel{\beta \beta}{\longleftrightarrow} ZI , & \alpha \beta &\stackrel{\alpha \alpha}{\longleftrightarrow} IZ \\
        \alpha &\stackrel{Z}{\leftrightarrow} \alpha', \\    
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    where the first four are the same as in Proposition \ref{prop:X2_Y2_Z2}, and the fifth one is the additional operation that breaks the parity constraint on $k_z$.

    Finally, by chaining together appropriate elements $XX$, $YY$, and $ZZ$, we can make any Pauli string with $k_x+k_y = 2l$ Pauli-$X$ or Pauli-$Y$ matrices, and identity or Pauli-$Z$ matrices on the remaining $n-2l$ components. The only element we cannot make is $Z^{\otimes n}$, as this operator commutes with all the generators.

    The dimension of the generated algebra can be found by summing over an index $k$, which counts the number of $X$ and $Y$ components in the string. For a given $l$, there are $2^{2l}$ options for the $X$ and $Y$, $2^{n-2l}$ options of the remaining $I$ or $Z$, and $\binom{n}{2l}$ options to arrange the $X$ and $Y$ in between the $I$ and $Z$. This gives the total number of options as
    \begin{equation}
        \sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor } 2^{2l} 2^{n-2l} \binom{n}{2l} = 2^n 2^{n-1} = \frac12 4^n,
    \end{equation}
    where we have to subtract 2 for the 2 Pauli strings $I^{\otimes n}$ and $Z^{\otimes n}$ that we can not make to find the dimension of the generated subspace.

    For the last extension with both $X_1$ and $Z_1 Z_2$, the addition of the $X_1$ onto the extension of $Z_1 Z_2$ means that one can now also drop the requirement $k_x \equiv k_y \mod{2}$, and, furthermore, one can also make $Z^{\otimes n}$.

\label{thm:commutators_algorithmically}
    In the settings of Theorem \ref{thm:XdoubleZsingle_extensions}, any Pauli string can be written as a chain of commutators using the generators
    \begin{equation}

<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
We provide an explicit algorithm, which we build using three local subroutines. Each subroutine starts with a specific type of Pauli string and applies a sequence of commutators with elements of the generator set to change the Pauli string. We will denote $W_{\leq k}$ for an arbitrary Pauli string on indices $1,2,\ldots, k$ and $W_{\geq k}$ for an arbitrary Pauli string on indices $k, k+1, \ldots, n$. The identity operator $I$ acts on any indices not mentioned.

The three routines are
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    1)&& \quad W_{\leq k-1} Z_k I_{k+1} W_{\geq k+2} &\to W_{\leq k-1} I_k Z_{k+1} W_{\geq k+2}, \\
    2)&& \quad Z_1 I_2 W_{\geq 3} & \to Z_1 Z_2 W_{\geq 3}, \\
    3) &&\quad Z_1 W_{\geq k+1} & \to Z_1 X_k W_{\geq k+1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The first subroutine interchanges a $Z$ operator in a Pauli chain with a neighbouring $I$ operator and the second subroutine adds an extra $Z$ operator at the beginning of the Pauli string. These two subroutines together allow us to create any Pauli string consisting of only $I$ and $Z$. The third subroutine makes a $X$ or $Y$ at position $k$ without touching any of the elements past index $k$.

These three subroutines together allow us to make any Pauli string by starting with $Z_1$ and then creating any Pauli string iteratively from highest to lowest index. If the Pauli string has leading $I$'s, we can get rid of the final $Z_1$ as well by either simply shifting it over with the first subroutine if the leading non-identity $\alpha_k = Z$ or using a slightly modified version of the third subroutine that still starts with $Z_1$ but makes $X_k$ instead of $Z_1 X_k$. If there are no leading $I$'s, the string on the first two indices $\alpha_1 \alpha_2$ can be fixed using commutators with $X_1, Z_1, Z_2, X_1 X_2,$ and $Z_1 Z_2$, as these operators fully control the first two qubits.

The first subroutine starts with $W_{\leq k-1} Z_k I_{k+1} W_{\geq k+2}$. Taking the commutators
\begin{equation}
    Z_k I_{k+1} \stackrel{X_k X_{k+1}}{\longleftrightarrow} Y_k X_{k+1} \stackrel{Z_k}{\longleftrightarrow} X_k X_{k+1} \stackrel{Z_{k+1}}{\longleftrightarrow}  X_k Y_{k+1} \stackrel{X_k X_{k+1}}{\longleftrightarrow} I_k Z_{k+1},
\end{equation}
the subroutine shifts one $Z$ one index at the cost of 4 commutations, which is the same as adding 4 to the length function $l$ defined in Definition \ref{def:length_function}.

The second subroutine starts with $Z_1 I_2 W_{\geq 3}$ and uses a sequence of commutations
\begin{equation}
    Z_1 I_2 W_{\geq 3} \stackrel{X_1}{\longleftrightarrow} Y_1 I_2 W_{\ge 3} \stackrel{Z_1 Z_2}{\longleftrightarrow} X_1 Z_2 W_{\ge 3} \stackrel{Z_1}{\longleftrightarrow}Y_1 Z_2 W_{\ge 3} \stackrel{X_1}{\longleftrightarrow} Z_1 Z_2 W_{\geq 3}.
\end{equation}
to increase the number of $Z$ matrices in the Pauli chain by 1 at the cost of 4 commutations. Together with the first subroutine, this allows us to make any string consisting of only $I$ and $Z$. The cost of creating $Z_k$ instead of $I_k$ is 4 commutations to make an extra $Z$ at index 2, and $4(k-2)$ to shift the index over from $Z_2$ to $Z_k$, for a total of $4(k-1)$ commutations.

The third and final subroutine creates an element $Z_1 Y_k W_{\geq k+1}$ or $Z_1 X_k W_{\geq k+1}$ from $Z_1 W_{\geq k+1}$. 
The subroutine starts with $Z_1 I_2 Z_3 Z_4 \cdots Z_k W_{\geq k+1}$, which can be made from $Z_1 W_{\geq k+1}$ using the previous two subroutines for $\sum_{j=3}^k 4(k-1) = 2k(k-1)-4$ commutations. Then the subroutine adds $X$ to the second index via the commutators
\begin{equation}
    Z_1 I_2 Z_3 Z_4 \cdots Z_k W_{\geq k+1} \stackrel{X_1 X_2}{\longleftrightarrow} Y_1 X_2 Z_3 Z_4 \cdots Z_k W_{\geq k+1} \stackrel{X_1}{\longleftrightarrow} Z_1 X_2 Z_3 Z_4 \cdots Z_k W_{\geq k+1}.
\end{equation}
After that, the subroutine shifts the $X$ to the end of the chain of $Z$'s by eating up the $Z$ matrices. This is implemented by commuting with $X_j X_{j+1}$ and $Z_{j+1}$ for $j = 2, 3 \cdots k-1$ to get
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
        Z_1 X_2 Z_3 \cdots Z_kW_{\geq k+1}  &\stackrel{X_2 X_3}{\longleftrightarrow} Z_1 I_2 Y_3 \cdots Z_kW_{\geq k+1}  \stackrel{Z_3}{\longleftrightarrow} Z_1 I_2 X_3 Z_4 \cdots Z_kW_{\geq k+1} \\
        &\cdots  \\
        &\stackrel{X_{k-1} X_k}{\longleftrightarrow} Z_1 I_2 \cdots I_{k-1} Y_k W_{\geq k+1} \stackrel{Z_k}{\longleftrightarrow} Z_1 I_2 \cdots I_{k-1} X_k W_{\geq k+1}. 
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Note that applying the last $Z_k$ instead yields  $Z_1 Y_k W_{\geq k+1}$. In total, the cost of creating $Z_1 X_k W_{\geq k+1}$ from $Z_1 W_{\geq k+1}$ is $2k(k-1) - 4 + 2 + \sum_{j=2}^{k-1} 2 = 2k^2-4$ and the cost of creating $Z_1 Y_k W_{\geq k+1}$ from $Z_1 W_{\geq k+1}$ is $2k^2-5$.

This cost calculation does not change if the leading symbols are identities. In that scenario, we can use a modified version of the third subroutine, where we start with
\begin{equation}
    I_1 X_2 Z_3 Z_4 \cdots Z_k W_{\geq k+1}.
\end{equation}
Applying then the commutators with $X_j X_{j+1}$ and $Z_{j+1}$ for $ k = 2, \cdots, k-1$ results in
\begin{equation}
    I_1 I_2 \cdots I_{k-1} X_k W_{\geq k+1}.
\end{equation}

Note that this approach is not necessarily the most efficient one in how the commutators are applied at the first 2 indices, but gives a bound on the necessary number of commutators. The most expensive Pauli string under this algorithm consists of $X_1 X_2 \cdots X_n$ and costs
\begin{equation}
    2+\sum_{k=2}^n (2k^2-4) = \frac23 n^3 +O(n^2).
\end{equation}
See Appendix \ref{app:alg-computation} for a pseudocode writeup of the described approach.

The fourth subroutine can be set up in various ways. In the most straightforward setting, one can follow along with the proof of Corollary \ref{Ising} and use iterated commutators to create $X_k X_{k+1}$ from $J_{\gamma, \lambda}$. However, since this is done iteratively and uses $X_k X_{k+1}$ twice to create $X_{k+1} X_{k+2}$, the commutator cost of a single $X_k X_{k+1}$ becomes exponential in $k$.

For $\gamma \in \{-1,1\}$, one can create each element of $\mathcal{X}(2)$ and $\mathcal{Y}(2)$ directly by commuting with the appropriate $Z_i$. For $\gamma \notin \{-1,0,1\}$, one can instead create each $a_k \coloneqq (1+\gamma) X_k X_{k+1} + (1-\gamma) Y_k Y_{k+1}$ and the flipped version $a_k' \coloneqq (1-\gamma) X_k X_{k+1} + (1+\gamma) Y_k Y_{k+1}$ by 
    \begin{equation}
        a_k \stackrel{a_{k+1}}{\longrightarrow} X_k Z_{k+1} Y_{k+2} - Y_k Z_{k+1} X_{k+2} \stackrel{a_{k+1}}{\longrightarrow} (1-\gamma) X_k X_{k+1} + (1+\gamma) Y_k Y_{k+1} \eqqcolon a_k'.
    \end{equation}
    Now $a_k$ and $a_k'$ span the same space as $X_k X_{k+1}$ and $Y_k Y_{k+1}$, so one can set up the same polynomial algorithm but in a different basis build around the $a_k$ and $a_k'$.

\label{Lipschitz} Let us state the main inequalities for the exponential map. The upper estimate  
 \begin{equation}\label{up}
  \|e^{a}-e^{b}\|\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \|a-b\| (1+e^{\|a\|}e^{\|b\|})
  \end{equation} 
holds in Banach algebras. For the unitary group we may assume $u=e^{ia}$ with $\|a\|_{\infty}\le \pi$ and hence $(1+e^{2\pi})$ is a bound for the  Lipschitz constant. The lower estimate
 \[ \frac{\|e^{ia}-e^{ib}\|}{\|a-b\|} \hspace{.1cm} \ge \hspace{.1cm} 0.4 \]
was proved in \cite[Lemma 4]{Szarek1998MetricSpaces} assuming that $\|a\|_{\infty} \le \frac{\pi}{4}, \|b\|\le \frac{\pi}{4}$, using the fact that  $\|e^{ia}-1\|
\le \|a\|$ for $\|a\|\le \pi$. This shows that in a fixed neighbourhood of the identity the exponential map is bi-Lipschitz for what might be called `full groups' such as $SO(n)$ and $SU(n)$. For general Lie subgroups $G\subset U(n)$ we only have the lower estimate 
\[ (\frac{c}{\varepsilon})^d \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} N(G_0,\varepsilon)  \hspace{.1cm}. \]
Unfortunately, we usually  need an upper bound for the local Lie groups.

\label{basic} Let $\varepsilon\le 1$. 
 \begin{enumerate}
  \item[i)] Let $K_1\subset K_2$ be a subset, then $N(K_1,\varepsilon)\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} N(K_2,\frac{\varepsilon}{2})$
  \item[ii)]  Let $g_n$ be the Lie algebra of $n$ commuting Pauli strings, whose Lie group is the torus $\mathbb{T}^n$. Then   \[ N(g_n ,\varepsilon) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} (\frac{C}{\varepsilon})^{2n} \hspace{.1cm}. \]
  \item[iii)] For every Hamiltonian $H\in {\mathbb M}_{2^n}$ and the one-parameter group $G_H=\{e^{itH}|t\in {\mathbb R}\}$,
   \[ N(G_H,\varepsilon) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} (\frac{C}{\varepsilon})^{2^{n+1}} \hspace{.1cm}. \]    
    \item[iv)] Let $g=g_1\oplus \cdots \oplus g_m$ be a Lie algebra with commuting sectors $[g_j,g_k]=0$. Then    
    \[ N(G,\varepsilon) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \prod_j N(G_j,\frac{\varepsilon}{m}) \hspace{.1cm}. \]
 \end{enumerate}

<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
i) is well-known \cite{PSV,KSV}. Indeed, for any $\varepsilon/2$ covering $S_2$ of the large set $K_2$ we define $S_1$ as the elements $s\in S_1$ such that for some $x_s\in K_1$ we have $d(s,x_s)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$.   \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \sum_{j=1}^m \|u_1\cdots u_{j-1}(u_j-v_j)v_{j+1}\cdots v_m\|
  \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \sum_{j=1}^m \|u_j-v_j\| \hspace{.1cm}. \]

<!-- prop-4: body_md -->
\label{finite} Let $H$ be a magic Hamiltonian with respect to $S_0$. Then $l_H(\varepsilon|S_0)$ is finite. Moreover, a finite set of times for which $H$ is evolved is sufficient.

<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Let us start with a basis $B=\{b_1,...,b_k\}$ of the Lie algebra of $\mathfrak{su}(2^n)$. We may define the norm 
 \[ \|\sum_j \alpha_j b_j\|_1  \hspace{.1cm} = \hspace{.1cm} \sum_j |\alpha_j|  \hspace{.1cm}. \]
Since we are dealing with a finite-dimensional real Banach space, we find a $\delta$-net $N({\rm ball}_1,\delta)\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} (C/\delta)^{m}$. Of course, this norm is equivalent to the operator norm, i.e. 
  \[ \frac{1}{c_1} \|a\|_{\infty} \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \|a\|_1 \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} c_2 \|a\|_{\infty} \hspace{.1cm} .\]
As usual, we consider the exponential map $\phi(a)=e^{a}$. The set 
\[ K=\{a| \|a\|_{\infty}\le \pi\}\subset \frac{1}{c_1}{\rm Ball}_1\hspace{.1cm}\]
is compact and contained in $C_2{\rm Ball}_{\|\hspace{.1cm}\|_1}$ and hence admits a $\delta$-net $S$ in the $\|\hspace{.1cm}\|_1$-norm of cardinality $(1+\frac{2c_1}{\delta})^{k}$. According to  Remark  \ref{Lipschitz}, we deduce that $\exp(S)$ is a $(1+2^{2\pi})\delta$-net for $G=U(2^n)$. We choose $(1+e^{2\pi})\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$. Since the set $S$ is finite it suffices to find an alternating approximation for every individual $s=\sum_j \alpha_j b_j$. First we apply Trotter's formula \cite{Suzuki1976}:
 \[ \|e^s-(\prod_{j=1}^k e^{\alpha_j b_j/N})^N\| \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \frac{2}{N} (\sum_{j=1}^k |\alpha_j| \|b_j\|)^2 \exp(2 \sum_{j=1}^m |\alpha_j| \|b_j\|) \hspace{.1cm} .\]
Let $C'=\max_j\|b_j\|$ and $\alpha=\frac{C'}{c_1}$. Then we can choose $N\hspace{.1cm} \ge \hspace{.1cm} \frac{8}{\varepsilon \alpha}$ and obtain an $\varepsilon/4$-approximation of  the exponential. Since $N$ and $k$ are now fixed, it suffices to find an approximation of $e^{\alpha_j b_j}$. This is exactly given by the concrete formula for the Chow-Rashevskii theorem \cite{Giannotti2024ProvingRashevskii} for some $l=l(\alpha_s,b_j)$, depending on the designed error $\frac{\varepsilon}{4Nk}$. Taking the supremum of $kN(s)l(\alpha_j,b_j)$ provides a total bound in terms of a given bracket-generating set. 
Recall that for a magic Hamiltonian $H$ the set $S_0\cup H$ is bracket-generating. Of course the total number of generators used in this approximation is an overcount of the actual alternating usage. However, in all our approximations only a finite number of terms $e^{it_jH}$ have been used. Collecting these $t_j$ in a set $T$ yields the last assertion.

 \begin{enumerate}
 \item[i)] If $H$ has integer spectrum, then there exists a constant $C_1(\varepsilon)$ such that 
  \[ \frac{4^n}{n} \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} C_1(\varepsilon) ~\max \{\ln (1+\|H\|),d(1+\ln a)\}~ l_H(\varepsilon) \hspace{.1cm} .\] 
 \item[ii)] If $H$ has rank $k$, then 
   \[ \frac{4^n}{n} \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} C_2(\varepsilon) ~\max \{k(1+\ln k),d(1+\ln a) \}~ l_H(\varepsilon) \hspace{.1cm} .\] 
 \item[iii)] For arbitrary $H$

\end{enumerate} 
Here $C_j(\varepsilon)$ are absolute constants.

Let $\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2c}$, where $c$ is the constant of Szarek's entropy result. Then we can find $m=m(\frac{\varepsilon}{2})$ such that for all $u \in U(2^n)$, there exist $u_{2j+1} \in G_0$ and $u_{2j} \in \{ e^{it H} ~:~ t \in \mathbb{R} \}$ such that
\[ 
    \|u-u_1\cdots u_{2m+1}\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2} \hspace{.1cm}
\] 
holds.
For every $u_{2j+1}$ we can find some $\hat{u}_{2j+1}\in S$ in a set of cardinality $|S|\le N(G_0,\frac{\varepsilon}{2m})$ such that 
 \[ \|u_{2j+1}-\hat{u}_{2j+1}\|\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \frac{\varepsilon}{2(2m+1)} \hspace{.1cm} .\] 
Similarly, we can find a subset $\tilde{S}\subset {\mathbb R}$ such that $\|u_{2j}-e^{isH}\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2(2m+1)}$ for $s\in \tilde{S}$ and $|\tilde{S}|\le N(G_H,\frac{\varepsilon}{2(2m+1)})$.

 \[ \|u-\hat{u}_1\cdots \hat{u}_{2m+1}\|< \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{2m+1}\|u_{j}-\hat{u}_j\| \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \varepsilon \hspace{.1cm} .\] 
 This implies
\[ 
    \Big(\frac{c}{\varepsilon} \Big)^{2^{2n}-1} \leq N(U(2^n),\varepsilon)\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} N\Big(G_0,\frac{\varepsilon}{2(2m+1)} \Big)^{m+1}  N \Big(G_H,\frac{\varepsilon}{2(2m+1)} \Big)^{m}.
\]
Now we take the logarithm and deduce that  
 \begin{align*}
     (2^{2n}-1)\ln \frac{c}{\varepsilon} & \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} (m+1)\ln  N\big(G_0,\frac{\varepsilon}{2(2m+1)} \big)+m \ln  N\big(G_H,\frac{\varepsilon}{2(2m+1)} \big) \\

     &\le (2m+1) \max \Big\{ d \ln  \frac{Ca}{\varepsilon} + d \ln (2m+1),~ \ln N \big(G_H,\frac{\varepsilon}{2(2m+1)} \big) \Big\}. 
     \end{align*}
Now, we use the different assumptions for the estimate of $N(G_H)$. For arbitrary $H$, we have by Lemma \ref{lem:covering_exp(tH)}iii) that  
\[ 
    \ln N(G_H,\frac{\varepsilon}{2(m+1)}) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} 2^{n+1} \big(\ln \frac{C}{\varepsilon}+\ln (2m+1) \big)  \hspace{.1cm} .

This leaves us with four cases:

      \item[iv)] $(2^{2n}-1)\ln \frac{c}{\varepsilon}\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} 4  \cdot 2^{n+1} (2m+1)\ln (2m+1)$.
      \end{enumerate}
We use Lemma \ref{ylny} and deduce the assertion.  If $H$ has rank $k$, we have
 \[ \ln N(G_H,\frac{\varepsilon}{2(m+1)}) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm}  k \ln (1+\frac{Ck(2m+1)}{\varepsilon}) 
 \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} k (1+\ln \frac{Ck}{\varepsilon})(1+\ln (2m+1)) \hspace{.1cm}. \]
This means the cases iii) and iv) are replaced by better estimates and yield ii). If we have integer spectrum, we get 
 \[ \ln N(G_H)\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm}  \ln 4+ \ln \frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\ln (2\pi +\|H\|) \hspace{.1cm} .\]
 Replacing again iii) and iv) by better alternatives, we deduce the assertion.

<!-- prop-5: body_md -->
\label{mp} Either $l_H(\frac{\varepsilon}{2p})=0$, or 
 \[ l_H(\varepsilon)^{\frac{1-p}{p}} \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \frac{p}{1-p} \frac{Cl_H^p(\varepsilon/2)\|H\|}{\varepsilon}  \hspace{.1cm}, \]
 holds for an absolute constant $C$.
<!-- end prop-5 -->
<!-- proof-26: body_md -->
Let us assume that for every $u$ we find an approximation as above with $m_p(\varepsilon/4)\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} M$. Let $t_j^*$ be the largest $t_j$ that occurs. Let us choose a subset $I$ such that  
 \[ \sum_{j\notin I} |t_j|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2\|H\|}. \] 
By replacing all the $e^{t_jH}$ with $j\notin I$ by $1$, we find
 \[ \|u_1\cdots u_{2m+1}-\hat{u_1}\cdots \hat{u}_{2m+1}\|
 \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \sum_{j\in I} \|u_{2j}-\hat{u}_{2j}\| \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \hspace{.1cm} .\] 
Since we are looking at an alternating product, for $j\notin I$ we see that $u_{2j-1}u_{2j+1}\in G$. Therefore, we effectively have only $j\in I$ alternating elements. 
 \[ l_{H}(\varepsilon) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \sup_u |I(u)| \hspace{.1cm} .\] 
Now assume that $p<1$ and $l_{H}^p(\varepsilon/2)<
\infty$. Then
 \[ j^{1/p}t_j^* \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \|t\|_p \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} l_{H}^p(\varepsilon/2)  \] 
holds for the non-increasing rearrangement. Then note that 
 \[ \sum_{j\hspace{.1cm} \ge \hspace{.1cm} k} t_j^* \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm}   l_{H}^p(\varepsilon/2) \sum_{j>k} j^{-1/p} 
 \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \frac{C}{\frac{1}{p}-1}  l_{H}^p(\varepsilon/2) k^{1-1/p} \hspace{.1cm} .\] 
We deduce that for $k^{1/p-1}(1/p-1)\hspace{.1cm} \ge \hspace{.1cm} \frac{2\|H\| l_H^p(\frac{\varepsilon}{2})}{\varepsilon}\hspace{.1cm} \ge \hspace{.1cm} (k-1)^{1/p-1}(1/p-1)$ we have
 \[ l_H(\varepsilon) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} k-1 \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm}  \Big(\frac{p}{1-p}\Big)^{\frac{p}{1-p}} \bigg(\frac{2\|H\|l_H^p(\varepsilon/2)}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{1-p}}  \hspace{.1cm} . \]    
Taking this to the power $\frac{1-p}{p}$ yields the assertion. If we find no such $k$, then $\frac{2\|H\|l_H^p(\frac{\varepsilon}{2})}{\varepsilon}\le 1/p-1$. This means
 \[ l_H^1(\varepsilon/2) \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} \frac{\varepsilon}{2\|H\|}(1/p-1), \]
and we can approximate every $u$ with an element in $G_0$ of distance $\frac{\varepsilon}{2p}$.

<!-- theorem-8: body_md -->
\label{thm:exponential_m} For all magic Hamiltonians in Theorem \ref{themagic}, namely cases 1), 2), 4), 5), 7), 8), 9), the alternating length is exponential.
<!-- end theorem-8 -->
<!-- proof-27: body_md -->
For 1), 2) we use the Jordan-Wigner transform; more precisely, Lemma \ref{covspin} iii) for $m=2n$ or $m=2(n+1)$. This leads to a constant $n\ln n$ coming from local  Lie group $G_0$ generated by $S_0$. On the other hand, the Hamiltonian can be considered in ${\mathbb M}_4$ embedding isometrically into ${\mathbb M}_{2^n}$. Therefore, we obtain a lower bound $l_H(\varepsilon)\hspace{.1cm} \ge \hspace{.1cm} c(\varepsilon) \frac{4^n}{n^2\ln n}$. 

For 4) and 5) we have a very small local Lie group $G_0$, either contained in $\mathbb{T}^n$ or $SU(4)\times \mathbb{T}^{n-2}$. The Hamiltonian has integer spectrum and $\|H\|\le n$, hence $l_H(\varepsilon)\hspace{.1cm} \ge \hspace{.1cm} c_{\varepsilon}\frac{4^n}{n}$. 

In 8) and 9) we have again small local Lie algebras. Indeed, we can work with the Lie algebra $g_{0,1}=\langle \mathfrak{su}(2),Z_1Z_2\rangle$ which commutes with $g_{0,2}=\langle Z_{2}Z_3,...,Z_{n-1}Z_n\rangle$. Therefore the Lie group is contained in the product 
 \[ G_{0,1}\times G_{0,2} \subset SU(4)\times \mathbb{T}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor} \hspace{.1cm}. \]
This will give a term of order $n$. However, since we have no additional benefit for the Hamiltonian, we deduce a lower bound $c_{\varepsilon}\frac{2^n}{n}$. 

In number 7) we split the generators into two commuting sets
\[  S_{lloc}=\{X_1,Z_1,X_1X_2X_3X_4, Z_1Z_2Z_3Z_4,Z_1Z_2,Z_2Z_3,Z_4Z_5\}  , 
T=\{Z_5Z_6,Z_6Z_7,...,Z_{n-1}Z_n\}\hspace{.1cm} .\] 
In our PauLie map we can replace $Z_5Z_6$ by $Z_6$. In the new picture, we find a product group.  $SU(2^5)\times \mathbb{T}^{n-6}$ and ransport the covering number estimates back. Therefore we can combine Proposition \ref{covspin}ii) and iv) with Szareks results Theorem  \ref{Szarek} and find 
 \[ N(G,\varepsilon)\hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} (\frac{C}{\varepsilon})^{2^{5+1}+2(n-6)} \hspace{.1cm}. \]
We use Lemma \ref{basic} and obtain $a=1$ and $d=54+2n$. Again, we have to accept the general Hamiltonian estimate and get $c(\varepsilon)\frac{2^n}{n}$ as a lower bound.

<!-- rem-7: body_md -->
The case 5) with $H=\sum_j X_jX_{j+1}$, i.e. $\gamma=1$, $\lambda=0$ is still accessible with our methods. Indeed for even $n$ we generate a copy of $G=SU(2^{n/2})\times SU(2^{n/2})$. Szarek's argument is easily seen to remain valid for a product of two groups. Therefore, we can use 
 \[ (\frac{c}{\varepsilon})^{2(4^{n/2}-1)} \hspace{.1cm} \le \hspace{.1cm} N(G,\varepsilon) \]
in the proof of Theorem \ref{entropy}. The good news is that we have $a=1$ and $d=n/2$ and $\|H\|=n$ for our local estimate and Hamiltonian. Thus, we get a lower bound of $l_H(\varepsilon)\hspace{.1cm} \ge \hspace{.1cm} c(\varepsilon) \frac{2^n}{n}$ to approximate every unitary in the smaller group $G$ (not $SU(2^n)$). We note that the difference between 5) and 6) corresponds exactly to the predicted phase transition at $\gamma=1$ fixed  and  $\lambda=0$ versus $\lambda\neq 0$ from operator algebra theory, see Appendix \ref{app:operator_algebra}. 
On the other hand 7) and 8) correspond to phase transitions in both parameters.

<!-- rem-8: body_md -->
It remains completely open whether these lower bounds are sharp. An upper bound for $e^{tV}$, with $V$ is a Pauli string, can be obtained by applying our algorithmic formula for iterated commutators together with Rashevskii's technique. Even for small $t^{1/k}$, the number of terms in these formula grows as $l_{j+1}=2+2l_j$ and hence at least exponentially ($2^k$) in the number $k$ of iterated commutators. This can then be combined with Trotterization, see \ref{finite}.  This method will require at least $2^{n^3}$ many H\"ormander generators and the algorithm will tell us how many of them are from the magic Hamiltonian.
