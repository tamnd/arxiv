\label{thm:q-der}
Assume that $\mbox{$\textup{E}$}[(D^{(k)}h(u,X))^m]<\infty$ for all $u\in\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$}$, all $k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}$ and $m=1,2$. Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ and assume that $(u,q_{\alpha}(u))$ is a regular value of $\pi$. 
Then the gradient is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{e:grad}
 \frac{\delta q_{\alpha}}{\delta u}
 = \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{q_{\alpha}(u)}\Big[\frac{\delta h}{\delta u}\Big]
\end{equation}
and the Hessian is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{e:Hess_q}
 \frac{\delta^2 q_{\alpha}}{\delta u^2}
 = 
 \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{q_{\alpha}(u)}\Big[\frac{\delta^2 h}{\delta u^2} \Big]
 - 
 f_Y(q_{\alpha}(u),u)^{-1}\,
 \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big|_{y = q_{\alpha}(u)}\,
 \Big(
 f_Y(y,u)\mbox{$\textup{Var}$}_y\Big[
  \frac{\delta h}{\delta u}
 \Big] 
 \Big)
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Ad \eqref{e:grad}. 
Since $f_Y$ is smooth, so is the distribution function $F_Y$. Therefore, smoothness of $q_{\alpha}$ follows from the implicit function theorem, and moreover differentiating $F_Y(q_{\alpha}(u),u) = \alpha$ with respect to $u$ yields
\begin{equation}
\label{e:impl_der}
 f_Y\frac{\delta q_{\alpha}}{\delta u} + \frac{\delta F_Y}{\delta u} 
 = 0
\end{equation}
In order to compute $\delta F_Y / \delta u$ we observe first that
\begin{equation} \label{e:Heaviside}
    F_Y(y, u) = \mbox{$\textup{E}$}[ H(y - h(u, X)) ],
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the Heaviside function given by $H(t) = 1$ for $t > 0$ and $H(t) = 0$ otherwise. 
To differentiate this expression, we fix a mollifier $\rho\ge 0$. That is,  $\rho\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})$ with compact support $[0, 1]$, and $\int_{\mathbb{R}} \rho = 1$. 
Let $\rho_{\varepsilon}(t) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \rho(\frac{t}{\varepsilon})$ and set
\begin{equation}
    H_{\varepsilon}(t)
    := \int_{-\infty}^t \rho_{\varepsilon}(s) \,ds
\end{equation}
Define the mollified distribution function by
\begin{equation}
    F_Y^{\varepsilon}(y, u) 
    = \mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[ H_{\varepsilon}(y - h(u,X)) \Big]
    = \Big(\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast F_Y(.,u) \Big) (y) .
\end{equation}
Note that $F_Y^{\varepsilon}\to F_Y$ in $C^k_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$})$ for all $k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$. Now, 
\begin{align}
\notag 
 \frac{\delta F_Y^{\varepsilon}}{\delta u}(y,u)
 &= 
 -\mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[
   H_{\varepsilon}'\Big( y-h(u,X) \Big)\frac{\delta h}{\delta u}(u, X)
 \Big]
 \\
 \label{equ2}
 &= 
 -\mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[ 
  \rho_{\varepsilon}\Big(
   y - h(u,X)
  \Big) \frac{\delta h}{\delta u}(u, X)
 \Big] \\
 \notag 
 &= 
 -\mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[ 
  \rho_{\varepsilon}\Big(
   y - h(u,X)
  \Big) 
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u}(u, .)\Big| h(u,X) \Big]
 \Big]
 \\ 
 \notag 
 &=
 -
 \int_{\mathbb{R}} 
 \rho_{\varepsilon}\Big(
   y - z
  \Big) 
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,z)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u}(u, .)\Big]
  f_Y(z,u)\,dz
  \\
  \notag 
  &= 
  \Big(
  \rho_{\varepsilon} \ast
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,.)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u}\Big]
  f_Y(.,u)
  \Big)(y)
\end{align}
implies that 
\begin{equation}
 \label{e:magic1}
 \frac{\delta F_Y}{\delta u}(y,u) 
 = -f_Y(y,u) \, \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u}\Big]
\end{equation}
whence \eqref{e:impl_der} yields the gradient formula \eqref{e:grad}.

Ad \eqref{e:Hess_q}. 
Let $u_i$ be the $i$-th component of $u$. Differentiating \eqref{e:impl_der} again yields, componentwise,  
\begin{equation}
\label{e:impl_der2}
 \Big(
  \frac{\partial f_Y}{\partial y}\frac{\delta q_{\alpha}}{\delta u_j}
  +
  \frac{\delta f_Y}{\delta u_j}
 \Big)\frac{\delta q_{\alpha}}{\delta u_i}
 + f_Y \frac{\delta^2 q_{\alpha}}{\delta u_j \delta u_i}
 + \frac{\delta f_Y}{\delta u_i}\frac{\delta q_{\alpha}}{\delta u_j}
 + \frac{\delta^2 F_Y}{\delta u_j \delta u_i}
 = 0.
\end{equation}
To evaluate the last term we differentiate again the mollified version \eqref{equ2}: 
\begin{align}
 \frac{\delta^2 F_Y^{\varepsilon}}{\delta u_j \delta u_i}
 &= 
 -\mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[
  -\rho_{\varepsilon}'\Big(
   y - h(u,X)
  \Big)\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} 
  +
  \rho_{\varepsilon}\Big(
   y - h(u,X)
  \Big)\frac{\delta^2 h}{\delta u_j \delta u_i}
  \Big]  
  \\
  \notag 
  &= 
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[
  \rho_{\varepsilon}'\Big(
   y - h(u,X)
  \Big)
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i}
    \Big| h(u,X) \Big]
  \Big]
  -
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[
  \rho_{\varepsilon}\Big(
   y - h(u,X)
  \Big)
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}\Big[ \frac{\delta^2 h}{\delta u_j \delta u_i} 
    \Big| h(u,X) \Big]
  \Big]  
  \\
  \notag 
  &= 
  \Big( 
   \rho_{\varepsilon}'\ast  
   \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,.)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i}
    \Big] f_Y(.,u)
  \Big) (y)
  -
  \Big(
   \rho_{\varepsilon} \ast  
   \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,.)}\Big[ \frac{\delta^2 h}{\delta u_j \delta u_i}
    \Big] f_Y(.,u)
  \Big) (y)
\end{align}
and convergence in $C^k_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mbox{$\mathcal{U}$})$ implies
\begin{equation}
 \label{e:magic2}
 \frac{\delta^2 F_Y}{\delta u_j \delta u_i}
 = 
 \Big(\partial_y f_Y\Big) \, 
 \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ 
    \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i}
 \Big]
 + 
 f_Y\,
 \partial_y 
 \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ 
    \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i}
 \Big]
 - f_Y\,  
   \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta^2 h}{\delta u_j \delta u_i}
    \Big]
\end{equation}
Now we note that 
                              
$ 
\partial_y \mbox{$\textup{Cov}$}_{(u,y)}[ 
  \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}, \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i}
 ] 
 = 
  \partial_y \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}[ 
  \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j} \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} ] 
 -
  \partial_y 
  (\mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j} ]
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} ] 
 )
               
$.
From \eqref{e:impl_der2} with \eqref{e:grad}, \eqref{e:magic1} and \eqref{e:magic2} we thus get
\begin{align} 
 -f_Y\frac{\delta^2 q_{\alpha}}{\delta u_j \delta u_i}
 &= 
 \Big( 
  (\partial_y f_Y) \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j} \Big]
  + 
  \frac{\delta f_Y}{\delta u_j}
 \Big) \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} \Big] 
    
         
               
 + 
 \frac{\delta f_Y}{\delta u_i}\mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j} \Big]
 \\
 \notag 
 &\phantom{==}
 +
 (\partial_y f_Y)\Big( 
  \mbox{$\textup{Cov}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}, \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} \Big]
  + 
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j} \Big] 
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} \Big] 
 \Big)
  \\
 \notag 
 &\phantom{==}
 +
 f_Y\Big( 
  \partial_y\mbox{$\textup{Cov}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}, \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} \Big]
  + 
  \partial_y\Big( \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j} \Big] 
  \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} \Big] 
  \Big)
 \Big)
   \\
 \notag 
 &\phantom{==}
 -
 f_Y\,
   \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta^2 h}{\delta u_j \delta u_i} \Big]  
 \\
 \notag 
 &=
  (\partial_y f_Y) \,
  \mbox{$\textup{Cov}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}, \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} \Big]
  + 
  f_Y \,
  \partial_y\mbox{$\textup{Cov}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[\frac{\delta h}{\delta u_j}, \frac{\delta h}{\delta u_i} \Big]
   -
 f_Y\,
   \mbox{$\textup{E}$}_{(u,y)}\Big[ \frac{\delta^2 h}{\delta u_j \delta u_i} \Big]  
\end{align}
as desired.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:bc_sol}
Assume that $b_i>0$ for all $i=1,\dots,m+1$. Then there are $y_0$ and $y_1$ such that there exists a solution $x^*(y)$ to  \eqref{e:CPequ_m} for all $0<y\le y_0$ and all $y_1\le y<\infty$. Moreover, $x^*(y)$ depends smoothly on $y$, and
\[
 \lim_{y\to0} x^*(y)
 = x^*
 = \lim_{y\to\infty} x^*(y),
 \qquad
 x^* = \Big( \frac{b_i}{u_i\beta} \Big)_{i=1}^{m+1}.
\]
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let ${\tau}>0$ such that ${\tau} < \frac{b_i}{u_i\beta}$ for all $i=1,\dots,m+1$. Define $\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau} = \{x \in \mathcal{S}_1: x_i \ge {\tau}\}$. Let $\rho: \mathcal{S}_1^{\tau}\to \mathbb{R}^m$ denote the function defined by the right hand side of \eqref{e:CPequ_m}. This function is smooth and bounded by construction.  Thus \eqref{e:CPequ_m} is equivalent to 
\begin{equation}
 b_i - \sum_{j=1}^m M_{ij} x_j
 =
 b_i\Big(1-\sum_{k=1}^mu_k x_k\Big) - b_{m+1}u_i x_i
 = \frac{\rho_i(x)}{\log(y)}
\end{equation}
where the invertible matrix $M$ is defined by the first equation.
Hence \eqref{e:CPequ_m} is equivalent to the fixed point problem $x=\Psi_y(x)$ with parameter $y$ where 
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_y(x_{(m)}) = M^{-1} b_{(m)} - \frac{1}{\log(y)}M^{-1}\rho(x_{(m)})
\end{equation}
with $b_{(m)} = (b_1,\dots,b_m)$ and $x_{(m)}\in\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau}$.  By choosing $y_0$ sufficiently small (resp.\ $y_1$ sufficiently large), it follows that the non-constant part of $\Psi_y$ tends to $0$ uniformly in $x_{(m)}\in\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau}$. This implies that $\Psi_y: \mathcal{S}_1^m\to\mathcal{S}_1^m$ is a contraction with uniform and smooth dependency on $y$. By the uniform contraction principle there is therefore a solution $x_{(m)}(y) = \Psi_y(x_{(m)}(y))$, and the solution depends smoothly on $y$.  

Moreover, $M^{-1}b_{(m)} = (\frac{b_i}{u_i\beta})_{i=1}^{m}$ and since $u_{m+1}x^*_{m+1} = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^m b_k/\beta = b_{m+1}/\beta$ the claim follows.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
Since $\beta>0$ by positive definiteness of $B$, at least one $b_i$ has to satisfy $b_i>0$. If there is now a $b_j\le0$ then the construction in the proof above shows that the assumption of a fixed ${\tau}>0$ and a critical point solution $x_{(m)}(y)\in\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau}$ for $y\to0$ or $y\to\infty$ leads to a contradiction. Hence when there is at least one $b_j\le0$ critical point solutions are asymptotically confined to the boundary layer of $S_1$.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
(Corner solutions)\label{prop:corn_sol}
Let $m=1$ and $b_1>0$. The following are true. 
\begin{enumerate}
\item 
There is a $y_2>0$ and a critical point solution $x_1^*(y)$ of \eqref{e:CPequ2} which depends smoothly on $y>y_2$. Moreover, $x_1^*(y)\to0$ as $y\to\infty$. 
\item 
If $b_2>0$, then there is a $y_1>0$ and a critical point solution $x_1^*(y)$ of \eqref{e:CPequ2} which depends smoothly on $y>y_1$. Moreover, $x_1^*(y)\to u_1^{-1}$ as $y\to\infty$. 
\item 
If $b_2 < 0$, then there is a $y_0>0$ and a critical point solution $x_1^*(y)$ of \eqref{e:CPequ2} which depends smoothly on $y<y_0$. Moreover, $x_1^*(y)\to u_1^{-1}$ as $y\to 0$. 
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Item (1). 
Let $u_1^{-1} > {\tau} > 0$ and define $\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau} = \{x\in\mathcal{S}_1: x\le {\tau}\}$. 
We transform \eqref{e:CPequ2} to a fixed point equation $x = \Psi_y(x)$ for $x\in\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau}$, where $\Psi_y(x) = \exp(M_y(x))$ and
\begin{align}
 M_y(x)
 = 
  \Big( B_{11} - b_1 u_1 x \Big)^{-1}
  \Big(&
    (B\mu)_1 - 1 + B_{12}\log(u_2)
    - \Big( (B\mu)_1 + (B\mu)_2 - 2 + b_2\log(u_2) \Big) u_1 x_1 \\
    \notag
    &- \Big(B_{12} - b_2 u_1 x_1 \Big) \log(1 - u_1 x_1)
      - \log(y)\Big(b_1 - \beta u_1 x_1\Big)  
  \Big)
\end{align}
Since $|\Psi_y(z_2) - \Psi_y(z_1)| \le m(y) |z_2-z_1|$ with $m(y) = \exp(\max_{z\in\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau}}M_y(z))|\max_{z\in\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau}}M_y'(z)| \to 0$ as $y\to\infty$ it follows that $\Psi_y: \mathcal{S}_1^{\tau} \to \mathcal{S}_1^{\tau}$ is a contraction which is uniform and smooth in $y>y_2$ for a suitable $y_2$. Hence a fixed point solution $x^*(y) = \Psi_y(x^*(y))$ exists for $y>y_2$, and  $x^*(y)$ is smooth in $y$.  Moreover, $x^*(y) = \Psi_y(x^*(y))\to 0$ as $y\to\infty$. 

Items (2) and (3) follow analogously by restricting to $\mathcal{S}_1^{\tau} = \{x\in\mathcal{S}_1: x \ge {\tau}\}$.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:conc}
The following are true. 
\begin{enumerate}
\item 
Assume $b_i > 0$ for all $i = 1,\dots,m$.
If $q_{\alpha_0}(u) < \eta\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}(\sum_j(B\mu)_j+1)\Big)$ for some $0 < \eta < \min\{u_1, \dots,u_{m+1}\}$,  then $q_{\alpha}$ is strictly concave at $u$ for all $\alpha\le\alpha_0$. 
\item 
Let $m=1$ and $b_2 < 0$. Then there exists an $\alpha_0 > 0$ such that $q_{\alpha}$ is concave in $u$ for all $\alpha\le\alpha_0$. 
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Ad Item (1).
Fix $0 < \eta < \min\{u_1, \dots,u_{m+1}\}$ and consider $\tilde{u} = u/\eta$. Since $h(x,\tilde{u}) = 1$ implies that $x_i<1$ it follows that $a|S_1(\tilde{u}) < 0$. By equation~\eqref{e:del_yVar} it follows that $q_{\alpha}$ is strictly concave at $\tilde{u}$ if $q_{\alpha_0}(\tilde{u}) < \exp\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}(\sum_j(B\mu)_j+1)\Big)$. Since $q_{\alpha_0}(\tilde{u}) = \eta^{-1}q_{\alpha}(u)$ the claim follows. 

Ad Item (2). See Section~\ref{sec:b_2_le_0}.

<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:losses_sa}
Consider losses $L_1 = X_1^0 - X_1$ and $L_2 = X_2^0 - X_2$. Then there is confidence level $\alpha_0\in(0,1)$ such that $q_{1-\alpha}$ is concave at $(1-\lambda)e_1 + \lambda e_2$ for all $\lambda\in[0,1]$ and all $\alpha_0\le\alpha<1$. Hence 
\[
 \mbox{$\textup{VaR}$}_{\alpha}[L_1 + L_2] \le  \mbox{$\textup{VaR}$}_{\alpha}[L_1] + \mbox{$\textup{VaR}$}_{\alpha}[L_2]  
\]
i.e., sub-additivity holds.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Notice firstly that strict concavity of $q_{1-\alpha}$ extends to concavity at the boundary of $\mathcal{C}$, hence we may consider strategies $U_1 = e_1$ and $U_2 = e_2$. Since the line segment connecting $U_1$ and $U_2$ is compact, we can find $\alpha_0<1$ such that concavity holds along the segment for all $\alpha_0\le\alpha<1$. Hence $q_{1-\alpha}((1-\lambda)U_1 + \lambda U_2) \ge (1-\lambda)q_{1-\alpha}(U_1) + \lambda q_{1-\alpha}(U_2)$ by concavity. With $\lambda = 1/2$ this yields $\mbox{$\textup{VaR}$}_{\alpha}[L_1 + L_2] = X_1^0+X_2^0+ q_{\alpha}(-e_1-e_2) \le X_1^0+X_2^0+ q_{\alpha}(-e_1) + q_{\alpha}(-e_2) = \mbox{$\textup{VaR}$}_{\alpha}[L_1] + \mbox{$\textup{VaR}$}_{\alpha}[L_2]$.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
For linear portfolios of elliptically distributed risk factors it is well-known that Value-at-Risk is sub-additive for $1/2\le\alpha<1$ (see \cite[Theorem~6.8]{McNeilFreyEmbrechts2015}). 
To the best of our knowledge, this corollary is a new instance of the phenomenon of $\mbox{$\textup{VaR}$}$-sub-additivity in the tail of losses.

<!-- remark-9: body_md -->
Equation~\eqref{e:univ_prod} separates scale and shape. 
The shape function $q_0$ is universal in the sense that it depends only on the covariance structure of the Gaussian log-factors, but not on the position in the (deep) tail and it forgets the mean value, $\mu$, of the log-factors.
