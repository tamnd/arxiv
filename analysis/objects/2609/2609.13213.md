Moreover, $0 < \phi_{r}'\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(x\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) \leq \frac{\lambda\rho}{4}$, $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}$. Let $L_{2} = \frac{\tau\varepsilon\lambda\rho}{4} > 0$, we use \eqref{ine}: $\forall \delta_{2} > 0$, $\epsilon_{x, t}\epsilon_{z, t} \leq \frac{1}{2\delta_{2}}\epsilon_{x, t}^{2} + \frac{\delta_{2}}{2}\epsilon_{z, t}^{2}$. Taking into account that $\tau\varepsilon\phi_{r}'\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\zeta_{x}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)$ is positive, we get
\begin{equation}
\tau\varepsilon\phi_{r}'\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\zeta_{x}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\epsilon_{x, t}\epsilon_{z, t} \leq \frac{\tau\varepsilon\phi_{r}'\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\zeta_{x}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)}{2\delta_{2}}\epsilon_{x, t}^{2} + \frac{\tau\varepsilon\phi_{r}'\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\zeta_{x}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\delta_{2}}{2}\epsilon_{z, t}^{2} \leq \frac{L_{2}}{2\delta_{2}}\epsilon_{x, t}^{2} + \frac{L_{2}\delta_{2}}{2}\epsilon_{z, t}^{2}.
\label{eqF}
\end{equation}

\item $G\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\xi_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)$: For this term, we use the global linear growth bounds and the inequality $\forall a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, $a^{2} \leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(a - b\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{2} + 2b^{2}$. Taking $\beta \coloneqq \max\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{1, \alpha\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\} > 0$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
G\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(\xi_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) \leq \beta K\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(1 + \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\|\xi_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\|^{2}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) \leq 2\beta K\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(1 + \epsilon_{x, t}^{2} + \epsilon_{y, t}^{2} + \epsilon_{z, t}^{2} + \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(x^{\ast}, y^{\ast}, z^{\ast}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\|^{2}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right).

\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\noindent Inserting the bounds \eqref{eqE}--\eqref{eqG} into the equation \eqref{LV1}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
LV \leq \tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\epsilon_{x, t}^{2} + \tilde{B}\epsilon_{y, t}^{2} + \tilde{C}\epsilon_{z, t}^{2} + \tilde{D},
\label{LV2}
\end{equation}
\noindent where now:

\begin{itemize}
\item $\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) = A\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) + \frac{L_{1}}{2\delta_{1}} + \frac{L_{2}}{2\delta_{2}} + 2\beta K$.

\item $\tilde{B} = B + 2\beta K$.

\item $\tilde{C} = C + \frac{L_{1}\delta_{1}}{2} + \frac{L_{2}\delta_{2}}{2} + 2\beta K$.

\item $\tilde{D} = 2\beta K + 2\beta K\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(x^{\ast}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{2} + 2\beta K\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(y^{\ast}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{2} + 2\beta K\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(z^{\ast}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{2}$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent We now study each term separately. Firstly, note that $\tilde{D}$ is always positive and only depends on $K$ and the equilibrium. On the other hand, $\tilde{B} < 0$ because $\tilde{B} = B + 2\beta K = - \frac{\tau b_{0}a_{1}}{a_{0}} + 2\beta K$ is always negative as $K < \frac{\tau b_{0}a_{1}}{2a_{0}\beta}$.

\noindent Also, we choose $\delta_{1}$ and $\delta_{2}$ such that $\tilde{C} < 0$, i.e., we choose for example $0 < \delta_{1} < \frac{2}{L_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(- C - \frac{L_{2}\delta_{2}}{2} - 2\beta K\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)$ and $0 < \delta_{2} < \frac{2}{L_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(- C - 2\beta K\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)$. Note that, $- C - 2\beta K = \frac{\tau\varepsilon}{\tau_{z}} - 2\beta K$ is always positive as $K < \frac{\tau\varepsilon}{2\tau_{z}\beta}$.

\noindent Finally, we study the parabola $\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)$. Let $\tilde{\delta}$ a positive value chosen to prevent division by zero and let $L_{3} = \max\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right), \tilde{\delta}\mathbb{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{\max\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\} = 0\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\}}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\} \geq 0$, where $\mathbb{I}$ is the indicator function. Note that if the parabola is always negative, $L_{3} = 0$ and otherwise, $L_{3}$ is the value of the parabola in its vertex or $\tilde{\delta}$ if the parabola in its vertex is equal to zero. Let $x^{-}$ and $x^{+}$ be the roots (or root) of the parabola (if they exist), with $x^{-} \leq x^{+}$, we choose $\delta_{3}$ a positive value, such that $\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(x^{-} - \delta_{3}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) = \tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(x^{+} + \delta_{3}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) = - L_{3}$. Then, we split the parabola as follows:
\begin{equation*}
\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right) = \tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{+} + \tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{-},
\end{equation*}
\noindent where
\begin{equation*}
\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{+} \coloneqq \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{\begin{array}{ll}

\begin{equation*}
\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{-} \coloneqq \mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{\begin{array}{ll}

\end{equation*}

\noindent Note that $\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{-}$ is always negative. In particular, $\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{-} \leq - L_{3} < 0$ if the parabola is not necessarily negative. Also, $\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{+}$ is always nonnegative and is bounded by $2L_{3}$. \noindent where,

\item $C_{2} = \max{\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{\max{\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left\{\tilde{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left(X_{t}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right)^{-}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\}}, \tilde{B}, \tilde{C}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right\}} < 0$.

\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}
\noindent thus concluding the proof.

\label{remark2}
Note that in the reasoning of the previous theorem, all terms can be controlled except for the parabolic one.

It may happen that the parabola has no real roots and remains always negative. From its expression, this occurs when the equilibrium lies sufficiently far from the fold curves $\Gamma^{-}$ or $\Gamma^{+}$ (in the $x^{\ast}$ component). In this case, the constant $C_{1}$ depends only on $K$. Moreover, in the deterministic setting, that is, when $K$ can be taken equal to zero, the obtained result shows that the equilibrium point is globally asymptotically stable.

In general, since the parabola depends on $x^{\ast}$, the distance between the equilibrium point and the fold curves can be interpreted as a measure of the stability of the point: as this distance increases, i.e., as $\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left|x^{\ast}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right|$ grows, the constant $C_{1}$ decreases, and consequently so does $C$. This reflects the fact that equilibria located deeper inside the stable sheets $S_{l}$ or $S_{r}$ exhibit stronger stability properties.

On the other hand, a similar behaviour is observed with respect to the constant $K$: the smaller the value of $K$, the smaller the constant appearing in condition \eqref{condi}. Since $K$ controls the intensity of the stochastic perturbation, this is consistent with the fact that, as the noise level decreases, the stochastic trajectories remain closer, on average, to the deterministic equilibrium.

As will be shown in the numerical section, even in the case where the deterministic system possesses a stable equilibrium point (for instance when it lies on $S_{l}$ or $S_{r}$), the stochastic solution may jump to $S_{m}$. \label{remfin}
As in Remark~\ref{remark2}, the ideal situation would be that, whenever the equilibrium point is stable in the deterministic setting, the constant in \eqref{condi6d} were equal to zero. As in the three-dimensional case, the estimate obtained here shows that the deviation with respect to the equilibrium is governed by the corresponding parabolic terms, which now depend on both $x_{1}^{\ast}$ and $x_{2}^{\ast}$.

As will be illustrated in the numerical section, in the regime of total oscillation death, where the deterministic solution converges globally to the equilibrium, the stochastic trajectories may still exhibit occasional jumps. However, consistently with Remark~\ref{remark2}, such events become less likely as $\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\mathopen{}\mathclose\bgroup\left|x_{i}^{\ast}\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\aftergroup\egroup\right|$, $i = 1, 2$, increases and as the noise intensity $K$ decreases.

We finally consider together the uncoupled case, the almost-in-phase synchronization regime and the in-phase locking synchronization regime. In the stochastic framework, the three cases tend to evolve towards in-phase behaviour. Moreover, the larger the value of $c$, the faster this synchronization is observed.

As in the three-dimensional case, when MMOs appear they are no longer periodic. This behaviour is consistent with the fact that both neurons have identical parameters, which are affected by the stochastic perturbations, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{F}.
