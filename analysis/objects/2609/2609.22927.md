<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:positive}
Let $L\subset\mathbb R_+^d$ be compact, and let $E_1,\ldots,E_d$ be independent
exponential variables of mean one. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive}
 \gamma_{2,2}(L,\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_\infty)^2
 \lesssim\mathbb E\sup_{\ell\in L}\sum_{i=1}^d\ell_i^2E_i.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Write $Q=\{(\ell_i^2)_i:\ell\in L\}$. Nonnegativity gives
\[
 |x-y|^2\le|x^2-y^2|\quad(x,y\ge0).
\]
Pulling partitions of $Q$ back to $L$ therefore yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:square-pullback}
 \gamma_{2,2}(L,\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_\infty)^2
 \le\gamma_1(Q,\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_\infty).
\end{equation}
Let $Z_i=\varepsilon_iE_i$, where the $\varepsilon_i$ are independent
Rademacher signs, also independent of the $E_i$. For $h\in\mathbb R^d$ and $t\ge1$,
conditional Jensen's inequality, applied after fixing $Z_i$, gives
\[
 \left\lVert \sum_jh_jZ_j\right\rVert_{L_t}\ge |h_i|\left\lVert Z_i\right\rVert_{L_t}
 \gtrsim t|h_i|.
\]
Consequently $\left\lVert \sum_jh_jZ_j\right\rVert_{L_t}\gtrsim t\left\lVert h\right\rVert_\infty$.
The exponential variables satisfy the regular moment assumptions of
\cite[Theorem~1]{LT}, with absolute parameters; variance normalization only
rescales by an absolute factor. That theorem bounds the partition functional
formed with the $L_{2^k}$ increment metrics by the expected range of the
process. It follows that
\[
 \gamma_1(Q,\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_\infty)
 \lesssim\mathbb E\sup_{q,q'\in Q}\sum_i(q_i-q'_i)Z_i
 =2\mathbb E\sup_{q\in Q}\sum_iq_iZ_i.
\]
Since $q_i\ge0$, this last supremum is at most
$\sup_{q\in Q}\sum_iq_iE_i$ pointwise. Together with
\eqref{eq:square-pullback}, this proves the claim. All processes here are
continuous on compact finite-dimensional sets, so the usual separability
convention causes no additional issue.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:trace}
With $R=\sup_{z\in K}\left\lVert T_z\right\rVert_\mathrm{HS}$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace}
 R^2\le4(D_r^2+D_s^2).
\end{equation}
Moreover $\sup_{z\in K}\left\lVert F(z)\right\rVert_2\le a_*:=\max_{i,j}a_{ij}\le R$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
For fixed $j$, H\"older's inequality gives
\[
 \sum_i a_{ij}^2u_i^{2-4/s}
 \le\left\lVert (a_{ij})_i\right\rVert_s^2\qquad(u\inB_{2,+}^{m}).
\]
At $s=2$ this is an equality independent of $u$, under the zero-power
convention; at $s=\infty$ it is the maximum bound. Since
$\sum_jv_j^{2\beta}\le1$, it follows that
$\sum_{i,j}U_{ij}(z)^2\le\alpha^2D_s^2$.
The transposed argument gives
$\sum_{i,j}V_{ij}(z)^2\le\beta^2D_r^2$.
These sums add to $\left\lVert T_z\right\rVert_\mathrm{HS}^2$, proving \eqref{eq:trace}.
Also
\[
 \left\lVert F(u,v)\right\rVert_2^2\le a_*^2
   \Big(\sum_i u_i^{2\alpha}\Big)
   \Big(\sum_j v_j^{2\beta}\Big)\le a_*^2.
\]
Evaluating a derivative at $u=e_i,v=e_j$ for a largest entry shows
$a_*\le R$.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:moments}
Uniformly for $r,s\in[2,\infty]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-bound}
 H_r\lesssim\sqrt{r\wedge\operatorname{Log} n}\,D_r+S^*,
 \qquad
 H_s\lesssim\sqrt{s\wedge\operatorname{Log} m}\,D_s+S^*.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
It suffices to prove the row estimate. First take $2\le t<\infty$ and put
$f_i(g)=\left\lVert (a_{ij}g_j)_j\right\rVert_t$, $b_i=\max_j a_{ij}$, and
$D_t^{\mathrm{row}}=\max_i\left\lVert (a_{ij})_j\right\rVert_t$.
Because $t\ge2$, $f_i$ is $b_i$-Lipschitz for Euclidean distance. Also
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mean-row}
 \mathbb E f_i\le(\mathbb E f_i^t)^{1/t}
 =\left\lVert g_1\right\rVert_{L_t}\left\lVert (a_{ij})_j\right\rVert_t
 \lesssim\sqrt t\,D_t^{\mathrm{row}}.
\end{equation}
Discard zero $b_i$ and reorder the remaining values decreasingly. For
independent standard Gaussians $\xi_i$, set
$B=\mathbb E\max_i b_i|\xi_i|$. The elementary lower bound for the maximum of
$k$ Gaussian absolute values gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ordered}
 B\gtrsim b_k\sqrt{\log(k+1)}\quad(k\ge1).
\end{equation}
Choosing one maximizing entry in each row gives independent entries of the
original array; hence $B\le S^*$.

Gaussian concentration and a union bound, which do not require independence
of the $f_i$, now give, for all sufficiently large absolute $x$,
\begin{align*}
 \mathbb P\left\{\max_i(f_i-\mathbb E f_i)>xB\right\}
 &\le\sum_i\exp\left(-\frac{x^2B^2}{2b_i^2}\right)\\
 &\le\sum_{i\ge1}(i+1)^{-c x^2}
 \le C e^{-c' x^2}.
\end{align*}
Integrating this tail and using \eqref{eq:mean-row} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unsaturated}
 \left\lVert \max_i f_i\right\rVert_{L_2}\lesssim\sqrt t\,D_t^{\mathrm{row}}+S^*.
\end{equation}

If $t\ge\operatorname{Log} n$, including $t=\infty$, then
\[
 \max_i\left\lVert (a_{ij}g_j)_j\right\rVert_t
 \le e\max_{i,j}a_{ij}|g_j|
 =e\max_j c_j|g_j|,\qquad c_j=\max_i a_{ij}.
\]
The last maximum has $L_2$ norm at most an absolute multiple of its mean.
For example, Gaussian concentration bounds its variance by
$(\max_jc_j)^2$, while its mean is at least
$\sqrt{2/\pi}\max_jc_j$. Choosing one entry in each column shows that this
mean is at most $S^*$. Thus the row maximum has $L_2$ norm at most $CS^*$
in this range.
Combining this observation with \eqref{eq:unsaturated} proves the first
inequality in \eqref{eq:H-bound}. Transposition proves the second.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:polar}
For $z=(u,v),z'=(u',v')\in K$,
\begin{align}\label{eq:profile-domination}
 \rho(z,z')\le{}&\alpha\left\lVert L(z)-L(z')\right\rVert_\infty
       +\beta\left\lVert C(z)-C(z')\right\rVert_\infty\notag\\
 &+2\alpha\beta\big(N_r(v-v')+N_s(u-u')\big).
\end{align}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
For vectors $x,y$ in any normed space and $a,b\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polar}
 \left\lVert ax-by\right\rVert\le
   |a\left\lVert x\right\rVert-b\left\lVert y\right\rVert|+2\min(a,b)\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $a\ge b$, split $ax-by=(a-b)x+b(x-y)$ and use the reverse
triangle inequality to bound $(a-b)\left\lVert x\right\rVert$ by
$|a\left\lVert x\right\rVert-b\left\lVert y\right\rVert|+b\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert$. The other case is symmetric.

For the straight segment $v_t=(1-t)v+tv'$ in $B_{2,+}^{n}$, H\"older gives
\[
 \left\lVert (a_{ij}h_jv_{t,j}^{\beta-1})_j\right\rVert_2
 \le\left\lVert (a_{ij}h_j)_j\right\rVert_r,
 \qquad \frac12=\frac1r+\frac{\beta-1}{2}.
\]
This includes $r=2$ and $r=\infty$ with the stated conventions. Integrating
the derivative along the segment gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:power-difference}
 \max_i\left\lVert (a_{ij}(v_j^\beta-v_j'^\beta))_j\right\rVert_2
 \le\beta N_r(v-v').
\end{equation}
Apply \eqref{eq:polar} to each row, with $a=u_i^{\alpha-1}$,
$b=u_i'^{\alpha-1}$, $x=(a_{ij}v_j^\beta)_j$, and
$y=(a_{ij}v_j'^\beta)_j$. Since $a,b\le1$, the row part of $\rho$ is
bounded by $\alpha\left\lVert L-L'\right\rVert_\infty+2\alpha\beta N_r(v-v')$.
The column argument gives the remaining terms.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:lengths}
One has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:length-chains}
 \gamma_{2,2}(L(K),\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_\infty)\lesssim H_s,
 \qquad
 \gamma_{2,2}(C(K),\left\lVert \cdot\right\rVert_\infty)\lesssim H_r.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
For fixed nonnegative $E_i$ and $v$, optimize over $u$ to obtain
\begin{align}
 \sup_{u\inB_{2,+}^{m}}\sum_iE_iL_i(u,v)^2
 &=\sup_{u\inB_{2,+}^{m}}
    \sum_i u_i^{2-4/s}E_i\sum_j a_{ij}^2v_j^{2\beta}\notag\\
 &=\left\lVert \left(E_i\sum_j a_{ij}^2v_j^{2\beta}\right)_i\right\rVert_{s/2}.
 \label{eq:opt-u}
\end{align}
At $s=2$ the last norm is $\ell_1$, and at $s=\infty$ it is $\ell_\infty$.
Minkowski's inequality and $\sum_jv_j^{2\beta}\le1$ show that the supremum
over $v$ equals
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coupled-sup}
 \sup_{u,v}\sum_iE_iL_i(u,v)^2
 =\max_j\left\lVert (E_i a_{ij}^2)_i\right\rVert_{s/2}
 =\max_j\left\lVert (a_{ij}\sqrt{E_i})_i\right\rVert_s^2.
\end{equation}
Equality is achieved by taking $v$ to be a maximizing coordinate vector.

Take independent standard Gaussian vectors $g,h$. The independent variables
$(g_i^2+h_i^2)/2$ have the exponential distribution of mean one. Pointwise,
\[
 \max_j\left\lVert (a_{ij}\sqrt{(g_i^2+h_i^2)/2})_i\right\rVert_s
 \le\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(
   \max_j\left\lVert (a_{ij}|g_i|)_i\right\rVert_s+
   \max_j\left\lVert (a_{ij}|h_i|)_i\right\rVert_s\right).
\]
The $L_2$ norm of the right side is at most $\sqrt2 H_s$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:positive} applied to \eqref{eq:coupled-sup} proves the
first assertion. The transposed calculation proves the second.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
In addition to the two chains from Lemma~\ref{lem:lengths},
\eqref{eq:ball-chain} gives
\[
 \gamma_{2,2}(B_{2,+}^{n},N_r)\lesssim\mathbb E N_r(g)\le H_r,
 \qquad
 \gamma_{2,2}(B_{2,+}^{m},N_s)\lesssim\mathbb E N_s(g)\le H_s.
\]
Choose nested admissible partitions for these four sets, within a fixed
factor of their infima. Pull them back to $K$ by $L,C,v,u$, respectively.
At level $k\ge2$, intersect their cells from level $k-2$. The intersection
partition has at most
\[
 \big(2^{2^{k-2}}\big)^4=2^{2^k}
\]
cells, and the sequence is nested. Use $\{K\}$ at the initial levels.
The inequality $(x_1+\cdots+x_4)^2\le4\sum_{a=1}^4x_a^2$, together with
\eqref{eq:profile-domination}, bounds the branchwise sum of squared
$\rho$-diameters by $C(H_r^2+H_s^2)$. The initial diameters are bounded by
the same four chain budgets. Lemma~\ref{lem:moments} completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:remainder}
For every $x,y,b\in K$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:remainder}
 \left\lVert F(x)-F(y)-T_b(x-y)\right\rVert_2
 \le2\big(\rho(x,b)+\rho(y,b)\big)\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_2.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
First suppose all endpoint coordinates are positive. Join $y$ to $x$ by
the coordinatewise geometric path $w(t)=x^t y^{1-t}$, $0\le t\le1$.
H\"older's inequality shows that this path stays in $K$. Every coordinate of
the path is monotone, and every entry of either derivative profile stays
between its endpoint entries.

Write $x=(u,v)$ and $y=(u',v')$. After subtracting $T_b(x-y)$, the absolute
value of the contribution to coordinate $(i,j)$ from variation of $u_i$ is
at most
\[
 |u_i-u_i'|\big(|U_{ij}(x)-U_{ij}(b)|+
                         |U_{ij}(y)-U_{ij}(b)|\big).
\]
Its Frobenius norm is bounded by $\left\lVert u-u'\right\rVert_2$ times the sum of the two
row-profile distances. The $v$ contribution is bounded by
$\left\lVert v-v'\right\rVert_2$ times the corresponding sum of column-profile distances.
The triangle inequality gives \eqref{eq:remainder}. General endpoints follow
by approximation by positive points in $K$, since $F,U,V$ are continuous
under our zero-power convention.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:blocks}
There is a rooted, admissible hierarchy of original indices with a
rank-one block $A_v:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^{mn}$ on each nonzero edge $v$ at level
$n(v)$, and nonnegative coefficients $c_v$, such that along the branch
$h(\theta)$ of every original index $\theta$,
\begin{align}
 \sum_{v\in h(\theta)}c_v&\le1,\label{eq:block-coeff}\\
 F_{\mathrm{full}}(\theta)&=
   \sum_{v\in h(\theta)}A_v\sqrt{c_v},\label{eq:block-image}\\
 \sup_h\sum_{v\in h}2^{n(v)}\left\lVert A_v\right\rVert_\mathrm{op}^2&\lesssim M^2.
 \label{eq:block-op}
\end{align}
Here $F_{\mathrm{full}}(x,y)=(a_{ij}x_i y_j)_{ij}$. The hierarchy can also
be chosen so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-trace}
 \sup_h\sum_{v\in h}\left\lVert A_v\right\rVert_\mathrm{HS}^2\lesssim R^2.
\end{equation}
The supremum may include limit branches of the tree.
<!-- end proposition-3 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Take the nested chain just described, and at each node choose a
representative from its original cell. Set the root representative to
zero, which belongs to $\mathcal T$. Write $z_k(\theta)$ for the
representative image at level $k$. The diameter bound gives convergence to
$F_{\mathrm{full}}(\theta)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:increments}
 \sup_\theta\sum_{k\ge1}2^{k/2}
    \left\lVert z_k(\theta)-z_{k-1}(\theta)\right\rVert_2\le B_0,
 \qquad B_0\lesssim M.
\end{equation}
Increase $B_0$ if necessary so that $B_0\ge R$. For a nonzero matrix,
choose an integer $q\ge1$ with
\[
 2^q\asymp(B_0/R)^2.
\]
Replace the initial representatives through level $q-1$ by zero, and retain
the original representatives from level $q$ onward. This amounts to
collapsing the initial levels of the hierarchy; admissibility is preserved.
Every point of $\mathcal T$ has Euclidean norm at most $a_*\le R$.
The new jump at level $q$ therefore has weighted size at most
$2^{q/2}R\lesssim B_0$. The remaining increments are unchanged. Thus the
new sequence has branchwise weighted increment sum at most $B\lesssim B_0$.

For a nonzero increment $\Delta_v$ on the edge $v$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-definition}
 c_v=\frac{2^{n(v)/2}\left\lVert \Delta_v\right\rVert_2}{B},
 \qquad
 A_v(t)=\frac{t\Delta_v}{\sqrt{c_v}}.
\end{equation}
For a zero increment set $c_v=0$ and $A_v=0$. The definition gives
\eqref{eq:block-coeff}, while telescoping gives \eqref{eq:block-image}.
Since these blocks have one-dimensional domains,
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{v\in h}2^{n(v)}\left\lVert A_v\right\rVert_\mathrm{op}^2
 &=B\sum_{v\in h}2^{n(v)/2}\left\lVert \Delta_v\right\rVert_2\le B^2,\\
 \sum_{v\in h}\left\lVert A_v\right\rVert_\mathrm{HS}^2
 &=B\sum_{v\in h}2^{-n(v)/2}\left\lVert \Delta_v\right\rVert_2
 \le2^{-q}B^2\lesssim R^2.
\end{align*}
All finite prefixes satisfy the same bounds, so they extend to limit
branches. The zero matrix has the trivial representation.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
The equality in \eqref{eq:block-image} is asserted for the prescribed
coefficient sequence on each original branch. Its range is exactly
$\mathcal T$. If one also includes every infinite branch of the finitely
branching tree, its representative images converge to a point of
$\mathcal T$, since the tail bound is uniform and $\mathcal T$ is compact.
Allowing arbitrary vectors in the unit ball of the direct sum of block
domains would generally enlarge the image; no such equality is used or
claimed here. The weighted budget is $CM^2$, which includes the Gaussian
maximum term and both exponent-dependent row and column terms.
