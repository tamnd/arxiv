<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:axially-symmetric}
{\rm A set $\Omega\subset\mathbb{H}$ is called\\
\noindent $(i)$ axially symmetric if $\{hqh^{-1}:\ h\in\mathbb{H}\}\subset \Omega$ for any $q\in \Omega$ and\\
\noindent $(ii)$ a slice domain (or $s$-domain for short) if $\Omega$ is open, $\Omega\cap\mathbb{R}\neq\emptyset$ and $\Omega\cap\mathbb{C}_{I}$ is a domain in $\mathbb{C}_{I}$, for any $I\in\mathbb{S}$.}
<!-- end definition-1 -->
<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:finite-type}
Let $T\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$. A point $q\in\sigma_S(T)$ is a right eigenvalue
of finite type if $\mathcal H=\mathcal H_1\oplus\mathcal H_2$ is a topological direct sum
into closed $T$-invariant right submodules with
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\textup{F}\arabic*),leftmargin=3em]
\item $\dim_{\mathbb H}\mathcal H_1<\infty$,
\item $\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\cap\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})=\varnothing$,
\item $\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})=[q]$.
\end{enumerate}
The set of such $q$ is the discrete $S$-spectrum $\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$.
<!-- end definition-2 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:square}
In a real unital algebra, $a$ is invertible if and only if $a^2$ is.
Consequently, if $\mathcal H=\mathcal H_1\oplus\mathcal H_2$ is a topological direct sum
into closed $T$-invariant right submodules, then
$$Q_q(T)=Q_q(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\oplus Q_q(T|_{\mathcal H_2}) ~~\hbox{and}~~ \sigma_S(T)=\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\cup\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2}).$$
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For the first assertion, only the nontrivial implication requires proof,
since the converse is immediate. If $a$ is invertible, then so is
$$a^2=a\cdot a,$$ being a product of invertible elements, with inverse
$$a^{-1}a^{-1}.$$ Conversely, suppose $a^2$ is invertible. Since $a$
commutes with $a^2$, it also commutes with $$(a^2)^{-1},$$ hence
$$b=a(a^2)^{-1}=(a^2)^{-1}a$$ satisfies $ab=ba=1$. For the second
assertion, every real polynomial in $T$ preserves both summands, and an
operator that respects a topological direct sum is invertible precisely
when both of its restrictions are.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:real-spectrum}
If $T\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$ is self-adjoint, then $\sigma_S(T)\subset\mathbb R$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We refer also to \cite[Theorem 2.11(ii)]{AlpayColomboKimsey2016} for
this fact. One has
$$Q_q(T)=(T-\operatorname{Re}(q)\mathrm{Id})^2+|\operatorname{Im}(q)|^2\mathrm{Id}.$$ Given $q\notin\mathbb R$, set
$B=Q_q(T)$ and $c=|\operatorname{Im}(q)|^2>0$. Then $$B=B^* ~~\hbox{and}~~
\langle Bx,x\rangle\ge c\|x\|^2,$$ so Cauchy-Schwarz yields
$$\|Bx\|\ge c\|x\|.$$ Consequently $B$ is injective with closed range,
while $\operatorname{ran}(B)^\perp=\ker(B)=\{0\}$ shows that it is also surjective.
<!-- end proof-2 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:hierarchy}
If $T=T^*\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$, then
\[
 \sigma_{W}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e+}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e-}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{w+}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{w-}^{S}(T).
\]
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
The inclusion $\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)\subseteq\sigma_{W}^{S}(T)$ was already noted.
Conversely, let $q\notin\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$, so that $A=Q_q(T)$ is Fredholm; as
$Q_q$ has real coefficients and $T=T^*$, we have $A=A^*$, hence
$$\ker(A)=\ker(A^*) ~~\hbox{and} ~~\operatorname{ind}(A)=0.$$ Thus $$A\in\Phi_0(\mathcal H)~~\hbox{and} ~~q\notin\sigma_{W}^{S}(T),$$ this proves $$\sigma_{W}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e}^{S}(T).$$
The same observation, namely that $A=Q_q(T)=A^*$ for every $q\in\mathbb H$
whenever $T=T^*$, disposes of the remaining four spectra in one go.
First, since $\ker(A)=\ker(A^*)$, the two conditions defining
$\Phi_+(\mathcal H)$ and $\Phi_-(\mathcal H)$ closed range together with
$\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(A)<\infty$ and $\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(A^*)<\infty$, respectively,
become identical, so $A\in\Phi_+(\mathcal H)$ precisely when
$A\in\Phi_-(\mathcal H)$, equivalently $$\sigma_{e+}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e-}^{S}(T),$$ and because
$\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e+}^{S}(T)\cup\sigma_{e-}^{S}(T)$ by definition, the three sets
coincide $$\sigma_{e+}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e-}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e}^{S}(T).$$ Second, whenever
$Q_q(T)\in\Phi_+(\mathcal H)$ and therefore $Q_q(T)\in\Phi_-(\mathcal H)$ by the
foregoing, so that $Q_q(T)\in\Phi(\mathcal H)$ the identity
$\ker(A)=\ker(A^*)$ entails
$$\operatorname{ind}(A)=\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(A)-\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(A^*)=0.$$ Consequently the extra clauses
``$\operatorname{ind}(Q_q(T))>0$'' and ``$\operatorname{ind}(Q_q(T))<0$'' appearing in the
definitions of $\sigma_{w+}^{S}(T)$ and $\sigma_{w-}^{S}(T)$ are never activated once
$Q_q(T)\in\Phi_+(\mathcal H)$, respectively $Q_q(T)\in\Phi_-(\mathcal H)$, thus
$$\sigma_{w+}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e+}^{S}(T) ~~\hbox{and}~~ \sigma_{w-}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e-}^{S}(T).$$ Together with the
first part, this yields
$\sigma_{w+}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{w-}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{W}^{S}(T)$, and the chain of
equalities is complete.
<!-- end proof-3 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
Let $T=T^*\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$. There is a unique projection-valued measure
$E:\mathfrak B(\mathbb R)\to\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$, with values orthogonal projections, such
that $$T=\int_\mathbb R t\,dE(t),$$ it satisfies $\operatorname{supp}(E)=\sigma_S(T)$. For
every bounded Borel $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ the operator $$f(T)=\int_\mathbb R f\,dE$$
is bounded self-adjoint, $f\mapsto f(T)$ is a unital homomorphism of
real algebras, and
\begin{equation}
 \|f(T)x\|^2=\int_\mathbb R|f|^2\,d\mu_x,
 \qquad
 \mu_x(B)=\langle E(B)x,x\rangle=\|E(B)x\|^2 .
 \label{eq:scalar}
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:eigenspace}
For every $\lambda\in\mathbb R$, $\operatorname{ran}(E(\{\lambda\}))=\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Let $E(\{\lambda\})x=x$ then
$$\mu_x(\mathbb R\setminus\{\lambda\})=\|x-E(\{\lambda\})x\|^2=0,$$ so
\eqref{eq:scalar} with $f(t)=t-\lambda$ gives
$$\|(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})x\|^2=0.$$ Conversely $(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})x=0$ gives
$$\int|t-\lambda|^2d\mu_x=0.$$ The integrand is nonnegative and vanishes
only at $\lambda$, so $$\mu_x(\mathbb R\setminus\{\lambda\})=0 ~~\hbox{and}~~
\|x-E(\{\lambda\})x\|^2=\|E(\mathbb R\setminus\{\lambda\})x\|^2=0.$$
<!-- end proof-4 -->
<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:restriction}
Let $\Delta\subset\sigma_S(T)$ be nonempty, compact and open in
$\sigma_S(T)$, and put $$\Delta'=\sigma_S(T)\setminus\Delta,
\mathcal H_1=\operatorname{ran}(E(\Delta)), \mathcal H_2=\ker(E(\Delta)).$$ Then $\mathcal H_1,\mathcal H_2$
are closed, mutually orthogonal, $T$-invariant,
$\mathcal H=\mathcal H_1\oplus\mathcal H_2$, and
\[
 \sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})=\Delta,\qquad \sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})=\Delta' .
\]
Moreover $\mathcal H_1\neq\{0\}$, and
$\operatorname{dist}(\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1}),\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2}))>0$ when
$\Delta'\neq\varnothing$.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
The operator \(E(\Delta)\) is an orthogonal projection commuting with \(T\). Hence
\[
\mathcal H_1=\operatorname{ran} E(\Delta),\qquad \mathcal H_2=\operatorname{ran} E(\Delta')
\]
are closed, orthogonal, \(T\)-invariant, and \(\mathcal H=\mathcal H_1\oplus\mathcal H_2\). Since \(\Delta\) is compact and closed in \(\sigma_S(T)\), its complement \(\Delta'\) is compact and open in \(\sigma_S(T)\). Also \(E\) is carried by $$\operatorname{supp}(E)=\sigma_S(T),$$ so
\[
E(\mathbb R\setminus\Delta)=E(\Delta'),
\]
and therefore \(\mathcal H_2=\operatorname{ran}(E(\Delta'))\). For \(q\in\mathbb C\), define
\[
\varphi_q(t)=t^2-2\operatorname{Re}(q)t+|q|^2
=\bigl(t-\operatorname{Re}(q)\bigr)^2+|\operatorname{Im}(q)|^2 .
\]
Then \(Q_q(T)=\varphi_q(T)\) in the Borel functional calculus. We treat \(\Delta\), the argument for \(\Delta'\) is identical. If \(\Delta'=\varnothing\), then \(\mathcal H_2=\{0\}\) and \(\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})=\varnothing\).

\emph{Step 1: \(q\notin\Delta\) implies \(q\in\rho_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\).}
If \(q\notin\mathbb R\), then \(\varphi_q\ge |\operatorname{Im}(q)|^2>0\). If \(q\in\mathbb R\) and \(q\notin\Delta\), then
\[
\varphi_q(t)=(t-q)^2\ge \operatorname{dist}(q,\Delta)^2>0
\]
on the compact set \(\Delta\). Thus in either case
\[
\delta:=\inf_{\Delta}\varphi_q>0.
\]
Hence \(\psi:=\varphi_q^{-1}\chi_\Delta\) is bounded Borel with \(\|\psi\|_\infty\le\delta^{-1}\), and
\[
\varphi_q\psi=\chi_\Delta
\]
gives
\[
Q_q(T)\,\psi(T)=\psi(T)\,Q_q(T)=E(\Delta).
\]
Both operators preserve \(\mathcal H_1\), and \(E(\Delta)|_{\mathcal H_1}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal H_1}\). Therefore \(\psi(T)|_{\mathcal H_1}\) inverts \(Q_q(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\), so \(q\in\rho_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\).

\emph{Step 2: \(q\in\Delta\) implies \(q\in\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\).}
Such a \(q\) is real. Let \(\varepsilon>0\) and
\[
B_\varepsilon=(q-\varepsilon,q+\varepsilon)\cap\Delta .
\]
Since \(\Delta\) is only relatively open in \(\sigma_S(T)\), write \(\Delta=W\cap\sigma_S(T)\) with \(W\subset\mathbb R\) open. Then
\[
O=(q-\varepsilon,q+\varepsilon)\cap W
\]
is open in \(\mathbb R\) and contains \(q\in\operatorname{supp}(E)\). Hence \(E(O)\neq0\). Moreover,
\[
E(O)=E(O\cap\sigma_S(T))=E(B_\varepsilon),
\]
because \(E\) is carried by \(\operatorname{supp}(E)\). Choose a unit vector
\[
x\in\operatorname{ran}(E(B_\varepsilon))\subset\mathcal H_1 .
\]
Then \(\mu_x\) is carried by \(B_\varepsilon\), and on \(B_\varepsilon\),
\[
|\varphi_q(t)|=(t-q)^2<\varepsilon^2 .
\]
Thus
\[
\|Q_q(T|_{\mathcal H_1})x\|^2\le\varepsilon^4
\]
by \eqref{eq:scalar}. Since \(\varepsilon>0\) is arbitrary, \(Q_q(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\) is not bounded below. Hence \(q\in\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\).

Steps 1 and 2 yield
\[
\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})=\Delta .
\]
Applying the same argument to \(\Delta'\) gives
\[
\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})=\Delta' .
\]
Since \(\Delta\neq\varnothing\), Step 2 supplies a unit vector in \(\mathcal H_1\), so \(\mathcal H_1\neq\{0\}\). Finally, \(\Delta\) and \(\Delta'\) are disjoint compact sets.
<!-- end proof-5 -->
<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:fredholm}
For $\lambda\in\mathbb R$: $\lambda\notin\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$ if and only if
$T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}$ is Fredholm. If $T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}$ is Fredholm, then either
$\lambda\in\rho_S(T)$ or $\lambda$ is an isolated point of $\sigma_S(T)$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Since \(Q_\lambda(T)=(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^2\), we have
\(\pi(Q_\lambda(T))=\pi(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^2\). By Lemma \ref{lem:square},
the first of these is invertible in \(\mathcal C(\mathcal H)\) exactly when the
second is. Combining \eqref{eq:calkin} with Atkinson's theorem
\cite{MuraleetharanThirulogasanthar2018}, this gives the first
assertion.

For the second assertion, set \(A=T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}=A^*\). Because \(A\) is
Fredholm, its kernel is finite-dimensional and its range is closed,
moreover
$$\operatorname{ran}(A)=\ker(A^*)^\perp=\ker(A)^\perp.$$ Since
\(A(\mathcal H)=A(\ker(A)^\perp)\), it follows that the restricted operator
\(A_0=A|_{\ker(A)^\perp}\) maps \(\ker(A)^\perp\) bijectively onto
itself, and therefore \(A_0\) is boundedly invertible.

Let \(c=\|A_0^{-1}\|^{-1}>0\). If \(t\in\mathbb R\) satisfies \(|t|<c\), then
$$A_0-t\mathrm{Id}=A_0(\mathrm{Id}-tA_0^{-1})$$ is invertible by the Neumann series.
Hence \(Q_t(A_0)=(A_0-t\mathrm{Id})^2\) is invertible, so
$$t\notin\sigma_S(A_0).$$ On the other hand, since \(A|_{\ker A}=0\), we
have $$\sigma_S(A|_{\ker A})\subset\{0\}.$$ Applying Lemma
\ref{lem:square}, we obtain
\[
\sigma_S(A)\subset\{0\}\cup\bigl(\mathbb R\setminus(-c,c)\bigr),
\]
so \(0\) is either outside \(\sigma_S(A)\) or isolated in it. Finally, \(A\) and \(T\) are self-adjoint, so their \(S\)-spectra are
real. Since \(Q_t(A)=Q_{\lambda+t}(T)\) for real \(t\), it follows
that $$\sigma_S(A)=\sigma_S(T)-\lambda.$$
<!-- end proof-6 -->
<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:criterion}
Let $\lambda\in\sigma_S(T)$. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.4em]
\item $\lambda\notin\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$,
\item $\lambda\in\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$,
\item $\lambda$ is isolated in $\sigma_S(T)$ and
$\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})<\infty$,
\item $\lambda$ is isolated in $\sigma_S(T)$ and the atom
$E(\{\lambda\})$ has finite rank.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
We take (iii) as the central statement and prove
(iii) $\Leftrightarrow$ (iv), (iii) $\Leftrightarrow$ (i) and
(iii) $\Leftrightarrow$ (ii). The argument relies only on Lemma
\ref{lem:square}, Lemma \ref{lem:eigenspace}, Lemma
\ref{lem:restriction} and Lemma \ref{lem:fredholm}, each of which is
established in Section \ref{sec:prelim}, \ref{sec:spectral} or above
without reference to the present proposition. Consequently, none of
the implications below is used in the proof of any statement on which
it depends.

The equivalence (iii) $\Leftrightarrow$ (iv) is precisely Lemma
\ref{lem:eigenspace}.

Suppose now that (iii) holds, and set
$$\mathcal H_1=\operatorname{ran}(E(\{\lambda\})), \mathcal H_2=\ker(E(\{\lambda\})).$$ Since
$\lambda$ is isolated, Lemma \ref{lem:restriction} applies with
$\Delta=\{\lambda\}$ and yields
$\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})=\{\lambda\}=[\lambda]$ together with
$\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})=\sigma_S(T)\setminus\{\lambda\}$. These two $S$-spectra
are disjoint, furthermore $\mathcal H_1=\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})$ by Lemma
\ref{lem:eigenspace}, it is nonzero by Lemma \ref{lem:restriction},
and it is finite dimensional by hypothesis. Hence the orthogonal
decomposition $\mathcal H=\mathcal H_1\oplus\mathcal H_2$ fulfils (F1)--(F3), which means
$\lambda\in\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$, i.e.\ (ii) holds.

Still under (iii), observe that
$\lambda\notin\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})$, so
$Q_\lambda(T|_{\mathcal H_2})=((T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})|_{\mathcal H_2})^2$ is invertible;
Lemma \ref{lem:square} then makes $(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})|_{\mathcal H_2}$
invertible as well. Because $T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}$ annihilates $\mathcal H_1$, we
obtain
\[
 \ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})=\mathcal H_1,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{ran}(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})=\mathcal H_2 .
\]
Thus $T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}$ has finite-dimensional kernel and closed range of
codimension $\dim_{\mathbb H}\mathcal H_1<\infty$, so it is Fredholm, and Lemma
\ref{lem:fredholm} gives (i).

Conversely, assume (i). Lemma \ref{lem:fredholm} makes
$T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}$ Fredholm, whence $$\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})<\infty,$$
since $\lambda\in\sigma_S(T)$, it is isolated. This is (iii).

Finally, assume (ii), and let $\mathcal H=\mathcal H_1\oplus\mathcal H_2$ be a
decomposition as in Definition \ref{def:finite-type}. By Lemma
\ref{lem:square},
$\sigma_S(T)=\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_1})\cup\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})$. In view of (F2),
(F3) and Proposition \ref{prop:real-spectrum}, this is the union of
$\{\lambda\}$ with the compact set $\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})$, and the two
are disjoint, hence $\lambda$ is isolated. Moreover, since
$\lambda\notin\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})$, Lemma \ref{lem:square} shows that
$(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})|_{\mathcal H_2}$ is injective. Now let $x=x_1+x_2$ with
$x_j\in\mathcal H_j$ satisfy $(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})x=0$. The components
$(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})x_1\in\mathcal H_1$ and $(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})x_2\in\mathcal H_2$ must
vanish separately, so $x_2=0$. Therefore
$\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})\subset\mathcal H_1$ is finite dimensional by (F1),
which is (iii).

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:where-the-work-is}
Among the four equivalences, only (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (ii) is
non-trivial. Its converse (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii) is an immediate
consequence of Definition \ref{def:finite-type}, while
(i) $\Leftrightarrow$ (iii) is a classical Fredholm argument in
quaternionic form. One might expect (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (ii) to be
just as straightforward: if $\lambda$ is isolated and
$\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})<\infty$, simply take
$$\mathcal H_1=\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}) ~~\hbox{and}~~\mathcal H_2=\mathcal H_1^\perp ~~\hbox{in Definition
\ref{def:finite-type}}.$$ Conditions (F1) and (F3) then cause no
difficulty, but (F2) requires $\lambda\notin\sigma_S(T|_{\mathcal H_2})$, and
this is not furnished by any hypothesis: one must first know that the
orthogonal decomposition associated with $E(\{\lambda\})$ splits the
$S$-spectrum in the required way. This is precisely the content of
Lemma \ref{lem:restriction}, the sole substantive computation of the
paper, and it appears not to be available in the literature. The other
possible approach, namely choosing for $\mathcal H_1$ the range of the Riesz
projection and appealing to
\cite[Theorem 3.10]{BaloudiBelgacemJeribi2022}, would require
$\dim_{\mathbb H}\operatorname{ran}(P_{\{\lambda\}})<\infty$; however, this does not follow
from $\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})<\infty$ unless one already knows that
$\operatorname{ran}(P_{\{\lambda\}})=\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})$, that is, unless one
already has Theorem \ref{thm:riesz}. In fact, Example \ref{ex:jordan}
demonstrates that these two subspaces need not coincide in general.
Thus, in either approach, reversing \eqref{eq:inclusion} depends on
one of the two results established here and cannot be obtained from
\cite{BaloudiBelgacemJeribi2022} merely by rearrangement.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $T=T^*\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$ with $\dim_{\mathbb H}\mathcal H=\infty$. Then the essential
$S$-spectrum of $T$ is given by
\[
 \sigma_{e}^{S}(T)=\sigma_S(T)\setminus\sigma_{d}^{S}(T),
\]
and a point $\lambda\in\sigma_S(T)$ belongs to $\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$ precisely when
either $\lambda$ fails to be isolated in $\sigma_S(T)$, or the range of
the spectral projection $E(\{\lambda\})$ is infinite dimensional, that
is,
\[
 \sigma_{e}^{S}(T)
 =\Big\{\lambda\in\sigma_S(T):\ \lambda\ \text{is not isolated in}\
 \sigma_S(T),\ \text{or}\ \dim_{\mathbb H}\operatorname{ran}\big(E(\{\lambda\})\big)=\infty\Big\}.
\]
Equivalently, the $S$-spectrum of $T$ decomposes as
$\sigma_S(T)=\sigma_{W}^{S}(T)\cup\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$ with
$$\sigma_S(T)\setminus\sigma_{W}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{d}^{S}(T).$$
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Both $\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$ and $\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$ are contained in $\sigma_S(T)$, and by
Proposition \ref{prop:criterion} a point of $\sigma_S(T)$ lies outside
$\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$ precisely when it lies in $\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$; the second
description is the negation of (iv). The last formulation follows from
$\sigma_{W}^{S}(T)=\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$ (Lemma \ref{lem:hierarchy}).
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:weyl-criterion}
Let $\lambda\in\mathbb R$. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.4em]
\item $\lambda\in\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$,
\item there is a sequence $(x_n)$ with $\|x_n\|=1$,
$x_n\rightharpoonup0$ weakly and $\|Q_\lambda(T)x_n\|\to0$,
\item there is a sequence $(x_n)$ with $\|x_n\|=1$,
$x_n\rightharpoonup0$ weakly and $\|(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})x_n\|\to0$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
(i) $\Rightarrow$ (iii). Here $\lambda\in\sigma_S(T)\subset\mathbb R$. We
first claim that $E((\lambda-\varepsilon,\lambda+\varepsilon))$ has
infinite rank for every $\varepsilon>0$. Suppose otherwise, and put
$B=(\lambda-\varepsilon,\lambda+\varepsilon)$. Then $\sigma_S(T)\cap B$
must be finite. Indeed, if it were infinite, one could choose distinct
points $t_1,t_2,\dots$ in it together with pairwise disjoint open sets
$O_k\subset B$ satisfying $t_k\in O_k$. Each $E(O_k)$ is nonzero, since
$O_k$ is an open set meeting $\operatorname{supp}(E)=\sigma_S(T)$, and the ranges
$\operatorname{ran}(E(O_k))$ are mutually orthogonal and contained in
$\operatorname{ran}(E(B))$, which contradicts $\dim_{\mathbb H}\operatorname{ran}(E(B))<\infty$. But then
$\lambda$ would be isolated, with $E(\{\lambda\})\le E(B)$ of finite
rank, so Proposition \ref{prop:criterion} would give
$\lambda\notin\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$ a contradiction.

Now choose, by induction, unit vectors
$x_n\in\operatorname{ran}(E((\lambda-1/n,\lambda+1/n)))$ orthogonal to
$x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}$; this is possible because these subspaces are
infinite dimensional. The resulting sequence is orthonormal, hence
$x_n\rightharpoonup0$ by Bessel's inequality, and since $\mu_{x_n}$ is
carried by $(\lambda-1/n,\lambda+1/n)$, \eqref{eq:scalar} yields
$\|(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})x_n\|\le1/n$.

For (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (ii), it suffices to note that
$$\|Q_\lambda(T)x_n\|\le\|T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}\|\,\|(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})x_n\|.$$

(ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i). Assume $\lambda\notin\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$. Then
$A=Q_\lambda(T)=A^*$ is Fredholm, so $\ker(A)$ is finite dimensional,
$\mathcal H=\ker(A)\oplus\operatorname{ran}(A)$ is an orthogonal decomposition, and
$\|Ay\|\ge c\|y\|$ holds on $\operatorname{ran}(A)$ for some $c>0$. Decompose
$x_n=k_n+r_n$ accordingly. Since $x_n\rightharpoonup0$ and
$\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(A)<\infty$, we get $\|k_n\|\to0$, whence $\|r_n\|\to1$ and
therefore
$$\|Ax_n\|=\|Ar_n\|\ge c\|r_n\|\to c>0,$$ a contradiction.

\label{thm:bbj36}
Let $T\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$ and let $P\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$ be an idempotent commuting
with $T$. Put $T_1=T|_{\operatorname{ran}(P)}$ and $T_2=T|_{\ker(P)}$. If

isolated part of $\sigma_S(T)$ and
\[
 \operatorname{ran}(P)=\operatorname{ran}\big(P_{\sigma_S(T_1)}\big),
 \qquad
 \ker(P)=\ker\big(P_{\sigma_S(T_1)}\big).
\]

\label{thm:riesz}
Let $T=T^*$ and let $\Delta$ be an isolated part of $\sigma_S(T)$. Then
$$P_\Delta=E(\Delta).$$ In particular $P_\Delta$ is an orthogonal
projection.

<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
If $\Delta=\sigma_S(T)$, both operators equal $\mathrm{Id}$. Otherwise, put
$P=E(\Delta)$ and check the hypotheses of
\cite[Theorem 3.6]{BaloudiBelgacemJeribi2022} one by one: $P$ is an
idempotent of $\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$ commuting with $T$, and
$\operatorname{dist}(\sigma_S(T_1),\sigma_S(T_2))>0$ for $T_1=T|_{\operatorname{ran}(P)}$ and
$T_2=T|_{\ker(P)}$. The first holds since $E$ is the spectral measure
of $T$ and $E(\Delta)$ is an orthogonal projection. For the second,
$\Delta$ is compact, being closed in the compact set $\sigma_S(T)$, so
Lemma \ref{lem:restriction} gives $$\sigma_S(T_1)=\Delta \hbox{ and }
\sigma_S(T_2)=\sigma_S(T)\setminus\Delta$$ these are disjoint compact sets,
hence at positive distance. Theorem \ref{thm:bbj36} then applies,
giving $$\operatorname{ran}(E(\Delta))=\operatorname{ran}(P_\Delta) \hbox{ and }
\ker(E(\Delta))=\ker(P_\Delta).$$ Since an idempotent is determined by
its range and kernel, if $$\operatorname{ran}(P)=\operatorname{ran}(P'),
\ker(P)=\ker(P') \hbox{ and } x=y+z \hbox{ with }  y\in\operatorname{ran}(P), z\in\ker(P),$$ then
$$Py=y=P'y \hbox{ and } Pz=0=P'z,$$ we conclude $$P_\Delta=E(\Delta).$$

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:multiplicity}
Let $\lambda\in\sigma_S(T)$ be isolated. Then the multiplicity of
$\lambda$ satisfies
\[
 m_T(\lambda)=\dim_{\mathbb H}\operatorname{ran}\big(P_{\{\lambda\}}\big)
 =\dim_{\mathbb H}\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}).
\]
Moreover, if $\dim_{\mathbb H}\mathcal H=\infty$, then $\lambda\in\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$ exactly when
$m_T(\lambda)<\infty$. Consequently, the argument outlined in Section
\ref{sec:intro} is now justified: since
$\operatorname{ran}(P_{\{\lambda\}})$ reduces $T$, the restriction of $T$ to this
subspace is self-adjoint.

<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
\label{rem:chi}
Theorem \ref{thm:riesz} identifies $P_\Delta$ with $\chi_\Delta(T)$
computed in the Borel functional calculus. One cannot reach this by
feeding $\chi_\Delta$ into the $S$-functional calculus, since
$\chi_\Delta$ is not slice hyperholomorphic. Replacing it by a locally
constant, hence intrinsic, function $f$ equal to $1$ near $\Delta$ and
$0$ near $\sigma_S(T)\setminus\Delta$ gives $f(T)=P_\Delta$ from
\eqref{eq:riesz}, but identifying that $f(T)$ with $\int_\mathbb R f\,dE$
then requires a compatibility theorem between the two calculi
\cite{ColomboGantnerKimsey2018}. The proof above uses
\eqref{eq:riesz} only through the fact that $P_\Delta$ is an
idempotent commuting with $T$.

\label{ex:diagonal}
Let $\mathcal H=\ell^2(\mathbb N,\mathbb H)$ with canonical orthonormal basis
$(e_n)_{n\ge1}$ and $$Te_n=e_n\lambda_n$$ with $\lambda_n\subset\mathbb R$
bounded. Then $$T=T^*, \sigma_S(T)=\overline{\{\lambda_n\}},$$ and the
atom of $E$ at $\lambda$ is the orthogonal projection onto
$\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_n:\lambda_n=\lambda\}$, of rank
$\#\{n:\lambda_n=\lambda\}$. By Theorem \ref{thm:main}, $\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$
consists of the isolated values taken finitely often. For
$\lambda_n=1$ ($n$ even) and $\lambda_n=1/(n+1)$ ($n$ odd),
\[
 \sigma_S(T)=\{1\}\cup\{\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac16,\dots\}\cup\{0\},
 \qquad
 \sigma_{e}^{S}(T)=\{0,1\},
\]
so both mechanisms occur: $0$ is not isolated, $1$ is isolated with an
atom of infinite rank.

<!-- example-2: body_md -->
\label{ex:multiplication}
Let $(X,\mu)$ be $\sigma$-finite with $L^2(X,\mu,\mathbb H)$ separable and
infinite dimensional, and let $M_\phi f=\phi f$, where $\phi$ is a bounded real measurable function. Then $M_\phi$ is self-adjoint, and since $Q_q(M_\phi)$ acts as multiplication by $\varphi_q\circ\phi$, we have
\[
\sigma_S(M_\phi)=\operatorname{ess\,ran}(\phi).
\]

A point $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue
exactly when $\mu(\phi^{-1}(\lambda))>0$, with eigenspace
$L^2(\phi^{-1}(\lambda),\mu;\mathbb H)$, finite dimensional precisely when
$\phi^{-1}(\lambda)$ is, modulo null sets, a finite union of atoms of
$\mu$. For non-atomic $\mu-$Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb R$, then
\[
\sigma_{d}^{S}(M_\phi)=\varnothing
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\sigma_{e}^{S}(M_\phi)=\sigma_S(M_\phi)=\operatorname{ess\,ran}(\phi).
\]

Observe here that the $S$-spectrum is the essential range: modifying $\phi$ on a null set leaves both $M_\phi$ and its $S$-spectrum unchanged.

\label{ex:jordan}
Let $\mathcal H=\mathbb H^2$, the left multiplication acting componentwise so that
(A3) holds, and $T(x_1,x_2)=(x_2,0)$. Then, $T$ is nilpotent, $T^2=0$, and therefore

Q_q(T)=-2\operatorname{Re}(q)T+|q|^2\mathrm{Id}
\]
is invertible for every $q\neq0$. Consequently, $\sigma_S(T)=\{0\}$, which is an isolated part of the spectrum, and $P_{\{0\}}=\mathrm{Id}$. It follows that
\[
 m_T(0)=\dim_{\mathbb H}\operatorname{ran}\big(P_{\{0\}}\big)=2,
 \qquad
 \dim_{\mathbb H}\ker T=1,
\]
so $\operatorname{ran}(P_{\{0\}})\not\subset\ker T$, and Corollary \ref{cor:multiplicity} fails. This is exactly the step required by the argument in Section \ref{sec:intro}, and the step that Theorem \ref{thm:riesz} provides in the self-adjoint case. The example intentionally lies outside (A2), since $\mathcal H$ is finite dimensional; what it tests is the first assertion of Corollary \ref{cor:multiplicity}, which does not need $\dim_{\mathbb H}\mathcal H=\infty$. Nothing is claimed here about $\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)$, nor about Theorem \ref{thm:main}.

<!-- example-4: body_md -->
\label{ex:sphere}
Let $\mathcal H=\ell^2(\mathbb N,\mathbb H)$ with the componentwise left multiplication,
and define $T$ by
\[
 Te_n=\mathbf i\,e_n\,d_n,
 \qquad d_n=\tfrac1n .
\]
Each $L_{\mathbf i}$ and each real diagonal factor is right linear, so
$T\in\mathcal B(\mathcal H)$ with $\|T\|=1$, and $T^*=-T$; in particular $T$ is
normal but not self-adjoint. Since $T^2e_n=-e_nd_n^2$,
\[
 Q_q(T)\Big(\sum_n e_nx_n\Big)
 =\sum_n e_n\,c_n(q)\,x_n ,
 \qquad
 c_n(q)=\big(|q|^2-d_n^2\big)-2\operatorname{Re}(q)\,d_n\,\mathbf i ,
\]
where $c_n(q)$ acts by left multiplication. Thus $Q_q(T)$ is
invertible if and only if $$\inf_n|c_n(q)|>0.$$ Now $c_n(q)$ has real
part $|q|^2-d_n^2$ and $\mathbf i$-component $-2\operatorname{Re}(q)d_n$, the
$\mathbf j$- and $\mathbf k$-components being zero, so
\[
 |c_n(q)|^2=\big(|q|^2-d_n^2\big)^2+4\operatorname{Re}(q)^2d_n^2 ,
\]
which vanishes exactly when $|q|=d_n$ and $\operatorname{Re}(q)=0$, the $d_n$ being
nonzero. Since $d_n\to0$, the infimum over $n$ vanishes only at such
$q$ and at $q=0$. Hence
\[
 \sigma_S(T)=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{n\ge1}\tfrac1n\,\mathbb S ,
\]
a decreasing sequence of $2$-spheres accumulating at $0$. For
$q$ with $\operatorname{Re}(q)=0$ and $|q|=1/n$, the class $[q]=\frac1n\mathbb S$ is
an isolated part of $\sigma_S(T)$, and $\ker(Q_q(T))=e_n\mathbb H$ has
quaternionic dimension $1.$ Choosing $\mathcal H_1=e_n\mathbb H$ in Definition
\ref{def:finite-type} then yields $$q\in\sigma_{d}^{S}(T).$$ Since $T$ is
compact, $\pi(T)=0$, and by \eqref{eq:calkin} we get
$\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)=\{0\}$. Each of these assertions has been checked by direct
computation, and taken together they show that
$$\sigma_{e}^{S}(T)=\sigma_S(T)\setminus\sigma_{d}^{S}(T)$$ for this particular $T$ 
with the statement interpreted using $[q]$ in place of
$\{\lambda\}$ and $\ker(Q_q(T))$ in place of
$\ker(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})$.

We emphasize that this paper proves no general statement about
normal operators. What we have here is a single hand-computed
instance; its purpose is to demonstrate that the conclusion is not an
artefact of $\sigma_S(T)\subset\mathbb R$, and to pinpoint the two obstacles
described in Section \ref{sec:conclusion}. In particular, the proofs
of Section \ref{sec:main}, which rely on
$Q_\lambda(T)=(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^2$, do not carry over to it verbatim.
