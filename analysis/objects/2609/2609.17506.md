<!-- conjecture-1: body_md -->
Every probability measure $\mu$ on $\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb R)$ (or more generally $\operatorname{PSL}_{d}(\mathbb R)$) whose support is finite and generates a  Zariski-dense  discrete subgroup $\Gamma$ must have singular hitting measure  at infinity.
<!-- end conjecture-1 -->
<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$ whose support is finite and generates a Zariski-dense discrete  
subgroup of $\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb R)$. Then its stationary probability measure $\nu$ on
$\partial \mathbb D$ is singular with  $\operatorname{leb}$.
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Koberda \cite{Koberda} studies the action of pseudo-Anosov maps
on the homology of finite abelian covers. The character torus
allows him to compare eigenvalues as the cover varies, by using
continuity and compactness. Here, the character torus serves
a different purpose: to obtain a topological obstruction.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- head-1: body_md -->
We write $\mathbb D$ for  the Poincaré disk, endowed with a basepoint $o$ which we fix once and for all.
<!-- end head-1 -->
<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:periods}
The period map $\omega \mapsto p_{\omega}$ induces an isomorphism
$H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(S_N;\mathbb C)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(N,\mathbb C)$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
This is the degree-one de Rham isomorphism \cite[Chapter~18]{Lee};
we give a short direct proof for completeness. 


Exact forms have zero periods, so the map
$[\omega]\mapsto p_\omega$ is well defined.


Let $\omega \in \Omega^1_{\mathrm{cl}}(S_N;\mathbb C)$. If every period of $\omega$ vanishes, then $f_{\omega}$ is $N$-invariant, so it can be seen as a primitive of $\omega$ on $S_N$, i.e. $\omega$ is exact. This shows injectivity. 

We check surjectivity. To realize a homomorphism $p$, choose a smooth compactly
supported function $\rho$ on $\mathbb D$ with
$\sum_{n\in N}\rho(n^{-1}x)=1$. Such a function is obtained by taking
a nonnegative bump function positive on a compact fundamental set and
dividing by the sum of its translates. Proper discontinuity makes that
sum locally finite. The function
\[
                    f(x)=\sum_{n\in N}\rho(n^{-1}x)p(n)
\]
is smooth and satisfies $f(nx)=p(n)+f(x)$, by changing the summation
variable. Therefore $df$ descends to a closed form with periods $p$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:hodge}
Every de Rham class $[\omega]$ has a unique harmonic representative
$\omega^{\mathrm h}$. This representative is the unique minimizer of the $L^2$-norm
in its class. The quarter-turn $\mathsf r$ and pullback by an
orientation-preserving isometry preserve harmonic forms.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Existence and uniqueness follow from the Hodge theorem on the
closed oriented surface $S_N$ \cite[Chapter~6]{Warner}, applied
to real and imaginary parts. Orthogonality to exact forms gives
\[
 \lVert\omega^{\mathrm h}+df\rVert_{L^2}^2
 =\lVert\omega^{\mathrm h}\rVert_{L^2}^2+\lVertdf\rVert_{L^2}^2
 \qquad (f\in C^\infty(S_N;\mathbb C)),
\]
so $\omega^{\mathrm h}$ is the unique norm minimizer in its class.

In a positively oriented conformal coordinate,
$\omega=P\,\,\mathrm d x+Q\,\,\mathrm d y$ is harmonic exactly when
\[
 Q_x-P_y=0,\qquad P_x+Q_y=0.
\]
These equations are preserved by $(P,Q)\mapsto(-Q,P)$,
so $\mathsf r$ preserves harmonic forms. Finally, pullback by an
orientation-preserving isometry preserves closed and exact

\label{lem:polygon}

$\tau=\sigma_{q^{-1},v}$, and put

Let $P\subset\mathbb D$ be a lifted convex geodesic polygon for \eqref{eq:presentation},
whose positively oriented boundary has successive vertices $p_kx_0$ for some $x_{0}\in \mathbb D$. Triangulate $P$ by drawing geodesic segments from $x_0$ to the other vertices of $P$. For each obtained triangle $T$, define \[
 \varphi_T:\Delta\longrightarrow T,\qquad
 \Delta=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb R^2:s,t\ge0,\ s+t\le1\},
\]
the geodesic ray parametrization, respecting orientation and sending $0$ to the base vertex $x_{0}$. More precisely, 
$ \varphi_T|_{\partial \Delta}$ is the constant speed geodesic parametrization and is also geodesic on each ray starting from $x_{0}$.
By translating (freely) this polygon and the 
triangulation therein by $\Gamma$, one obtains a triangulation
$\mathcal T$ of $\mathbb D$. For $T\in \mathcal T$, we extend the notation $ \varphi_T$ so that $\varphi_{\gamma T}=\gamma\circ\varphi_T$ $(\gamma\in \Gamma)$. It is well defined because $\Gamma$ acts freely on the set of triangles. 



 Given $T\in \mathcal T$, we define $F,G:T\rightarrow \mathbb C$ by interpolating between the values of $\sigma$ and $\tau$ on the vertices. More formally, if $T$ has ordered vertices
$(\gamma_0x_0,\gamma_1x_0,\gamma_2x_0)=(\varphi_T(0,0), \varphi_T(1,0), \varphi_T(0,1))$, define for $(s,t)\in \Delta$,
\[
 F\circ\varphi_T (s,t)=\sum_{j=0}^2\lambda_j\sigma(\gamma_j),
 \qquad
 G\circ\varphi_T(s,t)=\sum_{j=0}^2\lambda_j\tau(\gamma_j),
 \qquad
 (\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\lambda_2)=(1-s-t,s,t).
\]
As $T$ varies, these definitions agree on shared edges, so we can see $F,G:\mathbb D\rightarrow \mathbb C$. Since $\sum_j\lambda_j=1$,
the cocycle identities give
\[
 F\circ\gamma=\sigma(\gamma)+\chi(\gamma)F,\qquad
 G\circ\gamma=\tau(\gamma)+\chi(\gamma)^{-1}G.

$\alpha,\beta$. Since

are $N$-invariant and descend to continuous piecewise smooth functions

\[

\]
The tangential traces of $h\zeta-k\alpha$ agree on shared edges, so
Stokes' theorem gives
\[
 \int_{S_N}(\eta\wedge\zeta-\alpha\wedge\beta)
 =\sum_{T\in N\backslash\mathcal T}
       \int_{\partial T}(h\zeta-k\alpha)=0.
\]

Moreover, $\gamma^*(dF\wedge dG)=dF\wedge dG$ for every
$\gamma\in\Gamma$, and $S_N$ consists of $d_q$ copies of $P$.
On the $k$th positively oriented boundary edge, the functions $F,G$

Consequently,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 B_q(u,v)
 &=\frac1{d_q}\int_{S_N}\eta\wedge\zeta
   =\int_P dF\wedge dG
   =\int_{\partial P}F\,dG\\
 &=\frac12\sum_{k=0}^{4g-1}
       (f_k+f_{k+1})(g_{k+1}-g_k)\\
 &=\frac12\sum_{k=0}^{4g-1}
       (f_kg_{k+1}-f_{k+1}g_k),
 \end{aligned}
\]
where the last equality uses
$\sum_k(f_{k+1}g_{k+1}-f_kg_k)=0$.
The Laurent-polynomial assertion follows from
\Cref{lem:word-coordinates}.

<!-- proposition-3: body_md -->
\label{prop:geometric}
For a real closed one-form $\omega$ on  $S_{N}$,
angular-almost-every ray has zero winding drift, i.e. $e_{\operatorname{leb}}(\omega)=0$. The angular ray CLT
and LIL have variance
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:geometric-variance}
 V_{\operatorname{leb}}(\omega)=\frac{2}{\operatorname{Area}(S_N)}

\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-3 -->

Put $M=S_N$, $A=\operatorname{Area}(M)$, and choose disk coordinates with $o=0$.
Writing $\omega=\omega^{\mathrm h}+dh$, one has
\[
 \lVertf_\omega-f_{\omega^{\mathrm h}}\rVert_\infty
 \le 2\lVerth\rVert_\infty.
\]
Thus it suffices to consider harmonic $\omega$; the case $\omega=0$
is immediate.

Set
\[
 f=f_\omega,\qquad K=\lVert\omega\rVert_\infty,\qquad
 V=V_{\operatorname{leb}}(\omega),
\]
where $V$ is the common variance in the ray CLT and LIL of
\cref{thm:winding-laws}.



Since $\operatorname{leb}$ is the Patterson--Sullivan measure of $N$ based at $o$,
\cite[Lemma~5.2]{Benard} applied to the  functional
$f_{\omega}$ gives $e_{\operatorname{leb}}(\omega)=0$.




Let $g_t$ be the geodesic flow on $T^1M$, with normalized Liouville
measure
\[
 \,\mathrm d\ell=\frac{\,\mathrm d A(x)}{A}\frac{\,\mathrm d\theta}{2\pi}.
\]
For a harmonic form $\alpha$, put $F_\alpha(v)=\alpha(v)$.
We claim that, for harmonic forms $\alpha,\beta$ and $t\in\mathbb R$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:correlation}
 C_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
 :=\int_{T^1M}\overline{F_\alpha(v)}F_\beta(g_t v)\,\mathrm d\ell(v)
 =\frac{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_{L^2(M)}}{2A}
       \operatorname{sech}^2(t/2).
\end{equation}
To compute the angular average at $x\in M$, lift $x$ to the disk
origin and write
\[
 \widetilde\alpha
 =a_\alpha(z)\,\mathrm d z+b_\alpha(\bar z)\,\mathrm d\bar z,
 \qquad
 \widetilde\beta
 =a_\beta(z)\,\mathrm d z+b_\beta(\bar z)\,\mathrm d\bar z,
\]
where all four coefficient functions are holomorphic in their

In the metric $4|\,\mathrm d z|^2/(1-|z|^2)^2$, the initial unit vector

 \right)

 \end{aligned}

   2\int_0^T\left(1-\frac tT\right)C_{\omega,\omega}(t)\,\mathrm d t\\

 \end{aligned}

the Lipschitz bound on $f$ gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}

 &=|f(r_{x,\xi}(T))-f(x)-f(r_\xi(T))|\\
 &\le 2K\,d(x,o).
 \end{aligned}
\]
The visual measure $\operatorname{leb}_x$ satisfies
\[
 \frac{d\operatorname{leb}_x}{d\operatorname{leb}_o}(\xi)
 =\frac{1-|x|^2}{|\xi-x|^2}.
\]
By \cite[Lemma~5.5]{Benard}, the ray CLT with variance $V$ remains
valid when $\xi$ has law $\operatorname{leb}_x$. The preceding bound therefore gives
\[
 \frac{W_T(v_{x,\xi})}{\sqrt T}
 \xrightarrow[\xi\sim\operatorname{leb}_x]{\mathrm{law}}\mathcal N(0,V)
 \qquad(x\in\mathbb D).
\]

\[
 \begin{aligned}

       e^{iuW_T(v_{x,\xi})/\sqrt T}\,\mathrm d\operatorname{leb}_x(\xi)\,\mathrm d A(x)\\
 &\longrightarrow e^{-u^2V/2}.
 \end{aligned}
\]

\eqref{eq:geometric-variance}. The LIL with this same variance

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:returns}
The return time has a finite exponential moment and $\mathbb E\tau=d$ (where $d=|D|$).
The measure $\mu_N$ has a finite exponential word moment, its support
generates $N$ as a group, and its hitting measure is $\nu$. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drift-return}
                         L_{\mu_N}=dL_\mu.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
The quotient walk on $D$ is irreducible, indeed its increments are permutations
of a finite set, so the inverse of each is a positive power. It follows that for every starting point, there is positive probability to visit $e$ in less than $k$ steps, provided $k$ is a fixed large integer. 
 Applying the
Markov property in blocks of length $k$ gives $\mathbb P(\tau>n)\le Cr^n$, with $r<1$. This justifies exponential moment for $\tau$.


For the mean, given $\delta\in D$, set
$a(\delta)=\mathbb E\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\mathbf1_{\{X_kN=\delta\}}$.
Counting instead the times $1,\ldots,\tau$ leaves these counts unchanged;
therefore $a$ is  $\mu$-invariant function, so it is constant. Now $a(e)=1$ gives $a(\delta)=1$ for every $\delta\in D$, whence $\mathbb E\tau=d$.

Let $|\cdot|$ be word length in $\Gamma$ and
$M(a)=\mathbb E_\mu e^{a|Y_1|}$. For small $a>0$, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
 \mathbb E e^{a|X_\tau|}
 \le\sum_{n\ge1}M(2a)^{n/2}\mathbb P(\tau=n)^{1/2}<\infty.
\]
A finite-index subgroup has word length bounded above by a constant
times the ambient word length, up to an additive constant. To see this,
insert coset representatives between letters in a fixed finite generating
set; the resulting subgroup factors lie in a fixed finite set.
Thus $\mu_N$ has the asserted moment.

For generation, choose positive support words $t_\delta$ from $e$ to
$\delta$, and positive words $r_\delta$ returning from $\delta$ to $e$.
For $s\in\operatorname{supp}\mu$, the subgroup label
\[
 t_\delta s t_{\delta s}^{-1}
       =(t_\delta s r_{\delta s})(t_{\delta s}r_{\delta s})^{-1}
\]
is a quotient of positive loops. Every such loop splits at intermediate
returns into first-return loops, whose products lie in the group generated
by $\operatorname{supp}\mu_N$. The displayed labels generate $N$: insert representatives
into any support word, allowing inverses, representing an element of $N$;
inverse letters contribute inverses of labels. This proves the claim even
when the original support is infinite.

Successive return blocks are independent and identically distributed.
If $\tau_k$ is the $k$th return time, then $\tau_k/k\to d$ by the law of
large numbers, and $X_{\tau_k}$ is the $\mu_N$-walk. It is a subsequence
of the original path, so its endpoint law is $\nu$. Finally,
$d(o,X_{\tau_k}o)/k\to dL_\mu$, proving \eqref{eq:drift-return}.

<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
The process $M_n^\omega=\sigma_\omega^0(X_n)$ is a martingale, with
increments
\[
 M_{n+1}^\omega-M_n^\omega
               =\chi(X_n)\sigma_\omega^0(Y_{n+1}).
\]
Since $|\chi(X_n)|=1$, the conditional second moment of an increment
is the constant $K_\omega=\mathbb E_\mu|\sigma_\omega^0|^2<\infty$.
For $m\ge n$, orthogonality of martingale increments gives
\[
 \mathbb E|M_{\tau\wedge m}^\omega-M_{\tau\wedge n}^\omega|^2
       =K_\omega\mathbb E[(\tau\wedge m)-(\tau\wedge n)].
\]
As $\mathbb E\tau=d<\infty$, the stopped martingales converge in $L^2$ to
$M_\tau^\omega$. The conditional cross moment for $\omega,\zeta$ is
likewise the constant $\mathbb E_\mu[\overline{\sigma_\omega^0}\sigma_\zeta^0]$.
Summing up to $\tau\wedge n$ and passing to the $L^2$ limit gives
\[
 \mathbb E[\overline{M_\tau^\omega}M_\tau^\zeta]
          =\mathbb E\tau\,\mathbb E_\mu[\overline{\sigma_\omega^0}\sigma_\zeta^0].
\]
Coboundaries vanish on $N$, so $M_\tau^\omega=p_\omega(X_\tau)$,
and similarly for $\zeta$. This proves the identity.

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:J}
The matrix $A(q)$ is positive definite on $\mathbb T^2$. Its entries, and the
entries of $J_{q}$ from \eqref{eq:J-rational}, belong to a ring
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ring}
                    R=\mathbb C[z^{\pm1},w^{\pm1},F^{-1}],
\end{equation}
where $F\in \mathbb C[z^{\pm1},w^{\pm1}]$ is a Laurent polynomial with no zero on $\mathbb T^2$.
The extension is continuous and satisfies $J^2=-\mathrm{Id}$ as an identity
over $R$.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
If $v^*A(q)v=0$, then $\sigma_{q,v}^0$ vanishes on $\operatorname{supp}\mu$.
The cocycle identity makes it vanish on inverses and products, hence on
all of $\Gamma$. Thus $[\sigma_{q,v}]=0$, and \cref{lem:coordinates}
gives $v=0$.

Put $\lambda(q)=\mathbb E_\mu\chi_q$ and $a_v(q)=\mathbb E_\mu\sigma_{q,v}$.
By finite support and \cref{lem:word-coordinates}, these are Laurent
polynomials (linear in $v$ for the second). The centered cocycle is
\[
 \sigma_{q,v}^0=\sigma_{q,v}+(\chi_q-1)\frac{a_v(q)}{1-\lambda(q)}.
\]
The denominator has no zero on $\mathbb T^2$ by \cref{lem:centered}.
For real coordinate vectors $e_j$, conjugation on the torus replaces
$q$ by $q^{-1}$, including in the centering formula. Therefore
\[
 A_{ij}(q)=\mathbb E_\mu[\sigma_{q^{-1},e_i}^0\sigma_{q,e_j}^0]
\]
is rational, with denominators dividing a power of
$F(q)=(1-\lambda(q))(1-\lambda(q^{-1}))$.
On the torus this $F$ equals $|1-\lambda(q)|^2>0$.

Since $\det M=1$, the inverse of $M(q^{-1})$ is Laurent-polynomial.
Thus \eqref{eq:J-rational} has entries in $R$ and no poles on the torus.
The identity $J_q^2=-\mathrm{Id}$ holds on the dense set $\mathbb T^2_{\mathrm{tor}}$, hence on all
of $\mathbb T^2$ by continuity. After clearing denominators, each entry of
$J^2+\mathrm{Id}$ is a Laurent polynomial vanishing on $\mathbb T^2$. Its coefficients
are its Fourier coefficients there, so they all vanish. This proves the
identity over $R$.

\label{lem:positive-projector}
The matrix
\begin{equation}

                         P_q=\frac{\mathrm{Id}-iJ_q}{2} \in M_{2g-2}(\mathbb C)

 v^*A(q)v=\begin{cases}

<!-- lemma-13: body_md -->
\label{lem:algebra}
Let $P\in M_n(R)$ satisfy $P^2=P$, where $R$ is as in \eqref{eq:ring}
and $F$ has no zero on $\mathbb T^2$. Suppose $P(q)$ has constant rank $p\ge1$
on the torus. Then $q\mapsto\det\operatorname{im} P(q)$ has a nowhere-zero continuous
section.
<!-- end lemma-13 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
By Gauss' lemma, $\mathbb C[z,w]$ is a unique factorization domain
\cite[Section~9.3]{DF}; so is its localization $R$.
Put $K=\mathbb C(z,w)$.

Since $P^2=P$, its eigenvalues over $K$ belong to $\{0,1\}$.
Thus $\operatorname{tr} P=\operatorname{rank}_KP$ is a constant integer, and evaluation on
$\mathbb T^2$ gives $\operatorname{rank}_KP=p$. Set
\[
 m=\binom np,\qquad Q=\bigwedge\nolimits^pP\in M_m(R).
\]
Then $Q^2=Q$, $\operatorname{rank}_KQ=1$, and
\[
 \operatorname{im} Q(q)=\bigwedge\nolimits^p\operatorname{im} P(q)
          =\det\operatorname{im} P(q)
 \qquad(q\in\mathbb T^2).
\]

Choose a nonzero column $c$ of $Q$ and put
\[
 d=\gcd(c_1,\ldots,c_m),\qquad a=d^{-1}c\in R^m.
\]
Then $\gcd(a_1,\ldots,a_m)=1$ and $Qa=a$.
Since $\operatorname{im}_KQ=Ka$, there exists $b\in K^m$ such that
$Q=ab^{\mathsf T}$.

We claim that $b\in R^m$. Write each $b_j=r_j/s_j$ with
$r_j,s_j\in R$, $s_j\ne0$, and $\gcd(r_j,s_j)=1$.
The identities $a_i b_j=Q_{ij}\in R$ imply
\[
 \bigl(s_j\mid r_j a_i\ \text{for every }i\bigr)
 \ \Longrightarrow\
 \bigl(s_j\mid a_i\ \text{for every }i\bigr)
 \ \Longrightarrow\
 s_j\in R^\times.
\]
Here the first implication uses unique factorization and
$\gcd(r_j,s_j)=1$, and the second uses
$\gcd(a_1,\ldots,a_m)=1$. Hence $b_j\in R$.

Now $a=Qa=a(b^{\mathsf T}a)$, so $b^{\mathsf T}a=1$.
Since $F$ has no zero on $\mathbb T^2$, evaluation at every $q\in\mathbb T^2$
is defined on $R$ and gives
\[
 b(q)^{\mathsf T}a(q)=1,\qquad Q(q)a(q)=a(q).
\]
Consequently,
\[
 a(q)\in\det\operatorname{im} P(q)\setminus\{0\}
 \qquad(q\in\mathbb T^2).
\]
The coordinates of $a$ belong to $R$ and are continuous on $\mathbb T^2$,
so $q\mapsto a(q)$ is the required section.

<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
By \cref{lem:algebra}, the planes $E_q$ supplied by
\cref{lem:positive-projector} have a nowhere-zero determinant section.

Let $E_q^-$ be the negative spectral space of $H_q$.
Projection onto $E_q^+$ along $E_q^-$ restricts to an isomorphism
$E_q\to E_q^+$. Indeed,  a non-zero vector in its kernel would be both positive
and negative for $H_q$, so the projection $E_q\to E_q^+$ is injective, whence an isomorphism as dimensions coincide. 

This gives a continuous family of isomorphisms, and its top exterior power
transfers the nowhere-zero section for $\det E_q$ to one for $\det E_q^+$. 
This contradicts \cref{prop:topology}. We have thus established that the standing assumption $\nu\not \perp \operatorname{leb}$ is false, in other terms $\nu \perp \operatorname{leb}$ as desired.

<!-- lemma-14: body_md -->
\label{lem:torsion-rational}
The matrix $A(q)$ defined by \eqref{eq:walk-matrix} with $\mu_0$ in place
of $\mu$ has its entries belonging to a ring
$\mathbb C[z^{\pm1},w^{\pm1},F^{-1}]$ with $F\in \mathbb C[z^{\pm1},w^{\pm1}]$ nonzero on $\mathbb T^2$.
<!-- end lemma-14 -->
<!-- proof-19: body_md -->
Put $\mathcal L=\mathbb C[z^{\pm1},w^{\pm1}]$, and let
$e_1,\ldots,e_m$ be the standard basis of $V$. Write
\[
 \chi=\chi_q,\qquad \widetilde\chi=\chi_{q^{-1}},\qquad
 b_j=\sigma_{q,e_j},\qquad
 \widetilde b_i=\sigma_{q^{-1},e_i}.
\]
For each $i,j$, consider the tensor product of affine representations
\[
 \varrho_{ij}
 =
 \begin{pmatrix}\chi&b_j\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
 \otimes
 \begin{pmatrix}\widetilde\chi&\widetilde b_i\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
 =
 \begin{pmatrix}
 1&\chi\widetilde b_i&b_j\widetilde\chi&b_j\widetilde b_i\\
 0&\chi&0&b_j\\
 0&0&\widetilde\chi&\widetilde b_i\\
 0&0&0&1
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Set $K^{ij}=K_{\varrho_{ij}}$. Its entries belong to $\mathcal L$
by \cref{lem:word-coordinates}, since the edge labels form a fixed
finite set.

We first justify the inverses in \eqref{eq:resolvent}.
On $\mathbb T^2$, characters have modulus one and edge-cocycle values are
uniformly bounded. Thus, for any sequence of edge labels
$h_1,\ldots,h_n$, the cocycle identity gives
\[
 \sup_{q\in\mathbb T^2}
 \lVert\varrho_{ij}(h_1\cdots h_n)\rVert
 \le C(1+n)^2.
\]
Combining this with the exponential survival estimate in
\cref{lem:returns}, and summing over paths staying in $T$, yields
\[
 \sup_{q\in\mathbb T^2}\lVert(K^{ij}_{TT})^n\rVert
 \le C(1+n)^2r^n,\qquad 0<r<1.
\]
Hence the Neumann series converges uniformly on $\mathbb T^2$, and
\[
 D(q):=\prod_{i,j}\det(I-K^{ij}_{TT}(q))\in\mathcal L
\]
has no zero there. When $T=\varnothing$, take $D=1$.
By \eqref{eq:resolvent} and the adjugate formula, the moment matrices
\[
 C_{ij}(q):=\mathbb E_{\mu_0}\varrho_{ij}
\]
satisfy $DC_{ij}\in M_4(\mathcal L)$.

It remains to center the cocycles. Set
\[
 \lambda=\mathbb E_{\mu_0}\chi,\qquad
 \widetilde\lambda=\mathbb E_{\mu_0}\widetilde\chi.
\]
These are diagonal entries of $C_{ij}$.
Since $\operatorname{supp}\mu_0$ generates $\Gamma_0$ and
$\chi(a_2)=\widetilde\chi(a_2)=-1$, \cref{lem:centered} gives
\[
 (1-\lambda(q))(1-\widetilde\lambda(q))\ne0
 \qquad(q\in\mathbb T^2).
\]
Consequently, the Laurent polynomials
\[
 f_+=D(1-\lambda),\qquad
 f_-=D(1-\widetilde\lambda),\qquad
 F=Df_+f_-
\]
have no zero on $\mathbb T^2$. In the ring $R=\mathcal L[F^{-1}]$, we have
\[
 c_j:=\frac{\mathbb E_{\mu_0}b_j}{1-\lambda}\in R,\qquad
 \widetilde c_i:=
 \frac{\mathbb E_{\mu_0}\widetilde b_i}{1-\widetilde\lambda}\in R,
\]
because the numerators are entries of $C_{ij}$.

On $\mathbb T^2$, conjugation gives
$\widetilde b_i=\overline{b_i}$ and
$\widetilde c_i=\overline{c_i}$.
Expanding the centered covariance therefore gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 A_{ij}
 &=\mathbb E_{\mu_0}\left[
   \bigl(b_j+(\chi-1)c_j\bigr)
   \bigl(\widetilde b_i+
         (\widetilde\chi-1)\widetilde c_i\bigr)\right]\\
 &=(C_{ij})_{14}
     +c_j(C_{ij})_{12}
     +\widetilde c_i(C_{ij})_{13}\in R.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The last equality uses
$\chi\widetilde\chi=1$,
$\mathbb E_{\mu_0}b_j=(1-\lambda)c_j$, and
$\mathbb E_{\mu_0}\widetilde b_i
 =(1-\widetilde\lambda)\widetilde c_i$.
This proves the assertion.
