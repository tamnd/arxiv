\label{thm:A}
Assume \textup{(H1)--(H4)}. Let $u_0\inL^1(\Omega)$ with $u_0\ge0$ and $U_0:=\int_\Omega u_0\,\mathrm{d} x>0$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), itemsep=3pt]
\item \eqref{eq:model} has a unique global mild solution $u\in C([0,\infty);L^1(\Omega))$ with $u(0)=u_0$; it is nonnegative.
\item The total population $U(t)$ is continuously differentiable and monotone, and $U(t)\toU^{*}$ as $t\to\infty$.
\item There are constants $C\ge1$ and $\lambda>0$, depending only on $\ell$, $D$ and $v$, such that for all $t\ge0$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:star}
\bigl\|u(t)-U(t)\psi\bigr\|\le Ce^{-\lambda t}\bigl\|u_0-U_0\psi\bigr\|+2C\int_0^te^{-\lambda(t-s)}\lvert U'(s)\rvert\,\mathrm{d} s ,
\end{equation}
and the right-hand side tends to zero. Hence $u(t)\toU^{*}\psi$ in $L^1(\Omega)$. One can take $\lambda=\lambda_1$, the spectral gap of the switching dynamics (Lemma~\ref{lem:gap}), and no larger $\lambda$ is possible in general (Remark~\ref{rem:constants}).
\item (\emph{Asymptotic equivalence}) For every $f\inL^1(\Omega)$ with $\int_\Omega f\,\mathrm{d} x=U^{*}$, the solution $u^*(t)=S(t)f$ of the linear equation satisfies $\lVert u(t)-u^*(t)\rVert\to0$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- maincor-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:landscape}
If $v=-P'$ for a potential $P\in C^2([0,\ell])$, the limit in Theorem~\ref{thm:A} is
\[
U^{*}\psi(x)=\frac{U^{*}}{Z}\exp\Bigl(-\int_0^x\frac{P'(y)}{D(y)}\,\mathrm{d} y\Bigr),
\]
and for constant $D$ this is the Boltzmann-type density $U^{*} Z^{-1}e^{-P(x)/D}$ (with a different normalising constant $Z$).

Let $r:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to[0,r_{\max}]$ be continuous, with $r(x,t)\ger_{\min}>0$ for all $x$ in some bounded interval $J$ of positive length and all $t\ge t_r$, for some $t_r\ge0$. <!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:markov}
A family $\{S(t)\}_{t\ge0}$ of bounded linear operators on $L^1(\Omega)$ is a \emph{Markov semigroup} if:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(S\arabic*)}, leftmargin=3em, itemsep=2pt]
\item (positivity) $f\ge0\ \Rightarrow\ S(t)f\ge0$;
\item (mass conservation) $\int_\Omega S(t)f\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_\Omega f\,\mathrm{d} x$ for all $f\inL^1(\Omega)$;
\item (semigroup) $S(0)=I$ and $S(t+s)=S(t)S(s)$;
\item (strong continuity at $0$) $\lVert S(t)f-f\rVert\to0$ as $t\downarrow0$, for every $f\inL^1(\Omega)$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:markov}
Let $S$ be a Markov semigroup. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=2pt]
\item $\lVert S(t)f\rVert\le\lVert f\rVert$ for all $f$, with equality if $f\ge0$ (\emph{contraction}).
\item If $\int_\Omega f=0$ then $\int_\Omega S(t)f=0$: the zero-mass subspace $L^1_0(\Omega):=\{f\inL^1(\Omega):\int_\Omega f\,\mathrm{d} x=0\}$ is invariant.
\item $(t,f)\mapsto S(t)f$ is jointly continuous from $[0,\infty)\timesL^1(\Omega)$ to $L^1(\Omega)$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
(i) For $f\ge0$, $\lVert S(t)f\rVert=\int S(t)f=\int f=\lVert f\rVert$ by (S1)--(S2). For general $f$, write $f=f^+-f^-$ with $f^\pm\ge0$ and $\lvert f\rvert=f^++f^-$. By (S1), $S(t)f^\pm\ge0$, so pointwise a.e.
\[
\lvert S(t)f\rvert=\lvert S(t)f^+-S(t)f^-\rvert\le S(t)f^++S(t)f^-=S(t)\lvert f\rvert ,
\]
and integrating, $\lVert S(t)f\rVert\le\int S(t)\lvert f\rvert=\int\lvert f\rvert=\lVert f\rVert$. This is the continuum version of the computation $\lvert e^{tA}v\rvert_1\le\sum_{i,j}(e^{tA})_{ij}\lvert v_j\rvert=\lvert v\rvert_1$ for compartmental models.

(ii) is (S2).

(iii) First, $t\mapsto S(t)f$ is continuous for each $f$. Right continuity: $S(t+h)f-S(t)f=S(t)(S(h)f-f)$ has norm at most $\lVert S(h)f-f\rVert\to0$, by (i) and (S4). Left continuity: for $0<h\le t$, $S(t-h)f-S(t)f=S(t-h)(f-S(h)f)$, with the same bound. Then
\[
\lVert S(t)f-S(t_0)f_0\rVert\le\lVert S(t)(f-f_0)\rVert+\lVert S(t)f_0-S(t_0)f_0\rVert\le\lVert f-f_0\rVert+\lVert S(t)f_0-S(t_0)f_0\rVert,
\]
which tends to zero as $(t,f)\to(t_0,f_0)$.

<!-- blackbox-1: body_md -->
\label{bb:green}
Assume \textup{(H3)}. There is a continuous function $\Gamma:[0,\ell]\times[0,\ell]\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ with the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), itemsep=3pt]
\item \emph{(Existence and uniqueness of classical solutions.)} For every $f\in C([0,\ell])$, the function
\[
u(x,t)=\int_0^\ell\Gamma(x,y,t)f(y)\,\mathrm{d} y\quad(t>0),\qquad u(x,0)=f(x),
\]
is continuous on $[0,\ell]\times[0,\infty)$; $\partial_tu$, $\partial_xu$, $\partial_{xx}u$ exist and are continuous on $(0,\ell)\times(0,\infty)$; $\partial_xu$ extends continuously to $[0,\ell]\times(0,\infty)$; $u$ satisfies $\partial_tu=\mathcal Lu$ in $(0,\ell)\times(0,\infty)$ and the no-flux condition \eqref{eq:noflux} for $t>0$. Moreover, it is the only function with these properties.
\item \emph{(Strict positivity.)} $\Gamma(x,y,t)>0$ for all $x,y\in[0,\ell]$ and $t>0$.
\item \emph{(Chapman--Kolmogorov.)} $\Gamma(x,y,t+s)=\int_0^\ell\Gamma(x,z,t)\,\Gamma(z,y,s)\,\mathrm{d} z$ for all $x,y$ and $t,s>0$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end blackbox-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
Property (b) is the counterpart of Lemma~A.1(ii) of \cite{Review}, namely: for an irreducible transition matrix $A$, \emph{all entries of $e^{\tau A}$ are positive}. There, positivity came from the existence of chains of transitions between any two compartments; here it comes from $D>0$ everywhere, which lets diffusion connect any two phenotypes in any positive time. If $D$ vanished on a sub-interval, cells could not cross it by diffusion, and property (b) could fail.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:mass-lin}
$\displaystyle\int_0^\ell\Gamma(x,y,t)\,\mathrm{d} x=1$ for all $y\in[0,\ell]$ and $t>0$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $f\in C([0,\ell])$ and let $u$ be the classical solution of Theorem~\ref{bb:green}(a). Write $J_{\rm flux}(x,t)=D(x)\partial_xu(x,t)-v(x)u(x,t)$ for the flux, so that $\partial_tu=\partial_xJ_{\rm flux}$; by Theorem~\ref{bb:green}(a), $J_{\rm flux}$ is continuous on $[0,\ell]\times(0,\infty)$ and vanishes at $x=0,\ell$. Fix $0<t_1<t_2$ and $0<a<b<\ell$. Since $u$ is smooth on $[a,b]\times[t_1,t_2]$, integrating $\partial_tu=\partial_xJ_{\rm flux}$ over this rectangle gives
\[
\int_a^b\bigl(u(x,t_2)-u(x,t_1)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\bigl(J_{\rm flux}(b,t)-J_{\rm flux}(a,t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d} t .
\]
Let $a\downarrow0$ and $b\uparrow\ell$. The left-hand side tends to $\int_0^\ell(u(x,t_2)-u(x,t_1))\,\mathrm{d} x$, and the right-hand side tends to $\int_{t_1}^{t_2}(J_{\rm flux}(\ell,t)-J_{\rm flux}(0,t))\,\mathrm{d} t=0$ by continuity of $J_{\rm flux}$ up to the boundary and by the no-flux condition. Hence $\int_0^\ell u(x,t)\,\mathrm{d} x$ is constant for $t>0$; since $u$ is uniformly continuous on $[0,\ell]\times[0,1]$, it tends to $\int_0^\ell f\,\mathrm{d} x$ as $t\downarrow0$. Therefore, for every $t>0$,
\[
\int_0^\ell f(y)\Bigl(\int_0^\ell\Gamma(x,y,t)\,\mathrm{d} x\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d} y=\int_0^\ell u(x,t)\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_0^\ell f(y)\,\mathrm{d} y,
\]
where we used Fubini's theorem ($\Gamma$ is bounded). Since this holds for every continuous $f$, and $y\mapsto\int_0^\ell\Gamma(x,y,t)\,\mathrm{d} x$ is continuous, this function is identically $1$.

<!-- proposition-1: body_md -->
\label{prop:S-markov}
Under \textup{(H3)}, the operators \eqref{eq:S} form a Markov semigroup on $L^1(\Omega)$. For $f\in C([0,\ell])$, $u(x,t)=(S(t)f)(x)$ is the classical solution of \eqref{eq:linear}--\eqref{eq:noflux} with initial datum $f$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
(S1) follows from $\Gamma>0$. (S2): by Fubini and Lemma~\ref{lem:mass-lin},
\[
\int_\Omega S(t)f\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_\Omega f(y)\Bigl(\int_\Omega\Gamma(x,y,t)\,\mathrm{d} x\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d} y=\int_\Omega f\,\mathrm{d} y .
\] (S3): by Chapman--Kolmogorov and Fubini, $S(t)S(s)f=S(t+s)f$ for $t,s>0$. (S4): by (S1)--(S2), each $S(t)$ is an $L^1$ contraction (Lemma~\ref{lem:markov}(i), whose proof uses only (S1)--(S2)). For continuous $f$, $S(t)f\to f$ uniformly on $[0,\ell]$ as $t\downarrow0$, by uniform continuity of the classical solution on $[0,\ell]\times[0,1]$; hence also in $L^1(\Omega)$. For $f\inL^1(\Omega)$, choose $f_k$ continuous with $\lVert f-f_k\rVert\to0$; then
\[
\lVert S(t)f-f\rVert\le\lVert S(t)(f-f_k)\rVert+\lVert S(t)f_k-f_k\rVert+\lVert f_k-f\rVert\le2\lVert f-f_k\rVert+\lVert S(t)f_k-f_k\rVert,
\]
so $\limsup_{t\downarrow0}\lVert S(t)f-f\rVert\le2\lVert f-f_k\rVert$ for every $k$, which gives (S4).

The density $\psi$ of \eqref{eq:psi} belongs to $C^2([0,\ell])$, is strictly positive, and satisfies $S(t)\psi=\psi$ for all $t\ge0$. It is the only probability density with this property.

The flux $D\psi'-v\psi$ is identically zero, so $\mathcal L\psi=\partial_x(D\psi'-v\psi)=0$, and \eqref{eq:noflux} holds. <!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:mix}
Assume \textup{(H3)}, fix $\tau>0$, and let $c:=\min_{x,y\in[0,\ell]}\Gamma(x,y,\tau)$. Then $0<c\le1/\ell$, and with $\bar c:=\min\{c,\,1/(2\ell)\}$,
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=3pt]
\item $\lVert S(\tau)w\rVert\le(1-\ell\bar c)\lVert w\rVert$ for every $w\inL^1_0(\Omega)$;
\item for all $t\ge0$ and $w\inL^1_0(\Omega)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mix}
\lVert S(t)w\rVert\le C\,e^{-\lambda t}\lVert w\rVert,\qquad C:=\frac1{1-\ell\bar c},\quad\lambda:=-\frac1\tau\ln(1-\ell\bar c)>0 ;
\end{equation}
\item $\psi$ is the only probability density $\phi$ with $S(t)\phi=\phi$ for all $t$, and $S(t)f\to\bigl(\int_\Omega f\bigr)\psi$ in $L^1(\Omega)$ for every $f\inL^1(\Omega)$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
\emph{The constant $c$.} $\Gamma(\cdot,\cdot,\tau)$ is continuous and strictly positive on the compact square $[0,\ell]^2$ (Theorem~\ref{bb:green}(b)), so its minimum $c$ is attained and positive. By Lemma~\ref{lem:mass-lin}, $1=\int_0^\ell\Gamma(x,y,\tau)\,\mathrm{d} x\ge c\,\ell$, so $c\le1/\ell$. We use $\bar c\le c$, which satisfies $\ell\bar c<1$ strictly; replacing $c$ by the smaller $\bar c$ only weakens the lower bound $\Gamma\ge c$, so nothing is lost below. (This is the counterpart of $n\epsilon\le1$ in the compartmental case.)

(i) Let $w\inL^1_0(\Omega)$ and write $w=w^+-w^-$ with $w^\pm=\max\{\pm w,0\}\ge0$. Since $\int w=0$, the two parts carry the same mass:
\[
\int_\Omega w^+\,\mathrm{d} x-\int_\Omega w^-\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_\Omega w\,\mathrm{d} x=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\int_\Omega w^+\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_\Omega w^-\,\mathrm{d} x=\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert .
\]
Because $\Gamma(x,y,\tau)\ge\bar c$ for all $x,y$ and $w^+\ge0$,
\[
\bigl(S(\tau)w^+\bigr)(x)=\int_0^\ell\Gamma(x,y,\tau)w^+(y)\,\mathrm{d} y\ \ge\ \bar c\int_0^\ell w^+(y)\,\mathrm{d} y=\tfrac{\bar c}2\lVert w\rVert\qquad\text{for every }x\in[0,\ell],
\]
and likewise for $w^-$. So the functions
\[
h_+:=S(\tau)w^+-\tfrac{\bar c}2\lVert w\rVert,\qquad h_-:=S(\tau)w^--\tfrac{\bar c}2\lVert w\rVert
\]
are nonnegative (we subtract, from each, a constant floor that both exceed at every phenotype). By mass conservation (S2) applied to $w^+$ and to $w^-$, their integrals are
\[
\int_\Omega h_+\,\mathrm{d} x=\underbrace{\int_\Omega S(\tau)w^+\,\mathrm{d} x}_{=\int w^+=\frac12\lVert w\rVert}-\ \ell\cdot\tfrac{\bar c}2\lVert w\rVert=\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert\,(1-\ell\bar c),
\]
and the same for $h_-$. Since $h_\pm\ge0$, their integrals are their $L^1$ norms. Finally $S(\tau)w=S(\tau)w^+-S(\tau)w^-=h_+-h_-$ (the floors cancel), so
\[
\lVert S(\tau)w\rVert=\lVert h_+-h_-\rVert\le\lVert h_+\rVert+\lVert h_-\rVert=(1-\ell\bar c)\lVert w\rVert .
\]

(ii) By Lemma~\ref{lem:markov}(ii), $S(\tau)w$ again has zero mass, so (i) can be applied to it, then to $S(\tau)^2w=S(2\tau)w$, and so on: $\lVert S(m\tau)w\rVert\le(1-\ell\bar c)^m\lVert w\rVert$ for every integer $m\ge0$. For $t\ge0$ write $t=m\tau+s$ with $m=\lfloor t/\tau\rfloor$ and $0\le s<\tau$; by the semigroup property and the contraction property (Lemma~\ref{lem:markov}(i), which needs no zero-mass condition) applied over the remaining time $s$,
\[
\lVert S(t)w\rVert=\lVert S(s)S(m\tau)w\rVert\le\lVert S(m\tau)w\rVert\le(1-\ell\bar c)^m\lVert w\rVert\le(1-\ell\bar c)^{t/\tau-1}\lVert w\rVert=Ce^{-\lambda t}\lVert w\rVert .
\]

(iii) If $\phi$ is a probability density with $S(t)\phi=\phi$, then $\phi-\psi\inL^1_0(\Omega)$ is fixed by every $S(t)$, so $\lVert \phi-\psi\rVert=\lVert S(t)(\phi-\psi)\rVert\le Ce^{-\lambda t}\lVert \phi-\psi\rVert\to0$; hence $\phi=\psi$. For $f\inL^1(\Omega)$, $f-(\int f)\psi\inL^1_0(\Omega)$ and $S(t)f-(\int f)\psi=S(t)\bigl(f-(\int f)\psi\bigr)$, whose norm tends to zero by (ii).

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:gap}
Assume \textup{(H3)}, and let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
\lambda_1:=\inf\Bigl\{\frac{\int_0^\ell D\psi\,(w')^2\,\mathrm{d} x}{\int_0^\ell w^2\,\psi\,\mathrm{d} x}:\ w\in C^1([0,\ell]),\ \int_0^\ell w\,\psi\,\mathrm{d} x=0,\ w\ne0\Bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Then $\lambda_1\ge\bigl(\int_0^\ell\frac{\,\mathrm{d} x}{D\psi}\bigr)^{-1}>0$, and there is $C_1\ge1$, depending only on $\ell$, $D$ and $v$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mix-gap}
\lVert S(t)w\rVert\le C_1e^{-\lambda_1t}\lVert w\rVert\qquad\text{for all }t\ge0\text{ and }w\inL^1_0(\Omega) .
\end{equation}
The rate $\lambda_1$ is optimal: there is $w\inL^1_0(\Omega)$, $w\ne0$, with $S(t)w=e^{-\lambda_1t}w$ for all $t\ge0$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
\emph{Positivity of $\lambda_1$.} Let $w\in C^1([0,\ell])$ with $\int w\psi=0$. Since $\psi>0$, $w$ vanishes at some point $x_0$, and by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, for every $x\in[0,\ell]$,
\[
w(x)^2=\Bigl(\int_{x_0}^xw'\,\mathrm{d} y\Bigr)^2\le\int_0^\ell D\psi\,(w')^2\,\mathrm{d} y\,\int_0^\ell\frac{\,\mathrm{d} y}{D\psi} .
\]
Multiplying by $\psi(x)$ and integrating over $[0,\ell]$ gives the lower bound on $\lambda_1$, since $\int\psi=1$.

\emph{Energy decay.} Let $f\in C([0,\ell])$ with $\int f=0$, let $u(x,t)=(S(t)f)(x)$ be the classical solution (Proposition~\ref{prop:S-markov}), and let $w=u/\psi$. By \eqref{eq:flux-w}, $\partial_tu=\partial_x(a\,\partial_xw)$ with $a:=D\psi$, and $\partial_xw=0$ at $x=0,\ell$ for $t>0$; moreover $w$ and $\partial_xw$ are continuous on $[0,\ell]\times(0,\infty)$. For $0<t_1<t_2$ and $0<\alpha<\beta<\ell$, integrate the identity
\[
\partial_t\bigl(\psi w^2\bigr)=2w\,\partial_x\bigl(a\,\partial_xw\bigr)=2\,\partial_x\bigl(w\,a\,\partial_xw\bigr)-2a\,(\partial_xw)^2
\]
over $[\alpha,\beta]\times[t_1,t_2]$, and let $\alpha\downarrow0$, $\beta\uparrow\ell$ as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:mass-lin}; the boundary terms vanish by the Neumann condition, and
\[
E(t_2)-E(t_1)=-2\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_0^\ell a\,(\partial_xw)^2\,\mathrm{d} x\,\mathrm{d} t,\qquad E(t):=\int_0^\ell\psi w^2\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_0^\ell\frac{u(x,t)^2}{\psi(x)}\,\mathrm{d} x .
\]
The inner integral is continuous in $t$, so $E\in C^1((0,\infty))$ with $E'(t)=-2\int_0^\ell a\,(\partial_xw)^2\,\mathrm{d} x$. Since $\int_0^\ell w(t)\psi\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_0^\ell u(t)\,\mathrm{d} x=0$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:mass-lin}) and $w(\cdot,t)\in C^1([0,\ell])$, the definition of $\lambda_1$ gives $E'\le-2\lambda_1E$, hence $E(t)\le e^{-2\lambda_1(t-s)}E(s)$ for $0<s\le t$.

\emph{From $L^2$ to $L^1$.} By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and $\int\psi=1$, $\lVert g\rVert\le\bigl(\int_0^\ell g^2/\psi\,\mathrm{d} x\bigr)^{1/2}$ for every $g$, so $\lVert S(t)f\rVert\le E(t)^{1/2}$. Let $\Gamma^*:=\max_{x,y}\Gamma(x,y,1)$ and $\psi_{\min}:=\min\psi>0$. Then $\lvert u(x,1)\rvert\le\Gamma^*\lVert f\rVert$ for all $x$, so $E(1)\le\ell\,(\Gamma^*)^2\lVert f\rVert^2/\psi_{\min}$, and for $t\ge1$
\[
\lVert S(t)f\rVert\le e^{-\lambda_1(t-1)}E(1)^{1/2}\le e^{\lambda_1}\Gamma^*\sqrt{\ell/\psi_{\min}}\;e^{-\lambda_1t}\lVert f\rVert ,
\]
while for $0\le t<1$, $\lVert S(t)f\rVert\le\lVert f\rVert\le e^{\lambda_1}e^{-\lambda_1t}\lVert f\rVert$. This is \eqref{eq:mix-gap} for continuous $f$, with $C_1:=e^{\lambda_1}\max\{1,\Gamma^*\sqrt{\ell/\psi_{\min}}\}$. A general $w\inL^1_0(\Omega)$ is the $L^1(\Omega)$-limit of continuous functions $f_k$, hence also of the continuous zero-mass functions $f_k-(\int f_k)\psi$, and \eqref{eq:mix-gap} passes to the limit because $S(t)$ is continuous on $L^1(\Omega)$.

\emph{Optimality.} By classical Sturm--Liouville theory \cite{CoddingtonLevinson1955}, the infimum in \eqref{eq:gap} is the smallest positive eigenvalue of the Neumann problem $-\psi^{-1}(D\psi\,e')'=\lambda e$ on $(0,\ell)$, $e'(0)=e'(\ell)=0$, and it is attained at an eigenfunction $e_1\in C^2([0,\ell])$, which is orthogonal to the constants in $L^2(\psi\,\mathrm{d} x)$: $\int e_1\psi=0$. By \eqref{eq:flux-w}, $u(x,t)=e^{-\lambda_1t}\psi(x)e_1(x)$ has the properties listed in Theorem~\ref{bb:green}(a), so $S(t)(\psi e_1)=e^{-\lambda_1t}\psi e_1$ by uniqueness, and $w:=\psi e_1\inL^1_0(\Omega)$.

<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
\label{def:mild}
Let $u_0\inL^1(\Omega)$ with $\int u_0>0$ and $0<T\le\infty$. A \emph{mild solution} of \eqref{eq:model} on $[0,T)$ is a continuous $u:[0,T)\toL^1(\Omega)$ with $U(t):=\int_\Omega u(t)\,\mathrm{d} x>0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mild}
u(t)=S(t)u_0+\int_0^tS(t-s)F(s)\,\mathrm{d} s,\qquad F(s):=N\bigl(s,u(s)\bigr)=g\bigl(U(s)\bigr)r(\cdot,s)u(s),
\end{equation}
for all $t\in[0,T)$.

<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
\label{rem:classical}
If $u$ is a classical solution of \eqref{eq:model} with the no-flux condition, then $u$ is a mild solution: for fixed $t$, differentiate $s\mapsto S(t-s)u(s)$ and integrate from $0$ to $t$, exactly as one derives the variation-of-constants formula for ODEs. Conversely, for smooth $u_0$ and $r$, the mild solution is classical, by parabolic regularity \cite[Ch.~6]{Pazy1983}. We never need this converse.

<!-- proposition-4: body_md -->
\label{prop:global}
Let $u_0\ge0$ with $U_0>0$. Then \eqref{eq:model} has a unique global mild solution $u\in C([0,\infty);L^1(\Omega))$; it is nonnegative. Its total population $U(t)=\int_\Omega u(t)\,\mathrm{d} x$ is continuously differentiable, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Uode}
U'(t)=\rho(t)\,g\bigl(U(t)\bigr),\qquad\rho(t):=\int_\Omega r(x,t)u(x,t)\,\mathrm{d} x\ \ge0 ,
\end{equation}
and $U$ is monotone, with values between $U_0$ and $U^{*}$. In particular $U(t)\ge m:=\min\{U_0,U^{*}\}>0$, $\lVert u(t)\rVert=U(t)\le M:=\max\{U_0,U^{*}\}$, $U(t)$ converges to a limit $U_\infty\in[m,M]$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:TV}
\int_0^\infty\lvert U'(t)\rvert\,\mathrm{d} t=\lvert U_\infty-U_0\rvert<\infty .
\end{equation}

\emph{The ODE for $U$.} Let $u$ be a mild solution on some interval $[0,T)$ (for instance the local one of Proposition~\ref{prop:local} with $t_0=0$, $\phi_0=u_0$, $a=U_0/2$, $R=\lVert u_0\rVert$). It is nonnegative by Proposition~\ref{prop:positive}. Integrate \eqref{eq:mild} over $\Omega$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:riemann}(i) the mass functional commutes with the time integral, and by mass conservation (S2) it is not changed by $S(t)$ or $S(t-s)$:
\begin{align*}
U(t)&=\int_\Omega S(t)u_0\,\mathrm{d} x+\int_0^t\Bigl(\int_\Omega S(t-s)F(s)\,\mathrm{d} x\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d} s\\
&=U_0+\int_0^t\Bigl(\int_\Omega F(s)\,\mathrm{d} x\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d} s=U_0+\int_0^tg\bigl(U(s)\bigr)\rho(s)\,\mathrm{d} s .
\end{align*}
The integrand $s\mapsto g(U(s))\rho(s)$ is continuous (Lemma~\ref{lem:Fcont} and continuity of the mass functional), so $U$ is $C^1$ and \eqref{eq:Uode} holds. This is exactly the compartmental computation ``summing the equations, the transition terms cancel'': switching does not change the total population.

\emph{Monotonicity.} Regard $\rho$ as a given continuous, nonnegative function of time. Then $U$ solves the scalar ODE $y'=\rho(t)g(y)$, and so does the constant $y\equivU^{*}$, since $g(U^{*})=0$. The right-hand side is locally Lipschitz in $y$ ($g$ is $C^1$), so solutions of this ODE are unique and two of them cannot cross. Hence $U(t)-U^{*}$ has a constant sign (or vanishes identically), and so do $g(U(t))$ and $U'(t)=\rho(t)g(U(t))$. Therefore $U$ is monotone and lies between $U_0$ and $U^{*}$; since $u\ge0$, also $\lVert u(t)\rVert=U(t)$.

\emph{Global existence.} Let $a=m/2$ and $R=M$, and let $T_0=T_0(a,R)$ be given by Proposition~\ref{prop:local}. As long as the solution exists, $\int u(t)=U(t)\ge m=2a$ and $\lVert u(t)\rVert\le M=R$, by what we have just shown. So from any time $t_0$ up to which the solution exists, Proposition~\ref{prop:local} with $\phi_0=u(t_0)$ extends it to $[t_0,t_0+T_0]$, with the \emph{same} $T_0$. Gluing, the solution exists on $[0,\infty)$; it is unique by the uniqueness part of Proposition~\ref{prop:local} applied on successive intervals. Finally, $U$ is monotone and bounded, so $U_\infty=\lim_{t\to\infty}U(t)$ exists, and $\int_0^\infty\lvert U'\rvert=\lvert \int_0^\infty U'\rvert=\lvert U_\infty-U_0\rvert$ because $U'$ does not change sign.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rem:timescale}
Splitting the integral in \eqref{eq:star} at any $T\le t$ gives
\[
\lVert u(t)-U(t)\psi\rVert\le Ce^{-\lambda t}\lVert u_0-U_0\psi\rVert+2Ce^{-\lambda(t-T)}\lvert U(T)-U_0\rvert+2C\lvert U^{*}-U(T)\rvert .
\]
If the population has essentially saturated by time $T$, the distortion caused by fitness differences decays like $e^{-\lambda(t-T)}$ afterwards. The statement ``fitness is irrelevant'' is therefore asymptotic: it holds on time scales long compared with $1/\lambda_1$, the time the switching dynamics needs to forget where cells started. In the example of Section~\ref{subsec:numerics} essentially all growth occurs before $t\approx5$, and the imprint of fitness then decays at the rate $\lambda_1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:numerics}); similarly, in the multi-well numerical example of \cite{Review} essentially all growth occurs before $t\approx10$, and the imprint of the fitness gradient disappears afterwards, on the time scale of the switching dynamics.

<!-- remark-5: body_md -->
\label{rem:treatment}
A treatment that kills cells in a phenotype-dependent way adds a term $-\delta(x,t)u$ to \eqref{eq:model}, which violates (H1). Suppose that $\delta$ is continuous, $0\le\delta\le\delta_{\max}$, and $\delta(\cdot,t)=0$ for $t\ge t_F$. The results of Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear} hold on $[0,t_F]$, with the same proofs, for the reaction term $\bigl(g(U)r-\delta\bigr)u$: there is a unique mild solution, and it is nonnegative. Its total population now satisfies $U'=g(U)\rho-\int_\Omega\delta u\,\mathrm{d} x$, so that $U'\ge-\delta_{\max}U$ while $U\leU^{*}$, and $U'\le0$ while $U\geU^{*}$; hence $U$ stays between $\min\{U_0,U^{*}\}e^{-\delta_{\max}t}$ and $\max\{U_0,U^{*}\}$, the solution exists on $[0,t_F]$, and $u(t_F)\ge0$ has positive mass. From $t_F$ on, the model is again \eqref{eq:model}, and Theorem~\ref{thm:A}, applied with initial time $t_F$, gives $u(t)\toU^{*}\psi$. As in the compartmental case \cite{Review}, a transient treatment shapes the transient, and with it the regrowth of the population, but not the limit; by \eqref{eq:star}, its imprint on the composition fades at the rate $\lambda_1$ once the population has regrown.

<!-- remark-6: body_md -->
\label{rem:replicator}
The composition $p(t):=u(t)/U(t)$ is a probability density. Lemma~\ref{lem:rescale} with $\beta(t)=U_0/U(t)$, for which $\beta'/\beta=-U'/U=-g(U)\,\bar r$ with $\bar r(t):=\int_\Omega r(x,t)p(x,t)\,\mathrm{d} x$, shows that $p$ is the mild solution of
\[
\partial_tp=\mathcal Lp+g\bigl(U(t)\bigr)\bigl(r(x,t)-\bar r(t)\bigr)p ,
\]
a replicator--mutator equation in which selection acts with intensity $g(U(t))$. By \eqref{eq:gint}, the total intensity $\int_0^\infty\lvert g(U(t))\rvert\,\mathrm{d} t$ is finite: whatever the fitness differences, they act during a finite effective time, after which only the switching dynamics remains. More precisely, since $U'=g(U)\,\bar r\,U$, the selection term has norm $\lVert g(U)(r-\bar r)p\rVert\le\lvert g(U)\rvert\int_\Omega(r+\bar r)p\,\mathrm{d} x=2\lvert (\ln U)'\rvert$, and, $U$ being monotone, the total selection that the composition undergoes is at most $2\lvert \ln(U^{*}/U_0)\rvert$: large imprints of fitness require large changes in population size. Under additive competition, $\partial_tu=\mathcal Lu+\bigl(r-d(U)\bigr)u$, with a death rate $d$ that increases with the total population but is the same for all phenotypes (for instance $d(U)=U$, as in Figure~\ref{fig:numerics}), the same computation gives $\partial_tp=\mathcal Lp+(r-\bar r)p$, in which selection acts at full strength forever; for time-independent $r$, $p$ then converges to the normalised principal eigenfunction of $\mathcal L+r$, and the limit depends on $r$ (Figure~\ref{fig:numerics}(d)). This is the continuum counterpart of the compartmental birth--death example discussed in \cite{Review}.

\label{bb:dirichlet}
Let $a\in C^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $\psi\in C(\mathbb{R})$ be strictly positive, with $\int\psi=1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), itemsep=3pt]
\item The form $\mathcal E$ of \eqref{eq:form}, defined on $C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$, is closable in $L^2(\mu)$. Let $(\mathcal E,\mathcal F)$ be its closure. There is a unique nonnegative self-adjoint operator $\mathcal A$ on $L^2(\mu)$ (the Friedrichs extension of $\mathcal A_0$) such that $w\in D(\mathcal A)$ and $\mathcal Aw=h$ if and only if $w\in\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal E(w,\tilde w)=\langle h,\tilde w\rangle_\mu$ for all $\tilde w\in\mathcal F$.
\item $(\mathcal E,\mathcal F)$ is a Dirichlet form. Consequently the self-adjoint contraction semigroup $T(t)=e^{-t\mathcal A}$ on $L^2(\mu)$ is \emph{Markovian}: $0\le w\le1$ implies $0\le T(t)w\le1$; in particular $T(t)$ preserves positivity.
\item Every $w\in\mathcal F$ has a locally absolutely continuous representative with $w'\in L^2_{\rm loc}$, and $\mathcal E(w,w)=\int a\,(w')^2\,\mathrm{d} x$. Every Lipschitz function with compact support belongs to $\mathcal F$.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- proposition-5: body_md -->
\label{prop:SR}
Assume \textup{(R1)--(R3)}, and for $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ with $\int f^2/\psi\,\mathrm{d} x<\infty$ put
\[
S(t)f:=\psi\,T(t)\bigl(f/\psi\bigr).
\]
Then $S(t)$ extends uniquely to a Markov semigroup on $L^1(\mathbb{R})$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=2pt]
\item $S(t)\psi=\psi$ for all $t\ge0$;
\item $S$ is asymptotically stable: \eqref{eq:E} holds;
\item $S$ solves the Fokker--Planck equation weakly: for $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $\zeta\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$,
\[
\int_\mathbb{R}\bigl(S(t)f\bigr)\zeta\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_\mathbb{R} f\zeta\,\mathrm{d} x+\int_0^t\int_\mathbb{R}\bigl(S(s)f\bigr)\,\mathcal L^*\zeta\,\mathrm{d} x\,\mathrm{d} s,\qquad\mathcal L^*\zeta:=(D\zeta')'+v\zeta' .
\]
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end proposition-5 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
\emph{Step (a): no loss of mass. This is where (R3) is used.} We show that the constant function $\mathbf 1$ belongs to $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal E(\mathbf 1,\mathbf 1)=0$. Let $\eta(x)=\int_0^x\,\mathrm{d} y/a(y)$ be the \emph{scale function}. It is increasing, and by (R3) it maps $\mathbb{R}$ onto $\mathbb{R}$. For $0<n<N$, let $\phi_{n,N}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]$ be the piecewise linear function equal to $1$ on $[\eta(-n),\eta(n)]$, to $0$ outside $[\eta(-N),\eta(N)]$, and linear in between, and set $w_{n,N}(x)=\phi_{n,N}(\eta(x))$. This is a Lipschitz function with compact support, so $w_{n,N}\in\mathcal F$ by Theorem~\ref{bb:dirichlet}(c). Since $w_{n,N}'=\phi_{n,N}'(\eta(x))/a(x)$, the change of variables $\xi=\eta(x)$, $\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\,\mathrm{d} x/a$, gives
\[
\mathcal E(w_{n,N},w_{n,N})=\int_\mathbb{R} a\Bigl(\frac{\phi_{n,N}'(\eta(x))}{a(x)}\Bigr)^2\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_\mathbb{R}\phi_{n,N}'(\xi)^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac1{\eta(N)-\eta(n)}+\frac1{\eta(-n)-\eta(-N)} .
\]
By (R3), $\eta(\pm N)\to\pm\infty$, so we can choose $N=N_n$ with $\mathcal E(w_n,w_n)\le1/n$, where $w_n:=w_{n,N_n}$. Then $0\le w_n\le1$ and $w_n\to1$ pointwise, so $w_n\to\mathbf 1$ in $L^2(\mu)$ by dominated convergence ($\mu$ is finite). Hence $(w_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence for the norm $\bigl(\mathcal E(\cdot,\cdot)+\lVert \cdot\rVert_{L^2(\mu)}^2\bigr)^{1/2}$, because $\mathcal E(w_n-w_m,w_n-w_m)\le2\mathcal E(w_n,w_n)+2\mathcal E(w_m,w_m)$. Since the form is closed, $\mathbf 1\in\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal E(\mathbf 1,\mathbf 1)=\lim\mathcal E(w_n,w_n)=0$. By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for $\mathcal E$, $\mathcal E(\mathbf 1,\tilde w)=0$ for every $\tilde w\in\mathcal F$, so Theorem~\ref{bb:dirichlet}(a) gives $\mathbf 1\in D(\mathcal A)$ with $\mathcal A\mathbf 1=0$, and therefore
\[
T(t)\mathbf 1=e^{-t\mathcal A}\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1\qquad\text{for all }t\ge0 .
\]
This is the continuum counterpart of the columns of $e^{tA}$ summing to one. Without (R3), the cut-offs could not be made to have small energy, and in general mass is lost through infinity.

\emph{Step (b): a Markov semigroup on $L^1$.} Let $w\in L^2(\mu)$. By positivity, $\lvert T(t)w\rvert\le T(t)\lvert w\rvert$ (as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:markov}(i)); by self-adjointness and Step~(a),
\[
\int_\mathbb{R} T(t)\lvert w\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\langle T(t)\lvert w\rvert,\mathbf 1\rangle_\mu=\langle\lvert w\rvert,T(t)\mathbf 1\rangle_\mu=\int_\mathbb{R}\lvert w\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mu,\qquad\int_\mathbb{R} T(t)w\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_\mathbb{R} w\,\mathrm{d}\mu .
\]
So $T(t)$ is a contraction for the norm of $L^1(\mu)$ and preserves $\int\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mu$. Since $\mu$ is finite, $L^2(\mu)$ is dense in $L^1(\mu)$, and $T(t)$ extends uniquely to a positive, mass-preserving contraction on $L^1(\mu)$; the semigroup property passes to the extension. For $w\in L^2(\mu)$, $\lVert T(t)w-w\rVert_{L^1(\mu)}\le\lVert T(t)w-w\rVert_{L^2(\mu)}\to0$ as $t\downarrow0$ (Cauchy--Schwarz, $\mu(\mathbb{R})=1$), and by density and contractivity this holds for every $w\in L^1(\mu)$. Finally, $f\mapsto f/\psi$ is an isometry from $L^1(\,\mathrm{d} x)$ onto $L^1(\mu)$ that preserves positivity and maps $\int f\,\mathrm{d} x$ to $\int(f/\psi)\,\mathrm{d}\mu$. Conjugating by it, $S(t)f=\psi\,T(t)(f/\psi)$ is a Markov semigroup on $L^1(\mathbb{R})$, and $S(t)\psi=\psi\,T(t)\mathbf 1=\psi$, which is (i).

\emph{Step (c): the switching dynamics forgets its initial state.} If $\mathcal Aw=0$, then $\int a\,(w')^2\,\mathrm{d} x=\mathcal E(w,w)=\langle\mathcal Aw,w\rangle_\mu=0$, so $w'=0$ a.e.\ (because $a>0$) and $w$ is constant (Theorem~\ref{bb:dirichlet}(c)). Thus $\ker\mathcal A=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf 1\}$, and the orthogonal projection onto it is $P_0w=\langle w,\mathbf 1\rangle_\mu\mathbf 1=\bigl(\int w\,\mathrm{d}\mu\bigr)\mathbf 1$. Let $E_w$ be the spectral measure of $\mathcal A$ associated with $w$, a finite measure on $[0,\infty)$ whose atom at $0$ is $\lVert P_0w\rVert^2$. By the spectral theorem \cite{ReedSimon1980},
\[
\lVert T(t)w-P_0w\rVert_{L^2(\mu)}^2=\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-2t\xi}\,E_w(\,\mathrm{d}\xi)\longrightarrow0\qquad(t\to\infty),
\]
by dominated convergence (the integrand is at most $1$ and tends to $0$ for every $\xi>0$). Now let $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ with $\int f^2/\psi<\infty$, and $w=f/\psi\in L^2(\mu)$. Since $\int w\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int f\,\mathrm{d} x$, the isometry of Step~(b) and Cauchy--Schwarz give
\[
\Bigl\|S(t)f-\Bigl(\int f\Bigr)\psi\Bigr\|_{L^1(\mathbb{R})}=\Bigl\|T(t)w-\int w\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Bigr\|_{L^1(\mu)}\le\lVert T(t)w-P_0w\rVert_{L^2(\mu)}\longrightarrow0 .
\]
Such $f$ are dense in $L^1(\mathbb{R})$ (they include the bounded functions with compact support, since $\psi$ is bounded below on compact sets). For general $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $f_k$ as above with $\lVert f-f_k\rVert\to0$,
\begin{align*}
\Bigl\|S(t)f-\Bigl(\int f\Bigr)\psi\Bigr\|&\le\lVert S(t)(f-f_k)\rVert+\Bigl\|S(t)f_k-\Bigl(\int f_k\Bigr)\psi\Bigr\|+\Bigl|\int f_k-\int f\Bigr|\\
&\le2\lVert f-f_k\rVert+\Bigl\|S(t)f_k-\Bigl(\int f_k\Bigr)\psi\Bigr\|,
\end{align*}
so the $\limsup$ is at most $2\lVert f-f_k\rVert$ for every $k$. This proves (ii).

\emph{Step (d): weak formulation.} Let $w_0\in L^2(\mu)$ and $w(t)=T(t)w_0$. For $t>0$, $w(t)\in D(\mathcal A)$ and $\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d} t}w=-\mathcal Aw$ in $L^2(\mu)$ (spectral theorem). For $\zeta\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})\subset D(\mathcal A_0)\subset D(\mathcal A)$, symmetry and \eqref{eq:form} give
\[
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d} t}\langle w,\zeta\rangle_\mu=-\langle\mathcal Aw,\zeta\rangle_\mu=-\langle w,\mathcal A_0\zeta\rangle_\mu=\int_\mathbb{R} w\,(a\zeta')'\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_\mathbb{R}(\psi w)\,\frac{(D\psi\zeta')'}{\psi}\,\mathrm{d} x .
\]
With $u=\psi w$, $\langle w,\zeta\rangle_\mu=\int u\zeta\,\mathrm{d} x$ and $(D\psi\zeta')'/\psi=(D\zeta')'+D\zeta'\psi'/\psi=(D\zeta')'+v\zeta'=\mathcal L^*\zeta$. Integrating in time, and letting $t\downarrow0$ using strong continuity, gives (iii) for $f=\psi w_0$. Both sides of (iii) are continuous in $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, because $\zeta$ and $\mathcal L^*\zeta$ are bounded and $S(s)$ is a contraction; so (iii) extends to all $f$ by density.

<!-- remark-7: body_md -->
\label{rem:unique}
Identity \eqref{eq:symm} shows that the flux $D\partial_xu-vu$ equals $a\,\partial_xw$, so the construction above is the Fokker--Planck dynamics with no additional condition imposed at $\pm\infty$. Condition (R3) is exactly what makes this unambiguous: when both ends are inaccessible, the operator $\mathcal A_0$ has only one Markovian self-adjoint realisation in $L^2(\mu)$, and no boundary condition at infinity can, or needs to, be imposed; see \cite{Eberle1999,FukushimaOshimaTakeda2011}. When (R3) fails, cells may reach $\pm\infty$ in finite time, and one must decide what happens to them there (absorption, reflection, \dots), which changes the model.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:R3}
If \textup{(R1)--(R2)} hold and $D\le D_{\max}$ on $\mathbb{R}$, then \textup{(R3)} holds.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, for every $n$,
\[
1=\Bigl(\int_n^{n+1}1\,\mathrm{d} x\Bigr)^2\le\int_n^{n+1}\psi\,\mathrm{d} x\int_n^{n+1}\frac{\,\mathrm{d} x}{\psi},\qquad\text{so}\qquad\int_n^{n+1}\frac{\,\mathrm{d} x}{D\psi}\ge\frac1{D_{\max}\int_n^{n+1}\psi\,\mathrm{d} x} .
\]
Since $\psi$ is integrable, $\int_n^{n+1}\psi\to0$ as $n\to\infty$, so the terms on the right tend to $+\infty$ and $\int_0^\infty\,\mathrm{d} x/(D\psi)=\sum_{n\ge0}\int_n^{n+1}\,\mathrm{d} x/(D\psi)=\infty$. The same argument applies at $-\infty$.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:muckenhoupt}
Assume \textup{(R1)--(R2)} and let $a=D\psi$. If
\[
B_+:=\sup_{x>0}\Bigl(\int_x^\infty\psi\,\mathrm{d} y\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_0^x\frac{\,\mathrm{d} y}{a(y)}\Bigr)<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_-:=\sup_{x<0}\Bigl(\int_{-\infty}^x\psi\,\mathrm{d} y\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_x^0\frac{\,\mathrm{d} y}{a(y)}\Bigr)<\infty ,
\]
then \eqref{eq:poincare} holds with $\lambda_P=1/(4\max\{B_+,B_-\})$.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-13: body_md -->
Let $w\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R})$. Since the mean $\int w\psi$ minimises $c\mapsto\int(w-c)^2\psi$, the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:poincare} is at most $\int_\mathbb{R}(w-w(0))^2\psi\,\mathrm{d} x$. \]
the same argument on $(-\infty,0)$ gives the corresponding bound with $B_-$. Adding the two inequalities proves the claim.

<!-- proposition-6: body_md -->
\label{prop:OUnorate}
For the OU semigroup and every $t>0$,
\[
\sup\Bigl\{\frac{\lVert S(t)w\rVert}{\lVert w\rVert}:\ w\in L^1(\mathbb{R}),\ \int w=0,\ w\ne0\Bigr\}=1 .
\]
Consequently no estimate of the form \eqref{eq:mix} with $\lambda>0$ can hold.
<!-- end proposition-6 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
The supremum is at most $1$ by contraction. For $y\in\mathbb{R}$ let $h_y(x)=\psi(x-y)$, the stationary Gaussian shifted to be centred at $y$, and $w_y=h_y-\psi$, so that $\int w_y=0$ and $\lVert w_y\rVert\le2$. By the Gaussian fact, $S(t)h_y$ is the density of $\mathcal N\bigl(ye^{-\theta t},\ e^{-2\theta t}\sigma^2+\sigma_t^2\bigr)=\mathcal N(ye^{-\theta t},\sigma^2)$, while $S(t)\psi=\psi$. So $\lVert S(t)w_y\rVert$ is the $L^1$ distance between two Gaussian densities with the same variance and means $ye^{-\theta t}$ apart. As $\lvert y\rvert\to\infty$ with $t$ fixed, this distance tends to $2$, since the two densities become essentially disjoint, while $\lVert w_y\rVert\le2$. Hence $\lVert S(t)w_y\rVert/\lVert w_y\rVert\to1$. If \eqref{eq:mix} held, this ratio would be at most $Ce^{-\lambda t}<1$ for large $t$.

<!-- remark-10: body_md -->
\label{rem:heat}
Consider pure diffusion on $\mathbb{R}$, $v\equiv0$ and constant $D$, for which (R2) fails. Then $S(t)$ is convolution with the heat kernel, and it is classical that $\lVert S(t)w\rVert\to0$ for every $w\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ with $\int w=0$ \cite{EscobedoZuazua1991}. If $r\ger_{\min}>0$ everywhere, Step~5 is immediate ($\rho\ger_{\min} U\ger_{\min} m$), and the argument above still applies once $U(t)\psi$ is replaced by $U(t)S(t)h$, for a fixed probability density $h$. Indeed, $U(t)S(t)h=S(t)(U_0h)+\int_0^tS(t-s)\bigl(U'(s)S(s)h\bigr)\,\mathrm{d} s$, so $d(t):=u(t)-U(t)S(t)h$ satisfies
\[
d(t)=S(t)(u_0-U_0h)+\int_0^tS(t-s)\bigl(F(s)-U'(s)S(s)h\bigr)\,\mathrm{d} s ,
\]
where the forcing has zero mass and norm at most $2\lvert U'(s)\rvert$, and Step~4 (without a rate) gives $\lVert d(t)\rVert\to0$. Hence $U\toU^{*}$ and
\[
\lVert u(t)-S(t)f\rVert\le\lVert d(t)\rVert+\lvert U(t)-U^{*}\rvert+\lVert S(t)(U^{*} h-f)\rVert\longrightarrow0
\]
for every $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ with $\int f=U^{*}$. The population spreads indefinitely, so there is no limiting profile, but it becomes indistinguishable from a solution of the heat equation: fitness is still asymptotically irrelevant, even without an equilibrium to converge to.

\label{lem:H}

<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:weighted}
Let $S$ be a Markov semigroup on $L^1$ with an invariant probability density $\psi>0$ a.e., and let $H:=\{f\in L^1:\lVert f\rVert_\psi<\infty\}$. Then $\lVert f\rVert\le\lVert f\rVert_\psi$ for $f\in H$; $S(t)H\subset H$ and $\lVert S(t)f\rVert_\psi\le\lVert f\rVert_\psi$ for $f\in H$; and $t\mapsto S(t)f$ is continuous in $H$ for every $f\in H$.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-17: body_md -->
The first inequality is the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, as in \eqref{eq:L1H}. Let $f\in H$ and $t\ge0$. For every $\theta\in\mathbb{R}$, $(f-\theta\psi)^2/\psi=f^2/\psi-2\theta f+\theta^2\psi$ is a nonnegative function in $L^1$, so, by (S1) and $S(t)\psi=\psi$,
\[
S(t)\bigl(f^2/\psi\bigr)-2\theta\,S(t)f+\theta^2\psi\ \ge\ 0\qquad\text{a.e.}
\]
This holds a.e.\ simultaneously for all rational $\theta$, hence, by continuity in $\theta$, for all real $\theta$, and the discriminant of this quadratic polynomial in $\theta$ is nonpositive: $(S(t)f)^2\le\psi\,S(t)(f^2/\psi)$ a.e. Dividing by $\psi$, integrating and using (S2), $\lVert S(t)f\rVert_\psi^2\le\int f^2/\psi=\lVert f\rVert_\psi^2$. For the continuity, $H$ is a Hilbert space for the scalar product $\langle f,g\rangle_\psi=\int fg/\psi$, in which the functions $g$ with $g/\psi\in L^\infty$ are dense. For such $g$, $\langle S(t)f,g\rangle_\psi\to\langle f,g\rangle_\psi$ as $t\downarrow0$, because $S(t)f\to f$ in $L^1$. Since $\lVert S(t)f\rVert_\psi\le\lVert f\rVert_\psi$, it follows that $S(t)f\to f$ weakly in $H$, and then $\lVert f\rVert_\psi\le\liminf_{t\downarrow0}\lVert S(t)f\rVert_\psi\le\lVert f\rVert_\psi$ gives convergence in norm. Continuity at $t>0$ follows as in Lemma~\ref{lem:markov}(iii).

<!-- lemma-10: body_md -->
\label{lem:doeblin}
Let $S$ be a Markov semigroup on $L^1$, and suppose that there are $\tau>0$, $c>0$ and a probability density $\phi$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:doeblin}
S(\tau)f\ \ge\ c\Bigl(\int f\Bigr)\phi\qquad\text{for every }f\in L^1\text{ with }f\ge0 .
\end{equation}
Then $c\le1$ and, with $\bar c:=\min\{c,1/2\}$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=2pt]
\item $\lVert S(t)w\rVert\le Ce^{-\lambda t}\lVert w\rVert$ for all $t\ge0$ and $w\in L^1_0$, where $C=1/(1-\bar c)$ and $\lambda=-\tau^{-1}\ln(1-\bar c)\ge\bar c/\tau$;
\item $S$ has a unique invariant probability density $\psi$, and $\psi\ge c\,\phi$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-10 -->
<!-- proof-18: body_md -->
Integrating \eqref{eq:doeblin} and using (S2) gives $c\le1$. Part~(i) is proved exactly as Lemma~\ref{lem:mix}(i)--(ii): the positive and negative parts of $w\in L^1_0$ satisfy $S(\tau)w^\pm\ge\frac{\bar c}2\lVert w\rVert\,\phi$, the floor $\frac{\bar c}2\lVert w\rVert\,\phi$ has integral $\frac{\bar c}2\lVert w\rVert$, and therefore $\lVert S(\tau)w\rVert\le(1-\bar c)\lVert w\rVert$; iterating gives (i). (Lemma~\ref{lem:mix} is the case $\phi=1/\ell$, with $\ell\bar c$ in the role of $\bar c$.) For (ii), the set of probability densities is closed in $L^1$ and invariant under $S(\tau)$, and $\lVert S(\tau)f-S(\tau)f'\rVert\le(1-\bar c)\lVert f-f'\rVert$ for any two of them, by (i). By the Banach fixed-point theorem, $S(\tau)$ has a unique fixed point $\psi$ among probability densities. For $t\ge0$, $S(t)\psi$ is a probability density and $S(\tau)S(t)\psi=S(t)S(\tau)\psi=S(t)\psi$, so $S(t)\psi=\psi$; conversely, every invariant probability density is a fixed point of $S(\tau)$. Finally, $\psi=S(\tau)\psi\ge c\,\phi$.

\label{prop:nonlocal}
Assume \textup{(N1)}, and let $\mathcal Ku:=\int_\Omega K(\cdot,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d} y-\kappa u$. \[

By Fubini's theorem, $\int Bu=\int\kappa u$, so $\int\mathcal Ku=0$ for every $u$, and $\int S(t)u=\int u$ follows from the series; this is (S2). For (S1), write $S(t)=e^{-t\kappa_{\max}}e^{tM}$ with $M:=B+(\kappa_{\max}-\kappa)$. The equivalence between mild and $C^1$ solutions holds because $S$ is a group, differentiable in operator norm: if $u$ is mild, then $u(t)=S(t)\bigl(u_0+\int_0^tS(-s)F(s)\,\mathrm{d} s\bigr)$ is $C^1$ with $u'=\mathcal Ku+F$; conversely, if $u$ is a $C^1$ solution, then $\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d} s}\bigl(S(t-s)u(s)\bigr)=S(t-s)F(s)$, and integrating over $[0,t]$ gives the mild formula. By Proposition~\ref{prop:nonlocal} and Lemma~\ref{lem:doeblin}, $S$ is a Markov semigroup with a unique invariant probability density $\psi$, and it satisfies (M1) with the stated constants. <!-- remark-11: body_md -->
\label{rem:nonlocal-examples}
In general the stationary density $\psi$ is not explicit, but the conclusion does not depend on it: whatever $\psi$ is, it does not depend on $r$. Three examples:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), itemsep=3pt]
\item \emph{Independent jumps.} $K(x,y)=k(y)\phi(x)$, with $k$ measurable and $0<k_{\min}\le k\le k_{\max}$: a cell of phenotype $y$ switches at rate $k(y)$, and its new phenotype is drawn from $\phi$, independently of $y$. Then $\kappa=k$, (N1)--(N2) hold with $n=1$ and $k_0=k_{\min}$, and the stationarity equation $k\psi=\phi\int k\psi$ gives $\psi=(\phi/k)/\int(\phi/k)$: each phenotype is represented in proportion to the time that cells spend in it. This example allows unbounded $\Omega$, for instance $\Omega=\mathbb{R}^d$, and, in contrast with the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck dynamics (Proposition~\ref{prop:OUnorate}), the rate is uniform, because a cell returns from an arbitrarily distant phenotype in a single jump.
\item \emph{Detailed balance.} If $K(x,y)\pi(y)=K(y,x)\pi(x)$ for a probability density $\pi$, then $\mathcal K\pi=0$, so $\psi=\pi$. For instance, let $\Omega$ be bounded, let $\pi$ be a probability density with $0<\pi_{\min}\le\pi\le\pi_{\max}$ on $\Omega$, and let $q\ge0$ be even and integrable on $\mathbb{R}^d$, with $q\ge q_0>0$ on $\{x-y:x,y\in\Omega\}$. The Metropolis kernel $K(x,y)=q(x-y)\min\{1,\pi(x)/\pi(y)\}$ satisfies $K(x,y)\pi(y)=q(x-y)\min\{\pi(x),\pi(y)\}=K(y,x)\pi(x)$, $\kappa\le\lVert q\rVert_{L^1}$ and $K\ge q_0\pi_{\min}/\pi_{\max}$, so (N1)--(N2) hold with $n=1$, $\phi=1/\lvert \Omega\rvert$ and $k_0=\lvert \Omega\rvert q_0\pi_{\min}/\pi_{\max}$. With $\pi\propto e^{-P/D}$, jumps in the landscape $P$ lead to the same Boltzmann-type limit as the local switching dynamics with $v=-P'$ and constant $D$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:landscape} and Figure~\ref{fig:examples}(c)); for a symmetric kernel, $K(x,y)=K(y,x)$, $\psi$ is uniform.
\item \emph{Short jumps.} If $\Omega=(0,\ell)$ and $K(x,y)\ge k_1>0$ whenever $\lvert x-y\rvert<\ell_0$, then (N2) holds with any integer $n>2\ell/\ell_0$, $\phi=1/\ell$ and $k_0=\ell\,k_1^n\min\{\ell_0/4,\ell\}^{n-1}$. Indeed, for $x,y\in\Omega$ let $p_i=x+i(y-x)/n$, so that $\lvert p_i-p_{i-1}\rvert<\ell_0/2$. If $z_0=x$, $z_n=y$ and $z_i\in I_i:=(p_i-\ell_0/4,p_i+\ell_0/4)\cap\Omega$ for $1\le i\le n-1$, then $\lvert z_i-z_{i-1}\rvert<\ell_0$ for all $i$, and each $I_i$ has length at least $\min\{\ell_0/4,\ell\}$; hence $K_n(x,y)\ge k_1^n\min\{\ell_0/4,\ell\}^{n-1}$. As for irreducible matrices, connectivity comes from chains of transitions.
\end{enumerate}

<!-- remark-12: body_md -->
\label{rem:unified}
The unified model of \cite{Review} combines both mechanisms. On $\Omega=(0,\ell)$, its switching generator is $\mathcal L+\varepsilon\mathcal K$, with $\mathcal L$ as in (H3), $\mathcal K$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:nonlocal} under (N1), and $\varepsilon\ge0$. Since $\varepsilon\mathcal K$ is a bounded perturbation of the generator of the semigroup $S$ of Section~\ref{sec:linear}, the sum generates a semigroup $S_\varepsilon$ on $L^1(\Omega)$, given by the Dyson--Phillips series \cite[Section~3.1]{Pazy1983}. Write $\varepsilon\mathcal K=\varepsilon M-\varepsilon\kappa_{\max}$, with the positive operator $M$ of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:nonlocal}. Then every term of the Dyson--Phillips series of $e^{\varepsilon\kappa_{\max}t}S_\varepsilon(t)$ is a positive operator, and the first one is $S(t)$; hence $S_\varepsilon(t)\ge e^{-\varepsilon\kappa_{\max}t}S(t)$, and, with $c$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:mix}, $S_\varepsilon(\tau)f\ge e^{-\varepsilon\kappa_{\max}\tau}c\int f$ for $f\ge0$. Mass is conserved, because $\int\mathcal Ku=0$. So $S_\varepsilon$ is a Markov semigroup that satisfies Doeblin's condition \eqref{eq:doeblin} with $\phi=1/\ell$, with invariant density $\psi_\varepsilon\ge e^{-\varepsilon\kappa_{\max}\tau}c$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:doeblin}). Hence (M2) follows from (H4), and Theorem~\ref{thm:C}(a)--(e) applies, without (N2): under (H1), (H2) and (H4), the solution converges to $U^{*}\psi_\varepsilon$, which again does not depend on $r$. If the jumps satisfy detailed balance with respect to $\psi$, as for the Metropolis kernel of Remark~\ref{rem:nonlocal-examples}(2) with $\pi=\psi$, then $\mathcal K\psi=0$ and $\psi_\varepsilon=\psi$ for every $\varepsilon$: the jumps then affect how fast fitness is forgotten, but not the limit.

\label{cor:Rd}
Assume \textup{(H2)} and the above conditions on $\Omega$, $D$ and $v$, and let $r:\bar\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to[0,r_{\max}]$ be continuous, with $r(x,t)\ger_{\min}>0$ for all $x$ in a measurable set $J\subset\Omega$ of positive measure and all $t\ge t_r$, for some $t_r\ge0$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=2pt]
\item the switching dynamics \eqref{eq:Rd} has a unique invariant probability density $\psi$, which is continuous and bounded below by a positive constant on $\bar\Omega$;
\item for every $u_0\inL^1(\Omega)$ with $u_0\ge0$ and $U_0>0$, the mild solution of \eqref{eq:model-abstract} with this switching dynamics is global and nonnegative, $U$ is monotone and $U(t)\toU^{*}$, $\lVert u(t)-U^{*}\psi\rVert\to0$, and $\lVert u(t)-S(t)f\rVert\to0$ for every $f\inL^1(\Omega)$ with $\int f=U^{*}$; estimate \eqref{eq:star} holds with the constants of Lemma~\ref{lem:doeblin}, computed with $\bar c=\min\{c\lvert \Omega\rvert,1/2\}$, and, if $g'(U^{*})<0$, the convergence to $U^{*}\psi$ is exponential;
\item the stationary flux $\mathbf j_\psi:=D\nabla\psi-v\psi$ is a continuous, divergence-free vector field on $\Omega$, tangent to $\partial\Omega$. It vanishes identically if and only if $D^{-1}v=\nabla\Phi$ for some function $\Phi$, and then $\psi\propto e^{\Phi}$; otherwise the stationary state carries a circulating probability flux.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end maincor-5 -->
<!-- proof-21: body_md -->
By the discussion above and Lemma~\ref{lem:doeblin}, applied with the constant $c\lvert \Omega\rvert$ and $\phi=1/\lvert \Omega\rvert$, $S$ satisfies (M1), with an exponential rate, and has a unique invariant probability density $\psi$, with $\psi\ge c\lvert \Omega\rvert\,\phi=c$. Moreover $\psi=S(1)\psi$ is continuous on $\bar\Omega$, because $\Gamma(\cdot,\cdot,1)$ is. This proves (i), and (M2) holds with $h=\psi$, $\delta=r_{\min} c\lvert J\rvert$ and $\mathcal T=[t_r,\infty)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:C}(a)--(e) gives (ii). For (iii), the function $u(x,t)=(S(t)\psi)(x)=\psi(x)$ is, for $t>0$, the classical solution of Theorem~\ref{bb:greenRd}(a) with initial datum $\psi$. Hence $\psi\in C^2(\Omega)$, $\nabla\psi$ is continuous up to $\partial\Omega$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf j_\psi=\mathcal L\psi=\partial_tu=0$ in $\Omega$, and $\mathbf j_\psi\cdot n=0$ on $\partial\Omega$. If $\mathbf j_\psi\equiv0$, then $D^{-1}v=\nabla\ln\psi$. Conversely, if $D^{-1}v=\nabla\Phi$, then $\Phi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar\Omega)$, and $\tilde\psi:=e^\Phi/\int_\Omega e^\Phi$ satisfies $D\nabla\tilde\psi-v\tilde\psi=\tilde\psi\,(D\nabla\Phi-v)=0$. The time-independent function $\tilde\psi$ therefore has the properties listed in Theorem~\ref{bb:greenRd}(a), so $S(t)\tilde\psi=\tilde\psi$ by uniqueness, and $\tilde\psi=\psi$ by (i).

<!-- lemma-11: body_md -->
\label{lem:riemann}
Let $h:[a,b]\toL^1(\Omega)$ be continuous.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=2pt]
\item If $T:L^1(\Omega)\to Y$ is a bounded linear operator into a Banach space $Y$, then $T\int_a^bh(s)\,\mathrm{d} s=\int_a^bTh(s)\,\mathrm{d} s$. In particular, for the mass functional $f\mapsto\int_\Omega f\,\mathrm{d} x$,
\[
\int_\Omega\Bigl(\int_a^bh(s)\,\mathrm{d} s\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d} x=\int_a^b\Bigl(\int_\Omega h(s)\,\mathrm{d} x\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d} s .
\]
\item If $H:[a,b]\times[a,b]\toL^1(\Omega)$ is continuous, then $t\mapsto\int_a^tH(s,t)\,\mathrm{d} s$ is continuous on $[a,b]$.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-22: body_md -->
(i) holds for Riemann sums and passes to the limit by continuity of $T$. (ii) $H$ is uniformly continuous on the compact square; write $\int_a^{t'}H(s,t')\,\mathrm{d} s-\int_a^tH(s,t)\,\mathrm{d} s=\int_a^t[H(s,t')-H(s,t)]\,\mathrm{d} s+\int_t^{t'}H(s,t')\,\mathrm{d} s$ and bound both terms.

\label{lem:duhamel-cont}
Let $S$ be a Markov semigroup and $h:[t_0,T]\toL^1(\Omega)$ continuous. Then $t\mapsto\int_{t_0}^tS(t-s)h(s)\,\mathrm{d} s$ is continuous on $[t_0,T]$, and its norm is at most $\int_{t_0}^t\lVert h(s)\rVert\,\mathrm{d} s$.

<!-- lemma-13: body_md -->
\label{lem:Fcont}
If $u:[a,b]\toL^1(\Omega)$ is continuous with $\int u(t)>0$, then $F(t)=N(t,u(t))$ is continuous from $[a,b]$ to $L^1(\Omega)$.
<!-- end lemma-13 -->
<!-- proof-24: body_md -->
$t\mapsto U(t)=\int u(t)$ is continuous, hence so is $t\mapsto g(U(t))$. For $t,t_0\in[a,b]$,
\[
\lVert r(t)u(t)-r(t_0)u(t_0)\rVert\ler_{\max}\lVert u(t)-u(t_0)\rVert+\int_\Omega\lvert r(x,t)-r(x,t_0)\rvert\,\lvert u(x,t_0)\rvert\,\mathrm{d} x .
\]
The first term tends to $0$ as $t\to t_0$. In the second, the integrand tends to $0$ pointwise (continuity of $r$) and is bounded by $2r_{\max}\lvert u(x,t_0)\rvert$, an integrable function, so it tends to $0$ by dominated convergence.
<!-- end proof-24 -->
<!-- proof-25: body_md -->
\emph{Bounds on the nonlinearity.} Let $Y:=\{\phi\inL^1(\Omega):\ \int\phi\ge a,\ \lVert \phi\rVert\le R+a\}$. For $\phi\in Y$, $a\le\int\phi\le\lVert \phi\rVert\le R+a$. Let $G:=\max_{[a,R+a]}\lvert g\rvert$ and let $\Lambda$ be a Lipschitz constant of $g$ on $[a,R+a]$ (it exists since $g$ is $C^1$). For $\phi,\phi_1,\phi_2\in Y$ and all $t$:
\[
\lVert N(t,\phi)\rVert\le Gr_{\max}(R+a)=:B,
\]
and, writing $N(t,\phi_1)-N(t,\phi_2)=g(\textstyle\int\phi_1)\,r(t)(\phi_1-\phi_2)+\bigl(g(\int\phi_1)-g(\int\phi_2)\bigr)r(t)\phi_2$ and using $\lvert \int\phi_1-\int\phi_2\rvert\le\lVert \phi_1-\phi_2\rVert$,
\[
\lVert N(t,\phi_1)-N(t,\phi_2)\rVert\ler_{\max}\bigl(G+\Lambda(R+a)\bigr)\lVert \phi_1-\phi_2\rVert=:L_N\lVert \phi_1-\phi_2\rVert.
\]

\emph{A complete metric space.} Let $T_0:=\min\{a/B,\,1/(2L_N)\}$, $I=[t_0,t_0+T_0]$, and let $X$ be the set of continuous $u:I\toL^1(\Omega)$ with $\sup_{t\in I}\lVert u(t)-S(t-t_0)\phi_0\rVert\le a$, with the distance $\sup_{t\in I}\lVert u_1(t)-u_2(t)\rVert$. It is complete (a closed subset of the Banach space $C(I;L^1(\Omega))$). If $u\in X$, then $u(t)\in Y$ for all $t\in I$: indeed, by mass conservation and contraction,
\[
\int u(t)\ge\int S(t-t_0)\phi_0-\lVert u(t)-S(t-t_0)\phi_0\rVert\ge2a-a=a,\qquad\lVert u(t)\rVert\le\lVert \phi_0\rVert+a\le R+a .
\]

\emph{A contraction.} For $u\in X$ let $\Phi(u)(t)$ be the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:mild-t0}. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:Fcont} and~\ref{lem:duhamel-cont}, $\Phi(u)$ is continuous, and
\[
\lVert \Phi(u)(t)-S(t-t_0)\phi_0\rVert\le\int_{t_0}^t\lVert N(s,u(s))\rVert\,\mathrm{d} s\le T_0B\le a,
\]
so $\Phi$ maps $X$ into $X$. For $u_1,u_2\in X$,
\[
\lVert \Phi(u_1)(t)-\Phi(u_2)(t)\rVert\le\int_{t_0}^t\lVert N(s,u_1(s))-N(s,u_2(s))\rVert\,\mathrm{d} s\le T_0L_N\sup_I\lVert u_1-u_2\rVert\le\tfrac12\sup_I\lVert u_1-u_2\rVert .
\]
By the Banach fixed-point theorem, $\Phi$ has a unique fixed point in $X$. For uniqueness among all continuous solutions of \eqref{eq:mild-t0} on $I$ with $\int u\ge a$, let $u_1,u_2$ be two of them. Their ranges are compact, hence contained in $\{\phi:\int\phi\ge a,\ \lVert \phi\rVert\le R'\}$ for some $R'\ge R+a$, and the computation above, with $R'$ in place of $R+a$, gives a Lipschitz constant $L'$ of $N(t,\cdot)$ on this set. Hence $e(t):=\lVert u_1(t)-u_2(t)\rVert$ satisfies $e(t)\le L'\int_{t_0}^te(s)\,\mathrm{d} s$, and Gronwall's inequality gives $e\equiv0$.

<!-- lemma-14: body_md -->
\label{lem:rescale}
Let $q:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}$ be bounded and such that $t\mapsto q(\cdot,t)w(t)$ is continuous in $L^1(\Omega)$ whenever $w$ is, let $\beta\in C^1([0,T])$ be positive with $\beta(0)=1$, and let $w:[0,T]\toL^1(\Omega)$ be continuous. Then $w$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lin-q}
w(t)=S(t)w_0+\int_0^tS(t-s)\,q(s)w(s)\,\mathrm{d} s
\end{equation}
if and only if $W(t):=\beta(t)w(t)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lin-qc}
W(t)=S(t)w_0+\int_0^tS(t-s)\Bigl(\frac{\beta'(s)}{\beta(s)}+q(s)\Bigr)W(s)\,\mathrm{d} s .
\end{equation}
In particular, for $\beta(t)=e^{kt}$ with $k\in\mathbb{R}$, the coefficient in \eqref{eq:lin-qc} is $k+q$.
<!-- end lemma-14 -->
<!-- proof-26: body_md -->
Assume \eqref{eq:lin-q}. Substitute \eqref{eq:lin-q} for $w(s)$ inside the term $\int_0^tS(t-s)\frac{\beta'(s)}{\beta(s)}W(s)\,\mathrm{d} s=\int_0^t\beta'(s)S(t-s)w(s)\,\mathrm{d} s$, and use $S(t-s)S(s)=S(t)$, $S(t-s)S(s-\sigma)=S(t-\sigma)$, and Fubini on the triangle $0\le\sigma\le s\le t$:
\[
\int_0^t\beta'(s)S(t-s)w(s)\,\mathrm{d} s=\Bigl(\int_0^t\beta'(s)\,\mathrm{d} s\Bigr)S(t)w_0+\int_0^t\Bigl(\int_\sigma^t\beta'(s)\,\mathrm{d} s\Bigr)S(t-\sigma)q(\sigma)w(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma .
\]
Since $\int_0^t\beta'=\beta(t)-1$ and $\int_\sigma^t\beta'=\beta(t)-\beta(\sigma)$, the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:lin-qc} equals
\[
S(t)w_0+(\beta(t)-1)S(t)w_0+\int_0^t\bigl(\beta(t)-\beta(\sigma)\bigr)S(t-\sigma)q(\sigma)w(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_0^t\beta(\sigma)S(t-\sigma)q(\sigma)w(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma ,
\]
which is $\beta(t)\bigl(S(t)w_0+\int_0^tS(t-\sigma)q(\sigma)w(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\bigr)=\beta(t)w(t)=W(t)$. The converse is the same computation with $\beta$ replaced by $1/\beta$, whose logarithmic derivative is $-\beta'/\beta$, and with the roles of \eqref{eq:lin-q} and \eqref{eq:lin-qc} exchanged.

<!-- lemma-15: body_md -->
\label{lem:volterra}
Let $q$ be as in Lemma~\ref{lem:rescale} with $\lvert q\rvert\le Q$. Then \eqref{eq:lin-q} has exactly one continuous solution on $[0,T]$, and it is the uniform limit of the Picard iterates $w^{(0)}(t)=S(t)w_0$, $w^{(k+1)}(t)=S(t)w_0+\int_0^tS(t-s)q(s)w^{(k)}(s)\,\mathrm{d} s$. If $q\ge0$ and $w_0\ge0$, the solution is nonnegative.
<!-- end lemma-15 -->
<!-- proof-27: body_md -->
By contraction, $\lVert w^{(k+1)}(t)-w^{(k)}(t)\rVert\le Q\int_0^t\lVert w^{(k)}(s)-w^{(k-1)}(s)\rVert\,\mathrm{d} s$, so by induction $\lVert w^{(k+1)}(t)-w^{(k)}(t)\rVert\le\frac{(Qt)^k}{k!}\sup_{[0,T]}\lVert w^{(1)}-w^{(0)}\rVert$. The series $\sum_k(QT)^k/k!$ converges, so the iterates converge uniformly to a continuous solution. If $w,\tilde w$ are two solutions, $e(t)=\lVert w(t)-\tilde w(t)\rVert$ satisfies $e(t)\le Q\int_0^te(s)\,\mathrm{d} s$, and Gronwall's inequality gives $e\equiv0$. If $q\ge0$ and $w_0\ge0$, every iterate is nonnegative by (S1) (integrals of nonnegative functions are nonnegative), and so is their $L^1$ limit.
