    Moreover, the iterates of the Sinkhorn scaling algorithm converge to~$P_\star$.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:scale}
The (unique) doubly stochastic scaling of $M_\delta$ is given by
\[
 T_\delta=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 t_\delta&1-t_\delta&&&\\
 &t_\delta&1-t_\delta&&\\
 &&\ddots&\ddots&\\
 &&&t_\delta&1-t_\delta\\
 1-t_\delta&&&&t_\delta
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 t_\delta=\frac{1}{1+\delta^{1/n}}.
\]
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
Matrix scaling will never change the support of the starting matrix~$M_\delta$.                                                 
On the other hand, any doubly stochastic matrix with the same support as~$M_\delta$ must have a common diagonal entry~$t_\delta\in(0,1)$ and a common cyclic off-diagonal entry~$1-t_\delta$: for~$1\leq i<n$, comparing row~$i$ with column~$i+1$ forces successive diagonal entries to agree.
Thus, if~$M_\delta$ has a doubly stochastic scaling, it must have the displayed form of~$T_\delta$ for some value of~$t_\delta$.
To determine the latter, we use that the cross-ratio
\[
 \chi(M)=\frac{M_{1,2}M_{2,3}\cdots M_{n-1,n}M_{n,1}}
 {M_{1,1}M_{2,2}\cdots M_{n,n}}
\]
is invariant under row and column scaling (indeed, both the numerator and the denominator change by the same factor: the product of all scaling factors).
Thus, if $T_\delta$ is a scaling of $M_\delta$, we must have
\begin{align*}
    \delta = \chi(M_\delta) = \chi(T_\delta) = \parens*{ \frac{1-t_\delta}{t_\delta} }^n,
\end{align*}
which gives the stated value of~$t_\delta$.
Finally, we observe that $T_\delta$ is indeed a scaling of $M_\delta$:
\begin{equation*}
    \operatorname{diag}(1,\delta^{-1/n},\dots,\delta^{-(n-1)/n}) M_\delta \operatorname{diag}(t_\delta, t_\delta \delta^{1/n}, \dots, t_\delta \delta^{(n-1)/n})
= T_\delta.
\end{equation*}
         
                
                                
                                                                
               
                                   
                                                               
                         
                   
                                 
                                                  
                                     
                         
                  
              
This concludes the proof.

<!-- theorem-9: body_md -->
\label{thm:instability}
For every $n\ge2$, let $N=2n+1$, $\delta=2^{-n}$, and $\delta'=2^{-3n}$.
Then the real orthogonal matrices $U_\delta,U_{\delta'}$ have the same support (zero pattern w.r.t.\ the standard basis), both fix the state~$\rho$, and they satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-separation}
 \lVert U_\delta-U_{\delta'}\rVert_{\max} < 2^{-n},\qquad
 \lVert P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\rho,U_\delta)-P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\rho,U_{\delta'})\rVert_{\max} > \frac1{10N}.
\end{equation}
Moreover, $P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\rho,U_\delta)$ and $P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\rho,U_{\delta'})$ have constant total-variation distance~$2/9$.
<!-- end theorem-9 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
By \cref{lem:scale}, $t_\delta = \frac23$ and $t_{\delta'} = \frac89$.
Then \cref{eq:Pdelta} and the fact that the joint distributions are zero outside the active subspace yield
\[
  \lVert P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\rho,U_\delta)-P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\rho,U_{\delta'})\rVert_{\max}
= \frac {\lVert T_\delta - T_{\delta'}\rVert_{\max}} {4n}
= \frac {\abs{t_\delta - t_{\delta'}}} {4n}
= \frac1{18n}
> \frac1{10N}.
\]
Using \cref{eq:Pdelta}, we can also compute the total-variation distance:
\begin{align*}
    d_{\mathrm{TV}}\mleft( P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\rho,U_\delta), P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\rho,U_{\delta'}) \mright)
                                                                       
= \frac1{2n} \sum_{j,i} \abs*{ (T_\delta)_{ji} - (T_{\delta'})_{ji} }
= \abs{t_\delta - t_{\delta'}}
= \frac29.
\end{align*}
It remains to verify the claimed properties of the unitaries and bound their distance.
Since $0<\delta,\delta'<1$, $U_\delta$ and $U_{\delta'}$ have the same support.
Moreover, they both fix the uniform superposition of the first $2n$ basis states.
Finally we verify the upper bound on their distance.
To this end, note that
\begin{align*}
    U_\delta - U_{\delta'}
= (\delta - \delta') \parens*{ \lvert n,-\rangle\!\langle 1,-\rvert-\lvert \bot\rangle\!\langle \bot\rvert }
+ \parens*{ \sqrt{1-\delta^2}-\sqrt{1-{\delta'}^2} }
 \parens*{ \lvert \bot\rangle\!\langle 1,-\rvert + \lvert n,-\rangle\!\langle \bot\rvert }.
\end{align*}
The four rank-one matrices in this formula have pairwise disjoint support, so we see that
\begin{align*}
    \lVert U_\delta - U_{\delta'}\rVert_{\max}
= \max \braces*{
    \abs{\delta - \delta'},
    \frac {\abs{\delta - \delta'}} 2,
    \frac {\abs{\sqrt{1-\delta^2}-\sqrt{1-{\delta'}^2}}} {\sqrt 2}
  }
= \abs{\delta - \delta'}
< 2^{-n},
\end{align*}
where the second equality uses that $\delta,\delta'\leq1/2$.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:C-rho-u feasible}
For every density matrix~$\rho$ and every unitary~$U$, the set $\mathcal{C}(\rho, U)$ is nonempty.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Since~$p$ and~$q$ are both probability distributions, the max-flow min-cut theorem (\cref{thm:max-flow min-cut}) shows that it suffices to prove that, for every $I, J \subseteq [N]$,
\begin{align*}
    p(I) + q(J) \leq 1 + C(I,J),
\end{align*}
where $p(I) \coloneqq \sum_{i\in I} p_i$, $q(J) \coloneqq \sum_{j \in J} q_j$, and $C(I,J) \coloneqq \sum_{i \in I,j \in J} C_{ji}$.
To this end, let $P = \sum_{i \in I} \lvert i\rangle\langle i\rvert$ and $Q = \sum_{j \in J} U^\dagger \lvert j\rangle \langle j\rvert U$.
Then,
\begin{align*}
    p(I) + q(J) - 1 = \operatorname{Tr} \rho (P + Q - I).
\end{align*}
On the other hand,
\begin{align*}
    (P + Q - I)^2 = I - P - Q + PQ + QP
\end{align*}
is a positive semidefinite operator, hence $P + Q - I \preceq PQ + QP$ and
\begin{align*}
&\qquad  \operatorname{Tr} \rho (P + Q - I)
\leq \operatorname{Tr} \rho (PQ + QP)
= 2 \sum_{i \in I, j \in J} \operatorname{Re} \ \langlej | U \rho | i\rangle \langlei | U^\dagger | j\rangle \\
&\leq 2 \sum_{i \in I, j \in J} \abs{U_{ji}} \cdot \abs{\langlej | U \sqrt\rho \sqrt\rho | i\rangle}
\leq 2 \sum_{i \in I, j \in J} \abs{U_{ji}} \cdot \sqrt{\langlej | U \rho U^\dagger | j\rangle} \cdot \sqrt{\langlei | \rho | i\rangle}
= C(I, J),
\end{align*}
where the last inequality is the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality.

<!-- definition-2: body_md -->
Given any density matrix $\rho$ and unitary~$U$, in any dimension~$N$, we define the joint distribution
\begin{align*}
  P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U)
\coloneqq \operatorname*{\arg\min}_{P \in \mathcal{C}(\rho,U)} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\Vert\, \abs U)
= \operatorname*{\arg\min}_{P \in \mathcal{C}(\rho,U)} \sum_{\substack{j,i, \\ U_{ji} \neq 0}} P_{ji} \ln \frac {P_{ji}} {\abs{U_{ji}}}.
\end{align*}
This is well-defined because~$\mathcal{C}(\rho,U)$ is nonempty (\cref{lem:C-rho-u feasible}), compact, and convex and the Kullback--Leibler divergence is strictly convex.
Then the \emph{capped Schr\"odinger theory} is defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:SCST via PCST}
  S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U)_{ji} = \begin{cases}
    P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho, U)_{ji}/p_i, & p_i>0, \\
    \abs{U_{ji}}^2,&p_i=0.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This is a column stochastic matrix because each column is obtained either by conditioning the joint distribution~$P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U)$ on an event with nonzero probability ($p_i>0$), or by taking the entrywise absolute value squared of a column of the unitary matrix~$U$.
We note that $P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U) = S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U) \operatorname{diag}(\rho_{11},\dots,\rho_{NN})$, so the former is indeed the joint distribution arising from the latter transition matrix.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:cst tensor identity}
Identify $\mathbb{C}^N \cong \mathbb{C}^A \otimes \mathbb{C}^B$ and $\mathbb{R}^N \cong \mathbb{R}^A \otimes \mathbb{R}^B$ using the same ordering of the product bases (e.g., the lexicographic ordering).
Let $\rho = \rho_A \otimes \rho_B$ be a product state and $\mathcal U=U_A\otimes I_B$ and $\mathcal V=I_A\otimes V_B$ local unitaries.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] We have
  \begin{align*}
    P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A \otimes \rho_B, U_A \otimes I_B) & = P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A, U_A) \otimes D_{\rho_B}, \\
    P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A \otimes \rho_B, I_A \otimes V_B) & = D_{\rho_A} \otimes P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_B, V_B),
  \end{align*}
  where $D_\sigma$ denotes the diagonal matrix with the same diagonal entries as $\sigma$.
\item[(b)] Consequently,
  \begin{align*}
      S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A \otimes \rho_B, U_A \otimes I_B) (I_A \otimes P_{\rho_B}) = S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A, U_A) \otimes P_{\rho_B}, \\
      S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A \otimes \rho_B, I_A \otimes V_B) (P_{\rho_A} \otimes I_B) = P_{\rho_A} \otimes S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_B, V_B),
  \end{align*}
  where $P_\sigma = \sum_{k : \sigma_{kk}>0} \lvert k\rangle\!\langle k\rvert$ is the projection onto the support of~$D_\sigma$.
\item[(c)] The capped Schr\"odinger theory satisfies distributional product commutativity.
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
(a)~We only prove the first identity; the proof of the second identity is completely analogous.
For every~$P \in \mathcal{C}(\rho_A \otimes \rho_B, U_A \otimes I_B)$, by the capacity constraint we have~$P_{j_A j_B, i_A i_B} = 0$ whenever~$j_B \neq i_B$, as the corresponding entry of~$\mathcal U$ is zero.
Therefore, we can regard~$P$ as a tuple of $A \times A$-matrices~$P^{(i_B)}$, satisfying the constraints
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{j_A} P_{j_A,i_A}^{(i_B)} = p_{i_A} r_{i_B}, \quad
    \sum_{i_A} P_{j_A,i_A}^{(i_B)} = q_{j_A} r_{i_B}, \quad
    0 \leq P_{j_A,i_A}^{(i_B)} \leq 2 \abs{(U_A)_{j_A,i_A}} \sqrt{q_{j_A} p_{i_A}} \, r_{i_B},
\end{align*}
where $p_{i_A}\coloneqq(\rho_A)_{i_A,i_A}$, $q_{j_A}\coloneqq(U_A\rho_AU_A^\dagger)_{j_A,j_A}$, and $r_{i_B}\coloneqq(\rho_B)_{i_B,i_B}$;
in other words, $P^{(i_B)} \in r_{i_B} \mathcal{C}(\rho_A, U_A)$ for all~$i_B$.
The Kullback--Leibler divergence~$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\Vert\, \abs{\mathcal U})$ also decomposes as
\begin{align*}
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\Vert\, \abs{\mathcal U})
                                                                                                  
= \sum_{i_B} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{(i_B)} \,\Vert\, \abs{U_A}).
\end{align*}
Thus, minimizing the left-hand side over~$P \in \mathcal{C}(\rho, \mathcal U)$ is equivalent to separately minimizing each of the right-hand side terms over~$P^{(i_B)} \in r_{i_B} \mathcal{C}(\rho_A, U_A)$.
By definition, the unique minimizer of the left-hand side is $P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A \otimes \rho_B, U_A \otimes I_B)$.
For the right-hand side terms there are two cases: if $r_{i_B}=0$, then we must have~$P^{(i_B)}=0$, while if~$r_{i_B}>0$ we have $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{(i_B)} \,\Vert\, \abs{U_A}) = r_{i_B} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{(i_B)}/r_{i_B} \Vert\, \abs{U_A}) + r_{i_B} \ln r_{i_B}$; in either case, the unique minimizer is given by~$r_{i_B} P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A, U_A)$.
Therefore, $P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A \otimes \rho_B, U_A \otimes I_B)^{(i_B)} = r_{i_B} P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A, U_A)$, i.e., $P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A \otimes \rho_B, U_A \otimes I_B) = P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A, U_A) \otimes D_{\rho_B}$.

(b)~This follows from part~(a) and \cref{eq:SCST via PCST}.

(c)~We first note that
\begin{align}\label{eq:p does not matter}
    P_{U_A \rho_A U_A^\dagger} P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A) = P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A).
\end{align}
Indeed, if $(U_A \rho_A U_A^\dagger)_{j_A,j_A}=0$ then we must have $P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A)_{j_A,i_A} = 0$ for every~$i_A$, because the former is the~$j_A$-th row sum of the latter.
We verify \cref{eq:propercomm}:
\begin{align*}
    S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\mathcal U\rho\mathcal U^\dagger,\mathcal V) P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,\mathcal U)
&=  S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\mathcal U\rho\mathcal U^\dagger,\mathcal V) \bigl( P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A) \otimes D_{\rho_B} \bigr) \\
&=  S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\mathcal U\rho\mathcal U^\dagger,\mathcal V) \bigl( P_{U_A \rho_A U_A^\dagger} \otimes I_B  \bigr) \bigl( P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A) \otimes D_{\rho_B} \bigr) \\
&=   \bigl( P_{U_A \rho_A U_A^\dagger} \otimes S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_B,V_B) \bigr) \bigl( P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A) \otimes D_{\rho_B} \bigr) \\
&=   P_{U_A \rho_A U_A^\dagger} P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A) \otimes S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_B,V_B) D_{\rho_B} \\
&=   P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A) \otimes P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_B,V_B)
\end{align*}
where the first equality follows from part~(a);
next we use \cref{eq:p does not matter} to add the projection;
the third equality follows from part~(b);
the penultimate equality is simply by rearranging;
and for the last equality we use $P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_B,V_B) = S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_B,V_B) D_{\rho_B}$ and again \cref{eq:p does not matter}.
An analogous calculation gives $S_{\mathrm{CST}}(\mathcal V\rho\mathcal V^\dagger,\mathcal U) P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,\mathcal V) = P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_A,U_A) \otimes P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho_B,V_B)$.

\label{thm:transportation correction}
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{N \times N}$ and let $r,s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^N$ such that the transportation polytope~$\mathcal{T}(r,s|A)$ defined in~\eqref{eq:transpoly} is nonempty.
                                           
Let $X \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{N \times N}$ be such that $X_{ji} \leq A_{ji}$ for all~$i,j\in[N]$.
Then there exists~$Y \in \mathcal{T}(r,s|A)$ such that
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{i,j} \abs{Y_{ji} - X_{ji}} \leq N \left( \sum_i \abs*{ \sum_j X_{ji} - r_i } + \sum_j \abs*{ \sum_i X_{ji} - s_j } \right).
\end{align*}

Let $Y \in \mathcal{T}(r,s|A)$ be the element of minimal $\ell^2$-distance 
\footnote{Alternatively one can use a minimizer for the $\ell^1$-distance.}
to~$X$, and set~$Z \coloneqq Y - X$.
We can use~$Z$ to define a directed bipartite graph~$G$ with both vertex sets indexed by~$[N]$:                                                                                     
there is an edge between left vertex~$j$ and right vertex~$i$ if and only if~$Z_{ji} \neq 0$; it is oriented from left to right if~$Z_{ji} < 0$ and otherwise from right to left.
We claim that~$G$ is acyclic.
Suppose for contradiction that there is a directed simple cycle~$i_1 \to j_1 \to i_2 \to j_2 \to \ldots \to i_\ell \to j_\ell \to i_{\ell+1} = i_1$.
Define~$\Delta \coloneqq \sum_{m=1}^\ell E_{j_m,i_m} - E_{j_m,i_{m+1}}$, where~$E_{j,i} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ denotes the elementary matrix with a single entry in row~$j$ and column~$i$.
Choose $0 < \varepsilon < \min_{\text{cycle}} \abs{Z_{ji}}$.
For entries on the cycle, if $Z_{ji}>0$, then $0 < (\varepsilon\Delta)_{ji} < Z_{ji}$, while if $Z_{ji}<0$ we have $Z_{ji} < (\varepsilon\Delta)_{ji} < 0$.
Thus, each altered entry of $Y - \varepsilon \Delta$ lies between the corresponding entries of~$X$ and~$Y$, so it remains in~$[0,A_{ji}]$, and it moves strictly closer to~$X_{ji}$.
Since the row and column sums are also unchanged, this contradicts the minimality of~$Y$.

Because~$G$ is acyclic, we may choose a topological ordering~$v_1 < \ldots < v_{2N}$ of its vertices, i.e., there is an edge~$v_a \to v_b$ only if $a < b$.
We define a flow~$f$ on~$G$ by assigning $\abs{Z_{ji}}$ to the unique edge between left vertex $j$ and right vertex $i$ (if $Z_{ji}\neq0$).
Then:
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{j,i} \abs{Y_{ji} - X_{ji}}
= \sum_{j,i} \abs{Z_{ji}}
= \sum_e f(e)
\leq \sum_{v_a \to v_b} (b-a) \, f(v_a \to v_b)
= \sum_{b=1}^{2N} b \, \delta(v_b)
= \sum_{b=1}^{2N} \left( b - \frac{2N+1}2 \right) \, \delta(v_b),
\end{align*}
where $\delta(v) \coloneq \sum_{u : u \to v} f(u \to v) - \sum_{w : v \to w} f(v \to w)$ denotes the inflow minus outflow at vertex~$v$;
the last equality holds because $\sum_v \delta(v) = 0$.
Therefore:
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{j,i} \abs{Y_{ji} - X_{ji}}
\leq \frac{2N-1}2 \sum_v \abs{\delta(v)}
= \frac{2N-1}2 \left( \sum_i \abs*{ \sum_j Z_{ji} } + \sum_j \abs*{ \sum_i Z_{ji} } \right),
\end{align*}
and now the claim follows from $Z = Y - X$ and $Y \in \mathcal{T}(r,s|A)$.

<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
(a)~We may assume without loss of generality that $x > y$.
    By concavity and using $h(0)=0$, we have $h(y) \geq \frac y x h(x)$ and $h(x-y) \geq \frac{x-y} x h(x)$, hence
    \begin{align*}
        h(x) - h(y)
    \leq \frac{x - y} x h(x)
    \leq h(x-y)
    \leq \sqrt{x-y} \max_{t \in [0,1]} \sqrt t \ln(1/t)
    = \frac2e \sqrt{x-y},
    \end{align*}
    as the right-hand side is maximized at $t=e^{-2}$.
    On the other hand, $h' \geq -1$ and $0 \leq h \leq h(e^{-1}) = e^{-1}$ on~$[0,1]$, hence
    \begin{align*}
        h(x) - h(y) \geq -\min \{ x-y, \frac1e \} \geq -\sqrt{\frac{x-y}e} \geq -\frac2e\sqrt{x-y}.
    \end{align*}

(b)~This follows at once from part~(a), because $\eta_c(x) = -c \, h(x/c)$ for $c>0$ and $x/c \in [0,1]$ (the case $c=0$ is immediate).
                    
                                     
                                   
                                           
                  

(c)~Let $z \coloneqq \min(y,c)$.
    Then,
    \begin{align*}
        \abs{\eta_c(x) - \eta_c(z)} \leq \frac2e \sqrt{c \abs{x-z}} \leq \frac2e \sqrt{c \abs{x-y}}
    \end{align*}
    by part~(b), and it suffices to prove that
    \begin{align*}
        \abs{\eta_c(z) - \eta_{c'}(y)} \leq \abs{c - c'}.
    \end{align*}
    Indeed, if $y>c$ then $z=c$ and $\eta_c(z)=0$, hence
    \begin{align*}
        \abs{\eta_c(z) - \eta_{c'}(y)}
    = \abs{\eta_{c'}(y)}
    = y \ln(c'/y)
    \leq c' - y \leq c' - c
    \end{align*}
    using the concavity of the logarithm, while if $y \leq c$ then $z = y$ and, similarly,
    \begin{align*}
        \abs{\eta_c(z) - \eta_{c'}(y)}
    =   \abs{\eta_c(y) - \eta_{c'}(y)}
    =   y \abs{\ln(c'/c)}
    \leq \abs{c'-c}
    \end{align*}
    (here we assume $y>0$ so that $c,c'>0$, otherwise both functions vanish).
    This concludes the proof of part~(c).

(d)~Because $Q_{ji} \leq C_{ji}$ and $\tilde Q_{ji} \leq \tilde C_{ji}$, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \abs*{ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\Vert C) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde Q\Vert \tilde C) }
    \leq \frac2e \sum_{j,i} \sqrt{ C_{ji} \abs{Q_{ji} - \tilde Q_{ji}}} + \norm{C - \tilde C}_1
    \leq \frac2e \sqrt{\sum_{j,i} C_{ji}} \sqrt{\norm{Q - \tilde Q}_1} + \norm{C - \tilde C}_1
    \end{align*}
    using part~(c) and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality.
    Because $U$ is a unitary and $p$, $q$ are probability distributions, again using the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
    \begin{align*}
        \sum_{j,i} C_{ji}
    = 2 \sum_{j,i} \abs{U_{ji}} \sqrt{q_j p_i}
    \leq 2 \sqrt{\sum_{j,i} \abs{U_{ji}}^2} \sqrt{\sum_{j,i} q_j p_i}
    \leq 2 \sqrt N,
    \end{align*}
    and now the bound follows.

\label{thm:cst quantitative robustness}
Let $\delta\geq0$.
Suppose~$(\rho, U)$,~$(\tilde\rho, \tilde U)$ satisfy~$\lVert \rho - \tilde\rho\rVert_{\max}, \lVert U - \tilde U\rVert_{\max} \leq \delta$.
Then:
\[
    \lVert P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U) - P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\tilde\rho, \tilde U)\rVert_{\max}
\leq \frac12\norm{P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U) - P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\tilde\rho, \tilde U)}_1
\leq 5 N \delta^{1/8}.
\]
In particular, the capped Schr\"odinger theory is robust.

<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Since both $P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U), P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\tilde\rho, \tilde U)$ are probability distributions by definition, the bound is trivial if $5 N \delta^{1/8} \geq 1$.
So without loss of generality we may assume that $\delta\leq1/(5 N)^8\leq1$.
First, we establish $\ell^1$-distance bounds between the Born marginals and the capacity matrices associated with~$(\rho,U)$ and $(\tilde\rho,\tilde U)$, respectively.
Let $p$ and $\tilde p$ denote the Born marginals of $\rho$ and $\tilde \rho$.
Then:
\begin{align*}
    \abs{p_i - \tilde p_i} = \abs{ \langlei | \rho - \tilde\rho | i\rangle } \leq \lVert \rho - \tilde\rho\rVert_{\max} \leq \delta \quad (\forall i).
\end{align*}
Next, we bound the distance between the Born marginals $q$ and $\tilde q$ of $U \rho U^\dagger$ and $\tilde U \tilde\rho \tilde U^\dagger$:
\begin{align*}
    \abs{q_j - \tilde q_j}
= \abs{ \langlej | U \rho U^{\dagger} - \tilde U \tilde\rho \tilde U^\dagger | j\rangle }
\leq \norm{U \rho U^\dagger - \tilde U \tilde\rho \tilde U^\dagger}_{\mathrm{op}}
\leq 2 \norm{U - \tilde U}_{\mathrm{op}} + \norm{\rho - \tilde\rho}_{\mathrm{op}}
\leq 3 N \delta,
\end{align*}
because $\norm{X}_{\mathrm{op}} \leq N \lVert X\rVert_{\max}$ for any $N\times N$ matrix~$X$.
Finally, we bound the distance between the capacity matrices~$C, \tilde C$ defined as in \cref{eq:p q C} for the pairs $\rho,U$ and $\tilde\rho,\tilde U$, respectively:
\begin{align*}
    \abs{C_{ji} - \tilde C_{ji}}
&= 2 \abs*{ \abs{U_{ji}} \sqrt{q_j p_i} - \abs{\tilde U_{ji}} \sqrt{\tilde q_j \tilde p_i} } \\
&\leq 2 \abs*{ \abs{U_{ji}} - \abs{\tilde U_{ji}} } \sqrt{q_j p_i} + 2 \abs{\tilde U_{ji}} \abs*{ \sqrt{q_j p_i} - \sqrt{\tilde q_j \tilde p_i} } \\
&\leq 2 \delta + 2 \sqrt{\abs*{ q_j p_i - \tilde q_j \tilde p_i} } \\
&\leq 2 \delta + 2 \sqrt{\abs*{ q_j - \tilde q_j} p_i + \tilde q_j \abs*{ p_i - \tilde p_i} } \\
&\leq 2 \delta + 2 \sqrt{3 N \delta + \delta}
\leq 6 \sqrt{N \delta}.
\end{align*}
Thus:
\begin{align*}
    \norm{p - \tilde p}_1 \leq N\delta \leq \sqrt\delta, \qquad
    \norm{q - \tilde q}_1 \leq 3N^2\delta \leq \sqrt\delta, \qquad
    \norm{C - \tilde C}_1 \leq 6 N^{5/2} \sqrt\delta \eqqcolon \kappa.
\end{align*}

Now set~$P \coloneqq P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\rho,U)$ and define $P'_{ji} \coloneqq \min(P_{ji}, \tilde C_{ji})$.
Since $P_{ji} \leq C_{ji}$, we have $\norm{P' - P}_1 \leq \kappa$.
Denoting the row and column sums of~$P'$ by~$q'$ and $p'$, respectively, it follows that $\norm{p' - p}_1, \norm{q' - q}_1 \leq \kappa$; hence we have $\norm{p' - \tilde p}_1, \norm{q' - \tilde q}_1 \leq \sqrt\delta + \kappa$.
Thus, \cref{thm:transportation correction} shows that there exists~$\tilde Q \in \mathcal{C}(\tilde\rho,\tilde U)$ such that
$\norm{P' - \tilde Q}_1
    \leq N \left( \norm{p' - \tilde p}_1 + \norm{q' - \tilde q}_1 \right)
    \leq 2 N (\sqrt\delta + \kappa)
    \leq 14 N^{7/2} \sqrt\delta$,
and hence
\[ \norm{\tilde Q - P}_1 \leq \norm{\tilde Q - P'}_1 + \norm{P' - P}_1 \leq 14 N^{7/2} \sqrt\delta + \kappa \leq 20 N^{7/2} \sqrt\delta. \]
Likewise, for $\tilde P \coloneqq P_{\mathrm{CST}}(\tilde\rho,\tilde U)$, there exists $Q \in \mathcal{C}(\rho,U)$ such that
\[ \norm{Q - \tilde P}_1 \leq 20 N^{7/2} \sqrt\delta. \]
Recall from \cref{eq:KL absU to KL C} that we can also define $P$ as the minimizer of~$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot\Vert C)$ over $\mathcal{C}(\rho,U)$, and likewise for~$\tilde P$.
We show that $Q$ is an approximate minimizer for this objective:
\begin{align}
\nonumber
&\quad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \Vert C) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \Vert C) \\
\nonumber
&= [ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \Vert C) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde P \Vert \tilde C) ]
+ \underbrace{[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde P \Vert \tilde C) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde Q \Vert \tilde C) ]}_{\leq0}
+ [ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde Q \Vert \tilde C) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \Vert C) ] \\
\nonumber
&\leq \frac{2\sqrt2}e N^{1/4} \left( \sqrt{\norm{Q - \tilde P}_1} + \sqrt{\norm{\tilde Q - P}_1} \right) + 2 \norm{C - \tilde C}_1 \\
\label{eq:approx min}
&\leq \frac{8\sqrt{10}}e N^2 \delta^{1/4} + 12 N^{5/2} \sqrt\delta
                                                                                      
                                                                             
\leq 10 N^2 \delta^{1/4},
\end{align}
where we used \cref{lem:elementary estimates}~(d) to upper bound the first and the last term; the underbraced middle term is nonpositive because~$\tilde P$ is a minimizer for $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot \Vert \tilde C)$ over $\mathcal{C}(\tilde\rho,\tilde U)$;
the final estimate uses our running assumption that $\delta \leq 1/(5N)^8$.

On the other hand, using the fact that $P$ is a minimizer on $\mathcal{C}(\rho,U)$ and the strong convexity estimate~\eqref{eq:strong convex} for $P_t \coloneqq (1-t) P + t Q$ ($t \in (0,1)$):
\begin{align*}
     D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \Vert C) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \Vert C)
\geq \frac1t \Bigl( (1-t) D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \Vert C) + t D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \Vert C) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_t \Vert C) \Bigr)
\geq \frac{1-t}2 \norm{P - Q}_1^2.
\end{align*}
Letting $t \downarrow 0$, we obtain $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \Vert C) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \Vert C) \geq \frac12 \norm{P - Q}_1^2$, and hence $\norm{P - Q}_1 \leq \sqrt{20} N \delta^{1/8}$, using \cref{eq:approx min}.
Thus:
\begin{align*}
    \norm{P - \tilde P}_1
\leq \norm{P - Q}_1 + \norm{Q - \tilde P}_1
\leq \sqrt{20} N \delta^{1/8} + 20 N^{7/2} \sqrt\delta
                                                                           
                                                              
\leq 5 N \delta^{1/8}
\end{align*}
and now the claim follows from the relation $\lVert P - \tilde P\rVert_{\max} \leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,\tilde P) = \frac12\norm{P-\tilde P}_1$.

We will assume both indifference and product commutativity to obtain a contradiction in dimension~$N=12$.
Let us denote the standard basis of~$\mathbb{C}^{12}$ by $\lvert 0\rangle$, \dots, $\lvert 11\rangle$.
We will consider the pure state~$\rho = \lvert \psi\rangle\!\langle \psi\rvert$, where $\lvert \psi\rangle=(\lvert 0\rangle + \lvert 1\rangle + \lvert 2\rangle - \lvert 3\rangle)/2$, which is a product state with respect to two distinct tensor product factorizations: $\mathbb{C}^{12} = \mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^6$ and $\mathbb{C}^{12} = \mathbb{C}^3 \otimes \mathbb{C}^4$.
Likewise, the unitary~$\mathcal V = I_6 \otimes H$, where $H$ is the Hadamard gate, is a local unitary with respect to either factorization.
By indifference, for any state~$\sigma$, the transition matrix $S(\sigma, \mathcal V)$ is block diagonal with $2\times2$ diagonal blocks, i.e., it preserves the span of $\{\lvert 2k\rangle,\lvert 2k+1\rangle\}$ for~$k=0,\dots,5$.
In particular, $S(\rho, \mathcal V) \lvert 6\rangle = p \lvert 6\rangle + (1-p) \lvert 7\rangle$ for some~$p\in[0,1]$.
The key idea will be to use product commutativity twice, once for each tensor product decomposition, to derive contradictory values of~$p$.

We first consider the factorization $\mathbb{C}^{12} = \mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^6$ and the local unitary $\mathcal U = H \otimes I_6$.
By product commutativity, we must have
\begin{align}\label{eq:prod comm 1}
    S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V) S(\rho, \mathcal U)
= S(\mathcal V \rho \mathcal V^\dagger, \mathcal U) S(\rho, \mathcal V).
\end{align}
Now, $\mathcal U\lvert \psi\rangle = (\lvert 0\rangle + \lvert 1\rangle + \lvert 2\rangle - \lvert 3\rangle + \lvert 6\rangle + \lvert 7\rangle + \lvert 8\rangle - \lvert 9\rangle)/\sqrt8$ and $\mathcal V\mathcal U\lvert \psi\rangle = (\lvert 0\rangle + \lvert 3\rangle + \lvert 6\rangle + \lvert 9\rangle)/2$.
The transition matrix $S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V)$ has to map the Born marginals of the former to the Born marginals of the latter.
By indifference, it follows that $S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V) \lvert 0\rangle = \lvert 0\rangle$ and $S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V) \lvert 6\rangle = \lvert 6\rangle$.
Also by indifference, for any state~$\sigma$, $S(\sigma, \mathcal U)$ preserves the span of $\{\lvert k\rangle, \lvert k+6\rangle\}$ for~$k=0,\dots,5$.
It follows that $S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V) S(\rho, \mathcal U) \lvert 6\rangle$
is a probability distribution supported on~$\{0,6\}$.
On the other hand:
\begin{align*}
    S(\mathcal V \rho \mathcal V^\dagger, \mathcal U) S(\rho, \mathcal V) \lvert 6\rangle
= p S(\mathcal V \rho \mathcal V^\dagger, \mathcal U) \lvert 6\rangle
+ (1-p) S(\mathcal V \rho \mathcal V^\dagger, \mathcal U) \lvert 7\rangle.
\end{align*}
Again by indifference, this is a probability distribution with probability mass~$p$ on~$\{0,6\}$ and probability mass~$1-p$ on~$\{1,7\}$.
By \cref{eq:prod comm 1}, we must have $p=1$.

Now we consider the factorization $\mathbb{C}^{12} = \mathbb{C}^3 \otimes \mathbb{C}^4$ and the local unitary $\mathcal U = (H \oplus 1) \otimes I_4$.
By product commutativity, we must have
\begin{align}\label{eq:prod comm 2}
    S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V) S(\rho, \mathcal U)
= S(\mathcal V \rho \mathcal V^\dagger, \mathcal U) S(\rho, \mathcal V).
\end{align}
This time around, $\mathcal U\lvert \psi\rangle = (\lvert 0\rangle + \lvert 1\rangle + \lvert 2\rangle - \lvert 3\rangle + \lvert 4\rangle + \lvert 5\rangle + \lvert 6\rangle - \lvert 7\rangle)/\sqrt8$ and $\mathcal V\mathcal U\lvert \psi\rangle = (\lvert 0\rangle + \lvert 3\rangle + \lvert 4\rangle + \lvert 7\rangle)/2$.
The transition matrix $S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V)$ has to map the Born marginals of the former to the Born marginals of the latter.
By indifference, it follows that $S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V) \lvert 2\rangle = \lvert 3\rangle$ and $S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V) \lvert 6\rangle = \lvert 7\rangle$.
Also by indifference, for any state~$\sigma$, $S(\sigma, \mathcal U)$ preserves the span of~$\{\lvert k\rangle,\lvert k+4\rangle\}$ for $k=0,\dots,3$ as well as the span of~$\{\lvert k\rangle\}$ for $k=8,\dots,11$.
Thus, $S(\mathcal U \rho \mathcal U^\dagger, \mathcal V) S(\rho, \mathcal U) \lvert 6\rangle$ is a probability distribution supported on~$\{3,7\}$.
On the other hand:
\begin{align*}
    S(\mathcal V \rho \mathcal V^\dagger, \mathcal U) S(\rho, \mathcal V) \lvert 6\rangle
= p S(\mathcal V \rho \mathcal V^\dagger, \mathcal U) \lvert 6\rangle
+ (1-p) S(\mathcal V \rho \mathcal V^\dagger, \mathcal U) \lvert 7\rangle.
\end{align*}
Again by indifference, this is a probability distribution with probability mass~$p$ on $\{2,6\}$ and probability mass~$1-p$ on~$\{3,7\}$.
By \cref{eq:prod comm 2}, we must have $p=0$.
This is the desired contradiction.

Take $U = H \oplus 1$ on $\mathbb{C}^3$, where $H$ is the Hadamard gate, so
\begin{align*}
    U = \begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2 & 1/\sqrt2 & 0 \\ 1/\sqrt2 & -1/\sqrt2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align*}
Clearly, the minimal blocks are $(\{1,2\},\{1,2\})$ and $(\{3\},\{3\})$, hence indifference implies that the stochastic matrix must look like:
\begin{align*}
    S(\rho, U) = \begin{pmatrix}\star & \star & 0 \\ \star & \star & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
Let $\lvert \pm\rangle = (\lvert 0\rangle \pm \lvert 1\rangle)/\sqrt2$; here and below we enumerate the standard basis of~$\mathbb{C}^3$ as $\lvert 0\rangle,\lvert 1\rangle,\lvert 2\rangle$.
Then, $H\lvert +\rangle=\lvert 0\rangle$ and $H\lvert -\rangle=\lvert 1\rangle$, hence compatibility with the Born marginals of the final states forces
\begin{align*}
    S(\lvert +\rangle\!\langle +\rvert, U) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad
    S(\lvert -\rangle\!\langle -\rvert, U) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},
\end{align*}
(the stochasticity of $S$ forces the nonzero entries to be one).
Likewise, the same argument shows that for the initial states $\rho_\pm \coloneqq \frac12 \lvert \pm\rangle\!\langle \pm\rvert + \frac12 \lvert 2\rangle\!\langle 2\rvert$,
\begin{align*}
    S(\rho_+, U) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad
    S(\rho_-, U) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align*}
On the other hand, decomposition invariance yields
\begin{align*}
    S(\rho_\pm, U) = \frac12 S(\lvert \pm\rangle\!\langle \pm\rvert, U) + \frac12 S(\lvert 2\rangle\!\langle 2\rvert, U)
\end{align*}
and hence we must have
\begin{align*}
    S(\lvert +\rangle\!\langle +\rvert, U) = S(\lvert 2\rangle\!\langle 2\rvert, U) = S(\lvert -\rangle\!\langle -\rvert, U),
\end{align*}
a contradiction.

A closely related three-dimensional construction is used in~\cite[Sec.~IV]{Aaronson} to show that for any indifferent hidden-variable theory, the assignment~$(\rho, U) \mapsto S(\rho,U)$ cannot be continuous in~$\rho$.
  For our unitary, the analogous pure-state construction would be to use~$\lvert \psi_\pm(\delta)\rangle = \delta \lvert \pm\rangle + \sqrt{1 - \delta^2} \lvert 2\rangle$.
  Alternatively, one can use the mixed states~$\rho_{\pm}(\delta) = \delta \lvert \pm\rangle\!\langle \pm\rvert + (1-\delta) \lvert 2\rangle\!\langle 2\rvert$ for~$\delta\in(0,1)$.
  Both versions give the claimed discontinuity: $S(\rho_{\pm}(\delta), U)$ is given by the same expressions as in the proof of \cref{thm:decompinvcom}; therefore the two transition matrices have different limits as~$\delta \to 0$, yet~$\rho_{\pm}(\delta) \to \lvert 2\rangle\!\langle 2\rvert$.
