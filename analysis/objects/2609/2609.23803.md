<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Say Alice chooses to color \(w\) as per Algorithm~\ref{A:Algorithm}.
    First, consider the case when \(p(w)\) is colored, say with color \(1\).
    If the colors \(2\) and \(3\) are forbidden for \(w\), then either \(w\) has two children that are colored \textbf{[2]} and \textbf{[3]}, or \(w\) has one child colored \textbf{[2]} and \(\lfloor \deg(w)/2 \rfloor + 1\) children colored \(3\) (or vice-versa).
    The first scenario is not possible because we assume that $\textnormal{config}_{5}$ does not arise.
    For the second scenario, a case analysis shows that since \(\deg(w) \leq \Delta(T) \leq 4\), \(w\) does not have enough children to have both a majority colored child with the color \textbf{[2]} as well as \(\lfloor \deg(w)/2 \rfloor + 1\) children colored \(3\).
    Indeed, for \(\deg(w) = 1,2,3,4\), we find that the second scenario requires \(w\) to have at least \(\lfloor \deg(w)/2 \rfloor + 2 = 2,3,3,4\) children, respectively.
    But this is not possible since \(w\) also has a parent.
    So, at least one of the colors \(2\) and \(3\) is available for Alice at the vertex \(w\).

    Next, consider the case when \(p(w)\) is not colored.
    As per Algorithm~\ref{A:Algorithm}, this situation only arises when the vertex \(w\) has a colored child.
    In particular, \(w\) is not a leaf: this is crucial because if \(w\) were a leaf then by Observation~\ref{obs_checkx}(\ref{obs_checkx_a}), any color for \(w\) would make it majority colored, violating the requirements of Algorithm~\ref{A:Algorithm}.
    Now, by Observation~\ref{obs_checkx}(\ref{obs_checkx_c}), if Alice has at least two choices of colors available for \(w\), then she can choose one for \(w\) such that \(w\) does not become majority colored.
    So, suppose (without loss of generality) that Alice cannot color \(w\) with colors \(2\) and \(3\).
    Then, either \(w\) has two children that are colored \textbf{[2]} and \textbf{[3]}, or \(w\) has one child colored \textbf{[2]} and \(\lfloor \deg(w)/2 \rfloor + 1\) children colored \(3\) (or vice-versa).
    We have seen in the previous paragraph that neither scenario can arise, so at least one of the colors \(2\) and \(3\) is available for Alice at the vertex \(w\).
    Say that it is the color \(3\).
    If coloring \(w\) with \(3\) does not make \(w\) majority colored, then we are done.
    If not, Alice would be forced to use the color \(3\) on \(w\) only if the colors \(1\) and \(2\) are both forbidden at \(w\).
    By the same argument repeated for this pair of colors, at least one of them is available at \(w\), say the color \(2\).
    Recall by Observation~\ref{obs_checkx}(\ref{obs_checkx_c}) that when the color \(3\) makes \(w\) majority colored, the color \(2\) does not make \(w\) majority colored.
    So, Alice can color \(w\) such that \(w\) does not become majority colored.
    This completes the proof.

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:Extended3coloring}
 $G$ can be properly colored with $3$ colors if and only if the given partial coloring of $H$ can be extended to a majority coloring of $H$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
Consider a proper coloring of $G$.
Color each vertex $v^\prime \in V^\prime \subset V(H)$ with the color of the corresponding vertex $v$ in $G$.
This is indeed a majority coloring of $H$, for each vertex $v^\prime\in V^\prime$ has degree (in $H$) equal to $3\cdot \deg_G(v)$ and has exactly $\frac{1}{3}$ of its neighbors colored with color $i$, (for $i\in\{1,2,3\})$.
Therefore, the majority condition is satisfied at all the vertices of $V^\prime$.
Now, consider a vertex $w^{i}_{uv}\in W$, which has two neighbors $u^\prime$ and $v^\prime$.
Note that $u^\prime$ and $v^\prime$ have different colors, because the corresponding vertices $u$ and $v$ have different colors in a proper coloring of $G$.
Therefore, at most one of the two neighbors of $w^{i}_{uv}$ has the same color as $w^{i}_{uv}$, i.e., the color $i$.
Hence, the majority condition is satisfied at each vertex in $W$ as well, and thus at all the vertices of $H$.

Now, we show that whenever $H$ has a majority coloring with $3$ colors which is an extension of the given partial coloring, $G$ has a proper coloring with $3$ colors.
For each vertex $v\in V(G)$, assign it the color of the corresponding vertex $v^\prime\in V^\prime\subset V(H)$.
Suppose that there are two vertices $u$ and $v$ in $G$ which get assigned the same color, say $i$, due to this coloring.
This is possible only when the corresponding vertices $u^\prime$ and $v^\prime$ are assigned the same color $i$ in the coloring of $H$.
But this violates the majority condition at $w^{i}_{uv}$, hence we have a contradiction. Thus, the described coloring is a proper $3$-coloring of $G$.

<!-- lemma-8: body_md -->
\label{lem:fwd}
    If Alice has a winning strategy in the proper coloring game on $G$, then she has a winning strategy in the majority precoloring extension game on $H$.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
If Alice's move on the graph \(G\) in her winning strategy for the proper coloring game is to color the vertex $u \in V(G)$ with color $i$, then her strategy on \(H\) is to color the corresponding vertex \(u^\prime \in V(H)\) with the same color \(i\).
When Bob colors a vertex $v^\prime \in V(H)$, Alice responds by playing according to the strategy in $G$ where Bob would have colored the corresponding vertex $v \in V(G)$ with the same color. Note that Bob can only color a vertex in $V^\prime$ and not in $W$, as the vertices in $W$ are already colored.

We need to show that this is a well-defined strategy.
Suppose not, for the sake of contradiction.
Consider the first instance where the strategy violates the conditions of either the proper coloring game on \(G\) or the majority precoloring extension game on \(H\).
In the former case, it must be that Bob just colored a vertex \(u^\prime \in V(H)\) with the color \(i\), but coloring the vertex \(u \in V(G)\) with the color \(i\) violated the properness condition at \(u\).
Thus there is some neighbor \(v\) of \(u\) which was already colored \(i\), and hence the vertex \(v^\prime \in V(H)\) was already colored \(i\).
So, the vertex \(w^i_{uv} \in V(H)\), which was pre-colored with the color \(i\), would have been majority colored before Bob colored \(u\), and so the color \(i\) would in fact have been forbidden at the vertex \(u\).
In the latter case, it must be that Alice just colored the vertex \(u \in V(G)\) with the color \(i\), but coloring the vertex \(u^\prime \in V(H)\) violated the majority condition at some vertex \(x \in N_H[u^\prime]\).
This vertex \(x\) cannot be \(u^\prime\), since \(u^\prime\) has \(k \geq 2\) neighbors that are pre-colored with \(k\) different colors.
So \(x\) must be of the form \(w^i_{uv}\) for some edge \(uv \in E(G)\).
For the majority condition to be violated at \(w^i_{uv}\) after coloring \(u\) with the color \(i\), it must have been that \(v^\prime\) was already colored with the color \(i\).
But then \(v \in V(G)\) would have been colored \(i\) before the vertex \(u\) was colored, so the color \(i\) would have been forbidden for Alice at the vertex \(u\).
Thus in either case we get a contradiction, and so the strategy is well-defined.

Lastly, we show that this defines a winning strategy for Alice on the majority precoloring extension game on \(H\).
Suppose not, for the sake of contradiction.
By the well-definedness of the strategy, it must be Bob who is forced to make a move which violates the majority condition at a vertex.
That is, for every remaining uncolored vertex \(u^\prime\) of \(H\), and each choice of color \(i\) violates the majority condition at a vertex \(w^i_{uv}\) for some edge \(uv \in E(G)\).
But this means that the other neighbor \(v^\prime\) of \(w^i_{uv}\) must have already been colored \(i\).
In the graph \(G\), this means that at the same stage of partial coloring, for every uncolored vertex \(u\) and every color \(i\), there is some neighbor \(v\) of \(u\) that has been colored \(i\).
This means that Alice has lost the proper coloring game on \(G\), contradicting that she plays as per her winning strategy on \(G\).
Hence, this defines a winning strategy for Alice on the majority precoloring extension game on \(H\).
<!-- end proof-7 -->
<!-- lemma-9: body_md -->
\label{lem:rev}
    If Alice has a winning strategy in the majority precoloring extension game on $H$, then she has a winning strategy in the proper coloring game on $G$.
<!-- end lemma-9 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
The proof is very similar to the previous one.
If Alice's move on the graph \(H\) in her winning strategy for the majority precoloring extension game is to color the vertex $u^\prime \in V(H)$ with color $i$, then her strategy on \(G\) is to color the corresponding vertex \(u \in V(G)\) with the same color \(i\).
When Bob colors a vertex $v \in V(G)$, Alice responds by playing according to the strategy in $H$ where Bob would have colored the corresponding vertex $v^\prime \in V(H)$ with the same color. Note that both Alice and Bob can only color a vertex in $V^\prime$ and not in $W$, as the vertices in $W$ are already colored.

Again, we need to show that this is a well-defined strategy.
Suppose Alice's move on the graph \(H\) of coloring the vertex \(u^\prime\) with the color \(i\) does not correspond to a proper coloring on the graph \(G\).
Then it must be that some neighbor \(v\) of \(u\) in \(G\) is already colored \(i\).
But then \(v^\prime \in V(H)\) would have already been colored \(i\), and so \(w^i_{uv} \in V(H)\) would have been majority colored with the color \(i\), so the color \(i\) would not have been available on the vertex \(v^\prime\) for Alice in the first place.
Similarly, suppose Bob's move on the graph \(G\) of coloring the vertex \(u\) with the color \(i\) violates the majority condition in \(H\) at a vertex \(w^i_{uv}\) for some edge \(uv\) of \(G\).
Then, it must be that \(v \in V(G)\) was already colored with the color \(i\), so this color was not available at the vertex \(u\) in the first place.
Hence, this strategy is well-defined.

Lastly, we need to show that this is a winning strategy for Alice.
If not, then it must be that it is Bob's turn, and any choice of color on any remaining uncolored vertex in \(G\) violates the properness condition at that vertex.
This means that for each uncolored vertex \(u \in V(H)\) and each color \(i\), there is some neighbor \(w^i_{uv}\) which is majority colored, as the other neighbor of this vertex is already colored with the color \(i\).
But then Alice has lost the majority precoloring extension game on \(H\), contradicting that she plays as per her winning strategy on this graph.
Hence, this defines a winning strategy for Alice on the proper coloring game on \(G\).
<!-- end proof-8 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
Note that all the vertices of the graph $G$ end up being colored when Alice plays according to this strategy and we now show that the coloring obtained is indeed a majority coloring of the graph $G$, and hence all the moves of the players were valid in the majority $2$-precoloring extension game.
Clearly, all the vertices in $D$ satisfy the majority condition because all the vertices in $D$ are red and have exactly one neighbor (in $C$), which is blue.
The vertices in $R^\prime$ satisfy the majority condition because they are colored red and each has at most one red neighbor and exactly one blue neighbor.
The vertices in $B^\prime$ satisfy the majority condition because they are colored blue and each has exactly one red neighbor.
Therefore, the majority condition is satisfied by the vertices in $D\cup B^\prime \cup R^\prime$. 

Note that for each $i\in[1,n]$, the vertices $x_i$ and $\bar{x_i}$ cannot have the same color.

Suppose $x_i$ ($\bar{x_i}$) is colored red (blue) and Bob attempts to color $\bar{x_i}$ ($x_i$) red (blue): this will not be possible according to the rules of the game as the majority condition will be violated at $r_i$ ($b_i$).
Therefore, $x_i$ and $\bar{x_i}$ must have opposite colors.

Since $x_i$ and $\bar{x_i}$ must have opposite colors (for each $i\in [1,n]$), the majority condition is satisfied at each $r_i$ and each $b_i$ (for all $i\in [1,n]$).

Therefore, the majority condition is satisfied by the vertices in $R\cup B\cup L$. 

Consider a positive vertex $x_i$ (where $i\in [1,n]$) in $X$, such that the variable $x_i$ appears in $k$ clauses of $\Phi$.
By the construction, the positive vertex $x_i$ has $k$ blue neighbors in $C$, $k$ red neighbors in $R$, one red neighbor $r_i$, and one blue neighbor $b_i$.
Therefore, the majority condition is not violated at $x_i$.

Now consider a clause vertex $C_j$ (where $j\in [1,m]$) in $C$. Suppose the clause $C_j$ has $k$ literals in $\Phi$.
Then the clause vertex $C_j$ has $k-1$ red neighbors in $D$ and $k$ neighbors in $X$. Since Alice wins in the \textnormal{\textsc{POS-CNF}} game, in the final assignment there exists a variable $x_i$ in $C_j$ which was set to True by Alice (recall that we have assumed without loss of generality that Bob never sets any variables to True).
According to Alice's strategy in the majority $2$-precoloring extension game, she must have colored the positive vertex $x_i$ red.
This is possible, because if Bob had already colored $\bar{x_i}$ red, then according to Alice's strategy, she considers the variable $x_i$ to be already set to False, hence the strategy would not have permitted her to color $x_i$.
Coloring the vertex $x_i$ red also cannot violate the majority condition at any clause vertex because all the clause vertices are colored blue.
When $x_i$ is colored by Alice, $\bar{x_i}$ was uncolored as seen above, and thus the majority condition is not violated at $r_i$ or $b_i$.
Lastly, as we have seen above, coloring a vertex in $X$ does not violate the majority condition at a vertex in $R$.
Thus, Alice has assigned the color red to $x_i$ and hence the clause vertex $C_j$ has at least one red neighbor and at most $k-1$ blue neighbors in $X$.
Thus, the majority condition is satisfied at each vertex $C_j$ in $C$.

Consider the final assignment of the variables after Bob plays according to the winning strategy of the \textnormal{\textsc{POS-CNF}} game.
Since this is a winning strategy for Bob, there exists a clause $C_j=x_{i_1}\lor x_{i_2} \lor \dotsb \lor x_{i_k}$ such that $C_j$ is False, i.e., $x_{i_1}, x_{i_2}, \dotsc, x_{i_k}$ are all set to False.
Now consider the situation in the majority $2$-precoloring extension game, when Bob has set $x_{i_1}, x_{i_2}, \dotsc, x_{i_k}$ to False in the POS-CNF game (recall that Alice never sets a variable to False).
The negative vertices $\bar{x_{i_1}}, \bar{x_{i_2}}, \dotsc, \bar{x_{i_k}}$ are colored red by Bob (because if Alice had colored either $x_{i_\ell}$ or $\bar{x_{i_\ell}}$, then in the strategy for Bob, the variable $x_{i_\ell}$ is assumed to be set to True).
Now, all the vertices $x_{i_1}, x_{i_2}, \dotsc, x_{i_k}$ must be colored blue because coloring any $x_{i_\ell}$ red violates the majority condition at $r_{i_\ell}$.
Without loss of generality, we assume that $x_{i_1}, x_{i_2}, \dotsc, x_{i_{k-1}}$ are colored blue when $x_{i_k}$ is uncolored.
Recall that $x_{i_k}$ cannot be colored red because $\bar{x_{i_k}}$ is colored red and the majority condition at $r_{i_k}$ will be violated.
If $x_{i_k}$ is colored blue, then the clause vertex $C_j$ has $k$ blue neighbors ($x_{i_1}, x_{i_2}, \dotsc, x_{i_k}$), and $k-1$ red neighbors $D^1_j, D^2_j, \dotsc, D^{k-1}_j$.
Therefore, the majority condition is violated at $C_j$, since $C_j$ is colored blue.
Therefore, the positive vertex $x_{i_k}$ cannot be assigned the color blue as well.
Hence, all the vertices of the graph $G$ cannot be colored and Bob wins in the majority $2$-precoloring extension game.
