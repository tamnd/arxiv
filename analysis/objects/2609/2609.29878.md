<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:binary}
Let $\{v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4\}$ be a basis of $\mathbb F_2^4$, and put
$$\Omega=\{v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4,\,
 v_2+v_3+v_4,\,
 v_1+v_3+v_4,\,
 v_1+v_2+v_4\}.$$
Every nonempty subset of $\Omega$ with sum zero has
cardinality four.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
With weight taken relative to the chosen basis, the set
$\Omega$ consists of the eight odd-weight vectors with
$w=v_1+v_2+v_3$ removed.
Let $B\subseteq\Omega$ be a nonempty subset with
$\sum_{v\in B}v=0$. Relative to the chosen basis, define
the linear map
$$\lambda:\mathbb F_2^4\longrightarrow\mathbb F_2,\qquad
\lambda(a_1v_1+a_2v_2+a_3v_3+a_4v_4)
=a_1+a_2+a_3+a_4,$$
where the sum on the right is taken modulo two.
Each element of $\Omega$ has either one or three nonzero
coordinates, so $\lambda(v)=1$ for every $v\in\Omega$.
Consequently,
$$0=\lambda\left(\sum_{v\in B}v\right)
=\sum_{v\in B}\lambda(v)=|B|\cdot1
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2.$$
Thus $|B|$ is even. Since $B$ is nonempty and $|\Omega|=7$,
we have $|B|\in\{2,4,6\}$.
The case $|B|=2$ is impossible, since two vectors in
$\mathbb F_2^4$ sum to zero only when they are equal,
whereas the elements of $B$ are distinct.

Among the eight odd-weight vectors, each coordinate is
equal to one exactly four times. Their sum is therefore
zero. Since $\Omega$ consists of these eight vectors
with $w$ removed, we obtain
$$\sum_{v\in\Omega}v=-w=w.$$
If $|B|=6$, let $u$ be the unique element of
$\Omega\setminus B$. Then
$w=\sum_{v\in\Omega}v
=\sum_{v\in B}v+u=u,$
contradicting $u\in\Omega$ and $w\notin\Omega$.
Therefore $|B|=4$.
<!-- end proof-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
We first construct a zero-sum-free sequence over
\begin{equation}\label{equation:Bm}
B_m=C_m^3\oplus C_{2m}^4\oplus C_{6m}
\end{equation}
such that the support generates the group $B_m$
for every integer $m\ge1$.  Let $z_1,z_2,z_3,y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4,e$ generate the respective
cyclic direct summands of $B_m$, so that
$$\operatorname{ord}(z_i)=m\quad(1\le i\le3),\qquad
  \operatorname{ord}(y_j)=2m\quad(1\le j\le4),\qquad
  \operatorname{ord}(e)=6m.$$
Define
\begin{align}
 q_j&=y_j+2e &&(1\le j\le4),\label{eq:q-first}\\
 q_{4+i}&=z_i+\sum_{\substack{1\le j\le4\\j\ne i}}y_j+2e
       &&(1\le i\le3),\label{eq:q-last}
\end{align}
and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:explicit-sequence}
 S_m=e^{\,4m-1}\prod_{j=1}^7q_j^{\,2m-1}.
\end{equation}
We have
$$|S_m|=(4m-1)+7(2m-1)=18m-8.$$
Moreover, $\operatorname{supp}(S_m)$ generates $B_m$, since it contains
$e,q_1,\ldots,q_7$, and the relations $y_j=q_j-2e$
together with \eqref{eq:q-last} show that all the $y_j$
and $z_i$ belong to $\langle\operatorname{supp}(S_m)\rangle$.

To prove that $S_m$ is zero-sum free, suppose, to the contrary, that it
contains a {\sl nonempty} zero-sum subsequence
$$T=e^c\prod_{j=1}^4q_j^{a_j}\prod_{i=1}^3q_{4+i}^{b_i},   \qquad \text{ where } 0\le c\le4m-1 \text{ and } 0\le a_j,b_i\le2m-1.$$
Considering the direct summand $\langle z_i\rangle$ of $B_m$,
we obtain $b_i z_i=0$ from $\sigma(T)=0$, and hence
$m\mid b_i$ for each $i\in\{1,2,3\}$.
Considering the direct summand
$\langle y_j\rangle$ of $B_m$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:y-coordinate}
 a_j+\sum_{\substack{1\le i\le3\\i\ne j}}b_i
 \equiv0\pmod{2m}, \qquad \text{ where } j\in\{1,2,3,4\}.
\end{equation}
Consequently $m\mid a_j$ for every $j$.
Similarly, considering the direct summand $\langle e\rangle$,
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:e-coordinate}
 c+2\sum_{j=1}^4a_j+2\sum_{i=1}^3b_i
 \equiv0\pmod{6m},
\end{equation}
and therefore $m\mid c$. Write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c'aj'bi'}
 c=mc',\qquad a_j=ma_j',\qquad b_i=mb_i',
 \qquad \text{ where }
 0\le c'\le3\quad \text{ and } \quad a_j',b_i'\in\{0,1\}.
\end{equation}
Dividing each congruence in \eqref{eq:y-coordinate} by $m$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{equation:a'j+=0mod2}
 a'_j+\sum_{\substack{1\le i\le3\\i\ne j}}b'_i
 \equiv0\pmod2
 \qquad (1\le j\le4).
\end{equation}
Let $\{v_1,\ldots,v_4\}$ be a basis of $\mathbb F_2^4$.
The four congruences in \eqref{equation:a'j+=0mod2}
are equivalent to the single vector equation
\begin{equation}\label{equation:coordinatEqu}
 \sum_{j=1}^4a'_jv_j
 +b'_1(v_2+v_3+v_4)
 +b'_2(v_1+v_3+v_4)
 +b'_3(v_1+v_2+v_4)=0
 \quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2^4.
\end{equation}
Since every coefficient is either zero or one, the vectors
with coefficient one form a possibly empty zero-sum subset
of the set $\Omega$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:binary}.
Its cardinality is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h=}
 h=\sum_{j=1}^4a'_j+\sum_{i=1}^3b'_i.
\end{equation}
Lemma~\ref{lem:binary} therefore gives $h\in\{0,4\}$.
Dividing \eqref{eq:e-coordinate} by $m$ and using
\eqref{eq:h=}, we obtain
$$c'+2h\equiv0\pmod6.$$
If $h=4$, this congruence would give $c'\equiv4\pmod6$,
contrary to $0\le c'\le3$. Hence $h=0$, and the same
congruence then gives $c'=0$.
Since the summands in \eqref{eq:h=} are nonnegative,
$h=0$ forces $a'_j=0$ for every $j$ and $b'_i=0$ for
every $i$. By \eqref{eq:c'aj'bi'}, all the original
multiplicities $c,a_j,b_i$ are therefore zero.
Thus $T$ is the empty sequence, a contradiction.
This proves that $S_m$ is zero-sum free.

Now let $r\ge8$, as in the theorem.
Choose a decomposition
$$G_{m,r}=B_m\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r-8}\langle f_i\rangle,
 \qquad \operatorname{ord}(f_i)=m\quad(1\le i\le r-8),$$
and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:padded-sequence}
 S_{m,r}=S_m\prod_{i=1}^{r-8}f_i^{\,m-1}.
\end{equation}
When $r=8$, the additional direct sum is trivial and
the product is empty, so $S_{m,8}=S_m$.
Since $S_m$ is zero-sum free, it follows from \eqref{eq:padded-sequence} that $S_{m,r}$ is zero-sum free.
Its support generates $G_{m,r}$, since $\operatorname{supp}(S_m)$
generates $B_m$, and every $f_i$ occurs in $S_{m,r}$ (when $m>1$).
Moreover,
$$|S_{m,r}|=18m-8+(r-8)(m-1)=(r+10)m-r,$$
which yields $\mathsf d(G_{m,r})\ge(r+10)m-r$.
We check that
$\mathsf d^*(G_{m,r})
 =(r-5)(m-1)+4(2m-1)+(6m-1)
 =(r+9)m-r.$
This gives $\delta(G_{m,r})=\mathsf d(G_{m,r})-\mathsf d^*(G_{m,r})\ge((r+10)m-r)-((r+9)m-r)= m$, and we are done.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:lifting}
Let $A=C_{n_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_r}$, where
$1<n_1\mid\cdots\mid n_r$.
Let $T=\prod_{i=1}^k g_i^{t_i}$ be a zero-sum-free
sequence of length $L$ over $A$, where $t_i\in\mathbb N_0$
and $g_1,\ldots,g_k$ are distinct and generate $A$.
For every integer $m\ge2$, the group
$$A_{m,k}=C_m^{k-r}\oplus
C_{mn_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{mn_r}$$
has a zero-sum-free sequence of length $m(L+k)-k$
whose support is a generating set of cardinality $k$.
In particular, $\mathsf r(A_{m,k})=k$ and
$$\delta(A_{m,k})\ge m\bigl(L-\mathsf d^*(A)\bigr).$$
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Let $\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_k$ be a basis
of $\mathbb Z^k$, and let
$\varphi:\mathbb Z^k\rightarrow A$
be the group epimorphism determined by
\begin{equation}\label{equation:var(epsilon)=g}
\varphi(\epsilon_i)=g_i \qquad \text{ for every } i\in \{1,\ldots,k\}.
\end{equation}
Denote $\Lambda=\ker \varphi$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{equation:A/ZZk=lmabda}
A\cong\mathbb Z^k/\Lambda
\end{equation} and
the index $[\mathbb Z^k: \ker \varphi]=|A|$.
By the subgroup theorem for free abelian groups,
$\Lambda$ is free abelian of rank at most $k$.
Since $|A|\mathbb Z^k\subseteq\Lambda$, it contains the $k$
linearly independent elements
$|A|\epsilon_1,\ldots,|A|\epsilon_k$.
Hence $\Lambda$ has rank exactly $k$.




Define
$$B=\mathbb Z^k/m\Lambda.$$ Recall $\varphi$ is surjective, i.e., every element of $A$ can be written as $\varphi(z)$ for some $z\in\mathbb Z^k$.
Then we see that the map
\begin{equation}\label{equation:iota}
\iota:A\longrightarrow B,\qquad \text{ given by }
\iota(\varphi(z))=mz+m\Lambda\quad(z\in\mathbb Z^k)
\end{equation}
is well defined, since $\varphi(z)=\varphi(z')$ implies
$z-z'\in\Lambda$ and hence $m(z-z')\in m\Lambda$.
It is easy to check that $\iota$ is a homomorphism. Moreover, if
$\iota(\varphi(z))=0$, then $mz=m\lambda$ for some
$\lambda\in\Lambda$. Since $\mathbb Z^k$ is torsion-free,
we obtain $z=\lambda\in\Lambda$, so $\varphi(z)=0$.
Thus $\iota$ is injective.


Since $m\Lambda\subseteq m\mathbb Z^k$, the universal property
of quotient groups
(see \cite[Chapter~I, Theorem~5.6]{Hungerford} for example)
shows that the canonical epimorphism
$\mathbb Z^k\to\mathbb Z^k/m\mathbb Z^k$ induces an epimorphism
$$\pi:B\longrightarrow\mathbb Z^k/m\mathbb Z^k\cong C_m^k,\qquad \text{ given by }
\pi(z+m\Lambda)=z+m\mathbb Z^k.$$
Its kernel is
$$\ker\pi=\{mz+m\Lambda:z\in\mathbb Z^k\}=\iota(A).$$
Thus the first isomorphism theorem gives
$B/\iota(A)\cong C_m^k$.
Moreover,
$$\pi(\epsilon_i+m\Lambda)=\epsilon_i+m\mathbb Z^k \qquad \text{ for every } i\in \{1,\ldots,k\}.$$
These images form a basis of $\mathbb Z^k/m\mathbb Z^k$.
Since $\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_k$ form a basis of $\mathbb Z^k$
and $m\ge2$, we have $\epsilon_i\notin m\mathbb Z^k$ for every $i$
and $\epsilon_i-\epsilon_j\notin m\mathbb Z^k$ whenever $i\ne j$.
As $m\Lambda\subseteq m\mathbb Z^k$, it follows that the cosets
$\epsilon_1+m\Lambda,\ldots,\epsilon_k+m\Lambda$
are nonzero and pairwise distinct.


We define a sequence $U_m$ over $B$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lifted-sequence}
 U_m=\prod_{i=1}^k
 (\epsilon_i+m\Lambda)^{\,m(t_i+1)-1}.
\end{equation}
Now we show that $U_m$ is zero-sum free.
Suppose, to the contrary, that it has a nonempty
zero-sum subsequence
$$V=\prod_{i=1}^k(\epsilon_i+m\Lambda)^{c_i},$$
where  $0\le c_i\le m(t_i+1)-1$ for every $i\in \{1,\ldots,k\}$.
Since $\sigma(\pi(V))=\pi(\sigma(V))=\pi(0)=0$, it follows that
$\sum_{i=1}^k c_i(\epsilon_i+m\mathbb Z^k)=0.$
Equivalently,
$\sum_{i=1}^k c_i\epsilon_i\in m\mathbb Z^k.$
Since $\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_k$ form a basis of
$\mathbb Z^k$, we conclude that $m\mid c_i$ for every $i\in \{1,\ldots,k\}$.
Write
\begin{equation}\label{equation:ci=mai}
c_i=ma_i
\end{equation}
 with $a_i\in\mathbb N_0$.
The inequality $c_i<m(t_i+1)$ gives
$0\le a_i\le t_i$ where $1\le i\le k.$ Since $\sum_{i=1}^k a_i \epsilon_i\in \mathbb Z^k$,
it follows from \eqref{equation:var(epsilon)=g}, \eqref{equation:iota} and \eqref{equation:ci=mai} that
$$0=\sigma(V)=\sum_{i=1}^k c_i \epsilon_i+m\Lambda
=m\sum_{i=1}^k a_i\epsilon_i+m\Lambda=\iota\left(\varphi(\sum_{i=1}^k a_i \epsilon_i)\right)
=\iota\left(\sum_{i=1}^k a_i g_i\right).$$
Since $\iota$ is injective, it follows that
$\sum_{i=1}^k a_i g_i=0.$
Thus $\prod_{i=1}^k g_i^{a_i}$ is a zero-sum
subsequence of $T$.
As $T$ is zero-sum free, all $a_i$ are zero,
and hence all $c_i$ are zero.
This contradicts the assumption that $V$ is nonempty.
Therefore $U_m$ is zero-sum free.

Since $m\ge2$ and $t_i\ge0$, every exponent (multiplicity) in
\eqref{eq:lifted-sequence} is positive. Hence
$$\operatorname{supp}(U_m)=\{\epsilon_i+m\Lambda:1\le i\le k\}.$$
This set has cardinality $k$ and generates $B$,
since $\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_k$ generate $\mathbb Z^k$.
Moreover,
$$|U_m|=\sum_{i=1}^k\bigl(m(t_i+1)-1\bigr)
=m(L+k)-k.$$


Since $g_1,\ldots,g_k$ generate $A$, we have $k\ge r$.
Since $\Lambda$ has rank $k$, by the elementary divisors theorem for subgroups of free abelian groups (see \cite[Chapter~II, Theorem~1.6, p.~73]{Hungerford} or \cite[Chapter~III, Theorem~7.8, p.~153]{Lang}), there exists a basis $\{u_1,\ldots,u_k\}$ of $\mathbb Z^k$ and positive integers $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_k$ such that
$\{d_1u_1,\ldots,d_ku_k\}$ forms a basis of $\Lambda$. Thus,
$$\Lambda=d_1\mathbb Z u_1\oplus\cdots\oplus d_k\mathbb Z u_k.$$
Consequently,
$$\mathbb Z^k/\Lambda
\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^k\bigl(\mathbb Z u_i/d_i\mathbb Z u_i\bigr)
\cong C_{d_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{d_k}.$$
On the other hand, recall \eqref{equation:A/ZZk=lmabda},
$$\mathbb Z^k/\Lambda\cong A
=C_{n_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_r},
\qquad 1<n_1\mid\cdots\mid n_r.$$
By the uniqueness of the invariant factors, exactly $r$
of the integers $d_1,\ldots,d_k$ are greater than $1$,
and these are $n_1,\ldots,n_r$, in this order. Hence
$$d_i=1\quad(1\le i\le k-r),\qquad
d_{k-r+j}=n_j\quad(1\le j\le r).$$
It follows that
$\{u_1,\ldots,u_{k-r},
n_1u_{k-r+1},\ldots,n_ru_k\}$
forms a basis of $\Lambda$. Consequently,
$\{mu_1,\ldots,mu_{k-r},
mn_1u_{k-r+1},\ldots,mn_ru_k\}$
is a basis of $m\Lambda$, and therefore
$$B=\mathbb Z^k/m\Lambda
\cong C_m^{k-r}\oplus
C_{mn_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{mn_r}
=A_{m,k}.$$ Observe $\mathsf r(A_{m,k})=k$.
Under this isomorphism, $U_m$ gives a zero-sum-free
sequence over $A_{m,k}$ of length $m(L+k)-k$
whose support is a generating set of cardinality $k$.
Finally,
$$\mathsf d^*(A_{m,k})
=(k-r)(m-1)+\sum_{j=1}^r(mn_j-1)
=m\bigl(\mathsf d^*(A)+k\bigr)-k.$$
Since $\mathsf d(A_{m,k})\ge |U_m|$, we obtain
$\delta(A_{m,k})
=\mathsf d(A_{m,k})-\mathsf d^*(A_{m,k})
\ge |U_m|-\mathsf d^*(A_{m,k})
=m\bigl(L-\mathsf d^*(A)\bigr).$ This completes the proof of the theorem.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:rank-five}
Let $r\ge5$ and $m\ge1$ be integers, and put
$F_{m,r}=C_m^{r-4}\oplus C_{3m}^3\oplus C_{6m}.$
Then  $\mathsf d(F_{m,r})-\mathsf d^*(F_{m,r})\ge m.$
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
We first consider
$$F_{m,5}=C_m\oplus C_{3m}^3\oplus C_{6m}.$$
Let $f_1,\ldots,f_5$ generate the respective cyclic direct
summands, and put
\begin{gather*}
g_1=f_1+f_2+2f_3+f_4+5f_5,\qquad
g_2=f_2+f_5,\qquad g_3=f_3+f_5,\\
g_4=f_4,\qquad g_5=f_5,\qquad
g_6=g_2+g_3+g_4+g_5,\qquad g_7=g_4+g_5.
\end{gather*}
We shall show that the sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rank-five-sequence}
 S_m=g_1^{3m-1}g_2^{3m-1}g_3^{3m-1}
     g_4^{2m-1}g_5^{2m-1}g_6^{2m}g_7^{2m}
\end{equation}
is zero-sum free.
Let $V=\prod_{i=1}^7g_i^{c_i}$ be a zero-sum subsequence
of $S_m$. By the definitions of $g_1,\ldots,g_7$, we have
$$
\begin{aligned}
0=\sum_{i=1}^7c_i g_i
&=c_1f_1+(c_1+c_2+c_6)f_2
  +(2c_1+c_3+c_6)f_3\\
&\quad +(c_1+c_4+c_6+c_7)f_4+(5c_1+c_2+c_3+c_5+3c_6+c_7)f_5.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $f_1,\ldots,f_5$ generate distinct cyclic direct
summands whose orders are all divisible by $m$,
comparison of coordinates and reduction modulo $m$ give
$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1&\equiv0\pmod m,\\
c_1+c_2+c_6&\equiv0\pmod m,\\
2c_1+c_3+c_6&\equiv0\pmod m,\\
c_1+c_4+c_6+c_7&\equiv0\pmod m,\\
5c_1+c_2+c_3+c_5+3c_6+c_7&\equiv0\pmod m.
\end{aligned}
$$
Substituting $c_1\equiv0\pmod m$ into the next three
congruences yields
$c_2+c_6\equiv c_3+c_6
\equiv c_4+c_6+c_7\equiv0\pmod m.$
Finally, writing the last congruence as
$5c_1+(c_2+c_6)+(c_3+c_6)+(c_5+c_6+c_7)
\equiv0\pmod m$
gives $c_5+c_6+c_7\equiv0\pmod m$. Therefore,
$$
c_1\equiv c_2+c_6\equiv c_3+c_6
\equiv c_4+c_6+c_7\equiv c_5+c_6+c_7
\equiv0\pmod m.
$$
Put
$$a_i=\left\lfloor\frac{c_i}{m}\right\rfloor\ (1\le i\le5),
\qquad a_6=\left\lceil\frac{c_6}{m}\right\rceil,
\qquad a_7=\left\lceil\frac{c_6+c_7}{m}\right\rceil-a_6.$$
The identity $\lfloor x\rfloor+\lceil y\rceil=x+y$ when
$x+y$ is an integer gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rank-five-multiplicities}
\begin{aligned}
c_1&=ma_1,\\
c_i+c_6&=m(a_i+a_6) &&(i=2,3),\\
c_i+c_6+c_7&=m(a_i+a_6+a_7) &&(i=4,5).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Using $g_6=g_2+g_3+g_4+g_5$ and $g_7=g_4+g_5$,
we first collect the coefficients of $g_1,\ldots,g_5$.
Then \eqref{eq:rank-five-multiplicities} gives
$$\begin{aligned}
0=\sum_{i=1}^{7}c_i g_i
&=c_1g_1+(c_2+c_6)g_2+(c_3+c_6)g_3+(c_4+c_6+c_7)g_4+(c_5+c_6+c_7)g_5\\
&=m\bigl[a_1g_1+(a_2+a_6)g_2+(a_3+a_6)g_3+(a_4+a_6+a_7)g_4+(a_5+a_6+a_7)g_5\bigr]\\
&=m\left[\sum_{i=1}^{5}a_i g_i
+a_6(g_2+g_3+g_4+g_5)+a_7(g_4+g_5)\right]\\
&=m\sum_{i=1}^{7}a_i g_i.
\end{aligned}$$
The multiplicity bounds in $S_m$ give
\begin{equation}\label{equation:aitakesvalues}
a_1,a_2,a_3,a_6,a_7\in\{0,1,2\},\qquad
  a_4,a_5\in\{0,1\}.
\end{equation}
Expanding the definitions of $g_1,\ldots,g_7$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
m\sum_{i=1}^7a_i g_i
=m\bigl[&
a_1f_1+(a_1+a_2+a_6)f_2
+(2a_1+a_3+a_6)f_3\\
&+(a_1+a_4+a_6+a_7)f_4+(5a_1+a_2+a_3+a_5+3a_6+a_7)f_5\bigr].
\end{aligned}
$$
Since the orders of $f_2,f_3,f_4$
are $3m$ and the order of $f_5$ is $6m$, it follows that
$$
\begin{aligned}
3m&\mid m(a_1+a_2+a_6),\\
3m&\mid m(2a_1+a_3+a_6),\\
3m&\mid m(a_1+a_4+a_6+a_7),\\
6m&\mid m(5a_1+a_2+a_3+a_5+3a_6+a_7),
\end{aligned}
$$
and so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rank-five-reduced-equations}
 \begin{aligned}
 a_1+a_2+a_6&=0,& -a_1+a_3+a_6&=0,\\
 a_1+a_4+a_6+a_7&=0,& -a_1+a_5+a_6+a_7&=0
 \end{aligned}
 \quad\text{in }\mathbb F_3.
\end{equation}
Here the fourth equation in \eqref{eq:rank-five-reduced-equations} follows as
$$
\begin{aligned}
0
&=(5a_1+a_2+a_3+a_5+3a_6+a_7)-(a_1+a_2+a_6)-(2a_1+a_3+a_6)\\
&=2a_1+a_5+a_6+a_7\\
&=-a_1+a_5+a_6+a_7
\qquad\text{in }\mathbb F_3.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last divisibility relation above also gives
$5a_1+a_2+a_3+a_5+3a_6+a_7\equiv0\pmod6,$
so
$$Q=a_1+a_2+a_3+a_5+a_6+a_7$$
is even. Subtracting the last two equations in
\eqref{eq:rank-five-reduced-equations} gives
$a_4-a_5=a_1$ in $\mathbb F_3$.
If $a_1=1$, by \eqref{equation:aitakesvalues} and the same equations, we have
$a_4=1$, $a_5=0$, $a_2+a_6=2$, $a_3=a_7,$
so $Q=3+2a_7$ is odd, a contradiction.
If $a_1=2$, they give
$a_4=0,$ $a_5=1$, $a_3+a_6=2$, $a_2=a_7,$
so $Q=5+2a_7$ is again odd. Then
\eqref{equation:aitakesvalues} gives
$a_1=0$. Combined with \eqref{eq:rank-five-reduced-equations}, we have $a_2=a_3$, $a_4=a_5$, and
$$a_4+a_6+a_7\in\{0,3\},\qquad
  Q=2a_2+(a_4+a_6+a_7).$$
Since $Q$ is even, $a_4+a_6+a_7=0$, and the equations force
all $a_i=0$. By \eqref{eq:rank-five-multiplicities} and
nonnegativity, all $c_i=0$. Thus $V$ is empty, proving that
$S_m$ is zero-sum free.

For $r\ge5$, write
$$F_{m,r}=F_{m,5}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r-5}\langle h_i\rangle,
\qquad \operatorname{ord}(h_i)=m.$$
The sequence $S=S_m\prod_{i=1}^{r-5}h_i^{m-1}$ is zero-sum
free  and
$|S|=17m-5+(r-5)(m-1)=(r+12)m-r.$
Since $\mathsf d^*(F_{m,r})=(r+11)m-r$, the desired inequality follows.

<!-- theorem-4: body_md -->
\label{thm:cross}
For every fixed integer $r\ge6$ and every real number $M>0$,
there exist a finite abelian group $G$ of rank $r$ and a
zero-sum-free sequence $S$ over $G$ such that
$$|S|=\mathsf d^*(G),\qquad \mathsf k(S)>M.$$
In particular, Conjecture~\ref{conj:girard} is false.
<!-- end theorem-4 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Fix an integer $d>2M$, and take $d$ distinct primes $p_1,\ldots,p_d$.
Put
$$m=\prod_{i=1}^d p_i,\qquad
  \ell=\sum_{i=1}^d(p_i-1),\qquad
  \kappa=\sum_{i=1}^d\left(1-\frac1{p_i}\right).$$
The inequality $(a-1)+(b-1)\le ab-1$ for positive integers
$a,b$ gives $\ell\le m-1$ by induction. Clearly, $\ell\ge1$.
Let $\langle f\rangle$ be a cyclic group of order $m$, and put
$$R=\prod_{i=1}^d\left(\frac{m}{p_i}f\right)^{p_i-1}$$
Since $\operatorname{ord}((m/p_i)f)=p_i$ and the primes $p_i$ are distinct,
the subgroups $\langle(m/p_i)f\rangle$ are independent.
Consequently, $R$ is zero-sum free and
$$|R|=\ell,\qquad \mathsf k(R)=\kappa\ge d/2>M.$$

By Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-five}, $H=F_{m,r-1}$ has a zero-sum-free
sequence $T$ of length $\mathsf d^*(H)+m$.
Since $1\le\ell\le m-1$, choose $V\mid T$ with
$$|V|=\mathsf d^*(H)+m-1-\ell.$$
Now consider $G=H\oplus\langle f\rangle=F_{m,r}$ and put
$S=V\cdot R$. Since $V$ and $R$ are zero-sum free and lie in
independent direct summands, $S$ is zero-sum free. Moreover,
$$|S|=|V|+|R|=\mathsf d^*(H)+m-1=\mathsf d^*(G),\qquad
  \mathsf k(S)\ge\mathsf k(R)=\kappa>M.$$
This completes the proof.

<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
\label{rem:cross-threshold}
For every fixed $r\ge6$ and every $L,M\ge0$, there also exist
$G$ and $S$ with
$$|S|-\mathsf d^*(G)>L,\qquad \mathsf k(S)>M.$$
Retain $T$ in full in the preceding proof and choose
enough distinct primes that $\ell+1>L$ and $\kappa>M$.
Then $T\cdot R$ has excess length $\ell+1$ and cross number at least
$\kappa$. Consequently, no finite-valued functions
$f,h:\mathbb N\to[0,\infty)$ can ensure
$\mathsf k(S)\le h(\mathsf r(G))$ for every zero-sum-free $S$ satisfying
$|S|\ge\mathsf d^*(G)+f(\mathsf r(G))$.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The significance of Theorem~\ref{thm:cross} lies in
obtaining unbounded cross numbers at the prescribed
length $\mathsf d^*(G)$ while keeping the rank fixed.
Without a restriction on the sequence length,
unbounded cross numbers already occur over cyclic
groups, as the preceding cyclic construction shows.
The sequences constructed here are not asserted to have
maximum possible length $\mathsf d(G)$.
Also, Conjecture~\ref{conj:girard} uses invariant factors.
The classical cross-number conjectures
$\mathsf k(G)=\mathsf k^*(G)$ and $\mathsf K(G)=\mathsf K^*(G)$
concern the maximal cross numbers of zero-sum-free and
minimal zero-sum sequences, respectively. Their proposed
values use the prime-power elementary divisors and may
themselves grow without bound at fixed rank
\cite[Section~3.8 and Appendix~B, Problem~5]{GGZ26}.
Thus the unboundedness established here does not
by itself contradict these conjectures.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:direct-amplification}
Let $H=C_{n_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_s}$, where
$1<n_1\mid\cdots\mid n_s$, and let $T$ be a zero-sum-free
sequence over $H$ of length $L$. For integers $q\ge1$ and $k\ge0$, put
$$B_{q,k}=H^{\oplus q}\oplus C_{n_1}^k.$$
Then $B_{q,k}$ has a zero-sum-free sequence of length
$qL+k(n_1-1)$. Its rank is $qs+k$, its exponent is $n_s$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:direct-amplification}
 \delta(B_{q,k})\ge q\bigl(L-\mathsf d^*(H)\bigr).
\end{equation}
If $\operatorname{supp}(T)$ generates $H$, the constructed sequence also
generates $B_{q,k}$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
Let $T_j$ be the copy of $T$ in the $j$th summand $H$, and let
$f_1,\ldots,f_k$ generate the additional cyclic summands. Put
$$S=\prod_{j=1}^q T_j\prod_{i=1}^k f_i^{n_1-1}.$$
Now we show that $S$ is zero-sum free. Suppose, to the contrary,
that $V\mid S$ is a nonempty zero-sum subsequence. Projecting
onto each copy of $H$, we obtain a zero-sum subsequence of $T_j$,
which must be empty. Projecting onto each $\langle f_i\rangle$
then shows that the multiplicity of $f_i$ in $V$ is zero, since
it lies between zero and $n_1-1$. Thus $V$ is empty, a contradiction.

The displayed decomposition of $H$ gives
$$\mathsf d^*(B_{q,k})=q\mathsf d^*(H)+k(n_1-1).$$
Subtracting this from $|S|=qL+k(n_1-1)$ proves
\eqref{eq:direct-amplification}. The assertions about rank,
exponent, and generation follow from the same decomposition.

<!-- proposition-2: body_md -->
\label{prop:eighteenth}
For every integer $r\ge6$, put $q=\lfloor r/6\rfloor$ and
$$K_r=C_3^{r-q}\oplus C_6^q.$$
Then $\mathsf r(K_r)=r$, $\exp(K_r)=6$, and $\delta(K_r)\ge2q$.
In particular, there are infinitely many groups $G$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eighteenth}
 \delta(G)\ge\frac{\mathsf r(G)\exp(G)}{18}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end proposition-2 -->
<!-- proof-11: body_md -->
Let $H=C_3^5\oplus C_6$, with independent generators
$e_1,\ldots,e_5,z$ of orders $3,\ldots,3,6$, and put
$$T=\prod_{i=1}^5(e_i+z)^2(e_i+3z)
       (e_1+\cdots+e_5+5z)^2.$$
Now we show that $T$ is zero-sum free. Suppose, to the contrary,
that $V\mid T$ is a nonempty zero-sum subsequence. Let
$u\in\{0,1,2\}$ be the multiplicity of
$e_1+\cdots+e_5+5z$ in $V$, and let $c_i$ be the number of
terms selected from $(e_i+z)^2(e_i+3z)$. Considering the
$e_i$-coordinate, we obtain
$$c_i+u\equiv0\pmod3,\qquad 0\le c_i\le3.$$
If $u\in\{1,2\}$, then $c_i=3-u$ for every $i$, and hence
$$|V|=5(3-u)+u=15-4u$$
is odd. Every term of $T$ has odd $z$-coordinate, so the
$z$-coordinate of $\sigma(V)$ cannot be zero modulo six,
a contradiction. Thus $u=0$. Each $c_i$ is then zero or three,
so $V$ is a product of complete blocks. Each complete block
has sum $5z$. Since there are at most five blocks and
$\operatorname{ord}(5z)=6$, their sum is zero only when no block is selected.
This again contradicts the nonemptiness of $V$.

Therefore $T$ is zero-sum free. Since $|T|=17$ and
$\mathsf d^*(H)=5\cdot2+5=15$, its excess over $\mathsf d^*(H)$ is two.
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:direct-amplification} with $k=r-6q$.
The resulting group is
$$H^{\oplus q}\oplus C_3^{r-6q}
   =C_3^{r-q}\oplus C_6^q=K_r,$$
and $\delta(K_r)\ge2q$. Finally, taking $r=6t$ gives
$$\delta(K_{6t})\ge2t
 =\frac{(6t)\cdot6}{18}
 =\frac{\mathsf r(K_{6t})\exp(K_{6t})}{18}$$
for every $t\ge1$, proving \eqref{eq:eighteenth}.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{ub:lem:lcm}
Let $\psi(x)=\sum_{\ell^a\le x}\log\ell$, where $\ell$ ranges
over primes and $a\ge1$. For integers $n\ge1$,
$\psi(n)=\log\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n)$. For all real $x>0$,
$$\psi(x)<\min\{1.03883x,\ 1.01624x+1.42620\sqrt{x}\}.$$
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-12: body_md -->
The identity follows by prime factorization of the least common
multiple. Writing $\vartheta(x)=\sum_{\ell\le x}\log\ell$,
Rosser and Schoenfeld \cite[Theorems~9, 12, and~13]{RS62} proved
$\vartheta(x)<1.01624x$, $\psi(x)<1.03883x$, and
$\psi(x)-\vartheta(x)<1.42620\sqrt{x}$ for all $x>0$.
Adding the first and third estimates gives the second bound.

The estimate in Lemma~\ref{ub:lem:lcm} already

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{ub:lem:nilpotence}
Let
$$H=\bigoplus_{j=1}^r C_{p^{a_j}},
\qquad a_j\in\mathbb N_0,\qquad
b=\sum_{j=1}^r(p^{a_j}-1),$$
and let $K$ have characteristic $p$. If $I$ is the
augmentation ideal of $K[H]$, then $I^{b+1}=0$.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-14: body_md -->
Choose generators $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ such that
$$H=\langle e_1\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle e_r\rangle,
\qquad \operatorname{ord}(e_j)=p^{a_j}.$$
Put
\begin{equation}\label{equation:yj=Xej-1}
y_j=X^{e_j}-1\in K[H],
\end{equation} where $1=X^0$ is the
multiplicative identity of the group algebra.
Since $\operatorname{char}(K)=p$, we have
$$y_j^{p^{a_j}}
=(X^{e_j}-1)^{p^{a_j}}
=X^{p^{a_j}e_j}-1=0.$$
Consider the $K$-algebra homomorphism
$$\varphi:K[Y_1,\ldots,Y_r]\longrightarrow K[H],
\qquad
Y_j\longmapsto y_j\quad(1\le j\le r).$$
Since every $h\in H$ can be written as
$h=\sum_{j=1}^r b_je_j$, where $0\le b_j<p^{a_j}$, it follows from \eqref{equation:yj=Xej-1} that
$\varphi\left(\prod_{j=1}^r(1+Y_j)^{b_j}\right)
=\prod_{j=1}^r(X^{e_j})^{b_j}
=X^h.$
Thus the image of $\varphi$ contains the $K$-basis
$\{X^h:h\in H\}$ of $K[H]$, so $\varphi$ is surjective.

Since $\varphi(Y_j^{p^{a_j}})=y_j^{p^{a_j}}=0$ for every $j$,
the ideal $(Y_1^{p^{a_1}},\ldots,Y_r^{p^{a_r}})$ is contained
in $\ker\varphi$. Hence $\varphi$ induces a $K$-algebra epimorphism
$$\overline{\varphi}:
K[Y_1,\ldots,Y_r]/
(Y_1^{p^{a_1}},\ldots,Y_r^{p^{a_r}})
\longrightarrow K[H].$$
Let $\overline Y_j$ denote the residue class of $Y_j$
in this quotient. The monomials
$$\overline Y_1^{\,b_1}\cdots\overline Y_r^{\,b_r},
\qquad
0\le b_j<p^{a_j}\quad(1\le j\le r),$$
form a $K$-basis of the quotient. Thus both algebras have
dimension $\prod_{j=1}^r p^{a_j}=|H|$ over $K$.
Since $\overline{\varphi}$ is surjective, it is also
injective, and hence is a $K$-algebra isomorphism.

Let $\varepsilon:K[H]\longrightarrow K$ be the augmentation
map, and let $J$ be the ideal of
$$K[Y_1,\ldots,Y_r]/
(Y_1^{p^{a_1}},\ldots,Y_r^{p^{a_r}})$$
generated by $\overline Y_1,\ldots,\overline Y_r$.
For every $j$, we have
$$\varepsilon\bigl(\overline{\varphi}(\overline Y_j)\bigr)
=\varepsilon(y_j)
=\varepsilon(X^{e_j}-1)=1-1=0.$$
Since both maps are $K$-algebra homomorphisms, every
polynomial $f\in K[Y_1,\ldots,Y_r]$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{equuation:rightside}
(\varepsilon\circ\overline{\varphi})
\bigl(f(\overline Y_1,\ldots,\overline Y_r)\bigr)
=f\bigl(\varepsilon(y_1),\ldots,\varepsilon(y_r)\bigr)
=f(0,\ldots,0).
\end{equation}
If $f,g\in K[Y_1,\ldots,Y_r]$ represent the same element
of the quotient, then
$f-g=\sum_{j=1}^r Y_j^{p^{a_j}}q_j$
for suitable polynomials $q_j\in K[Y_1,\ldots,Y_r]$.
Since each $p^{a_j}\ge1$, evaluating at
$Y_1=\cdots=Y_r=0$ gives
$f(0,\ldots,0)=g(0,\ldots,0)$.
Thus the value $f(0,\ldots,0)$ on the right-hand side of
\eqref{equuation:rightside}, which is the constant term of $f$,
is independent of the chosen representative.
Consequently, $\varepsilon\circ\overline{\varphi}$ sends
each element of the quotient to this well-defined constant term.

Since $\varepsilon\circ\overline{\varphi}$ sends every
generator $\overline Y_j$ of $J$ to zero, we have
$$J\subseteq\ker(\varepsilon\circ\overline{\varphi}).$$
Conversely, if $f(\overline Y_1,\ldots,\overline Y_r)$
belongs to this kernel, then $f(0,\ldots,0)=0$.
Every monomial occurring in $f$ is therefore divisible
by at least one variable, so
$f=\sum_{j=1}^r Y_jf_j$
for suitable polynomials $f_j\in K[Y_1,\ldots,Y_r]$.
Passing to residue classes shows that
$f(\overline Y_1,\ldots,\overline Y_r)\in J$.
Therefore,
$\ker(\varepsilon\circ\overline{\varphi})=J.$
Since $\overline{\varphi}$ is an isomorphism, it follows that
$$\overline{\varphi}(J)=\ker\varepsilon=I.$$


Recall that $b=\sum_{j=1}^r(p^{a_j}-1)$.
Since $J$ is generated by $\overline Y_1,\ldots,\overline Y_r$,
expanding products of $b+1$ elements of $J$ shows that
$J^{b+1}$ is generated by all products of $b+1$ of these
generators, with repetitions allowed.
By commutativity, these products have the form
$$\overline Y_1^{\,c_1}\cdots\overline Y_r^{\,c_r},
\qquad
c_1,\ldots,c_r\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},
\quad
\sum_{j=1}^r c_j=b+1.$$
For each such monomial, at least one index $j$ satisfies
$c_j\ge p^{a_j}$, since otherwise
$\sum_{j=1}^r c_j
\le\sum_{j=1}^r(p^{a_j}-1)=b.$
Since $\overline Y_j^{\,p^{a_j}}=0$ in the quotient,
every such monomial vanishes. Hence $J^{b+1}=0$.
As $\overline{\varphi}(J)=I$ and $\overline{\varphi}$
is an algebra isomorphism, we obtain
$
I^{b+1}
=\overline{\varphi}(J)^{b+1}
=\overline{\varphi}(J^{b+1})
=0.$

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{ub:lem:characters}
Let $H$ be a finite abelian $p$-group, let $A$ be a finite
abelian group with $p\nmid|A|$, and let $K=\overline{\mathbb F_p}$.
Writing $\widehat A=\operatorname{Hom}(A,K^\times)$, the map
$$\Phi:K[H\oplus A]\longrightarrow
\prod_{\chi\in\widehat A}K[H],
\qquad
X^{(h,a)}\longmapsto\bigl(\chi(a)X^h\bigr)_\chi$$
is an injective $K$-algebra homomorphism.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
Since $A$ is finite abelian and $K$ is algebraically closed
with $\operatorname{char}(K)\nmid |A|$, standard character
theory gives $|\widehat A|=|A|$ and the orthogonality relation
\begin{equation}\label{equation:takesallchi}
\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}\chi(a)=
\begin{cases}
|A|,&a=0,\\
0,&a\ne0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Each component of $\Phi$ is a $K$-algebra homomorphism,
since $\chi(a+a')=\chi(a)\chi(a')$ for every
$\chi\in\widehat A$ and all $a,a'\in A$.
Thus $\Phi$ is a $K$-algebra homomorphism.

To prove injectivity, let
$$F=\sum_{h\in H,\ a\in A}c_{h,a}X^{(h,a)}
\in\ker\Phi.$$
For every $\chi\in\widehat A$, the corresponding component
of $\Phi(F)$ is zero, so
$$\sum_{h\in H}
\left(\sum_{a\in A}c_{h,a}\chi(a)\right)X^h=0.$$
Since $\{X^h:h\in H\}$ is a $K$-basis of $K[H]$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{equation:takesalla=0}
\sum_{a\in A}c_{h,a}\chi(a)=0
\qquad(h\in H,\ \chi\in\widehat A).
\end{equation}
Fix $h\in H$ and $a_0\in A$. Multiplying by $\chi(-a_0)$
and summing over all characters, by \eqref{equation:takesallchi} and \eqref{equation:takesalla=0}, we derive that
$$
0=\sum_{a\in A}c_{h,a}
\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}\chi(a-a_0)
=|A|c_{h,a_0}.$$
The scalar $|A|$ is nonzero in $K$, so $c_{h,a_0}=0$.
Since $h$ and $a_0$ were arbitrary, every coefficient of
$F$ is zero. Hence $\ker\Phi=\{0\}$, proving injectivity.

<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
Write $r=\mathsf r(G)$, and retain
$c,u,h$ from Lemma~\ref{ub:lem:numerical}. For a prime $\ell$, let
$v_\ell(\exp(G))$ denote the exponent of $\ell$ in the prime factorization
of $\exp(G)$. Choose a prime $p\mid \exp(G)$ such that
$$P=p^{v_p(\exp(G))}=\max_{\ell\mid \exp(G)}\ell^{v_\ell(\exp(G))},$$
where the maximum is over prime divisors $\ell$ of $\exp(G)$.
Let $H$ be the Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$ and $A$ its complementary
Hall subgroup, i.e.,
$$G=H\oplus A,\qquad
\exp(H)=P,\qquad
\exp(A)=Q=\exp(G)/P,\qquad
p\nmid|A|.$$
We may assume that $$Q\geq 2$$ since otherwise, $G$ is a $p$-group and $\mathsf D(G)=\mathsf D^*(G)\leq \mathsf r(G)\exp(G)$ and $\delta(G)=0$ is well-known.
Every exact prime-power factor $\ell^{v_\ell(\exp(G))}$ of $\exp(G)$ is at most
$P$, and these factors are pairwise coprime. Therefore
$$\exp(G)\mid\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,P).$$

By Lemma~\ref{ub:lem:lcm},
$\log Q=\log \exp(G)-\log P\le\log\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,P)-\log P=\psi(P)-\log P$.
Since $A\cong G\diagup H$ is a quotient group of $G$, it can be generated by at most $r$
elements. Its exponent is $Q$, so $A$ is a quotient of $C_Q^r$.
Consequently, $|A|\le Q^r$, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{ub:eq:hall-size}
\log|A|\le r\log Q\le r(\psi(P)-\log P).
\end{equation}




Write
$$H=\bigoplus_{j=1}^r C_{p^{a_j}},
\qquad 0\le a_j\le v_p(\exp(G)),$$
where trivial cyclic factors are included if necessary.
Since $\exp(H)=P$, we have
$$\max_{1\le j\le r}p^{a_j}=P.$$
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{ub:eq:b-bound}
b=\sum_{j=1}^r(p^{a_j}-1)
\le r(P-1).
\end{equation}
Let $K=\overline{\mathbb F_p}$, and let $I$ be the augmentation
ideal of $K[H]$. Lemma~\ref{ub:lem:nilpotence} gives
\begin{equation}\label{ub:eq:nilpotence}
I^{b+1}=0.
\end{equation}

To prove the conclusion, we suppose, to the contrary, that there exists a zero-sum free sequence
$T=g_1\cdots g_L$ of length $L=\lfloor cr\exp(G)\rfloor$. Write $g_i=(h_i,a_i)\in H\oplus A$ for each $i\in [1,L]$.
Let
$$\Omega=\{\xi\in K^\times:\xi^Q=1\}.$$
Because $p\nmid Q$, the set $\Omega$ has exactly $Q$
elements. Choose independent random variables $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_L$
taking values in $\Omega$, each with the uniform distribution,
that is,
$$\Pr(\xi_i=\omega)=\frac1Q
\qquad
(1\le i\le L,\ \omega\in\Omega).$$
For a character
$\chi\in\widehat A$, define
$$Z_\chi=\#\{i:\xi_i=\chi(a_i)\}.$$


Now we fix $\chi\in\widehat A$. Since $\exp(A)=Q$, we have
$$\chi(a_i)^Q=\chi(Qa_i)=\chi(0)=1
\qquad(1\le i\le L).$$
Thus $\chi(a_i)\in\Omega$ for every $i$.
As $\xi_i$ is uniformly distributed on $\Omega$ and
$|\Omega|=Q$, it follows that
$$\Pr\bigl(\xi_i=\chi(a_i)\bigr)=\frac1Q
\qquad(1\le i\le L).$$
Each event $\{\xi_i=\chi(a_i)\}$ depends only on the
corresponding random variable $\xi_i$.
Since $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_L$ are independent, these
matching events are mutually independent.


For the fixed character $\chi$, let $\mathbb E$ denote
the expected value of the random variable $c^{-Z_\chi}$,
that is,
$$
\mathbb E=\sum_{k=0}^{L}c^{-k}\Pr(Z_\chi=k).
$$
Since $c>1$, we have $c^{-k}\ge c^{-b}$ whenever $k\le b$.
All terms in this sum are nonnegative, so
\begin{equation}\label{equation:forE}
\mathbb E
\ge\sum_{\substack{0\le k\le L\\k\le b}}
c^{-k}\Pr(Z_\chi=k)\ge
c^{-b}\sum_{\substack{0\le k\le L\\k\le b}}
\Pr(Z_\chi=k)=c^{-b}\Pr(Z_\chi\le b).
\end{equation}


We next compute $\mathbb E$.
Since the matching events are independent and each has
probability $1/Q$, the probability of exactly $k$ matches is
$$
\Pr(Z_\chi=k)
=\binom{L}{k}\left(\frac1Q\right)^k
 \left(1-\frac1Q\right)^{L-k}, \qquad \text{where } 0\le k\le L.
$$
By the definition of expectation and the independence
of the matching events, we have
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E
&=\sum_{k=0}^{L}c^{-k}\Pr(Z_\chi=k)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{L}c^{-k}\binom{L}{k}
  \left(\frac1Q\right)^k
  \left(1-\frac1Q\right)^{L-k}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{L}\binom{L}{k}
  \left(\frac1{cQ}\right)^k
  \left(1-\frac1Q\right)^{L-k}\\
&=\left(1-\frac1Q+\frac1{cQ}\right)^L\\
&=\left(1-\frac{u}{Q}\right)^L,
\end{aligned}
$$
where the last equality uses $u=1-1/c$.
Multiplying the lower bound for $\mathbb E$ given in \eqref{equation:forE}
by $c^b$ and using $1-x\le\exp(-x)$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{equation:tail}
\Pr(Z_\chi\le b)
\le c^b\mathbb E
=c^b\left(1-\frac{u}{Q}\right)^L
\le\exp\left(b\log c-\frac{uL}{Q}\right).
\end{equation}
The estimate  holds for every
$\chi\in\widehat A$. Since $|\widehat A|=|A|$, $L\ge crPQ-1$ and $uc=c-1$, it follows from \eqref{ub:eq:hall-size},
\eqref{ub:eq:b-bound} and \eqref{equation:tail}  that
\begin{align}
\Pr\bigl(\exists\chi\in\widehat A:Z_\chi\le b\bigr)
&\le\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}\Pr(Z_\chi\le b) \notag\\
&\le\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}
\exp\left(b\log c-\frac{uL}{Q}\right)\notag\\
&=|A|\exp\left(b\log c-\frac{uL}{Q}\right) \notag\\
&\le\exp\left(
r\bigl(\psi(P)-\log P+(P-1)\log c-(c-1)P\bigr)
+\frac{u}{Q}\right) \notag\\
&=\exp\left(
r\bigl(\psi(P)-hP-\log(cP)\bigr)
+\frac{u}{Q}\right)<1.
\label{ub:eq:unionnew}
\end{align}
The last equality uses $h=c-1-\log c$, and the term $u/Q$ comes from the inequality $L\ge crPQ-1$.
Since $P\ge2$, Lemma~\ref{ub:lem:numerical} gives
$$
\psi(P)-hP-\log(cP)<-u.
$$
Consequently,
$$
r\bigl(\psi(P)-hP-\log(cP)\bigr)+\frac{u}{Q}
<-ru+\frac{u}{Q}<0,
$$
where the last inequality follows from
$r\ge1$, $Q\ge2$, and $u>0$.
Since $Z_\chi$ and $b$ are integers, it follows from \eqref{ub:eq:unionnew} that
$$\Pr\bigl(Z_\chi\ge b+1
          \text{ for every }\chi\in\widehat A\bigr)=
1-\Pr\bigl(\exists\chi\in\widehat A:Z_\chi\le b\bigr)>0.$$
Consequently, there exists
a choice of $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_L$ such that
$$
Z_\chi\ge b+1
\qquad\text{for every }\chi\in\widehat A.
$$
Fix such a choice for the remainder of the proof.
Consider
$$F=\prod_{i=1}^L(X^{g_i}-\xi_i)\in K[G].$$
Under the homomorphism $\Phi$ from
Lemma~\ref{ub:lem:characters}, the $\chi$-component of
$\Phi(F)$ is
$$\prod_{i=1}^L\bigl(\chi(a_i)X^{h_i}-\xi_i\bigr).$$
Every matching index satisfies $\xi_i=\chi(a_i)$ and
therefore contributes a factor
$$
\chi(a_i)X^{h_i}-\xi_i
=\chi(a_i)(X^{h_i}-1)\in I.$$
There are at least $b+1$ such factors. Since $K[H]$
is commutative, their product belongs to $I^{b+1}=0$
by \eqref{ub:eq:nilpotence}. Hence $\prod_{i=1}^L\bigl(\chi(a_i)X^{h_i}-\xi_i\bigr)=0$ for every $\chi\in\widehat A$,
so $\Phi(F)=0$. The injectivity of $\Phi$ gives $F=0$.

On the other hand, expanding the product gives
$$F=\sum_{J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,L\}}
(-1)^{L-|J|}
\left(\prod_{i\notin J}\xi_i\right)
X^{\sum_{j\in J}g_j}.$$
Since $T$ is zero-sum free, only $J=\varnothing$
contributes to the coefficient of $X^0$ in $F$.
This coefficient is
$$(-1)^L\prod_{i=1}^L\xi_i,$$
which is nonzero because every $\xi_i$ is nonzero.
Thus $F\ne0$, contradicting $F=0$.
Therefore every sequence of length $\lfloor cr\exp(G)\rfloor$
has a nonempty zero-sum subsequence, and hence
$\mathsf D(G)\le\lfloor cr\exp(G)\rfloor
\le\frac{16}{5}r\exp(G),$ completing the proof of the theorem.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{ub:rem:sharper-bound}
The constant in Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-exponent} can be sharpened to
give, for every nontrivial finite abelian group $G$ of rank
$r=\mathsf r(G)$,
$$\mathsf D(G)\le\lfloor c_0r\exp(G)\rfloor-r\le c_0r\exp(G)-r,
\qquad c_0=3.158464724.$$
We retain the simpler constant $16/5$ in the theorem and its
applications, since this refinement does not change the scale $r\exp(G)$.

To justify the refinement, use the decomposition $G=H\oplus A$
and the quantities $P,Q,b$ from the proof of the theorem, so that
$\exp(G)=PQ$, $\log|A|\le r(\psi(P)-\log P)$, and $b\le r(P-1)$.
If $Q=1$, then $G$ is a $p$-group, and
$\mathsf D(G)\le r(\exp(G)-1)+1\le\lfloor c_0r\exp(G)\rfloor-r.$
Suppose $Q\ge2$. Necessarily $P\ge3$, because all exact
prime-power factors of $\exp(G)$ are at most $P$.
Put $L=\lfloor c_0r\exp(G)\rfloor-r$, $t=c_0P/(P-1)$, and $u=1-1/t$.
For a sequence of length $L$, choose the scalars and define
$Z_\chi$ as in the proof of the theorem.
Replacing the moment $c^{-Z_\chi}$ there by $t^{-Z_\chi}$ gives
$$\Pr\bigl(\exists\chi\in\widehat A:Z_\chi\le b\bigr)
\le |A|t^b(1-u/Q)^L.$$
Since $L\ge c_0rPQ-r-1$ and
$\log(1-z)\le-z-z^2/2$ for $0<z<1$, we obtain
$$\log\bigl(|A|t^b(1-u/Q)^L\bigr)
\le rF(P;c_0,t)\quad+\frac{u}{Q}\left[(r+1)\left(1+\frac{u}{2Q}\right)
-\frac{rc_0uP}{2}\right]<0.$$
The first term is negative by
Lemma~\ref{ub:lem:refined-numerical}.
Also $t>c_0>3$ implies $c_0uP>(c_0-1)P>6$, while
$1+u/(2Q)<5/4$. The bracket is therefore less than
$5(r+1)/4-3r<0$ for every $r\ge1$.
There is consequently a choice of the scalars with $Z_\chi\ge b+1$
for every character.
By the same probabilistic group-algebra argument as in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-exponent}, every sequence of length $L$ contains
a nonempty zero-sum subsequence. Hence $\mathsf D(G)\le L$, giving the
stated bound.

If $G$ is cyclic, then every subgroup $H$ is cyclic and
$\mathsf D(H)=|H|\mid |G|=\mathsf D(G)$; this also includes the trivial group.
Suppose conversely that a noncyclic group
$$
 G=C_{n_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_r},\qquad
 1<n_1\mid\cdots\mid n_r,\quad r\ge2,
$$
satisfies \eqref{eq:subgroup-property}. If $r=2$, then
$\exp(G)<\mathsf D(G)=n_1+\exp(G)-1<2\exp(G)$, contradicting $\exp(G)\mid\mathsf D(G)$.
Thus $r\ge3$.

Fix a prime $p\mid n_1$ and put $$K=16r/5\in \mathbb{R}.$$
The subgroup $C_{\exp(G)}$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-exponent} give
$$\mathsf D(G)=k\exp(G),\qquad 1\le k\le K.$$


Since $C_p\oplus C_{\exp(G)}$ is a subgroup of $G$ and $\mathsf D(C_p\oplus C_{\exp(G)})=\exp(G)+p-1$, it follows from
property \eqref{eq:subgroup-property} that
$\exp(G)+p-1\mid \mathsf D(G)=k\exp(G)$. Since
$k(p-1)=k(\exp(G)+p-1)-k\exp(G),$
and both terms on the right are divisible by $\exp(G)+p-1$, we obtain
$\exp(G)+p-1\mid k(p-1)$. As $k(p-1)>0$, it follows that
$\exp(G)+p-1\le k(p-1),$
and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:divisibilityexponent}
 \exp(G)\le(k-1)(p-1).
\end{equation}
Since $C_p^j$ is a subgroup of $G$, it follows that
$1+j(p-1)=\mathsf D(C_p^j)\mid k\exp(G)$, where $1\le j\le r$.  Define
\begin{equation}\label{equation:Lrp}
L_{r,p}=\operatorname{lcm}\{1+j(p-1):0\le j\le r\}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{equation:LrpmidkE}
L_{r,p}\mid k\exp(G).
\end{equation}
Applying Lemma \ref{lem:hf-lcm} with $a=1$, $d=p-1$, and $s=r$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:divisibility-lcm}
 p^r\le L_{r,p}\le k\exp(G)\le K(K-1)(p-1).
\end{equation}
Since $r\ge3$, it follows that
$\frac{p^r}{p-1}
=p^{r-2}\frac{p^2}{p-1}
\ge2^{r-2}\cdot4
=2^r.$
Combining this with \eqref{eq:divisibility-lcm}, we obtain
$$2^r\le\frac{p^r}{p-1}\le K(K-1).$$

We now show that
\begin{equation}\label{equation:rin3to9}
3\le r\le9.
\end{equation}
For every integer $s\ge10$, we have
$\frac{2^{s+1}/(s+1)^2}{2^s/s^2}
=\frac{2s^2}{(s+1)^2}>1.$
Thus $2^s/s^2$ is increasing for $s\ge10$. Consequently, if $r\ge10$,
then
$2^r\ge\frac{2^{10}}{10^2}r^2
=\left(\frac{16r}{5}\right)^2=K^2>K(K-1),$
contradicting $2^r\le K(K-1)$. This proves \eqref{equation:rin3to9}.


Since $k$ is a positive integer and
$k\le K=16r/5\le144/5<29$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{equation:kle28}
k\le28.
\end{equation}
Moreover, \eqref{eq:divisibilityexponent} and \eqref{eq:divisibility-lcm} give the sharper bound
\begin{equation}\label{equation:prleq}
p^r\le L_{r,p}\le k\exp(G)\le k(k-1)(p-1).
\end{equation}

We next show that
\begin{equation}\label{equation:p=2}
p=2.
\end{equation}
Suppose that $p\ge5$. If $r\in\{3,4\}$, then $k\le12.$
The integers $p$, $2p-1$, and $3p-2$ are pairwise coprime,
and hence
$$L_{r,p}\ge p(2p-1)(3p-2)
>p(6p-1)(p-1)
\ge145(p-1)>132(p-1)
\ge k(k-1)(p-1),$$
a contradiction with \eqref{equation:prleq}.
Hence $5\le r\le9$. Since $x^5/(x-1)$ is increasing
for $x\ge2$, we obtain
$\frac{p^r}{p-1}\ge\frac{p^5}{p-1}
\ge\frac{5^5}{4}>756\ge k(k-1),$
again a contradiction  with \eqref{equation:prleq}. Thus $$p\in\{2,3\}.$$
Suppose, furthermore, that $$p=3.$$ If $5\le r\le9$, by \eqref{equation:Lrp} and \eqref{equation:kle28}, we have
$L_{r,3}\ge\operatorname{lcm}(1,3,5,7,9,11)
=3465>1512\ge2k(k-1),$
a contradiction with \eqref{equation:prleq}.
If $r=4$, then $k\le12$ and
$L_{4,3}=\operatorname{lcm}(1,3,5,7,9)
=315>264\ge2k(k-1),$
a contradiction.
If $r=3$, then $k\le9$, and $3=p\mid \exp(G)$, and
$\exp(G)\le2(k-1)\le16$. By \eqref{equation:LrpmidkE}, we have
$105=L_{3,3}\mid k\exp(G)$, and by \eqref{equation:prleq}, we have $k\exp(G)\le2k(k-1)\le144.$ This implies that $k\exp(G)=105$.
The conditions $3\mid \exp(G)$, $\exp(G)\le16$, and $k\le9$,
then force $(\exp(G),k)=(15,7)$, contradicting
$\exp(G)\le2(k-1)=12$.
Therefore, \eqref{equation:p=2} is proved.

Then we show that
\begin{equation}\label{equation:rin[3,5]}
r\in\{3,4,5\}.
\end{equation}
If $7\le r\le9$, then
$L_{r,2}\ge\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,8)
=840>756\ge k(k-1),$
a contradiction.
If $r=6$, then $k\le96/5<20$, so $k\le19$ and
$L_{6,2}=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,7)
=420>342\ge k(k-1),$
again a contradiction. This proves \eqref{equation:rin[3,5]}.


By \eqref{equation:p=2}, the exponent $\exp(G)$ is even.
Furthermore, the divisibility $\exp(G)+p-1\mid k(p-1)$
and \eqref{eq:divisibilityexponent} become
$$\exp(G)+1\mid k,\qquad \exp(G)\le k-1.$$

Suppose first that $r=3$. Then $k\le9$ and $\exp(G)\le8$, so
$$\exp(G)\in\{2,4,6,8\}.$$
We also have $L_{3,2}=12\mid k\exp(G)$.
If $\exp(G)=4$ or $\exp(G)=6$, the condition $\exp(G)+1\mid k$
forces $k=5$ or $k=7$, respectively.
But neither $20$ nor $42$ is divisible by $12$.
If $\exp(G)=2$, then $3\mid k$ and $12\mid2k$ imply
$6\mid k$, so $k=6$.
If $\exp(G)=8$, then $9\mid k$ forces $k=9$.
Thus the only remaining possibilities are
$$(r,\exp(G),k)=(3,2,6)\quad\text{or}\quad(3,8,9).$$

Now suppose that $r\in\{4,5\}$.
Then $k\le16$, $\exp(G)\le15$, and
$$60=L_{r,2}\mid k\exp(G).$$
Since $\exp(G)$ is even,
$$\exp(G)\in\{2,4,6,8,10,12,14\}.$$
The case $\exp(G)=2$ is impossible because $k\exp(G)\le32<60$.
If $\exp(G)\ge6$, the conditions $\exp(G)+1\mid k$ and $k\le16$
give
$$k\exp(G)\in\{42,72,84,110,156,210\}.$$
None of these integers is divisible by $60$.
Therefore $\exp(G)=4$. Now $60\mid4k$ implies $15\mid k$,
and hence $k=15$.
Since $k\le12$ when $r=4$, this leaves only
$$(r,\exp(G),k)=(5,4,15).$$

Altogether, every remaining possibility belongs to
$$(r,\exp(G),k)\in\{(3,2,6),(3,8,9),(5,4,15)\}.$$
In each case, $\exp(G)$ is a power of two.
Since every invariant factor $n_i$ divides $\exp(G)$,
the group $G$ is a $2$-group.
The classical formula for its Davenport constant
\cite{Olson69I} therefore gives
$$k\exp(G)=\mathsf D(G)=1+\sum_{i=1}^r(n_i-1)
\le1+r(\exp(G)-1)<r\exp(G),$$
so $k<r$.
This contradicts each of the three remaining possibilities.
This completes the proof of the theorem.
