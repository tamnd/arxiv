<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:main}
For every positive integer $r$, there exists $\varepsilon_r>0$ such that,
for every simply connected irreducible compact Riemannian symmetric
space $M$ of rank $r$ and every subgroup $G\subset\operatorname{Isom}(M)$,
\[
 \Delta(M,G)=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\Delta(M,G)\ge\varepsilon_r.
\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- citedtheorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:sphere-gap}
There exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that, for every $n\ge2$ and every subgroup
$G\subset\operatorname{Isom}(S^n)$ of the unit sphere,
\[
 \operatorname{diam}(S^n/G)=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\operatorname{diam}(S^n/G)\ge\varepsilon.
\]
<!-- end citedtheorem-1 -->
<!-- citedtheorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:fixed-space}
Let $M$ be a compact connected homogeneous Riemannian manifold. Then $\pi_1(M)$
is finite if and only if there exists $\varepsilon_M>0$ such that every
subgroup $G\subset\operatorname{Isom}(M)$ satisfies
\[
 \operatorname{diam}(M/G)=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\operatorname{diam}(M/G)>\varepsilon_M.
\]
<!-- end citedtheorem-2 -->
<!-- question-1: body_md -->
\label{question:Q}
Is there a positive lower bound for $\Delta(M,G)$, whenever it is
nonzero, depending only on the rank of the simply connected compact
symmetric space $M$?
<!-- end question-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:cover}
Let $M$ be a compact connected Riemannian manifold whose Riemannian
universal cover is an irreducible compact symmetric space of rank $r$.
For every subgroup $G\subset\operatorname{Isom}(M)$, either $\Delta(M,G)=0$ or
$\Delta(M,G)\ge\varepsilon_r$, with the same constant as in \cref{thm:main}.
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:tail}
Let $N>\max\{28,2r\}$. Assume either $\mathbb F=\mathbb R$ and $r\ge2$, or
$\mathbb F\in\{\mathbb C,\mathbb H\}$ and $r\ge1$. If
$G\subset\operatorname{Isom}(X_{\mathbb F,r,N})$ is compact and nontransitive, with
$\dim G^0\ge1$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail}
 \Delta(X_{\mathbb F,r,N},G)\ge\frac1{\pi r}.
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
In the BDI row we exclude $(p,q)=(1,1)$, which gives a circle,
and $(p,q)=(2,2)$, which gives the reducible space $S^2\times S^2$.
The remaining BDI spaces are the oriented real Grassmannians
$\operatorname{Gr}_p^+(\mathbb R^{p+q})$; in particular, $p=1$ gives the sphere $S^q$. The AIII and CII rows are respectively
$\operatorname{Gr}_p(\mathbb C^{p+q})$ and $\operatorname{Gr}_p(\mathbb H^{p+q})$.
<!-- end remark-1 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
The lower bounds on $\ell$ merely remove repetitions:
$Sp(1)\cong\operatorname{Spin}(3)\cong SU(2)$,
$Sp(2)\cong\operatorname{Spin}(5)$, and
$\operatorname{Spin}(6)\cong SU(4)$.
In particular, the group-type row uses the simply connected groups
$\operatorname{Spin}(n)$, rather than $SO(n)$.
<!-- end remark-2 -->

\label{prop:fixed-rank-reduction}
Fix $r\ge1$ and an integer $B\ge2r$. Outside the three families
in \eqref{eq:families} with $N>B$, there are only finitely many
homothety classes of simply connected irreducible compact symmetric
spaces of rank $r$.
<!-- end proposition-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For types AI, AII, CI, and DIII, the rank determines or bounds the
parameter $n$. The same holds for the group-type spaces. There are
only finitely many exceptional spaces. In types AIII, BDI, and CII,
the smaller parameter is $p=r$, and imposing $N=p+q\le B$ leaves
only finitely many possibilities for $q$. Finally, an irreducible
compact symmetric space has a unique invariant symmetric metric
up to scale. This proves the assertion.

<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:principal-angles}
For two $r$-planes $P,Q\subset\mathbb F^N$, let
$T=\operatorname{pr}_P|_Q:Q\to P$ be orthogonal projection restricted to $Q$.
Its singular values satisfy $1\ge s_1\ge\cdots\ge s_r\ge0$.
The principal angles are
\begin{align*}
\theta_j=\arccos s_j,\qquad
 0\le\theta_1\le\cdots\le\theta_r\le\pi/2.
\end{align*}
For real oriented planes, this definition uses their underlying
unoriented subspaces.

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:distance}
For the unoriented Grassmannian with metric \eqref{eq:metric},
\begin{align*}
d(P,Q)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^r\theta_j^2\right)^{1/2},\qquad
 \cos\theta_r(P,Q)=
 \min_{\substack{x\in Q\\|x|=1}}|\operatorname{pr}_Px|,
\end{align*}
for any two $r$-planes $P,Q\subset\mathbb F^N$.
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Let $x=\sum_{j=1}^rq_j a_j\in Q$ be a unit vector, where $a_j\in \mathbb F$. The orthogonality of the
projected principal vectors gives $\operatorname{pr}_Px=\sum_{j=1}^rp_ja_j\cos\theta_j$, so
\begin{align*}
|\operatorname{pr}_Px|^2=\sum_{j=1}^r|a_j|^2\cos^2\theta_j,
 \qquad \sum_{j=1}^r|a_j|^2=1.
\end{align*}
The smallest possible value is $\cos^2\theta_r$, attained at
$x=q_r$. This proves the second formula.

For the upper bound on distance, we can rotate each positive-angle
direction independently:
\begin{align*}
u_j(t)=\cos(t\theta_j)p_j+\sin(t\theta_j)n_j
 \quad(\theta_j>0),\qquad
 u_j(t)=p_j\quad(\theta_j=0).
\end{align*}
Let $P(t)$ be their $\mathbb F$-linear span, for $0\le t\le1$.
The vectors $u_j(t)$ are orthonormal, $P(0)=P$, and $P(1)=Q$.
Moreover, every $\dot u_j(t)$ is perpendicular to $P(t)$, so
\begin{align*}
g_{P(t)}(\dot P(t),\dot P(t))=|\dot P(t)|^2=\sum_{j=1}^r|\dot u_j(t)|^2=\sum_{j=1}^r \theta_j^2.
\end{align*}
The length of this curve is the right side of the first formula.
For the lower bound, consider an arbitrary piecewise smooth plane
curve $P(t)$ from $P$ to $Q$. Choose a moving orthonormal frame
$u_1(t),\ldots,u_r(t)$ with terminal frame $q_1,\ldots,q_r$ and
with $\dot u_j(t)\perp P(t)$. Such a horizontal frame exists. Indeed,
starting with any orthonormal frame matrix $E(t)$, the matrix
$\Gamma(t)=E(t)^*\dot E(t)$ is skew-Hermitian. Replacing $E(t)$ by
$E(t)R(t)$, where $\dot R=-\Gamma R$ with prescribed terminal
orthogonal or unitary value, removes its internal frame rotation.
This construction also works for quaternionic unitary matrices.
Let $v_j=u_j(0)\in P$. The initial frame need not be a principal
frame. Nevertheless,
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{Re}\langle v_j,q_j\rangle
 =\operatorname{Re}\langle v_j,\operatorname{pr}_Pq_j\rangle\le|\operatorname{pr}_Pq_j|=\cos\theta_j.
\end{align*}
Thus the real spherical length
$\ell_j=\int_0^1|\dot u_j(t)|\,dt$ is at least $\theta_j$.
Using \eqref{eq:metric} and the integral triangle inequality in
$\mathbb R^r$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
L(P(t))
 =\int_0^1\left(\sum_j|\dot u_j(t)|^2\right)^{1/2}\,dt
 \ge\left(\sum_j\left(\int_0^1|\dot u_j(t)|\,dt\right)^2\right)^{1/2}
 =\left(\sum_j\ell_j^2\right)^{1/2}
 \ge\left(\sum_j\theta_j^2\right)^{1/2}.
\end{align*}
Taking the infimum over all connecting curves proves the distance
formula. The argument also covers zero and right principal angles.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:diameters}
For $r\ge1$ and $N\ge2r$, in the metric \eqref{eq:metric},
\begin{align}
\label{eq:diameters}
 \operatorname{diam} X_{\mathbb C,r,N}=\operatorname{diam} X_{\mathbb H,r,N}=\frac\pi2\sqrt r,
 \qquad \operatorname{diam} X_{\mathbb R,r,N}\le\pi\sqrt r.
\end{align}
The unoriented real Grassmannian also has diameter $\pi\sqrt r/2$.
For any two oriented real planes, distance is at least the distance
between their underlying planes.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
Since every principal angle is at most $\pi/2$,
\cref{lem:distance} gives the bound $\pi\sqrt r/2$ in each
unoriented case. Since $N\ge2r$, we can choose mutually orthogonal
$r$-planes. All their principal angles equal $\pi/2$, and the bound
is attained. This proves the three unoriented diameter formulas.

For the oriented real case, choose principal frames $p_j,q_j$ with
$\langle p_i,q_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}\cos\theta_j$.
By simultaneous sign changes of corresponding $p_j,q_j$, we may
assume that the $p$-frame has the prescribed orientation of $P$.
For $\theta_j>0$, set
\[
 n_j=\frac{q_j-\cos\theta_j\,p_j}{\sin\theta_j}.
\]
These are orthonormal vectors in $P^\perp$. Complete them to an
orthonormal $r$-frame $n_1,\ldots,n_r$ in $P^\perp$, assigning
the added vectors to the zero-angle indices. This is possible
because $N-r\ge r$.

If the principal $q$-frame has the prescribed orientation of $Q$,
take $\alpha_j=\theta_j$ for all $j$. Otherwise, take
$\alpha_1=\theta_1-\pi$ and $\alpha_j=\theta_j$ for $j>1$.
Then
\[
 q'_j=\cos\alpha_j\,p_j+\sin\alpha_j\,n_j
\]
is a positively oriented orthonormal frame of $Q$: in the second
case only $q_1$ is replaced by $-q_1$. This also works when
$\theta_1=0$, since $\alpha_1=-\pi$ gives $q'_1=-p_1=-q_1$.
In particular, the construction includes the case in which all
principal angles vanish but the two orientations are opposite.

For $0\le t\le1$, put
\[
 u_j(t)=\cos(t\alpha_j)p_j+\sin(t\alpha_j)n_j
\]
and give $P(t)=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{u_1(t),\ldots,u_r(t)\}$
the orientation of this frame. The planes
$\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{p_j,n_j\}$ are mutually orthogonal,
so the frame is orthonormal and each $\dot u_j(t)$ is perpendicular
to $P(t)$. Thus \eqref{eq:metric} gives
\[
 |\dot P(t)|^2=\sum_{j=1}^r|\dot u_j(t)|^2
             =\sum_{j=1}^r\alpha_j^2.
\]
This path joins $P$ to $Q$ and has length
$\sqrt{\sum_j\alpha_j^2}\le\pi\sqrt r$, proving the oriented
upper bound. Finally, the forgetful map is a local isometry and
preserves curve length, so oriented distance is at least the
distance between the underlying planes.

<!-- lemma-3: body_md -->
\label{lem:oscillation}
Let $G\subset\operatorname{Isom}(X_{\mathbb F,r,N})$ be a compact subgroup, and $F:X_{\mathbb F,r,N}\to\mathbb R$ be a smooth $G$-invariant function whose
range is exactly $[-1,1]$. Suppose its restriction to every rotation
\eqref{eq:rank-one-rotation} is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
at most an integer $d\ge1$. Then
\begin{align*}
\|\nabla F\|\le d\sqrt r,\qquad
 \operatorname{diam}(X_{\mathbb F,r,N}/G)\ge\frac{2}{d\sqrt r},
\end{align*}
 where $r\ge1$ and $N\ge2r$.
<!-- end lemma-3 -->
<!-- proof-4: body_md -->
Every unit rank-one tangent map is the velocity of a curve
\eqref{eq:rank-one-rotation}. Since $|F|\le1$ along the entire curve,
Bernstein's inequality gives $|\mathrm dF(A)|\le d$ in each such
direction $A$.
Note that an arbitrary unit tangent has a singular-value decomposition
\begin{align*}
A=\sum_{j=1}^k\sigma_j A_j,
 \qquad k\le r,\quad \sigma_j>0,\quad \sum_{j=1}^k\sigma_j^2=1,
\end{align*}
where $A_j$ are unit rank-one tangent maps. Consequently
\begin{align*}
|\mathrm dF(A)|\le d\sum_{j=1}^k\sigma_j
 \le d\sqrt{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^k\sigma_j^2\right)^{1/2}
 \le d\sqrt r.
\end{align*}
By integration along curves, the same global Lipschitz bound holds for
$F$. Since $F$ is invariant, for any $p,q$ and $g\in G$,
\begin{align*}
|F(p)-F(q)|=|F(p)-F(gq)|\le d\sqrt r\,d(p,gq).
\end{align*}
It follows that $d_{X_{\mathbb F,r,N}/G}(Gp,Gq)\geq \frac{1}{d\sqrt r}|F(p)-F(q)|.$
Choose points where $F$ attains $1$ and $-1$. Their quotient
distance is at least $2/(d\sqrt r)$.

<!-- lemma-4: body_md -->
\label{lem:subspace}
Let $0<r<N$. Let $H\subset O(N)$ if $\mathbb F=\mathbb R$, and let
$H\subset Sp(N)$ if $\mathbb F=\mathbb H$. If $\mathbb F=\mathbb C$, let $H$ be a subgroup
of the group of unitary and antiunitary transformations of $\mathbb C^N$.
Assume that $H$ is compact. These transformations preserve
$\mathbb F$-linear subspaces and induce isometries of $\operatorname{Gr}_r(\mathbb F^N)$.
Suppose there is an $\mathbb F$-linear vector subspace
$W\subset\mathbb F^N$ such that
\begin{align*}
0<\dim_\mathbb F W<N,\qquad hW=W\quad\text{for every }h\in H.
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{Gr}_r(\mathbb F^N)/H)\ge\pi/2.
\end{align*}
If a compact group $G$ acts on $\operatorname{Gr}_r^+(\mathbb R^N)$ and its induced
action on underlying planes is the action of such an $H$, then
$\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{Gr}_r^+(\mathbb R^N)/G)\ge\pi/2$ as well.
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-5: body_md -->
Put $d=\dim_\mathbb F W$. Since the transformations of $H$ are isometries and
preserve $W$, they also preserve $W^\perp$. An $r$-plane that splits
as $A\oplus B$ with $A\subset W$, $B\subset W^\perp$ can have
$\dim_\mathbb F A=a$ exactly when
\begin{align*}
a_-:=\max(0,r+d-N)\le a\le\min(r,d)=:a_+.
\end{align*}
These inequalities imply that $0\le a\le d$ and
$0\le r-a\le N-d$. Moreover $a_+>a_-$. Indeed, if $r+d\le N$, then
$a_-=0<a_+$; otherwise $r+d-N$ is strictly smaller than both $r$
and $d$, since $r<N$ and $d<N$.

Choose subspaces $A_\pm\subset W$ of dimensions $a_\pm$ and
$B_\pm\subset W^\perp$ of dimensions $r-a_\pm$, and put
$P_\pm=A_\pm\oplus B_\pm$. For each $h\in H$, the projection
map from $A_+$ to $hA_-$ has rank at most $a_-$ and therefore has
a nonzero kernel. Choose a unit vector $x_h$ in this kernel.
It is perpendicular to $hA_-$; it is also perpendicular to $hB_-$,
since $x_h\in W$ and $hB_-\subset W^\perp$. Hence
\begin{align*}
x_h\in P_+,\qquad \operatorname{pr}_{hP_-}x_h=0.
\end{align*}
The largest principal angle between $P_+$ and $hP_-$ is $\pi/2$.
By \cref{lem:distance}, $d(P_+,hP_-)\ge\pi/2$ for every $h$.
Taking the minimum over the group proves the quotient bound.
This argument only uses preservation of dimensions, orthogonality,
and subspaces, so it also permits antiunitary transformations.

For oriented planes, choose either orientation of $P_+$ and $P_-$.
Every translated underlying pair has the same lower bound just
proved. The orientation-forgetting map is a local isometry and therefore
does not increase distances. This finishes the proof.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:finite}
For every positive integer $r$, there exists $c_{\mathrm{fin},r}>0$
such that every finite isometry group $G$ of every
simply connected irreducible compact symmetric space $M$ of rank $r$
satisfies
\begin{align*}
\Delta(M,G)\ge c_{\mathrm{fin},r}.
\end{align*}
In particular, the assertion includes $r=1$.
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-6: body_md -->
We first obtain a quantitative separation estimate for a finite
orthogonal group. The measure formulation of Green's theorem
\cite[Definition~1.6 and Theorem~1.8]{Green} provides an absolute
constant $a>0$ with the following property. For every integer $m\ge1$
and every finite subgroup $H\subset U(m)$, there is a probability measure
$\mu$ on the complex unit sphere such that
\begin{align}
\label{eq:green}
 \int \max_{h\in H}|\langle hv,z\rangle|^2\,d\mu(z)
 \le\frac1{1+a\log m}
\end{align}
for every unit vector $v\in\mathbb C^m$.
The measure may depend on $H$, but it is the same measure for every
$v$. This quantifier is essential when separating an entire plane.

Let $H\subset O(m)$ be a finite group and let $E\subset\mathbb R^m$ be a real
$s$-plane, with orthonormal basis $e_1,\ldots,e_s$. Consider each
real matrix $h$ as a unitary matrix on $\mathbb C^m$. By applying the inequality
\eqref{eq:green} to each $e_j$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
\int\sum_{j=1}^s\max_{h\in H}|\langle he_j,z\rangle|^2\,d\mu(z)
 \le\frac{s}{1+a\log m}.
\end{align*}
Since $\mu$ is a probability measure, the minimum of this continuous
integrand on the compact sphere is at most its integral. Hence there
is one unit vector $z=u+iv$, with $u,v\in\mathbb R^m$, such that
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{j=1}^s\max_{k\in H}|\langle ke_j,z\rangle|^2
 \le\frac{s}{1+a\log m}.
\end{align*}
Here $z$ is chosen for the whole sum and is fixed for the rest of
the argument. Since $|u|^2+|v|^2=1$, one of these real
vectors has squared norm at least $1/2$. Denote it by $w$ and put
$x=w/|w|$. For every $h\in H$,
\begin{align*}
|\operatorname{pr}_{hE}x|^2
 =\frac1{|w|^2}\sum_{j=1}^s|\langle he_j,w\rangle|^2
 \le2\sum_{j=1}^s|\langle he_j,z\rangle|^2
 \le2\sum_{j=1}^s\max_{k\in H}|\langle ke_j,z\rangle|^2
 \le\frac{2s}{1+a\log m}.
\end{align*}
The first inequality uses that each real or imaginary part of
$\langle he_j,z\rangle$ has modulus at most $|\langle he_j,z\rangle|$.
We have obtained one real unit vector $x$ satisfying
\begin{align}
\label{eq:finite-separate}
 \max_{h\in H}|\operatorname{pr}_{hE}x|^2\le\frac{2s}{1+a\log m}.
\end{align}

 Fix an $\mathbb F$-linear $r$-plane $E\subset\mathbb F^N$ and a finite
ambient group $H$ acting on its Grassmannian. With
$\kappa=\dim_\mathbb R\mathbb F\in\{1,2,4\}$, the underlying real
representation has dimension $m=\kappa N$ and the real dimension
of $E$ is $s=\kappa r$. Every ambient element is real orthogonal,
including the antiunitary elements allowed in the complex case.
An $\mathbb F$-linear subspace and its Hermitian orthogonal complement
are also orthogonal complements for the real scalar product.
Thus the real projection in \eqref{eq:finite-separate} is exactly
the Hermitian projection needed in \cref{lem:distance}.

 Now, extend $x$ to an $\mathbb F$-orthonormal $r$-frame and let $Q$ be its
span. Since $x\in Q$ and $|x|=1$, for every $h\in H$,
\begin{align*}
\cos\theta_r(hE,Q)
 =\min_{\substack{y\in Q\\|y|=1}}|\operatorname{pr}_{hE}y|
 \le|\operatorname{pr}_{hE}x|
 \le\min\left\{1,\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa r}{1+a\log(\kappa N)}}\right\}.
\end{align*}
The function $\arccos$ is decreasing and distance is at least the
largest principal angle. Hence
\begin{align}
\label{eq:finite-angle}
 \operatorname{diam}(X_{\mathbb F,r,N}/H)\ge
 \arccos\min\left\{1,
 \sqrt{\frac{2\kappa r}{1+a\log(\kappa N)}}\right\}.
\end{align}
In the real oriented case, one can choose orientations on $E,Q$ and use the
distance comparison in \cref{lem:diameters}, so the same bound holds.
Let $N_0(r)>\max\{8,2r\}$ be an integer such that
$1+a\log N_0(r)\ge32r$. If $N\ge N_0(r)$, then
\begin{align*}
1+a\log(\kappa N)\ge32r\ge8\kappa r.
\end{align*}
The square root in \eqref{eq:finite-angle} is at most $1/2$, so
the absolute quotient diameter is at least $\pi/3$, uniformly in
$H$ and $\mathbb F$. Dividing by the common upper bound
$\pi\sqrt r$ from \cref{lem:diameters} gives
\begin{align*}
\Delta(X_{\mathbb F,r,N},H)\ge\frac1{3\sqrt r}.
\end{align*}
For a finite subgroup $G$ of the full isometry group, use the
finite-kernel covers of \cref{sec:reduction}. Its full inverse
image is finite. In the complex and quaternionic cases it has
exactly the same Grassmannian orbits; in the real case its image in
$O(N)$ describes the underlying-plane orbits, and the orientation
comparison still applies. Thus the preceding estimate holds for
every finite intrinsic isometry group in this parameter range.

By \cref{prop:fixed-rank-reduction}, the remaining spaces form
finitely many homothety classes at fixed $r$. Choose
representatives $M_1,\ldots,M_t$. By \cref{thm:fixed-space}, each
has a number $\delta_j>0$ bounding below every positive quotient
diameter. A finite group cannot act transitively on a
positive-dimensional connected manifold. Therefore all the finite-group
quotients here have positive diameter. The positive number
\begin{align*}
c_{\mathrm{fin},r}=
 \min\left\{\frac1{3\sqrt r},
 \frac{\delta_1}{\operatorname{diam} M_1},\ldots,
 \frac{\delta_t}{\operatorname{diam} M_t}\right\}
\end{align*}
proves the theorem.

\label{thm:GP}
Let a compact connected Lie group $H$ act in a Hamiltonian fashion
by holomorphic isometries on a compact connected K\"ahler manifold $M$.
Choose an $\operatorname{Ad}(H)$-invariant positive scalar product on its Lie
algebra $\mathfrak h$, and use it to identify an equivariant moment map with
a map $\mu:M\to\mathfrak h$. If $\|\mu\|^2$ is constant, then $M$ is

<!-- lemma-5: body_md -->
\label{lem:real-subsets}
Let $2\le r\le N-2$, and let $b_{ij}=b_{ji}$ be real numbers for
distinct $i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$. Suppose that
\begin{align}\label{eq:Sb}
\sum_{\substack{i<j; ~i,j\in S}}b_{ij}
\end{align}
has the same value for every $r$-element subset $S$ of
$\{1,\ldots,N\}$. Then all $b_{ij}$ are equal.
<!-- end lemma-5 -->
<!-- proof-7: body_md -->
Fix distinct indices $i,j$ and put $d_k=b_{ik}-b_{jk}$ for
$k\notin\{i,j\}$. By comparing the sums (\ref{eq:Sb}) for $T\cup\{i\}$ and
$T\cup\{j\}$, we obtain
\begin{align}
\label{eq:real-subset-difference}
 \sum_{k\in T}d_k=0
\end{align}
for every $(r-1)$-element subset $T$ avoiding $i,j$.
If $k,\ell$ are two distinct remaining indices, choose an
$(r-2)$-element set $U$ avoiding $i,j,k,\ell$. This is possible
because $0\le r-2\le N-4$; for $r=2$, take $U=\varnothing$.
Apply \eqref{eq:real-subset-difference} to $U\cup\{k\}$ and
$U\cup\{\ell\}$ and subtract. It follows that $d_k=d_\ell$.
Thus all $d_k$ have a common value $d$. By
\eqref{eq:real-subset-difference}, we have $(r-1)d=0$, so $d=0$.
Consequently $b_{ik}=b_{jk}$ whenever $i,j,k$ are distinct. All
edges incident to a fixed index therefore have the same value, and
an edge joining two indices identifies their common values. This
proves equality for every pair.

<!-- lemma-6: body_md -->
\label{lem:real-moment}
The map $\mu:\operatorname{Gr}_2^+(\mathbb R^N)\to\mathfrak{so}(N)$ given by
$\mu(P)=Z_P$ is an equivariant moment map for $SO(N)$.
For a Lie subgroup $H\subset SO(N)$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak h$,
the map $\mu_H=\operatorname{pr}_\mathfrak h\mu$ is an equivariant moment map for $H$.
<!-- end lemma-6 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Every tangent vector at $P$ has the form $Y^\#_P$, with
$Y\in\mathfrak{so}(N)$. Differentiate the identity
\[
 Z_{\exp(tY)P}=\exp(tY)Z_P\exp(-tY)
\]
to obtain
\[
 d\langle \mu,X\rangle_P(Y^\#_P)
 =\langle [Y,Z_P],X\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(N)}
 =\langle Z_P,[X,Y]\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(N)}.
\]
Write the off-diagonal blocks of $X$ and $Y$, relative to
$\mathbb R^N=P\oplus P^\perp$, as
\[
 X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{11}&-U^T\\U&X_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 Y=\begin{pmatrix}Y_{11}&-V^T\\V&Y_{22}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Their fundamental vectors are $U$ and $V$. Since $X_{11}$ and
$Y_{11}$ lie in the one-dimensional algebra $\mathfrak{so}(P)$, they
commute. The upper-left block of $[X,Y]$ is therefore
$-U^TV+V^TU$. As $Z_P|_P=J_P$, we obtain
\begin{align*}
 \langle Z_P,[X,Y]\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(N)}
 &=\frac12\operatorname{tr}\bigl(J_P(U^TV-V^TU)\bigr)\\
 &=\operatorname{tr}(J_PU^TV)
 =g_P(-UJ_P,V)=\omega_P(U,V).
\end{align*}
This proves the moment-map identity. Equivariance follows from
$Z_{aP}=aZ_Pa^{-1}$ for $a\in SO(N)$.
For $X\in\mathfrak h$, orthogonal projection gives
$\langle \mu_H,X\rangle=\langle \mu,X\rangle$, so the same differential identity
holds for $H$. The projection is $H$-equivariant because the
scalar product is invariant.

<!-- lemma-7: body_md -->
\label{lem:real-quadric}
Let $N>8$, let $H\subset SO(N)$ be a compact and connected subgroup, and suppose
that its Lie algebra satisfies $0\ne\mathfrak h\ne\mathfrak{so}(N)$.
The squared norm of the projected moment map is
\begin{align*}
\psi_\mathfrak h(x\wedge y)=\|\operatorname{pr}_\mathfrak h(x\wedge y)\|_{\mathfrak{so}(N)}^2,
\end{align*}
where $\{x,y\}$ is a positively oriented orthonormal basis of $P\in \operatorname{Gr}_2^+(\mathbb R^N)$. Then $\psi_\mathfrak h$ is nonconstant.
<!-- end lemma-7 -->
<!-- proof-9: body_md -->
The $H$-action is nontrivial, since the $SO(N)$-action on $\operatorname{Gr}_2^+(\mathbb R^N)$ has only
a finite kernel, and $\mathfrak h\ne0$. The space $\operatorname{Gr}_2^+(\mathbb R^N)$ is Riemannian
irreducible for $N>8$. If $\psi_\mathfrak h$ were constant,
then \cref{thm:GP} would make $H$ transitive on $\operatorname{Gr}_2^+(\mathbb R^N)$.
We now apply the compact transitivity classification in
\cite[p.~2484, equations~(1.2)--(1.5)]{WolfFlags}; see also \cite{On}.
Its hypotheses hold for this presentation. Modulo its finite
kernel, $SO(N)$ is the full connected group of Hermitian isometries
of the quadric; its Lie algebra is simple; and
$SO(2)\times SO(N-2)$ is the centralizer of the circle rotating
the distinguished two-plane. The image of $H$ is a compact
connected subgroup. In that classification the proper transitive
presentations of quadrics occur in ambient dimensions $7$ and $8$,
apart from smaller-dimensional identifications. The eight-dimensional case is listed explicitly in
\cite[p.~2484, equation~(1.5)]{WolfFlags}. Thus, for $N>8$, transitivity forces
$\mathfrak h=\mathfrak{so}(N)$, which contradicts the hypothesis. So $\psi_\mathfrak h$ is nonconstant.

\label{lem:real-energy}
Let $N>8$ and $2\le r\le N-2$. Let $H\subset SO(N)$ be a compact and
connected subgroup with $0\ne\mathfrak h\ne\mathfrak{so}(N)$, and choose a basis
$A_1,\ldots,A_s$ of $\mathfrak h$ orthonormal for
\eqref{eq:real-trace}. For a real rank-$r$ orthogonal projector
$P$, put
\begin{align}
\label{eq:real-energy}
 q_r(P)=\sum_{\nu=1}^s\|PA_\nu P\|_{\mathrm{Frob}}^2
 =-\sum_{\nu=1}^s\operatorname{tr}(PA_\nu PA_\nu).
\end{align}
Then $q_r$ is nonconstant. It is independent of the chosen
orthonormal basis and invariant under every ambient orthogonal
transformation normalizing $\mathfrak h$. Its pullback to oriented planes
is also invariant under orientation reversal.
<!-- end lemma-8 -->
<!-- proof-10: body_md -->
The equality in \eqref{eq:real-energy} follows from $P^2=P$, $P^T=P,$
$A_\nu^T=-A_\nu$, and cyclicity of trace. Note that orthogonal changes of
the basis of $\mathfrak h$ preserve the sum of squared norms. If an
orthogonal transformation normalizes $\mathfrak h$, then its conjugation action
preserves \eqref{eq:real-trace} and carries an orthonormal basis
of $\mathfrak h$ to another one. This proves the stated invariance.
Orientation reversal leaves the projector unchanged.

Suppose that $q_r$ is constant, and fix an arbitrary
orthonormal basis $e_1,\ldots,e_N$ of $\mathbb R^N$. Set
\begin{align*}
b_{ij}=\sum_{\nu=1}^s\langle A_\nu e_i,e_j\rangle^2=b_{ji}
 \qquad (i\ne j).
\end{align*}
Since $A_\nu$ is skew-symmetric, every diagonal entry is zero. Thus, on the
coordinate $r$-plane $P_S$,
\begin{align*}
q_r(P_S)=2\sum_{\substack{i<j;~i,j\in S}}b_{ij}.
\end{align*}
By \cref{lem:real-subsets}, all $b_{ij}$ are equal. Their total
sum is
\begin{align*}
\sum_{i<j}b_{ij}
 =\sum_{\nu=1}^s\langle A_\nu,A_\nu\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(N)}=s,
\end{align*}
so their common value is $2s/[N(N-1)]$, which is independent of the
orthonormal basis. Every orthonormal pair extends to such a basis;
consequently
\begin{align}
\label{eq:real-pair-energy}
 \sum_{\nu=1}^s\langle A_\nu x,y\rangle^2
 =\frac{2s}{N(N-1)}
 \quad (|x|=|y|=1,\ x\perp y).
\end{align}
Since
$\langle A_\nu,x\wedge y\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(N)}=\langle A_\nu x,y\rangle$ by \eqref{eq:real-trace},
the left side of \eqref{eq:real-pair-energy} is precisely $\psi_\mathfrak h(x\wedge y)$.
This contradicts
\cref{lem:real-quadric}.

Let $1\le r<N$, and equip $\operatorname{Gr}_r(\mathbb C^N)$ with
the metric \eqref{eq:metric}. Define
\begin{align*}
J_r(P)=i\left(P-\frac rN I\right).

The matrix $J_r(P)$ is skew-Hermitian and traceless, so it lies
in $\mathfrak{su}(N)$. At a plane $P$, decompose
$\mathbb C^N=P\oplus P^\perp$. A tangent map
$U\in\operatorname{Hom}_\mathbb C(P,P^\perp)$ corresponds, in the projector representation, to
\begin{align*}
\dot P_U=\begin{pmatrix}0&U^*\\ U&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align*}
Write an arbitrary $X\in\mathfrak{su}(N)$ as
\begin{align*}
X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{11}&-Z^*\\ Z&X_{22}\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align*}
Its infinitesimal action on the Grassmannian is the tangent map
$X^\#_P=Z$. Since
$\omega_P(Z,U)=\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{tr}(Z^*U)$, direct differentiation
gives
\begin{align*}
\mathrm d\langle \mu,X\rangle_{\mathfrak{su}(N)}(U)
 =\frac12\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}(i\dot P_U X)
 =\frac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(i\operatorname{tr}(U^*Z-Z^*U)\bigr)
 =\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{tr}(Z^*U)
 =\omega_P(X^\#_P,U).
\end{align*}

$J_r(aPa^{-1})=aJ_r(P)a^{-1}$ for any $a\in SU(N)$.

and nontransitively on $\operatorname{Gr}_r(\mathbb C^N)$, then

\label{lem:quaternionic-evaluation}
An $Sp(1)_{\mathrm R}$-invariant real alternating four-form on $V$
is determined by its values on $(x,Ix,Jx,Kx)$, for $x\in V$.
More precisely, the linear map
\begin{align*}
T:(\Lambda^4V^*)^{Sp(1)_{\mathrm R}}
   \longrightarrow (\operatorname{Sym}^4V^*)^{Sp(1)_{\mathrm R}},
 \qquad (T\Phi)(x)=\Phi(x,Ix,Jx,Kx),
\end{align*}
is an isomorphism. We identify a symmetric tensor of degree four
with its homogeneous quartic polynomial.
<!-- end lemma-11 -->
<!-- proof-15: body_md -->
We first prove that $(T\Phi)(xq)=(T\Phi)(x)$ for every $x\in V$
and every unit quaternion $q$. Let $R_qx=xq$. The map
$L\mapsto R_q^{-1}LR_q$ preserves
$\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{I,J,K\}$ and its Euclidean scalar
product. Denote its matrix in the ordered basis $(I,J,K)$ by $O_q$.
Then $O_q\in SO(3)$: it is orthogonal, depends continuously on $q$,
and equals the identity at $q=1$, while $Sp(1)$ is connected.
Define
\begin{align*}
\widetilde I=R_q^{-1}IR_q,\qquad
 \widetilde J=R_q^{-1}JR_q,\qquad
 \widetilde K=R_q^{-1}KR_q.
\end{align*}
Then $(\widetilde I,\widetilde J,\widetilde K)=(I,J,K)O_q$. Note that $\Phi(x,\widetilde Ix,\widetilde Jx,\widetilde Kx)
 =\det(O_q)\Phi(x,Ix,Jx,Kx).$
By the invariance of $\Phi$ we obtain
\begin{align*}
\begin{aligned}
 (T\Phi)(R_qx)
 &=\Phi(R_qx,IR_qx,JR_qx,KR_qx)\\
 &=\Phi(x,\widetilde Ix,\widetilde Jx,\widetilde Kx)
 =(T\Phi)(x).
\end{aligned}
\end{align*}
Since $T\Phi$ is a homogeneous quartic polynomial, the map
$T$ is well-defined.

The complexification of the quaternionic representation has the form
\begin{align*}
V_{\mathbb C}\cong E\otimes H,
\qquad
\dim_{\mathbb C}E=2N,\quad H=\mathbb C^2.
\end{align*}
The complexified right $Sp(1)$-action is the standard $SL_2(\mathbb C)$-action on $H$, and it acts trivially on the space $E$. Since $H^*\cong H$ as an $SL_2$-module, we can write $V_{\mathbb C}^*\cong E^*\otimes H.$ The symmetric and exterior Cauchy formulas give
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{Sym}^4(E^*\otimes H)
 &=\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash4}S_\lambda E^*\otimes S_\lambda H,\\
\Lambda^4(E^*\otimes H)
 &=\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash4}S_\lambda E^*\otimes S_{\lambda'}H.
\end{align*}
Here, $\lambda$ is a partition of $4$, $\lambda'$ is its transpose, and $S_\lambda$ denotes the corresponding Schur functor. These are the standard Cauchy identities; see \cite[\S~6.2.8]{SamSnowden}. For a partition $(a,b)$,
\begin{align*}
S_{(a,b)}H
\cong
\operatorname{Sym}^{a-b}H\otimes(\det H)^b.
\end{align*}
On $SL_2$, the determinant factor is trivial. Thus this representation has an invariant vector exactly when $a=b$. In degree four, $a+b=4$, so the only possibility is $(a,b)=(2,2).$ Therefore
\begin{align*}
\left((\operatorname{Sym}^4V^*)^{Sp(1)}\right)_{\mathbb C}
\cong S_{(2,2)}E^*, \qquad \left((\Lambda^4V^*)^{Sp(1)}\right)_{\mathbb C}
\cong S_{(2,2)}E^*.
\end{align*} This Schur module is irreducible under $GL(E)$.
Every $g\in GL(N,\mathbb H)$ commutes with $I,J,K$. Using the usual action on covariant tensors,
\begin{align*}
\begin{aligned}
T(g\cdot\Phi)(x)
&=\Phi(g^{-1}x,g^{-1}Ix,g^{-1}Jx,g^{-1}Kx)\\
&=\Phi(g^{-1}x,I g^{-1}x,J g^{-1}x,K g^{-1}x)
=(T\Phi)(g^{-1}x).
\end{aligned}
\end{align*}
Hence, $T$ is $GL(N,\mathbb H)$-equivariant.
The Lie algebra of this group satisfies
\begin{align*}
\mathfrak{gl}(N,\mathbb H)\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C
\cong\mathfrak{gl}(2N,\mathbb C)=\mathfrak{gl}(E).
\end{align*}
Thus, $T_{\mathbb C}$ intertwines the $\mathfrak{gl}(E)$-actions on the two irreducible modules above. For each $L=I,J,K$, the quaternionic relations give
\begin{align*}
(\eta_L\wedge\eta_L)(x,Ix,Jx,Kx)=2|x|^4.
\end{align*}
Consequently,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:evalOmega}
 (T\Omega)(x)=\Omega(x,Ix,Jx,Kx)=6|x|^4.
\end{align}
This is not the zero polynomial, so $T_{\mathbb C}\neq0$. Schur's lemma now makes the complexified map an isomorphism, and
therefore the original real map is an isomorphism as well.

<!-- theorem-7: body_md -->
\label{thm:quartic-rigidity}
Suppose $N>28$ and $0\ne\mathfrak h\subset\mathfrak{sp}(N)$ is a Lie subalgebra. If the quartic $f$ in
\eqref{eq:quaternionic-energies} is constant on the unit sphere of
$V$, then $\mathfrak h=\mathfrak{sp}(N)$.
<!-- end theorem-7 -->
<!-- proof-16: body_md -->
We first derive a four-form identity from the constancy of $f$.
We then use this identity to construct a compact quaternionic
symmetric space. The classification reduces the final step to two
explicit calculations.

\medskip\noindent\emph{Step 1. Positivity and irreducibility.}
By homogeneity, $f(x)=a|x|^4$ for some $a\ge0$. In fact, $a>0$.
If $a=0$, then $x^*A_\nu x=0$ for every $x$ and every $\nu$.
In particular, $\langle A_\nu x,Ix\rangle_V=0$ for every $x$. The real
endomorphism $A_\nu I=IA_\nu$ is symmetric, and
$\langle A_\nu I x,x\rangle_V=-\langle A_\nu x,Ix\rangle_V=0$.
This gives $A_\nu I=0$, hence, $A_\nu=0$ for every $\nu$,
contradicting $\mathfrak h\ne0$.
The constancy hypothesis also forces $\mathfrak h$ to act quaternionically
irreducibly. Indeed, suppose $V=W\oplus W^\perp$ is a proper
orthogonal splitting into $\mathfrak h$-invariant quaternionic subspaces,
and choose unit vectors $y\in W$ and $z\in W^\perp$. Each $A_\nu$
is block diagonal, so for every unit quaternion $u$,
\begin{align*}
(y+zu)^*A_\nu(y+zu)
   =y^*A_\nu y+\bar u(z^*A_\nu z)u.
\end{align*}
The two diagonal values are imaginary quaternions. For any imaginary quaternion $q$,
\begin{align*}
\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(1)}\bar uqu\,du=0,
\end{align*}
where $du$ is Haar probability measure.
Therefore integration with respect to Haar probability measure
gives
\begin{align*}
\int_{Sp(1)}f(y+zu)\,\mathrm d u=f(y)+f(z)=2a.
\end{align*}
On the other hand, $|y+zu|^2=2$, so the integrand is identically
$4a$. This contradiction proves irreducibility.

\medskip\noindent\emph{Step 2. The four-form identity.}
For $A\in\mathfrak h$, define the alternating two-form
$\omega_A(v,w)=\langle Av,w\rangle_V$. We use the wedge convention
\begin{align*}
(\omega\wedge\omega)(v_0,v_1,v_2,v_3)
 =2\bigl(\omega(v_0,v_1)\omega(v_2,v_3)
 -\omega(v_0,v_2)\omega(v_1,v_3)
 +\omega(v_0,v_3)\omega(v_1,v_2)\bigr).
\end{align*}
Since $A$ commutes with $I,J,K$, we obtain
\begin{align}
\label{eq:evalA}
 (\omega_A\wedge\omega_A)(x,Ix,Jx,Kx)=-2|x^*Ax|^2.
\end{align}
Indeed, let $b=\langle Ax,Ix\rangle_V$, $c=\langle Ax,Jx\rangle_V$,
and $d=\langle Ax,Kx\rangle_V$. The other three paired values are
\begin{align*}
\omega_A(Jx,Kx)=-b,\qquad
 \omega_A(Ix,Kx)=c,\qquad
 \omega_A(Ix,Jx)=-d.
\end{align*}
Their contribution is $-2(b^2+c^2+d^2)$, and
$b^2+c^2+d^2=|x^*Ax|^2$. Note that each $\omega_A$ is invariant under
$Sp(1)_{\mathrm R}$, and so is $\Omega$. Hence
\begin{align*}
\Phi:=\sum_{\nu=1}^s\omega_{A_\nu}\wedge\omega_{A_\nu}
       +\frac a3\Omega
\end{align*}
is an invariant four-form. By
\eqref{eq:evalOmega} and \eqref{eq:evalA},
$T\Phi(x)=-2f(x)+2a|x|^4=0$. By
\cref{lem:quaternionic-evaluation}, we obtain the following identity
\begin{align}
\label{eq:bianchi}
 \sum_{\nu=1}^s\omega_{A_\nu}\wedge\omega_{A_\nu}
 +\frac a3\bigl(\eta_I\wedge\eta_I+
                 \eta_J\wedge\eta_J+\eta_K\wedge\eta_K\bigr)=0.
\end{align}

\medskip\noindent\emph{Step 3. Construction of a compact symmetric pair.}
Let $\mathfrak s=\operatorname{span}_\mathbb R\{I,J,K\}\simeq\mathfrak{sp}(1)$
and $\mathfrak k=\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak s$, acting on the real vector space $V$.
Choose the invariant positive scalar product on $\mathfrak k$ for which
the two summands are orthogonal, the restriction to $\mathfrak h$ is
\eqref{eq:trace}, and
\begin{align*}
\langle I,I\rangle_{\mathfrak k}=\langle J,J\rangle_{\mathfrak k}=\langle K,K\rangle_{\mathfrak k}=\frac3a,
 \qquad \langle I,J\rangle_{\mathfrak k}=\langle J,K\rangle_{\mathfrak k}=\langle K,I\rangle_{\mathfrak k}=0.
\end{align*}
The action of $\mathfrak k$ on $V$ is faithful. To see this, suppose
$A+S=0$ as endomorphisms, with $A\in\mathfrak h$ and $S\in\mathfrak s$.
Since $A$ commutes with $I,J,K$, so does $S=-A$. The center of
$\mathfrak s\cong\mathfrak{sp}(1)$ is zero, and hence $S=A=0$.
The action is also real irreducible. Indeed, any real
$\mathfrak k$-invariant subspace is stable under $I,J,K$, hence quaternionic,
and the preceding argument proves $\mathfrak h$-irreducibility.
We use the symmetric-pair criterion of Moroianu--Semmelmann
\cite[Proposition~2.5]{MS}, with its construction in
\cite[Lemma~2.1, Definition~2.2, and Remark~2.3]{MS}.
For a faithful real irreducible orthogonal representation of a
compact Lie algebra with a specified positive invariant scalar
product, that criterion says the following. If $Z_\alpha$ is an
orthonormal basis of the algebra and
$\omega_{Z_\alpha}(v,w)=\langle Z_\alpha v,w\rangle_V$, then the bracket
construction below is a Lie bracket exactly when its
\emph{Casimir four-form} $\sum_\alpha
\omega_{Z_\alpha}\wedge\omega_{Z_\alpha}$ vanishes.
For our metric on $\mathfrak k$, an orthonormal basis is
\begin{align*}
A_1,\ldots,A_s,\quad \sqrt{a/3}\,I,\quad
 \sqrt{a/3}\,J,\quad \sqrt{a/3}\,K.
\end{align*}
Thus its Casimir four-form is exactly the left side of
\eqref{eq:bianchi}. So all hypotheses of the criterion are satisfied.
Here is the bracket in our conventions.
Define $C:\Lambda^2V\to\mathfrak k$ by
\begin{align}
\label{eq:symmetric-bracket}
 \langle C(v,w),Z\rangle_{\mathfrak k}=\langle Zv,w\rangle_V\qquad(Z\in\mathfrak k),
\end{align}
and set $[Z,v]=Zv$ and $[v,w]=C(v,w)$ on
$\mathfrak l=\mathfrak k\oplus V$, keeping the original bracket on $\mathfrak k$.
Clearly, $C$ is uniquely defined, and the skew-adjointness of $Z$
makes it alternating. The invariance of the scalar products makes $C$
a $\mathfrak k$-equivariant map, so Jacobi identities with an entry in $\mathfrak k$
hold. For the remaining identity, an orthonormal basis $Z_\alpha$
as above gives
\begin{align*}
&\langle C(u,v)w+C(v,w)u+C(w,u)v,z\rangle_V\\
&\qquad=\frac12\sum_\alpha
    (\omega_{Z_\alpha}\wedge\omega_{Z_\alpha})(u,v,w,z)=0.
\end{align*}
This is the Jacobi calculation in Proposition~2.5 of \cite{MS}.
It holds for either choice of sign in $[v,w]=\pm C(v,w)$.
The positive sign used here is the one for which the positive
scalar product
\[
 B=\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak k}\oplus\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle_V
\]
is invariant. Indeed, the mixed identity is
\begin{align*}
 B([v,w],Z)
 &=\langle C(v,w),Z\rangle_{\mathfrak k}
 =\langle Zv,w\rangle_V\\
 &=-\langle v,Zw\rangle_V=B(v,[w,Z]).
\end{align*}
All other invariance identities follow from the invariance of the
scalar product on $\mathfrak k$, the skew-adjointness of its action on $V$,
and the orthogonality of the two summands.
Moreover, $\sigma(Z+v)=Z-v$ is an involutive Lie algebra
automorphism. Thus $(\mathfrak l,\mathfrak k)$ is a symmetric pair with
a positive invariant scalar product.
The center of $\mathfrak l$ is zero. Indeed, it is preserved by
$\sigma$, and its $\mathfrak k$-component vanishes by faithfulness on $V$,
while its $V$-component is fixed by $\mathfrak s$ and therefore
vanishes. Consequently, $\mathfrak l$ is compact semisimple.
Let $L$ be its simply connected compact group and $K_0$ the identity
component of the subgroup fixed by the integrated involution.
The subgroup $K_0$ is closed, since it is the identity component
of the fixed subgroup of an automorphism. Thus $L/K_0$ is compact.
It is simply connected by the homotopy exact sequence of
$K_0\to L\to L/K_0$, since $L$ is simply connected and $K_0$ is
connected. The invariant metric induced from $V$ makes this quotient
a Riemannian symmetric space. It is irreducible because its isotropy
representation on $V$ is real irreducible.
Moreover, the holonomy algebra of this space is exactly $\mathfrak k$ in its action
on $V$. Indeed, $[V,V]$ spans $\mathfrak k$: if $Z\in\mathfrak k$ is orthogonal to
all $C(v,w)$, then \eqref{eq:symmetric-bracket} gives
$\langle Zv,w\rangle_V=0$ for all $v,w$. Faithfulness implies $Z=0$.

The curvature formula is $R(v,w)z=-C(v,w)z$; see
\cite[Proposition~3.1.2]{Gorodski}. In our sign convention it gives
\[
 \langle R(v,w)w,v\rangle_V=\|C(v,w)\|_{\mathfrak k}^2\ge0.
\]
More explicitly, if $e_1,\ldots,e_{4N}$ is a real orthonormal basis
of $V$, then
\begin{align*}
 \operatorname{Ric}(v,v)
 &=\sum_i\|C(e_i,v)\|_{\mathfrak k}^2
 =\sum_\alpha|Z_\alpha v|^2\\
 &=\sum_{\nu=1}^s|A_\nu v|^2+a|v|^2
 \ge a|v|^2.
\end{align*}
Here we used the orthonormal basis of $\mathfrak k$ displayed above.
Since $a>0$, the constructed space has positive Ricci curvature.
Since its curvature is parallel, its values generate the holonomy
algebra, which is therefore $\mathfrak k$; see
\cite[Remark~2.1.8]{Gorodski}.
The connected holonomy group preserves the algebra
$\mathbb R\operatorname{Id}\oplus\mathfrak s$, since its Lie algebra
$\mathfrak k=\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak s$ normalizes this algebra. As $L/K_0$
is simply connected, full holonomy equals restricted holonomy.
Parallel transport therefore defines a parallel field of quaternion
algebras on $L/K_0$. Moreover, its scalar holonomy algebra is the
whole of $\mathfrak s$.

factor is the standard

For the complex two-plane Grassmannian, the tangent space is
$M_{N,2}(\mathbb C)$. Its isotropy algebra consists of pairs
$(A,B)\in\mathfrak u(N)\oplus\mathfrak u(2)$ with
$\operatorname{tr} A+\operatorname{tr} B=0$, acting by $Z\mapsto AZ-ZB$.
Write $B=B_0+bI_2$, where $\operatorname{tr} B_0=0$. Then
$b=-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr} A$, and the action becomes
\[
 Z\longmapsto DZ-ZB_0,\qquad
 D=A+\tfrac12(\operatorname{tr} A)I_N.
\]
The map $A\mapsto D$ is an isomorphism of $\mathfrak u(N)$:
it is the identity on $\mathfrak{su}(N)$ and multiplication by
$1+N/2$ on the center. Under the real isometry
$(z,w)\mapsto z+w j$ from $M_{N,2}(\mathbb C)$ to $\mathbb H^N$,
the $B_0$-factor gives right quaternionic scalars, and $D$ gives

quaternions. This is the stated standard embedding of
$\mathfrak{so}(N)\oplus\mathfrak{sp}(1)$.

The five entries of the last row come from the compact spaces of
types $G_2,F_4,E_6,E_7,E_8$ in Wolf's table.

Let $\mathfrak h'\subset\mathfrak{sp}(N)$ be one of $\mathfrak{sp}(N)$, $\mathfrak{u}(N)$,
$\mathfrak{so}(N)\oplus\mathfrak{sp}(1)$ in standard position and
$\mathfrak k'=\mathfrak h'\oplus\mathfrak s$. We claim that {if $N>28$, there are $\psi\in Sp(N)$ and a standard $\mathfrak h'$ with $\psi\mathfrak h\psi^{-1}=\mathfrak h'$, and then
$f_{\mathfrak h'}(\psi x)=f_\mathfrak h(x)$ for all $x$.}

To prove the claim, use Wolf's classification. Since $N>28$,
there is a homothety of $L/K_0$ onto one of the three classical
quaternionic symmetric spaces that preserves base points.
Multiplying its differential by a positive scalar gives a real
linear isometry $\varphi:V\to V$. Holonomy algebras correspond
under the differential, and scalar multiplication does not affect
conjugation. Hence $\varphi\mathfrak k\varphi^{-1}=\mathfrak k'$ for one of the
three $\mathfrak k'$.

First we arrange $\varphi\mathfrak s\varphi^{-1}=\mathfrak s$. The image $\varphi\mathfrak{s}\varphi^{-1}$ is an ideal
of $\mathfrak k'$ isomorphic to $\mathfrak{sp}(1)$, hence one of the simple ideals of $\mathfrak k'$. For
$\mathfrak k'=\mathfrak{sp}(N)\oplus\mathfrak{s}$ and $\mathfrak k'=\mathfrak{su}(N)\oplus\mathfrak{u}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{s}$, the only such ideal is $\mathfrak{s}$. For
$\mathfrak k'=\mathfrak{so}(N)\oplus\mathfrak{sp}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{s}$, there are two, $\mathfrak{sp}(1)$ and $\mathfrak{s}$. Let
$c(x)=\bar x$ be coordinatewise conjugation on $\mathbb H^N=\mathbb R^N\otimes\mathbb H$. It is a linear isometry, commutes with
$\mathfrak{so}(N)$, and satisfies $c(xq)=\bar q\,c(x)$, so it normalizes $\mathfrak k'$ and interchanges the two ideals.
Replace $\varphi$ by $c\circ\varphi$ if necessary.

Next we make the intertwiner quaternionic-linear. Conjugation by $\varphi$ maps the algebra
$\mathbb R\oplus\mathfrak{s}=\{R_q:q\in\mathbb H\}$ of right multiplications onto itself, so $\varphi R_q\varphi^{-1}=R_{\alpha(q)}$
for a real algebra automorphism $\alpha$ of $\mathbb H$. Indeed,
$R_pR_q=R_{qp}$, and conjugating this identity gives
$\alpha(qp)=\alpha(q)\alpha(p)$. Thus $\varphi(xq)=\varphi(x)\alpha(q)$. Every
automorphism of $\mathbb H$ is inner, $\alpha(q)=uqu^{-1}$ with $|u|=1$. Then $\psi(x)=\varphi(x)u$ satisfies
$\psi(xq)=\psi(x)q$ and is an isometry, so $\psi\in Sp(N)$. Right multiplication by $u$ commutes with $\mathfrak h'$
and normalizes $\mathfrak{s}$, so still $\psi\mathfrak k\psi^{-1}=\mathfrak k'$ and $\psi\mathfrak{s}\psi^{-1}=\mathfrak{s}$.

Now $\mathfrak h$ is the centralizer of $\mathfrak{s}$ in $\mathfrak k$ (because $\mathfrak k=\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak{s}$, $[\mathfrak h,\mathfrak{s}]=0$ and $\mathfrak{s}$
has trivial center), and $\mathfrak h'$ is the centralizer of $\mathfrak{s}$ in $\mathfrak k'$. Hence $\psi\mathfrak h\psi^{-1}=\mathfrak h'$.

$\mathfrak h'$ when $A_\nu$ is one of $\mathfrak h$, and $(\psi x)^*(\psi A_\nu\psi^{-1})(\psi x)=x^*A_\nu x$. Therefore
$f_{\mathfrak h'}(\psi x)=f_\mathfrak h(x)$.

\end{align*}
The Casimir endomorphism is quaternionic Hermitian and positive

                  =\mathcal C.

 b_{ij}=\sum_\nu|(A_\nu)_{ij}|^2=b_{ji}\quad(i\ne j).
\end{align*}

let $P_S$ be projection onto its coordinate span. Then by (\ref{eq:quaternionic-energies}), we have
\begin{align}
\label{eq:coordinate-energy}
 q_r(P_S)=\sum_{i\in S}a_i+
       2\sum_{\substack{i<j;~i,j\in S}}b_{ij},\qquad
 c=a_i+\sum_{k\ne i}b_{ik}.
\end{align}

\begin{align*}
0=a_i-a_j+2\sum_{k\in T}(b_{ik}-b_{jk}).
\end{align*}

\sum_{k\ne i,j}(b_{ik}-b_{jk})=-(a_i-a_j).

vector $e\in P$ by $e(t)=\cos(t)e+\sin(t)u$, with $u\perp P$ unit.
Its projector is
$P(t)=P-ee^*+e(t)e(t)^*$, whose entries have trigonometric degree
at most two. Equation \eqref{eq:quadratic} therefore gives degree
at most four for $q_r(P(t))$, which is independent of $N$ and $\dim\mathfrak g$.
Let $m$ and $M$ be the minimum and maximum of $q_r$. Since $m<M$,
the invariant function
\begin{align*}
F(P)=\frac{2q_r(P)-M-m}{M-m}
\end{align*}
has range $[-1,1]$ and the same trigonometric degree. Applying
\cref{lem:oscillation} with $d=4$ gives
$\operatorname{diam}(X_{\mathbb H,r,N}/G)\ge 2/(4\sqrt r)$.
Finally, \eqref{eq:diameters} gives
$\operatorname{diam} X_{\mathbb H,r,N}=\pi\sqrt r/2$, so division yields
$\Delta(X_{\mathbb H,r,N},G)\ge1/(\pi r)$. This completes the proof.

Replace the acting group by its closure, which leaves the quotient
metric unchanged, and omit the transitive case. We first give the
rank-one reduction mentioned in \cite{GLLM}.
Let $\varepsilon_S>0$ be the constant in \cref{thm:sphere-gap}.
For a unit sphere, that theorem gives
$\Delta(S^n,G)\ge\varepsilon_S/\pi$.

For $\mathbb F=\mathbb C$ or $\mathbb H$, equip $\mathbb F P^m$, $m\ge2$,
with the quotient metric of the unit-sphere Hopf fibration.
Every isometry of $\mathbb C P^m$ is induced by a unitary or antiunitary
map on $\mathbb C^{m+1}$, and every isometry of $\mathbb H P^m$ is induced
by an element of $Sp(m+1)$; see \cite[Sections~3--4]{WolfGrass}.
Let $\mathcal L_{\mathbb C}$ be the group of unitary and antiunitary
maps on $\mathbb C^{m+1}$. Let $\mathcal L_{\mathbb H}$ be the orthogonal
group of transformations $x\mapsto Axq^{-1}$, where
$A\in Sp(m+1)$ and $q\in Sp(1)$. In each case there is a
surjective homomorphism
\[
 \rho:\mathcal L_{\mathbb F}\longrightarrow
 \operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb F P^m).
\]
Its kernel acts transitively on every Hopf fibre. Thus
$\widehat G=\rho^{-1}(G)$ is a compact group acting on the
unit sphere and containing the fibre action. This construction
uses an extension of $G$ and does not require a homomorphic
choice of lifts of its elements.

For unit vectors $x,y$, the distance formula is
\[
 d_{\mathbb F P^m}([x],[y])
 =\arccos|x^*y|
 =\min_{q\in\mathbb F,\ |q|=1}d_S(x,yq).
\]
Taking the infimum over $G$ shows that
$\mathbb F P^m/G$ and $S^{(\dim_{\mathbb R}\mathbb F)(m+1)-1}/\widehat G$
are isometric as metric spaces. Since the former has positive
diameter, \cref{thm:sphere-gap} applies to the latter.
As $\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb F P^m)=\pi/2$, this gives
$\Delta(\mathbb F P^m,G)\ge2\varepsilon_S/\pi$.
The spaces $\mathbb C P^1$ and $\mathbb H P^1$ are round spheres up to
homothety. Finally, choose one metric on the Cayley plane $M_O$.
By \cref{thm:fixed-space}, its positive quotient diameters are
bounded below by some $\delta_O>0$. The rank-one assertion
therefore holds with
\[
 \varepsilon_1=\min\{\varepsilon_S/\pi,\ \delta_O/\operatorname{diam} M_O\}>0.
\]

Now let $r\ge2$. On the three families of Grassmannians \eqref{eq:families} with
$N>\max\{28,2r\}$, the acting groups with positive-dimensional identity
component are covered by \cref{thm:tail}. A closed subgroup of the
isometry group is a compact Lie group. If its identity component is
zero-dimensional, the group is finite and
\cref{thm:finite} applies.
By \cref{prop:fixed-rank-reduction}, the remaining homothety
classes have finitely many representatives $M_1,\ldots,M_t$. Since each representative is compact homogeneous
and simply connected, \cref{thm:fixed-space} shows that there exists $\delta_j>0$
such that every positive-diameter isometric quotient of $M_j$ has
diameter at least $\delta_j$. Therefore
\begin{align*}
\varepsilon_r=\min\left\{\frac1{\pi r},\ c_{\mathrm{fin},r},
 \frac{\delta_1}{\operatorname{diam} M_1},\ldots,
 \frac{\delta_t}{\operatorname{diam} M_t}\right\}>0
\end{align*}
has the required property. Every term is independent of the acting
group, and overall metric scalings leave the quotient ratio unchanged. This completes the proof.

Let $p:\widetilde M\to M$ be the universal Riemannian covering and $\Gamma$ its deck group. Note that every isometry of $M$ lifts to an isometry of $\widetilde M$. Let $\widetilde G\subset\operatorname{Isom}(\widetilde M)$ be the group of all
lifts of elements of $G$. It contains $\Gamma$, and $p\circ\tilde g=g\circ p$ when $\tilde g$ lifts $g$.
For $\tilde x,\tilde y\in\widetilde M$ over $x,y\in M$ and $\tilde g$ over $g$, we have
$d_M(x,gy)=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma}d_{\widetilde M}(\tilde x,\gamma\tilde g\tilde y)$, hence
\begin{align*}
    \inf_{g\in G}d_M(x,gy)=\inf_{\tilde g\in\widetilde G}d_{\widetilde M}(\tilde x,\tilde g\tilde y).
\end{align*}
So $M/G$ and $\widetilde M/\widetilde G$ are isometric, and $\operatorname{diam}(M/G)=\operatorname{diam}(\widetilde M/\widetilde G)$. If
this number is positive, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} gives
$\operatorname{diam}(M/G)\ge\varepsilon_r\operatorname{diam}\widetilde M\ge\varepsilon_r\operatorname{diam} M$, because the covering map $p$ does not increase distances.

\label{ex:rank}
There is no positive lower bound independent of rank, even among
simply connected irreducible compact symmetric spaces.
For $r\ge2$, let $M_r=\operatorname{Spin}(2r+1)$ have the
bi-invariant metric pulled back from the scalar product
$-\operatorname{tr}(AB)/2$ on $\mathfrak{so}(2r+1)$. This is a simply connected
irreducible compact symmetric space of rank $r$. Right multiplication
by $G_r=\operatorname{Spin}(2r)$ has quotient the unit sphere
$S^{2r}$, of diameter $\pi$.
On $SO(2r+1)$, the element with $r$ planar rotation blocks of angle
$\pi$ and one fixed coordinate lies at distance $\pi\sqrt r$ from
the identity. Indeed, in this bi-invariant metric the distance is
the least norm of a skew-symmetric logarithm, and these blocks have
minimal rotation angles $\pi$. A covering local isometry does not
increase distances under projection, so $\operatorname{diam} M_r\ge\pi\sqrt r$.
Consequently
\begin{align*}
0<\Delta(M_r,G_r)\le\frac1{\sqrt r}\longrightarrow0.
\end{align*}
Thus dependence on $r$ in \cref{thm:main} is necessary.

\label{ex:products}
If factors may be scaled independently, a fixed-rank gap fails for
reducible symmetric spaces. Let
\begin{align*}
M_t=S^2(1)\times S^2(t),\qquad t>0,
\end{align*}
where the arguments denote the sphere radii. Let $SO(3)$ act
transitively on the first factor and trivially on the second. The
space is simply connected and symmetric of rank two, but
\begin{align*}
\Delta(M_t,SO(3))=
 \frac{\pi t}{\sqrt{\pi^2+\pi^2t^2}}
 =\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}\longrightarrow0.
\end{align*}
This explains the irreducibility hypothesis when only the total
diameter is normalized.

This example does not address the case in which every de Rham
factor has diameter $1$. A corresponding fixed-rank statement under
that normalization would require a further argument for actions
that couple or permute the factors.

\label{ex:rank-one-tail}
 Let $m\ge15$ and view the unit sphere $S^{2m-1}$ as the unit sphere
in the real matrix space $\mathbb R^{m\times2}$ with its Frobenius norm.
The connected group $SO(m)\times SO(2)$ acts by
$X\mapsto AXB^{-1}$. Its orbits are determined by the two singular values. To see why
special orthogonal matrices suffice, start with an ordinary singular-value
decomposition. A sign change in a right singular vector can be matched
by the same sign change in the corresponding left singular vector.
The determinant of the left matrix can then be corrected in the unused
orthogonal complement, whose dimension is $m-2>0$. Thus there exist matrices
$U\in {SO}(m)$ and $V\in{SO}(2)$ such that $ X=U\begin{pmatrix}
    \Sigma\\ O
    \end{pmatrix}V,$
where $\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)$ and
$\sigma_1\geq \sigma_2\geq 0$.
Therefore, every orbit contains the matrix $X_\theta$ whose only
nonzero entries are $(X_\theta)_{11}=\cos\theta$ and
$(X_\theta)_{22}=\sin\theta$, with $0\le\theta\le\pi/4$.
The curve $\theta\mapsto X_\theta$ has speed one, so the quotient
diameter is at most $\pi/4$. For the reverse inequality, the orbit of
$X_0$ consists of unit rank-one matrices $ab^T$, with $|a|=|b|=1$.
Their inner products with $X_{\pi/4}$ satisfy
\begin{align*}
\langle ab^T,X_{\pi/4}\rangle_{\mathrm{Frob}}
 =a^T X_{\pi/4}b\le\|X_{\pi/4}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\frac1{\sqrt2},
\end{align*}
where $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ denotes the operator norm of a matrix, i.e.,
$\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\max_{|v|=1}|Av|.$
Their spherical distance from $X_{\pi/4}$ is therefore at least
$\pi/4$. The quotient has diameter exactly $\pi/4$, and
\begin{align*}
\Delta(S^{2m-1},SO(m)\times SO(2))=\frac14<\frac1\pi.
\end{align*}
Here $S^{2m-1}=X_{\mathbb R,1,2m}$ and $2m>28$. This example explains the
real rank restriction in \cref{thm:tail}.
