<!-- definition-1: body_md -->
\label{def:maximal-affine-interval}
For a real sequence $(a_0,\dots,a_d)$, define its \textit{discrete Laplacian} by
\[
\Delta a_i \coloneqq 2a_i-a_{i-1}-a_{i+1}, \qquad 1\le i\le d-1.
\]
We intentionally suppress the negative sign by convention.
Note that $(a_i)_i$ is a concave sequence if and only if $\Delta a_i\ge0$ for all $1\le i\le d-1$.

An index interval $[r,s]$, with $0\le r<s\le d$, is \emph{affine} for $(a_i)_i$ if $\Delta a_i=0$ for every $r<i<s$,
and is \emph{maximal affine} if it is maximal under inclusion among affine index intervals.

<!-- theorem-1: body_md -->
\label{thm:endomorphism-peripheral-growth}
Let $X$ be a normal projective variety of dimension $d\ge2$ over $\mathbf{k}$,
and let $f\colon X\to X$ be a surjective endomorphism.
For every $1\le i\le d-1$, let $\mu_i$ be the $i$-th peripheral polynomial-growth exponent of $f$.
Then the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textnormal{(\arabic*)}]
\item If $\lambda_i(f)^2>\lambda_{i-1}(f)\lambda_{i+1}(f)$, then $\mu_i=0$.
Equivalently, every Jordan block of $f^*|_{\mathsf{N}^i(X)_\mathbf{C}}$ associated with an eigenvalue of modulus $\lambda_i(f)$ has size one.

\item If $\lambda_i(f)^2=\lambda_{i-1}(f)\lambda_{i+1}(f)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-discrete-Laplacian}
0\le \Delta \mu_i = 2\mu_i-\mu_{i-1}-\mu_{i+1}\le4.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, if $[r,s]$ is a maximal affine interval of $(\log\lambda_i(f))_i$, then
\[
\mu_i\le2(i-r)(s-i)
\qquad(r\le i\le s).
\]
In particular,
\[
\mu_i\le2i(d-i)
\qquad(0\le i\le d).
\]
<!-- end theorem-1 -->
<!-- corollary-1: body_md -->
\label{cor:zero-entropy-degree-growth}
Under the hypotheses of \cref{thm:endomorphism-peripheral-growth}, assume further that $f$ has zero entropy.
Then $\mu_i=\mu_{d-i}\in2\mathbf{Z}_{\ge0}$ for every $0\le i\le d$ and, for every $1\le i\le d-1$,
\[
\Delta \mu_i = 2\mu_i-\mu_{i-1}-\mu_{i+1}\in\{0,2,4\}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\deg_i(f^n)
\asymp
\left\|(f^n)^*|_{\mathsf{N}^i(X)_\mathbf{R}}\right\|
\asymp
n^{\mu_i}
=O(n^{2i(d-i)})
\qquad (n\to\infty).
\]
<!-- end corollary-1 -->
<!-- remark-1: body_md -->
At a strict break, i.e., the inequality \eqref{eq:lambda-log-concave} is strict, \cref{thm:endomorphism-peripheral-growth} gives semisimplicity only on the peripheral spectrum: it implies neither that $\lambda_i(f)$ is simple nor that the entire action on $\mathsf{N}^i(X)_\mathbf{C}$ is semisimple, although Jordan blocks at smaller-modulus eigenvalues affect only exponentially lower-order terms.
Dang--Favre \cite[Theorem~1.1]{DF21} proved a stronger codimension-one asymptotic for dominant rational maps in characteristic zero, and Favre--Wulcan \cite[Theorem~D]{FW12} proved a sharper arbitrary-codimension asymptotic for rational monomial maps; our result instead treats every codimension for projective endomorphisms in arbitrary characteristic and gives the sharp discrete-Laplacian bound and the resulting affine-interval bound.
All three values $0,2,4$ in \cref{cor:zero-entropy-degree-growth} can occur simultaneously.

<!-- corollary-2: body_md -->
\label{cor:kahler-growth}
Let $Y$ be a compact K\"ahler manifold of dimension $d\ge2$, and let $g\colon Y\to Y$ be a holomorphic automorphism of zero topological entropy.
Then for all $0\le p,q\le d$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-kahler-pq}
\left\|(g^n)^*|_{H^{p,q}(Y,\mathbf{C})}\right\|
=O\bigl(n^{p(d-p)+q(d-q)}\bigr)\qquad (n\to\infty).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, every Jordan block of $g^*|_{H^{p,q}(Y,\mathbf{C})}$ has size at most $p(d-p)+q(d-q)+1$.
<!-- end corollary-2 -->
<!-- remark-2: body_md -->
Polynomial degree and norm growth for zero-entropy automorphisms was known for surfaces (see, e.g., \cite[Lemma~5.4]{AVdB90} or \cite[Appendix]{DF01}).
It was revived by Lo Bianco \cite{LB19}, who proved $\mu_1\le4$ in dimension three.
Dinh--Lin--Oguiso--Zhang \cite[Theorem~1.1]{DLOZ22} proved the optimal bound $\mu_1\le2d-2$ in the K\"ahler setting and
obtained the general estimate $O\bigl(n^{(p'+q')(d-1)}\bigr)$ on $H^{p,q}$,
where $p'=\min\{p,d-p\}$ and $q'=\min\{q,d-q\}$;
the first-degree bound was later extended to arbitrary characteristic in \cite[Corollary~1.6]{HJ25}.
The exponent in \eqref{eq:intro-kahler-pq} improves their Hodge-bidegree exponent by $p'(p'-1)+q'(q'-1)$ and is sharp for every $(p,q)$ \cite[Remark~4.1]{DLOZ22}; see also \cref{thm:sharpness-main}\ref{thm:abelian-hodge-sharpness}.
Our result also recovers \cite[Theorem~1.1]{DLOZ22} and \cite[Corollary~1.6]{HJ25} by a different method; see \cref{rmk:specializations}.

<!-- theorem-2: body_md -->
\label{thm:zero-entropy-mixed}
Let $X$ be a normal projective variety of dimension $d\ge2$ over $\mathbf{k}$, and let $f\colon X\to X$ be a zero-entropy automorphism.
For every $\gamma\in\mathsf{Comp}(d)$ and every tuple $\mathbf{H}$ of ample Cartier divisors, $\Phi_{f,\gamma,\mathbf{H}}$ is a multivariate quasipolynomial whose period may be chosen to depend only on $f$.
Every constituent polynomial has even total degree, and the total degree of the
quasipolynomial satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-zero-entropy-mixed-bound}
\deg_{\mathrm{tot}}\Phi_{f,\gamma,\mathbf{H}}
\le
b_\gamma\coloneqq
 d^2-\|\gamma\|_2^2
 =
 2\sum_{1\le r<s\le d}\gamma_r\gamma_s.
\end{equation}
In particular, $\Phi_{f,\gamma,\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{n})=O\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{n}\|_\infty^{b_\gamma}\bigr)$, and for every $\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbf{Z}^d$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-zero-entropy-ray}
\Phi_{f,\gamma,\mathbf{H}}(n\boldsymbol{\ell})=O(n^{b_\gamma})
\qquad(n\to\infty).
\end{equation}
<!-- end theorem-2 -->
<!-- remark-3: body_md -->
The mixed orbit functions in \cref{thm:zero-entropy-mixed} are related to Xie's mixed degrees \cite[\S3.2]{Xie-SC}.
For dominant rational self-maps, Xie obtained two-sided comparisons with ordinary degree sequences and determined their exponential growth up to subexponential factors \cite[Proposition~3.3 and Corollary~3.4]{Xie-SC}.
At zero entropy, these estimates do not detect the remaining polynomial correction.
In the functorial setting, our \cref{thm:zero-entropy-mixed} gives multivariate quasipolynomiality and the sharp total-degree bound \eqref{eq:intro-zero-entropy-mixed-bound}.

<!-- remark-4: body_md -->
\label{rmk:specializations}
The two basic specializations of \cref{thm:zero-entropy-mixed} at once recover degree growth and polynomial volume growth.
Taking
\[
\gamma=(i,d-i,0,\ldots,0),\qquad
\boldsymbol{n}=(n,0,\ldots,0),\qquad
\mathbf{H}=(H,\ldots,H),
\]
gives
\[
\deg_i(f^n)=\Phi_{f,\gamma,\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{n})
=O\bigl(n^{2i(d-i)}\bigr).
\]
The case when $i=1$ was proved in \cite[Corollary~1.6]{HJ25}.
On the other hand, recall that
\[
\operatorname{plov}(f)
\coloneqq
\limsup_{n\to+\infty}
\frac{\log\bigl((\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}(f^m)^*H)^d\bigr)}
     {\log n}.
\]
Expanding the intersection product and applying the theorem with $\gamma=(1,\ldots,1)$ gives $n^d$ terms of order $O(n^{d^2-d})$, and hence $\operatorname{plov}(f)\le d^2$, recovering \cite[Corollary~1.2]{HJ25}; the refined estimate $\operatorname{plov}(f)\le(\mu_1/2+1)d$ was obtained in \cite[Theorem~1.1]{HJ25}.
                                                                                                                                                                                                    
Polynomial volume growth was introduced in \cite{CPR21}, and its relation to Gelfand--Kirillov dimension was observed in \cite{LOZ25}, building on \cite{ATVdB90,AVdB90,Keeler00}; the dynamical intersection polynomials introduced in our recent work \cite{HJ-plov-lower} to prove the sharp lower bound for $\operatorname{plov}$ are prototypes of the present mixed orbit functions.
Thus \eqref{eq:intro-zero-entropy-mixed-bound} places these one-parameter invariants inside one sharp bound for arbitrary weak compositions.

\label{def:stabQ}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space and let $Q\in\operatorname{Sym}^d(V^\vee)$ be a non-zero symmetric $d$-linear form.
\begin{enumerate}
\item An automorphism $T\in \operatorname{GL}(V)$ is called a \textit{$Q$-similitude} if \[Q(Tv_1,\ldots,Tv_d)
=
\chi(T)Q(v_1,\ldots,v_d)\]
for some $\chi(T)\in \mathbf{R}^*$ and all $v_1,\ldots,v_d\in V$. If $\chi(T)=1$, we call $T$ a \emph{$Q$-isometry}.                                                                                                                                   
    \item The \textit{infinitesimal stabilizer} of $Q$ is defined by
\[
\mathfrak{stab}(Q) \coloneqq \left\{ N\in\mathfrak{gl}(V): \sum_{i=1}^dQ(v_1,\ldots,Nv_i,\ldots,v_d)=0 \text{ for all }v_1,\ldots,v_d\in V \right\}.
\]
\end{enumerate}

<!-- lemma-1: body_md -->
\label{lem:stabQ}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space and let $Q\in\operatorname{Sym}^d(V^\vee)$ be a non-zero symmetric $d$-linear form.
For $N\in\mathfrak{gl}(V)$, one has $N\in\mathfrak{stab}(Q)$ if and only if $e^{tN}$ is a $Q$-isometry for every $t\in\mathbf{R}$, that is, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etN-preserves-Q}
Q(e^{tN}v_1,\ldots,e^{tN}v_d)=Q(v_1,\ldots,v_d)\, \text{ for every }  t\in\mathbf{R}  \text{ and } v_1,\ldots,v_d\in V.
\end{equation}
If moreover $N$ is nilpotent, it suffices to verify \eqref{eq:etN-preserves-Q} at $t=1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eN-preserves-Q}
Q(e^Nv_1,\ldots,e^Nv_d)=Q(v_1,\ldots,v_d)\, \text{ for all } v_1,\ldots,v_d\in V.
\end{equation}
<!-- end lemma-1 -->
<!-- proof-1: body_md -->
For fixed $v_1,\ldots,v_d$, define a real-valued function
\[
g(t)\coloneqq Q(e^{tN}v_1,\ldots,e^{tN}v_d), \qquad t\in \mathbf{R}.
\]
Multilinearity of $Q$ gives
\[
g'(t)=\sum_{i=1}^d
Q(e^{tN}v_1,\ldots,Ne^{tN}v_i,\ldots,e^{tN}v_d).
\]
Thus $N\in\mathfrak{stab}(Q)$ implies $g'\equiv0$.
Hence $g\equiv g(0)$ which is exactly \eqref{eq:etN-preserves-Q}.
The converse follows by differentiating the equation \eqref{eq:etN-preserves-Q} at $t=0$, which gives $g'(0)=0$.

If $N$ is nilpotent, then the above function $g$ is a polynomial.
If \eqref{eq:eN-preserves-Q} holds, iteration gives $g(n)=g(0)$ for every $n\ge1$; hence $g-g(0)$ has infinitely many zeros and is identically zero.
The reverse implication is immediate.

<!-- lemma-2: body_md -->
\label{lem:multi-JC}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space, let $0\ne Q\in\operatorname{Sym}^d(V^\vee)$, and let $T\in\operatorname{GL}(V)$ be a $Q$-similitude.         
   
                    
  
                     
   
                            
Write
\[
T=Se^N=e^NS
\]
for the multiplicative Jordan--Chevalley decomposition of $T$. Then $S$ and $e^N$ are  $Q$-similitudes and $N\in\mathfrak{stab}(Q)$.
<!-- end lemma-2 -->
<!-- proof-2: body_md -->
Denote by 
   
$\operatorname{GSim}(Q)$ the set of all $Q$-similitudes. 
                                                                                                                
Then it is the stabilizer of $[Q]$ for the natural $\operatorname{GL}(V)$-action on $\mathbf{P}(\operatorname{Sym}^d(V^\vee))$, and hence is an algebraic subgroup.
Its action on the invariant line $\mathbf{R} Q$ defines an algebraic character
\[
\chi\colon\operatorname{GSim}(Q)\longrightarrow\mathbf{G}_m.
\]
Applying the Jordan decomposition theorem for algebraic groups                                                                                                                                                                         
(see \cite[Theorem~4.4(1), (4)]{Borel91} or \cite[Theorem~15.3(a), (c)]{GTM21})  
          
to $T=Se^N\in \operatorname{GSim}(Q)$ gives
\[
S,e^N\in\operatorname{GSim}(Q)
\]
and identifies $\chi(e^N)$ with the unipotent part of $\chi(T)\in \mathbf{G}_m$, which is just $1$.
                                                                      
                             
Thus $e^N$ is a $Q$-isometry, and \cref{lem:stabQ} gives $N\in\mathfrak{stab}(Q)$.

<!-- definition-3: body_md -->
\label{def:HL-package}
A \emph{Hodge--Lorentz package of degree $d\ge2$} is a triple $(V,\mathcal{C},Q)$ consisting of a finite-dimensional real vector space $V$, a nonempty open convex cone $\mathcal{C}\subset V$, and a symmetric $d$-linear form $Q\in\operatorname{Sym}^d(V^\vee)$ satisfying the following conditions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textnormal{(\roman*)}, ref=\textnormal{(\roman*)}]
\item Positivity: For every $h_1,\ldots,h_d\in\mathcal{C}$,
\[
Q(h_1,\ldots,h_d)>0.
\]
\item Lorentzian: For every $h_1,\ldots,h_{d-2}\in\mathcal{C}$, the symmetric bilinear form
\[
Q(-,-,h_1,\ldots,h_{d-2})
\colon
V\times V\longrightarrow\mathbf{R}
\]
has positive index at most one.
                                             
                                                                                                                                                                                              
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end definition-3 -->
<!-- definition-4: body_md -->
\label{def:dynamical-HL-package}
A \emph{dynamical Hodge--Lorentz package of degree $d\ge2$} is a quadruple $(V,\mathcal{C},Q,T)$, where $(V,\mathcal{C},Q)$ is a Hodge--Lorentz package of degree $d$ and $T\in\operatorname{GL}(V)$ is  a $Q$-similitude satisfying 
 $T(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathcal{C}$.                                                                                                                  
Such $T$ is called a \emph{cone-preserving $Q$-similitude}.
If $T=e^N$ is unipotent, we call the package \emph{unipotent}.

\label{ex:geometric-HL-models}
The two principal geometric models in application are the following. The corresponding geometric applications will be developed in \Cref{sec:applications}. 
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textnormal{(\roman*)}]
\item Let $X$ be a normal projective variety of dimension $d\ge2$ over $\mathbf{k}$.
Denote by $\operatorname{Amp}(X)\subset\mathsf{N}^1(X)_\mathbf{R}$ the ample cone of $X$.
Then
\[
V=\mathsf{N}^1(X)_\mathbf{R},
\qquad
\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{Amp}(X),
\qquad
Q(D_1,\ldots,D_d)=D_1\cdots D_d
\]
is a Hodge--Lorentz package by the mixed Hodge index theorem (see, e.g., \cite[Proposition~2.9]{Hu20a}).
For a surjective endomorphism $f$ of $X$, $f$ is finite and by the projection formula, $T=f^*\in \operatorname{GL}(V)$ is a cone-preserving $Q$-similitude with $\chi(T)=\deg(f)$. So $(V,\mathcal{C},Q,T)$ is a dynamical Hodge--Lorentz package of degree $d$.
                                                                                                                                                                           

\item Let $Y$ be a compact K\"ahler manifold of dimension $d\ge2$.
Denote by $\mathcal{K}_Y\subset H^{1,1}(Y,\mathbf{R})$  the K\"ahler cone of $Y$.
Then
\[
V=H^{1,1}(Y,\mathbf{R}),
\qquad
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{K}_Y,
\qquad
Q(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)
=
\int_Y\alpha_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_d
\]
is a Hodge--Lorentz package by the mixed Hodge--Riemann relations \cite{Gromov90,DN06}.
For a surjective holomorphic endomorphism $g$ of $Y$, $g$ is also finite and by the numerical characterization of K\"ahler cone \cite{DP04}, $T=g^*\in \operatorname{GL}(V)$ is a cone-preserving $Q$-similitude. So $(V,\mathcal{C},Q,T)$ is a  dynamical Hodge--Lorentz package of degree $d$.
\end{enumerate}

\label{def:mixed-Q-coefficients}
Let $(V,\mathcal{C},Q)$ be a Hodge--Lorentz package of degree $d\ge2$,
let $\gamma\in\mathsf{Comp}(d)$, and let $\boldsymbol{v}=(v_1,\ldots,v_d)\in V^d$.
Define the \textit{mixed $Q$-coefficient} by
\[
Q_\gamma(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq Q(v_1^{[\gamma_1]},\ldots,v_d^{[\gamma_d]}),
\]
where $v_j^{[\gamma_j]}$ means that $v_j$ appears $\gamma_j$ times with $v_j^{[0]}$ omitted.
                                                                                                                                                                        
      
                                                                                                         
   
                                                                                       
   
Equivalently, polarization gives
\[
F_Q\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^d x_iv_i\Biggr)
=
\sum_{\gamma\in\mathsf{Comp}(d)}
Q_\gamma(\boldsymbol{v})\frac{x^{\gamma}}{\gamma!},
\]
where $\gamma!\coloneqq \gamma_1!\cdots\gamma_d!$ and $x^{\gamma}\coloneqq x_1^{\gamma_1}\cdots x_d^{\gamma_d}$.
Thus $Q_\gamma(\boldsymbol{v})$ is the coefficient of $x^\gamma/\gamma!$ in the expansion of $F_Q$.

\label{def:mixed-orbit-functions}
For $\boldsymbol{n}=(n_1,\ldots,n_d)\in\mathbf{Z}^d$, set
\[
T^{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{h}
\coloneqq
(T^{n_1}h_1,\ldots,T^{n_d}h_d)
\]
and define the \emph{mixed orbit function}
\[
\Phi_{T,\gamma,\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{n})
\coloneqq
Q_\gamma(T^{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{h})
=
Q_\gamma(T^{n_1}h_1,\ldots,T^{n_d}h_d).
\]

If the package is unipotent, i.e., $T=e^N$, then the mixed orbit function has the \textit{canonical polynomial extension}
\[
\Phi_{T,\gamma,\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{t})
\coloneqq
Q_\gamma\bigl(
e^{t_1N}h_1,\ldots,e^{t_dN}h_d
\bigr),
\qquad
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbf{R}^d.
\]

<!-- definition-7: body_md -->
\label{def:unipotent-shift-poly}
For $\boldsymbol{n}=(n_1,\ldots,n_d)\in\mathbf{Z}^d$ and $\boldsymbol{t}=(t_1,\ldots,t_d)\in\mathbf{R}^d$, define the \emph{unipotent-shift polynomial} of $\Phi_{T,\gamma,\boldsymbol{h}}$ at $\boldsymbol{n}$ by
\begin{align*}
\phi_{\gamma,\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{t})
&\coloneqq
Q_\gamma\bigl(
e^{t_1N}S^{n_1}h_1,\ldots,
e^{t_dN}S^{n_d}h_d
\bigr)=Q_\gamma\bigl(
e^{(t_1-n_1)N}T^{n_1}h_1,\ldots,
e^{(t_d-n_d)N}T^{n_d}h_d
\bigr).
\end{align*}
Thus $\phi_{\gamma,\boldsymbol{n}}$ is a polynomial in $\boldsymbol{t}$, even though $T$ may not be unipotent, and
$\phi_{\gamma,\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{n})=\Phi_{T,\gamma,\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{n})$.
If $T=e^N$ is unipotent, then for every $\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbf{R}^d$, one has
$\phi_{\gamma,\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{t})=\Phi_{T,\gamma,\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{t})$,
independently of $\boldsymbol{n}$.

When $\gamma=(i,d-i,0,\ldots,0)$ has only two nontrivial blocks and $\boldsymbol{n}=(n,0,\ldots,0)$, by \cref{lem:stabQ}, the corresponding unipotent-shift polynomial $\phi_{\gamma,\boldsymbol{n}}$ at $\boldsymbol{n}$ is
\[
Q\bigl((e^{t_1N}S^nh_1)^{[i]},(e^{t_2N}h_2)^{[d-i]}\bigr)=Q\bigl((e^{(t_1-t_2)N}S^nh_1)^{[i]},h_2^{[d-i]}\bigr).
\]
Define the one-variable unipotent-shift polynomial at $n$ by
\[
\phi_{i,n}(t)
\coloneqq 
Q\bigl((e^{tN}S^nh_1)^{[i]},h_2^{[d-i]}\bigr)
=Q\bigl((e^{(t-n)N}T^nh_1)^{[i]},h_2^{[d-i]}\bigr).
\]
If $T=e^N$ is unipotent, this polynomial is independent of $n$.

<!-- theorem-3: body_md -->
\label{thm:lorentzian-ptolemy} 
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space and let $B\colon V\times V\to \mathbf{R}$ be a symmetric bilinear form with positive index at most $1$.
                                                            
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textnormal{(\arabic*)}]
\item {\it Reverse Cauchy--Schwarz inequality}: For every $v_1, v_2\in V$ with $B(v_1, v_1)\geq 0$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RCS-KT}
B(v_1, v_2)^2\geq B(v_1, v_1)B(v_2, v_2).
\end{equation}

\item {\it Four-point Ptolemy inequality}: If $v_1, \dots, v_4\in V$ satisfy $B(v_i, v_j)\geq 0$ for all $1\leq i, j\leq 4$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ptolemy}
\left|\sqrt{B(v_1,v_2)B(v_3,v_4)}-\sqrt{B(v_1,v_4)B(v_2,v_3)}\right|
\le
\sqrt{B(v_1,v_3)B(v_2,v_4)}.
\end{equation}
                               
   
                                  
                                  
                            
   
                                   
\end{enumerate}
<!-- end theorem-3 -->
<!-- proof-3: body_md -->
(1) We may assume that $B(v_1, v_1)>0$, since otherwise the inequality is clear.
The Gram matrix
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
B(v_1,v_1) & B(v_1,v_2)\\
B(v_1,v_2) & B(v_2,v_2)
\end{pmatrix}
\]
of $v_1,v_2$ has exactly one positive eigenvalue by \cite[Corollary~4.5.11]{HJ13} and hence nonpositive determinant.
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          
                                                                                                                  
This proves the reverse Cauchy--Schwarz inequality \eqref{eq:RCS-KT}.

(2) Write $b_{ij}\coloneqq B(v_i,v_j)\ge0$.
The Gram matrix $(b_{ij})_{1\le i, j\le 4}$ has at most one positive eigenvalue by \cite[Corollary~4.5.11]{HJ13}.
Subtracting a positive-semidefinite matrix cannot increase the positive index.
Therefore, after subtracting the nonnegative main diagonal, the matrix
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}
0 & b_{12} & b_{13} & b_{14}\\
b_{12} & 0 & b_{23} & b_{24}\\
b_{13} & b_{23} & 0 & b_{34}\\
b_{14} & b_{24} & b_{34} & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\]
has at most one positive eigenvalue.
If $M$ is singular, then $\det(M)=0$.
If $M$ is nonsingular, then its zero trace and positive index at most one force its signature to be $(1,3)$.
Thus $\det(M)\leq0$ in all cases.
Set
\[
a=\sqrt{b_{12}b_{34}},
\qquad
b=\sqrt{b_{13}b_{24}},
\qquad
c=\sqrt{b_{14}b_{23}}.
\]
A direct computation gives
\[
\det M = \bigl(b^2-(a+c)^2\bigr)\bigl(b^2-(a-c)^2\bigr).
\]
Since $0\le (a-c)^2\le (a+c)^2$ and $b\ge0$, the inequality $\det M\le0$ is equivalent to
\[
(a-c)^2\le b^2\le(a+c)^2,
\]
or equivalently, $|a-c|\le b\le a+c$.
The first inequality is exactly \eqref{eq:Ptolemy}.

\label{lem:uniform-infinitesimal-ptolemy}

Then there exists a constant $C>0$, depending only on the package and on $\omega_1,\omega_2$, such that the following holds.

 B(T^{m_1}\omega_1,T^{m_2}\omega_2)

 -B(NT^{m_1}\omega_1,T^{m_2}\omega_2)
  B(T^{m_1}\omega_1,NT^{m_2}\omega_2)
\Bigr|\notag\\
&\qquad\le
C B(T^{m_1}\omega_1,T^{m_2}\omega_2)
\sqrt{
 B(T^{m_1}\omega_1,T^{m_1}\omega_1)
 B(T^{m_2}\omega_2,T^{m_2}\omega_2)
}.
            
\end{align}

Choose $\epsilon>0$ sufficiently small such that, for $i=1,2$,

\qquad

x_i-\epsilon x_{i,\epsilon}
=T^{m_i}(\omega_i-\epsilon\omega_{i,\epsilon})
\in\mathcal{C}.
\]
Apply the four-point Ptolemy inequality, \cref{cor:mixed-ptolemy}, to
\[
x_1,\quad x_2,\quad x_{1,\epsilon},\quad x_{2,\epsilon}.
\]
Multiplying the resulting inequality by the sum of its two left-hand square roots yields

D_\epsilon
&\le
\sqrt{
 B(x_1,x_{1,\epsilon})B(x_2,x_{2,\epsilon})}\
\Bigl(
 \sqrt{
  B(x_1,x_2)B(x_{1,\epsilon},x_{2,\epsilon})}
 +
 \sqrt{
  B(x_1,x_{2,\epsilon})B(x_2,x_{1,\epsilon})}\ 
\Bigr),
\end{align*}
where
\begin{align*}
D_\epsilon
&\coloneqq
 \bigl|B(x_1,x_2)B(x_{1,\epsilon},x_{2,\epsilon})
 -B(x_1,x_{2,\epsilon})B(x_{1,\epsilon},x_2)\bigr|.
\end{align*}

& \le 
B(x_i,\epsilon^{-1}x_j)
=\epsilon^{-1}B(x_i,x_j);\\
B(x_{i,\epsilon},x_{j,\epsilon})
& \le

=\epsilon^{-2}B(x_i,x_j).
\end{align*}

\begin{align}\label{eq:De<=BBB}

\le
2\epsilon^{-2}

\sqrt{
 B(x_1,x_1)B(x_2,x_2)
}.
\end{align}

\end{equation}

Dividing \eqref{eq:De<=BBB} by $\epsilon^2$ proves \eqref{eq:uniform-infinitesimal-ptolemy} with $C=2\epsilon^{-4}$.

\label{thm:turan-upper}
Let $(V,\mathcal{C},Q,T)$ be a dynamical Hodge--Lorentz package of degree $d\ge2$, and write $T=Se^N=e^NS$ for the multiplicative Jordan--Chevalley decomposition of $T$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:turan-upper}
\bigl|\mathscr{T}_{rs}(\phi_{\gamma,\boldsymbol{n}})(\boldsymbol{n})\bigr|
\le
C_{\gamma,r,s}\,\phi_{\gamma,\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{n})
\sqrt{
\phi_{\gamma^{r\leftarrow s},\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{n})
\phi_{\gamma^{s\leftarrow r},\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{n})
}.
\end{equation}
Here the $\phi_{*,\boldsymbol{n}}$ are the unipotent-shift polynomials at $\boldsymbol{n}$, as in \cref{def:unipotent-shift-poly}.

\label{lem:turan-degree-detection}
Let $d\ge2$ and let $P\in\mathbf{R}[t_1,\ldots,t_d]$ be a polynomial of positive total degree.
Assume that
\[
P(\boldsymbol{t}+c\boldsymbol{1})=P(\boldsymbol{t})
\]
for every $\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbf{R}^d$ and $c\in\mathbf{R}$, where $\boldsymbol{1}=(1,\ldots,1)$.
Then there exist distinct $r,s$ such that
\[
\deg_{\mathrm{tot}}\mathscr{T}_{rs}(P)=2\deg_{\mathrm{tot}} P-2.
\]
<!-- end lemma-4 -->
<!-- proof-8: body_md -->
Set $M\coloneqq \deg_{\mathrm{tot}}P\in \mathbf{Z}_{>0}$.
Let $P_M$ be the top homogeneous part of $P$.
Recall that
\[
\mathscr{T}_{ij}(P_M) \coloneqq (\partial_iP_M)(\partial_jP_M) - P_M\partial_i\partial_jP_M.
\]
Diagonal translation invariance gives $\sum_j\partial_jP=0$ and hence $\sum_j\partial_jP_M=0$.
It follows that for every $i$,
\begin{align}
    \sum_j\mathscr{T}_{ij}(P_M)=0. \label{eq: sum Tij=0}
\end{align}
Euler's identities give
\begin{align}
\sum_{1\le i\le d}t_i\partial_iP_M=MP_M,
\qquad
\sum_{1\le i,\, j\le d}t_it_j\partial_i\partial_jP_M=M(M-1)P_M. \label{eq: eulerx2}
\end{align}
Consequently, we have
\begin{align*}
\frac12\sum_{1\le i,\, j\le d}(t_i-t_j)^2\mathscr{T}_{ij}(P_M)
&=-\sum_{1\le i,\, j\le d}t_it_j\mathscr{T}_{ij}(P_M)\\
&=P_M\sum_{1\le i,\, j\le d}t_it_j\partial_i\partial_jP_M - \Biggl(\sum_{1\le i\le d}t_i\partial_iP_M\Biggr)^2\\
&=-MP_M^2\neq 0,
\end{align*}
where the first equality is by \eqref{eq: sum Tij=0} and the last is by \eqref{eq: eulerx2}.
                          
Hence there exist $r\ne s$ such that $\mathscr{T}_{rs}(P_M)\ne0$.
Note that the degree-$(2M-2)$ homogeneous component of $\mathscr{T}_{rs}(P)$ is exactly $\mathscr{T}_{rs}(P_M)$.
Hence $\deg_{\mathrm{tot}}\mathscr{T}_{rs}(P)=2M-2$.

\label{thm:sharpness-main}
Let $E$ be an elliptic curve over an algebraically closed field $\mathbf{k}$, let
$A=E^d$, and let $u\colon A\to A$ be induced by
\[
U=I_d+J_d\in\operatorname{GL}_d(\mathbf{Z}),
\]
where $J_d$ is the standard (upper-triangular) nilpotent Jordan block.
Then for every tuple $\mathbf{H}=(H_1,\ldots,H_d)$ of ample divisors on $A$, the following hold.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textnormal{(\arabic*)},ref=(\arabic*)]
\item \label{thm:abelian-mixed-sharpness} For every $\gamma\in\mathsf{Comp}(d)$,
$\Phi_{u,\gamma,\mathbf{H}}$ extends to a polynomial satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta-mixed-sharp}
\deg_{\mathrm{tot}}\Phi_{u,\gamma,\mathbf{H}}=b_\gamma\coloneqq d^2-\|\gamma\|_2^2 = 2\sum_{1\le j<k\le d} \gamma_j\gamma_k.
\end{equation}
Moreover, if the entries of
$\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbf{Z}^d$ are pairwise distinct on $\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta-sharp-ray}
\Phi_{u,\gamma,\mathbf{H}}(n\boldsymbol{\ell})\asymp n^{b_\gamma}.
\end{equation}
In particular, for every $0\le i\le d$ and ample divisor $H$ on $A$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-growth-sharp}
\deg_{i}(u^n)\coloneqq (u^n)^*H^i\cdot H^{d-i} \asymp n^{2i(d-i)}.
\end{equation}

\item \label{thm:abelian-affine-interval-sharpness}                                                                                                    
                                                        
                                                                                                                  
               
                                                                     
                                                        
                                                                
                                                  
               
For every $0\le r<s\le d$, there is an isogeny $f_{r,s}$ of $E^d$ such that $[r,s]$ is a maximal affine interval of $( \log\lambda_i(f_{r,s}))_i$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive-affine-interval}
\mu_i(f_{r,s})=2(i-r)(s-i)
\qquad(r\le i\le s).
\end{equation}

\item \label{thm:abelian-hodge-sharpness} Assume that $\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{C}$.
For every $0\le p,q\le d$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hodge-norm-growth-sharp}
\left\|(u^n)^*|_{H^{p,q}(A,\mathbf{C})}\right\|
\asymp
n^{p(d-p)+q(d-q)}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the largest Jordan block of $u^*|_{H^{p,q}(A,\mathbf{C})}$ has size
\[
p(d-p)+q(d-q)+1.
\]
\end{enumerate}

Thus the mixed, degree, affine-interval, and Hodge-bidegree bounds, as well as the discrete Laplacian upper bound $4$, in \cref{thm:endomorphism-peripheral-growth,thm:zero-entropy-mixed,cor:zero-entropy-degree-growth,cor:kahler-growth}, are sharp.

\label{lem:sum-square}
For every $\gamma\in\mathsf{Comp}(d)$ and $\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbf{R}^d$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta-sum-of-squares}
\frac{\Phi_{u,\gamma,\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{t})}{\gamma!}=
\sum_{\substack{\Gamma_j\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}\\[2pt] |\Gamma_j|=\gamma_j,\, 1\le j\le d}}\det
\begin{pmatrix}
(U^{t_1})_{\Gamma_1}\\
\vdots\\
(U^{t_d})_{\Gamma_d}
\end{pmatrix}^{\!2},
\end{equation}
where $(U^{t_j})_{\Gamma_j}$ denotes the submatrix formed by the rows indexed by $\Gamma_j$, and the corresponding block of rows is empty when $\gamma_j=0$.

Fix $\gamma\in \mathsf{Comp}(d)$.
Both sides of \eqref{eq:theta-sum-of-squares} are polynomials in $\boldsymbol{t}$.
Indeed, $N$ and $L=\log U$ are nilpotent, so that $e^{tN}\Theta$ and
$U^t=e^{tL}$ depend polynomially on $t$.
It therefore suffices to prove the identity for $\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbf{Z}_{\ge0}^d$.

Recall that $\Theta_n=(u^n)^*\Theta$.
For $\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbf{Z}_{>0}^d$, set
\[
\Theta(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq \sum_{j=1}^d x_j\Theta_{n_j}.
\]
Under the product principal polarization $\Theta$, the Rosati involution is transpose on integral matrices.
Hence the endomorphism
\[
\phi_\Theta^{-1}\phi_{\Theta(\boldsymbol{x})}
=
\sum_{j=1}^d x_j(u^{n_j})^\dagger u^{n_j}
\]
is represented by the matrix
\[
S(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq \sum_{j=1}^d x_j(U^{n_j})^{\mathsf{T}}U^{n_j}\in \operatorname{M}_d(\mathbf{Z})\cap\operatorname{GL}_d(\mathbf{Q}).
\]
The Riemann--Roch theorem \cite[\S16]{Mumford} and $\deg(M\colon E^d\to E^d)=(\det M)^2$ then yield
\[
\left(\frac{\Theta(\boldsymbol{x})^d}{d!}\right)^2 = \deg(\phi_{\Theta(\boldsymbol{x})})
= \deg(\phi_\Theta^{-1}\phi_{\Theta(\boldsymbol{x})}) = \det S(\boldsymbol{x})^2.
\]
Both quantities before squaring are positive, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intersection-determinant}
\frac{1}{d!}
\left(\sum_{j=1}^d x_j\Theta_{n_j}\right)^d
=
\det\left(\sum_{j=1}^d
x_j(U^{n_j})^{\mathsf{T}}U^{n_j}\right).
\end{equation}
Since both sides are polynomials in $\boldsymbol{x}$, this identity holds identically.

\[
\mathcal{U}=
\begin{pmatrix}
\sqrt{x_1}U^{n_1}\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{x_d}U^{n_d}
\end{pmatrix}
\in \operatorname{M}_{d^2\times d}(\mathbf{C}).
\]
Since
\[
\overline{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{U}
=
\sum_{j=1}^d x_j(U^{n_j})^{\mathsf{T}}U^{n_j},
\]
the Cauchy--Binet formula \cite[\S0.8.7, p.~28]{HJ13} applied to $\overline{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{U}$ gives
\[
\det\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^d

=
\sum_{\substack{\Gamma_j\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}\\[2pt]

\left(\prod_{j=1}^d x_j^{|\Gamma_j|}\right)

\begin{pmatrix}
(U^{n_1})_{\Gamma_1}\\
\vdots\\
(U^{n_d})_{\Gamma_d}
\end{pmatrix}^{\!2}.

Polynomiality together with the Zariski density of $\mathbf{Z}_{\ge0}^d$ completes the proof.
